# Research Commentary

# The Disciplines of Information: Lessons from the History of the Discipline of Medicine

David G. Schwartz

Graduate School of Business Administration, Bar-Ilan University, Ramat Gan 5290002 Israel, david.schwartz@biu.ac.il

n this research commentary we show that the discipline of information systems (IS) has much that can Ibe learned from the history of the discipline of medicine. We argue that as interest in historical studies of information systems grows, there are important historical lessons to be drawn from disciplines other than IS, with the medical discipline providing fertile ground. Of particular interest are the circumstances that surrounded the practice of the medical craft in the 1800’s—circumstances that drove a process of unification and specialization resulting in the modern conceptualization of medical education, research, and practice. In analyzing the history of the field of medicine, with its long-established methods for general practice, specialization, and sub-specialization we find that it serves as an example of a discipline that has dealt effectively with its initial establishment as a scientific discipline, exponential growth of knowledge and ensuing diversity of practice over centuries, and has much to say in regards to a number of discipline-wide debates of IS. Our objective is to isolate the key factors that can be observed from the writings of leading medical historians, and examine those factors from the perspective of the information systems discipline today. Through our analysis we identify the primary factors and structural changes which preceded a modern medical discipline characterized by unification and specialization. We identify these same historic factors within the present-day information systems milieu and discuss the implications of following a unification and specialization strategy for the future of the disciplines of information.

Keywords: critical perspectives on information technology; institutional aspects of information systems; medical discipline; specialization; history; academia; professions; DI

## 1. Introduction

Recent years have seen a surge of scholarship devoted to chronicling, analyzing, studying, and attempting to draw lessons from the history of the information systems (IS) discipline (Bryant et al. 2013, Gray 2012, Hirschheim and Klein 2011, Lukaitis et al. 2010, Lunt et al. 2010, Markus 2011). Studying the history of our own discipline is important in that, as Bryant et al. (2013) state, “disciplinary histories are ‘made’ at least as much as they are ‘discovered,’ and that the relationship between ‘the past itself’ and the ‘historian’s thought is a key factor.” (p. 3). Land (2010) argues that the historiography of IS is important to understanding IS and its evolution over time. Mitev and De Vaujany (2012) discuss alternative ways of both writing and studying the history of IS.

The historic study of IS serves not only to measure progress and provide a source of pride in our accomplishments, but as a basis for justification, formulation, or Elucidation of some aspect of the disciplinary nature of IS. As Hirscheim and Klein (2012) state, “Collectively, the insights derived from an historical analysis are a prerequisite for IS researchers to make informed judgments (not to mention to engage in a discourse across the many specializations) about the scope of IS research and teaching and where the field could and should go in the future.”

This burgeoning body of scholarship begs the questions of whether the lessons we should be learning are necessarily drawn from the history of our own discipline. For while our disciplinary history is both honorable and intense—it is also relatively brief.

In this article, we will argue that there is much to be gained from studying the history of other seemingly unrelated disciplines to draw historic lessons that can inform our future disciplinary path. For this purpose, through reasons that we will present below and not to the exclusion of other disciplines, we have chosen the discipline of medicine.

The history of IS and its sister disciplines is impressive by all measures. Over the past 50 years, advances in information and communication technologies (ICT) and the ubiquity of information appliances has brought ICT into every aspect of our organizational, professional, and personal lives (Yoo 2010). The discipline of IS has grown in parallel to this phenomenon alternatively leading, shaping, studying, and helping us understand wide-ranging impacts. Grover (2012) presents the IS discipline as a complex adaptive system, existing in an increasingly complex environment. Ignoring the need to consolidate the growing diversity and complexity of IS research can lead, in Grover’s (2012) words, to “make the sheer volume of literature ominous to navigate for a new researcher, leading to avoidance of potentially important topics” (p. 267). Many proposals and formulations, including Davis (2000), Lyytinen and King (2004), and Alter (2008) have been put forward with the goal of defining and solidifying the IS discipline and ensuring its vibrant future. More recently, Somers (2010) has taken the bold step of suggesting that the theory of professions might be useful in mapping a new view of the IS discipline—a view with which we wholeheartedly agree and will elaborate on. Bryant (2008) took a conceptual leap in suggesting that the basic notions being discussed in the IS literature regarding discipline purpose and discipline formation are mistaken and we must go about the process differently—a view that we will, to a certain degree, apply in a practical sense in this article. In assessing the future directions of the management information system (MIS) field, Markus succinctly wrote “0 0 0 it is time for the MIS field and for information management practitioners to assess and possibly re-engineer our own ‘legacy processes,’ not just those of other areas of management.” (Markus 2011, p. 13).

While the speed with which extreme levels of complexity and diversity have reached the IS discipline may be anomalous and unique, the phenomenon itself is not. The state of IS research and practice today parallels that of the medical discipline of the mid-1800s in a number of significant ways. In this paper, we will argue that not only can we seek disciplinary lessons from our own 50-year legacy, but can greatly benefit by studying the past 200 years of the medical discipline. We will further argue that the medical field,<sup>1</sup> with its long-established methods for general practice, specialization, and subspecialization can serve as an example of a discipline that has dealt effectively with its initial establishment as a scientific discipline, exponential growth of knowledge and ensuing diversity of practice over centuries, and has much to say regarding the disciplinewide debates collected in King and Lyytinen (2006). Our primary focus is on identifying lessons from the disciplinary changes seen in the history of medicine and questioning the potential value of those lessons to the IS discipline.

## 1.1. Why Medicine?

We recognize that there are many disciplines and professions from which lessons may be drawn. Medicine exhibits significant complexity characteristics that point toward its suitability for this task, including the complexity of medical knowledge, rate of growth of knowledge considered part of the discipline, and the level of codependence and interaction between seemingly independent subareas of knowledge (Flexner 1910, Gibson 1932, James and Hammond 2000, Prislin et al. 2010, Rastegar 2004). In fact, we find that disciplines as diverse as law (Ariens 1993, Greenwood and Frederickson 1964, Joiner 1953, Kilpatrick 1996, McCullough 1970, Siddall 1956) and chemistry (Lynch 1946) have turned to medicine seeking lessons that can inform their own disciplinary specialization processes. Bennis and O’Toole (2005) suggest medical education is one area to examine in efforts to find a new model for the business schools which many departments of IS currently call home.

The discipline of medicine has proven to us that seemingly intractable bodies of knowledge dealing with complex interrelated phenomenon, in which expertise is not only valued but required, can be effectively harnessed and managed. While hundreds of years ago a competent physician would possess all of the medical knowledge then available, today the complexity of medical knowledge has resulted in the creation of a complex organization of specialists where no one knows more than a small part but the community as a whole advances medical knowledge and integrated care (Chandrasekaran 1981). It has long been recognized (Hill 1962) that it is impossible to train a doctor in depth for any one of the branches of medicine until the postgraduate stage and that a broad core is sought in the early stages of medical education (Cumming and Ross 2008).

## 1.2. Things We May Not Want to Learn from Medicine

There are, of course, a number of reasons we might want to avoid following the path taken by medicine. In fact, 100 years after the Flexner report that revolutionized American medical education, severe criticisms have been leveled at a system that overly emphasizes scientific knowledge over biologic understanding, clinical reasoning, practical skill, and the development of character, compassion, and integrity (Cooke et al. 2006, Irby et al. 2010, Tousijn 2006), and calls made for additional reform. Challenges faced include outdated curricula, archaic assessment practices, and disproportionate regulatory constraints (Castellani and Hafferty 2006, Cooke et al. 2006). Other problems include an abandonment of social responsibility, unreasonable training costs and unwieldy costs of teaching hospitals (Beck 2004, Mechanic et al. 1998, Tosteson 1990,

Woollard 2006). Medical research and practice as well cannot escape criticisms not unlike those faced by medical education: systemic publication of poor-quality research (Altman 2002), an environment plagued by malpractice litigation (Cohn 1966, Sloan and Chepke 2008), ineffective medical specialization structures and processes (Leeming 2001), specialization leading to the loss of a holistic patient-centric view (Stevens 2003), and diagnostic errors (Hashem et al. 2003) are a few. Medical professionalism does not lack for challenges (Leeming 2001, Miles 2002).

These shortcomings do not necessarily leave us with nothing to learn from, as learning from the history of another discipline does not simply imply blindly following in its footsteps. On the contrary—given the opportunity to study both the historic considerations of another discipline, and the result of its historic choices, can make the lessons all the more valuable. So while medicine may have made some better or worse disciplinary choices, this only adds to the potential value of studying its history and to the need to make sure our study is thorough and in depth.

1.3. Ways in Which Medicine Differs Significantly Aside from being cognizant of the problems faced by the discipline of medicine, we must conduct our study fully aware of the fundamental areas in which that field differs from the IS discipline. This includes the significant differences in the object of study. For medicine, it is a finite entity—the human body—the science for which does not significantly change on a daily or even annual basis as oppose to the near continual change in IS. True, new diseases are encountered and new treatments discovered, but the borders of medicine are far narrower and far less fluid than those of IS in which an information technology (IT) artifact can take multiple forms and will continue to evolve into the unforeseen future (DeSanctis 2003).

The medical discipline also inhabits a complex and well-established institutional structure, which includes hospitals affiliated with and often owned by universities, hospitals funded and controlled by governments, various forms of health insurance that impact the hospital as well as clinic-based care, and a host of regulatory and licensing bodies around the world. Our intent is to neither blindly embrace nor indiscriminately vilify the medical model, but to consider how certain choices are made and elements adopted may benefit the IS discipline despite these differences.

## 1.4. Other Disciplines to Consider

The study of other disciplines—both historic and current—to inform the directions we take for IS should not be limited to medicine. We find such analogous learning to be widespread in disciplinary analysis. Some examples include: drawing from the discipline of organizational studies to inform nursing (Shanley 2007); the discipline of economics modeling certain aspects after the discipline of geology (Neal 2000); even the field of accounting can be seen drawing disciplinary lessons from law, medicine, and statistics (Miller et al. 1991). We are not claiming that medicine is the archetypal discipline that IS should follow, but rather we believe that the lessons from this field should be considered and debated alongside other well-established disciplines.

Perhaps medicine is an anomaly—a discipline of such unique characteristics that one cannot draw implications for any other field. Freidson, in his widely cited study of the sociology of applied knowledge as it relates to medicine (Freidson 1988), points to certain characteristics of the medical profession that set it apart from all others: “Among the traditional professions established in the European universities of the Middle Ages, it alone has developed a systematic connection to science and technology. Unlike law and the ministry, which have no important connections with modern science and technology, medicine has developed into a very complex division of labor, organizing an increasingly large number of technical and service workers around its central task of diagnosing and managing the ills of mankind.” (p. xiv). These same characteristics are shared by the field of IS in performing its central task of managing the information of mankind.

Yet, despite apparent differences that set medicine apart from other fields, Freidson (1988) argues, “in order to study medicine in such a way as to clarify and extend our understanding of professions in general, one must not adopt medicine’s own concepts of its mission, its skill, and its ‘science.’ Since professions are collective human enterprises as well as vehicles for special knowledge, belief, and skill, sociology can focus on their common organization as groups quite apart from their different concepts, providing the general concepts by which they may be made individually comparable,” and continues to declare that “the study of medicine can be a proving ground for the development of more adequate ways of analyzing occupations and professions than exist in sociology today.” (pp. xvii–xix). This essay builds on these solid foundations by studying the development of the modern field of medicine.

To paraphrase Dearden (1972) in his early and scathing attack on MIS,<sup>2</sup> “The true medical expert does not and cannot exist. A complete human body consists of such a huge assortment of different types of activities that no man can possess a broad enough set of special skills to apply to even a small proportion of them,” yet exist they do. How and why has the discipline of medicine evolved to produce generations of true experts who can deal with such a huge assortment of activities in such a complex environment?

## 2. Specialization and the Field of Medicine

Specialization, as an intellectual orientation, depends on a decision to concentrate on a relatively small field of scientific activity. Specialization as role differentiation depends on an educational system in which such specialized roles can be institutionalized as occupational roles (Stichweh 2001). The act of specializing entails pursuing a particular line of study or work (Dogan 2001). Organizational complexity is often operationalized and measured through professional specialization and the associated level of required professional qualifications (Dooley 2002). While IS specialties are prolific, IS specialization—in any meaningful sense—is not. Specialized roles such as database (DB) administrators, business process analysts, security specialists, human computer interaction (HCI) specialists, software engineers exist, however, these specialties are driven by innovation in hardware and software; driven by business and social necessity; driven by a myriad publication and conference venues incessantly pushing differentiation. Driven in many different directions often lacking strategy, structure, or standards. The Association for Information Systems (AIS) (2012) suggests 25 categories for research and teaching specialization. The Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) lists 37 special interest groups (SIGs) ranging from bioinformatics to electronic commerce with groups covering such interests such as the Ada programming language. Technical committees, of which International Federation for Information Processing (IFIP) has 13 covering 121 working groups (IFIP 2012a), have the ability to focus research efforts, identify important subjects, promote exchange of ideas, foster interdisciplinary work, and more (IFIP 2012b), but they have not resulted in, nor do they state as their goal, any form of professional or research specialization structure. SIGs and technical committees are common and perform an important role, yet should not be confused with specialization.

Medicine presents a very different picture.

Today the American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS 2011) recognizes more than 150 specialties and subspecialties; the UK General Medical Council (GMC 2011) approves 95 specialties and subspecialties although 288 specialties appear on the UK Specialist Register. The American Medical Association (AMA 2011) lists 179 specialties over 27 groups.

The current state is the result of over 150 years of organization and reorganization, standardization, research, and a nontrivial dose of political wrangling. Today the field of medicine is based on core studies common to all medical fields, internship-based clinical education tying knowledge to practice, and a system of specialization offering numerous and diverse focal areas.

It was not always so.

Until the 1800s the field of medicine was characterized by a simple hierarchy of physicians, surgeons, and apothecaries (Brunton 2004, Gelfand 1976, Schepers 1994, Weisz 2003). It suffered from charlatanism, poor quality control, abundant confusion, and a general lack of cooperation between practitioners across the different streams (Holloway 1964, Pelling 1987). However, in the 1800s a conceptual change took place and waves of unification swept across the medical landscape. By the late 1830s and early 1840s in Paris surgeons and physicians had united under the umbrella of medicine and 12 distinct medical specialties were being pursued. This was followed by similar trends in Vienna, other cities in Europe, and continued throughout the 1850s and 1860s in North America. The pace of change was such that between 1830 and 1850 the number of specialty courses listed in Paris medical directories grew from 10 to 89 (Weisz 2003).

What are the characteristics of a successful specialization structure and what must be in place for such a structure to thrive? Based on a historic review of the discipline of medicine, we identify a significant structural change and six primary factors that drove the modern conceptualization of the field of medicine. We then discuss the extent to which that same structural change and accompanying factors exist regarding the discipline of IS. Based on this analysis, we suggest how emulating the medical experience of unification and specialization would affect the IS discipline in a number of key areas, beginning with its very structure and component disciplines, and conclude with questions for further investigation.

We base our analysis on the work of six medical historians, Gelfand (1976, 1980), Holloway (1964), Juch (1996, 1997), Rosen (1944), Stevens (1966, 1971), and Weisz (1994a, b, 1997, 2003, 2006), as well as the landmark Flexner report of 1910 that is viewed as the single most significant publication influencing the direction taken by medical education in North America (Flexner 1910, Halperin et al. 2010, Irby et al. 2010, Johnson and Green 2010, Prislin et al. 2010). Through their insights into the field of medicine, we seek to shed some light on the state of the discipline of IS today.

## 3. A Brief History of Medical Specialization

Medical historians have produced a series of geographically focused studies that document the development of medical specialization. These studies have covered France (Rosen 1944; Weisz 1994a, 1997, 2003, 2006),

North America (Flexner 1910; Gelfand 1976, 1980; Stevens 1971; Weisz 1997), England (Holloway 1964, Stevens 1966, Weisz 1997), Belgium (Schepers 1994), and the Netherlands (Juch 1996, 1997). Analysis of this history reveals a significant structural change and six contributing specialization factors listed in Table 1 and presented graphically in Figure 1. We will now present each of these briefly. For details regarding the historical context, dates of occurrence, and examples of each factor, the reader is referred to the original historical works cited herein. After reviewing the processes and factors that drove medical specialization, we will discuss which factors are relevant to the IS field and how the resultant specialization structure might benefit the discipline of IS.

## 3.1. Structural Change Setting the Stage for Specialization

The major structural change, which took place as evidenced in the medical history literature was the unification of physicians and surgeons under the umbrella of a single medical discipline. The unification of medicine with surgery occurred both in professional practice and within medical training and research. In the early 1800s, physicians began to abandoned traditional notions of professional roles and started thinking in a new way about how the medical task should be divided. The work of Dr. John Morgan (1765) promoted an approach in which the physic and surgeon would unite and a common agreed-upon core of knowledge would be established for all medical practitioners, after which they could go on to specialize. Once the context of a large and unified domain of medical knowledge became clear, the division into subfields began to be viewed as a necessary conclusion. This unification drove a collective desire of the wide variety of medical practitioners to expand knowledge. It was believed that only specialization would allow the rigorous empirical observation of many cases that had become necessary in academic medicine and that this approach would guarantee a shared and common basis between the various specialties (Flexner 1910; Gelfand 1976, 1980; Holloway 1964; Juch 1997; Weisz 2003, 2006).

## 3.2. Factors Driving Specialization

Once the unification of doctors and surgeons took place, and the belief that specialization would benefit care became more widespread, a series of factors catalyzed the spread of specialization.

Subdivision of the central artifact of medical science into multiple, distinct yet interconnected artifacts. A new conceptualization of disease emerged, which emphasized local anatomy and local pathology. This emerging view of the central artifact of medicine, the human body, and the newfound ability to subdivide this artifact into multiple, distinct yet interconnected artifacts, created foci of interest on specific organ systems. The focus on specific organ systems was further buttressed by the impact of new technological developments such as the ophthalmoscope (Gelfand 1976, Holloway 1964, Juch 1997, Rosen 1944).

The development of specialized technologies. The development of medical technology, including the stethoscope in 1819, the ophthalmoscope in 1851, and the laryngoscope in 1855 drove specialization. Each had the dual characteristics of requiring greater expertise from the physician or operator, and leading to more focused insights by the practitioners who had access to such equipment (Juch 1997, Rosen 1944, Stevens 1971). This fueled demand for specialists leading to a shift in social attitudes.

Emergence of social forces and attitudes conducive to specialization. The institutions—hospitals, journals, scientific societies, teaching institutions—through which the physician acts, started promoting specialization and in the 1870s large hospitals began to officially recognize specialties. Concurrently, the public began to demand increasing levels of specialization and medical practitioners recognized that their income could be maximized by meeting the new demand. In many countries, the state began to encourage specialization as a means to develop the field of medicine as an academic pursuit, as this was believed to result in improved clinical teaching, which could be based on many more related cases (Flexner 1910; Juch 1997; Rosen 1944; Weisz 2003, 2006).

A desire to promote mastery. Mastery of subareas of medical knowledge that would otherwise be superficially treated would be achieved by focusing on the treatment of multiple similar cases. This mastery, achieving the reflective use of sophisticated knowledge, became the precursor of formal clinical medical research that dominates today. The desire to pursue mastery through such focused clinical research became a driver of specialization (Flexner 1910, Weisz 2003).

A desire to promote academic legitimacy. The medical discipline of the 1800s suffered from questions of academic legitimacy not unlike those faced by the discipline of IS. Lacking the epistemological status of the physical sciences, medicine followed a process of professionalization and discipline formation similar to that experienced by physics and chemistry in the preceding century in an attempt to establish academic legitimacy (Flexner 1910, Weisz 2003).

New educational concepts such as combining university and hospital began to emerge (Flexner 1910, Holloway 1964, Juch 1997, Weisz 2003).

## 4. Discussion: Implications for the IS Discipline

Mastery, academic legitimacy, and research and practice synergy—each of these is high on the list of important issues sought in and debated by our field. What might our field learn from the unification and specialization model of medicine? To address that question, we first present a model for unification and specialization as derived from medicine.

## 4.1. Unification

We define unification in the sense it is used here as the consolidation of multiple, possibly competing, intellectually diverging, and overlapping yet focally distinct fields of pursuit into a single disciplinary structure. The result of unification of physicians and surgeons in medicine did not mean that every physician must now be proficient at performing surgery. It meant that every surgeon would be required to have a broader part of what was traditionally physician’s knowledge, and every physician would be required to have a greater understanding of how surgery could be applied to their patient base. Unification did not eliminate two fields and create a single homogenized field—it allowed two fields to create a broad common base from which would emerge dozens of new subfields. What occurred in practice was delineation based on the unification of certain elements and differentiation of others.

There is no question that medicine today is one of the most complex, diverse, regulated, and specialized disciplines that exist. The leading medical historians have shown that the process of unification and specialization was a major turning point in the history of that discipline. The disciplines of IS (Agarwal and Lucas 2005, Benbasat and Zmud 2003, Khazanchi and Munkvold 2000, Lucas 1999), computer science (CS) (Comer et al. 1989, Denning 2005, Tedre 2011), information science (Froehlich 1989, Goffman 1970, Griffiths 2000, Wang and Pontes 2005), and IT (Lunt et al. 2010) have all being faced with challenges regarding their legitimacy, relevance, delineation, and academic standing at different points in their brief histories. We suggest that it is time to consider the creation of a unified field with a broad specialization structure. We will devote the remainder of this article to the following question: How might a process of unification and specialization benefit IS and a number of closely related disciplines as its well-established medical counterpart?

## 4.2. The Disciplines of Information (DI)

Unification provides fertile ground for discussion and debate. The first question that we need to consider: Unification of what? For the purpose of this discussion, let us consider four related areas as candidates for unification: CS, IS, information science, and IT. As this would result in a rather unwieldy acronym, and so as not to offend any sensibilities, let us refer to this group as the DI.

To be clear, we are not suggesting that all four disciplines be eliminated and replaced with a combined disciplinary morass. Rather following what occurred in medicine points us toward a new delineation based on the unification of certain elements and differentiation of others. For example, specifically as it regards CS, we are not suggesting that the totality of the discipline of CS dissolve and find itself incorporated in a new discipline together with the totality of the IS discipline. There are elements of CS, such as computability, operating systems, and compilers that are, and will remain, unique scientific components of that field. However there are elements of that noble field, such as DBs, applications, and networking that need to be incorporated into a unified DI that would coexists alongside CS.

Unification of the DI would undoubtedly require much deliberation as to the creation of a common core with elements drawn from each contributing discipline. Our purpose here is not to determine what ultimately that unified core should be but rather lead to the conclusion that it must, in fact, be created through a clear process. The unification of surgery and the different types of medical practice into a single recognized core of knowledge required adaption from all related practitioners and researchers. It was a difficult process, but one led by visionaries such as Morgan (Flexner 1910, Gelfand 1976).

Hirschheim and Klein (2011), in mapping the history of IS, do not explicitly discuss unification of fields. However, they do contend that the gaps that have developed through the growth and diversification of IS communities can be bridged through recognizing the shared historical backdrop of a number of constituent fields in what we have termed the DI, and declare dual goals of creating shared understanding and spurring IS toward a more consensual future. We suggest that the shared understanding that they advocate, and the common history that they identify, goes even broader and deeper than IS streams and, in fact, can be extended to encompass all of DI—making the endeavor they propose all the more difficult, yet all the more important to a unification process. Contrary to Robey’s concerns (1996), unification does not have to come at the expense of embracing diversity. Medicine shows us that the strength that comes from unification of the core can provide a platform from which even greater diversity emerges.

Unification implies the creation of a common core. Hirschheim and Klien (2003) sounding the alarm of a growing disconnect between academia and the world of business, argue for the creation of a common core of knowledge, though make it clear that “Such a structure should not be seen as an attempt to ‘unify the field.’ ” (p. 241). They even use a medical analogy (though not in the same sense that we are drawing upon the actual history of the medicine in this article). Much work in identifying a core has already been done, but how useful is it as a basis for a unification process? Sidorova et al. (2008) used latent semantic analysis of a decade’s published research in three of the leading IS journals to produce factor sets of 5 top areas, 13 midrange areas, and 100 peripheral areas of IS research. These sets of factors are presented as candidate taxonomies for the discipline of IS. Benbasat and Zmud (2003) propose a core set of properties that define the IS field. At the core of discipline, they place the IT artifact and its immediate nomological net. Another core candidate is provided by Avison and Elliot (2006). Surveying the IS literature, from both academic and industry sources, over the years 1988–2003, they find nine focal research categories.

In his discussion of Wirtschaftinformatik (WI), the discipline of research on IS in German-speaking countries, Frank (1998) supports this position and puts one of the necessary pieces for unification in place in his discussion of research methodologies. He advocates the inclusion of multiple research methodologies in an attempt to “develop a common conception of science that fits all disciplines and can be adapted to serve the peculiarities of IS research at the same time” (p. 1). The unification of research methodologies resulting in a broadening of comparable results serves as one basis for the type of disciplinary unification we are advocating and indicates an ability to subsume multiple research methods within DI.

Rather than “unnecessarily narrow4ing5 the borders of the field in the direction of computer science,” as feared by Lyytinen and King (2004), unification with elements of CS would provide a broader core, demand more expertise, and result in higher quality and more relevant IS research—in the same way that unification of physical medicine and surgery demanded greater core knowledge of the surgeon and created synergies between surgery and medicine that had not previously existed. Medicine is a hugely diverse field, yet the specialties of psychiatry and orthopedic surgery, for example, which may seem to have little in common, both stem from the same common scientific core. For us to achieve the same in DI means conducting a relentless and ongoing dialogue between industry leaders, academia, and professional societies to reach an initial core, an initial specialization structure, and a formal structured assessment process that will allow us to modify and improve the initial state.

The main shortcoming of much of the above research for our purpose, providing input to unification, is that they are descriptive. They are produced inductively from the study of what is currently considered IS research via the leading journals. The history of medicine teaches us how a common core can be transformational, but it also shows us that unification should commence before the core is determined. Once the core is established it becomes the common denominator for all practitioners and researchers, irrespective of how esoteric their desired specialization. It becomes the bedrock upon which generations of research and practice can be built.

In advocating unification, we are not advocating a position that every piece of IS research must include a CS-like IT artifact element. Nor are we suggesting that our field focus attention exclusively on an artifact for as Lyytinen and King (2004) note “artifacts never deliver value in their own right.” To do so would be similar to suggesting that every oncology research include the heart or brain or digestive system—when the research itself is focused on cell structure—it would serve no purpose. However, the oncologist has studied the heart, brain, and digestive system before specializing in oncology and is able to draw on that knowledge where relevant to their cell-level research or treatment or consider the impact of their research or treatment on those parts of medicine’s core artifact. An IS researcher or graduate who has not studied organizational forms, has not been trained in the basics of computer networks and system architectures, is not acquainted with the basics of DB structures, will be less able to effectively draw on the fundamentals of IS to create more robust and relevant research and practice.

This position is consistent with Orlikowski and Iacono’s clarification (2006).

By focusing attention on the IT artifact, we did not suggest that activities around conceptualizing or theorizing the artifact should constitute the only work of the field or that research that did not include such a focus was somehow illegitimate or irrelevant. What we did do was point to what appears to be a blind spot in our field, and that is that despite our common interest in IT, much of our research takes it for granted or ignores it when conducting empirical investigations or building theoretical models (p. 288).

Unification of the DI and creating a broad set of common core proficiencies moves us toward rectifying these deficiencies and providing a solid foundation for an effective specialization structure.

## 5. The Potential Benefits of Unification and Specialization

It may be helpful to frame our discussion of unification and specialization in terms of stability and change, whereby unification is the guardian of stability and specialization the advocate for change. Farjoun (2010) rethinks the classic dualism that views stability and change as separate, competing, antithetical forces. He suggests that a more accurate conceptualization is that of a duality in which stability and change enable each other in a supportive relationship—in that having a stable core frees an organization to pursue change, and that change, once achieved, serves to strengthen stability going forward. Applying the concept of Farjounian duality to unification and specialization in a disciplinary context, unification becomes the provider of the stable mechanisms and institutions, which lead to effective change. Specialization, associated with innovation and change, is thus enabled and supported by stable structures and formalizations.

Unification sets the stage for specialization, which can bring a number of clear benefits to our field. We will focus on three of these—research and practice synergies; mastery; and academic legitimacy, and touch briefly on the potential of changing social attitudes. A summary of the parallels and potential appears in Table 2.

## 5.1. Research and Practice Synergies

The tight coupling between medical practice and research began in the early days in which physicians would record their own patients case histories and attempt to draw conclusions regarding symptoms, diagnoses, and treatment protocols. The same tight coupling continues today in teaching and research hospitals where the practice of medical care informs research interests on a daily basis.

One needs to look no further than the intense focus on action research (Baskerville and Myers 2004, Kock and Lau 2001) to realize that the potential for synergies between research and practice abound in the IS discipline. Yet, such synergies are still lacking. In her analysis of the importance of context in IS, Avgerou (2001) decries the limited perceptions of context seen in our field calling for broader terms of reference to be found in organizational processes. Chiasson and Davidson (2005) call for more attention to industry in IS theory and research. McGrath (2005) finds a disconnect between theory and practice in IS critical research, with one not informing the other.

The European traditions of design science, design science research (Land et al. 2009, Winter 2008), and WI (Frank 1998, 2006) emphasize the importance of research and practice synergies, while recognizing the difficulties in achieving them with our current academic and industry structures.

A field that is based fundamentally on the interactions between humans, organizations, technologies, and information cannot flourish if the natural ties between research and practice are neglected. This synergy comes naturally for medicine due to the hospital clinical environment in which much medical research takes place and in which medical students spend clinical rotations in each specialty department as part of their basic training. Acquainting students with different specialized contexts as part of their IS education would enable them to better choose and focus on specialization areas following their core studies, becoming better practitioners or researchers.

The discipline of IS currently has no such equivalent structure, however, it is not for lack of opportunity. Every Fortune 500 and Forbes Global 2,000 company, without exception, is a potential clinical environment for IS education and research. And every major IT vendor exists in an ecosystem, which can foster education and research that is inherently linked to practice. This important development in medical training occurred more than 100 years ago and went through many difficulties of adaption as the relationship between hospital and university slowly took shape (Flexner 1910).

Indeed, we must remain aware of the potential pitfalls when research becomes too closely aligned with practice. At an extreme, these include overt commercialization of research agenda—a clear and present danger in the medical community (Baird 2003) and science in general (McGarity 2008); threats alongside opportunities in ICT research (Benhamou et al. 2010); and a threat to academic freedom leading to dilution of pure, long-term academic research goals that may not serve a near-term practical need (Lee 1996, Poyago-Theotoky et al. 2002). Maintaining such awareness will be necessary to minimizing the risk to new research horizons—and, in fact, such steps are being taken in medical research and teaching, as documented by a task force of the Association of American Medical Colleges (2008). Despite these risks, the lesson offered by medicine is that synergies between research and practice can be enhanced by an appropriate specialization structure.

## 5.2. Mastery Through Focus

The issue of “lack of focus,” as raised by Avison and Elliot (2006), echoes what Weisz (2003) found to be a key driver of medical specialization 150 years earlier. But contrary to the former state, lack of focus in the IS discipline is not necessarily due to “excessive diversity” in research. Diversity where there is no unified broad common core might result in lack of focus, but let us not confuse the lack of focus with the lack of a unified common core. Excessive diversity is not the cause of lack of focus—it is the result of lack of focus. A unified discipline in which mastery of the core precedes specialization allows those drawn to diversity to specialize from a position of strength.

Arguing the opposite, that lack of diversity is one of the major shortcomings of the IS discipline, Lucas (1999) and Agarwal and Lucas (2005) suggest that focusing on quality will be effective, however, fall short of a comprehensive systemic change. The question is not whether the IS discipline suffers from excessive diversity à la Avison and Elliot (2006) or lack of diversity à la Agarwal and Lucas (2005). The question is how effective diversity—mastery through focus—can be achieved.

What is effective diversity and how can it be achieved? Agarwal and Lucas (2005) provide a prime example in their discussion of the relationship of IS to other disciplines by questioning “0 0 0 the allocation of intellectual resources to develop unique theories in the IS domain when our time might be better spent understanding how existing theories can be fruitfully adapted to the unique nuances of the IT context” (p. 389). Not that IS is not deserving of its own theories, but that there is value in first mastering the existing core theories before pursuing diversity through theories of our own. More recently, Taylor et al. (2010) have argued specifically that we require “both focus and diversity while maintaining continuity and stability in the discipline” (p. 651) to maintain a polycentric field structure. They also raise concerns of excessive fragmentation by isolated subfields whose research is losing any reference to the core of IS—in essence providing a counterexample of how we stand to lose our disciplinary gravitas should the focus diversity balance be lost. The desire for mastery through focus drove medical specialization and it can inform us as to how IS can achieve effective diversity and specialization.

## 5.3. Academic Legitimacy

Agarwal and Lucas (2005) survey a wealth of literature and devote considerable attention to the pressing issues of IS academic legitimacy—“the IS academic discipline has experienced great difficulty in establishing itself in business schools and other departments such as information science and computer science programs” (p. 382). There can be no question that loss of academic legitimacy is a real and present danger facing the IS community. They argue convincingly against the risk of a narrow or microfocus resulting from adherence to the nomological network proposed by Benbasat and Zmud (2003). We agree wholeheartedly with the arguments put forth by Agarwal and Lucas (2005) regarding the need to broaden the core and scope of IS research to solidify legitimacy. However, based on our study of the history of medicine we suggest that their call for a “macrofocus” in IS research fall short of what is needed. It is not a question of choosing one over the other, but rather embracing the duality of macro- and microfocus, which similar to the arguments put forth by Farjoun (2010), can be supportive rather than divisive.

Furthermore, if the right balance between macrofocus and microfocus is to be found research, it must derive from our disciplinary view as a whole. In requiring its students to develop expertise in the full human body and all its organ systems before specializing in a specific organ system or disease group as pointed out above, medicine combines micro and macro needs quite well both in its education process and in applied research protocols—this too is a result of the unification and specialization structure (Gärtner and Dietze 1989).

Transdisciplinarity is an approach in which multiple disciplinary bodies are crossed to create a holistic approach (Hirsch Hadorn et al. 2008). Galliers (2003) contrasts the nomological net core-based approach with a transdisciplinary view of IS. The latter, he claims, is an opportunity that would enable us to have society as our boundary; people or information as our central artifact; an outbound focus; a broad scope; status as a reference discipline; and emergent properties. As he states, “Indeed, given the breadth of subject matter and interest, our academy may choose to embrace a number of different boundaries simultaneously” (p. 343). But how does one embrace transdisciplinarity without forsaking a disciplinary core?

We believe that all of the transdisciplinarity characteristics are apparent in today’s manifestation of the medical discipline and supported by its unique structure. Indeed one important lesson from medical history is that it is neither the macrofocus nor the microfocus alone that will promote academic legitimacy, rather an astute combination of the two.

Academic legitimacy is supported by enabling micro focus research that is based on macro focus perspective. In other words, it is achievable through a process of unification, which drives a macrofocus, and specialization, which drives a microfocus.

## 5.4. Social Attitudes

We stand to benefit from improved social attitudes toward our discipline. In applying a sociocultural model to view the discipline of IS, Avison and Ein-Dor (2007) find that lack of status and supporting power structures negatively impact the field. As Holloway (1964) notes, following the unification and specialization changes in England during the mid-1800s, both an increase in entry standards and an increase in candidates occurred. “This was an important factor in raising the status of the general practitioner both in society and within the profession, and this in turn led to further advances in educational standards.” (p. 320). The social forces that drove medical unification and specialization inform us that sociocultural perceptions and needs can support the restructuring of a discipline and improve its ability to attract quality people.

## 6. Related Work

Related work comes not from the component discussions above regarding potential benefits of unification and specialization, but rather from the very limited amount of scholarship related to how the discipline of IS should be formed. In other words, we are concerned with finding a viable process for disciplinary evolution rather than prescribing the individual elements that are central to the previously mentioned literature. Therefore the most relevant related work can be drawn from four areas of research: metascience, discipline formation, professionalization, and conceptual unification; and one practical initiative: the iSchools.

## Metascience

As early as 1970, when the DI were all still quite young, Otten and Debons (1970) published a sparsely cited opinion article advocating the creation of a “metascience of information,” which they tagged informatology. Perhaps it was the uninspiring choice of discipline name that has led to the lack of recognition of their thesis, for the ideas they put forth are inspiring indeed.

Otten and Debons (1970) hypothesize that “information and operations on information are phenomena, the principles of which provide the basis for a metascience of information.” Their argument in favor of a metascience of information is based, in part, on three important factors (p. 92): (i) a metascience would provide a common basis upon which all information-oriented specialized sciences and technologies can be understood and studied; (ii) a metascience would provide a common framework and language to serve technologists concerned with information in some form or another; (iii) a metascience would build bridges between the abstract theories attempting theoretical explanations of the phenomena of information on the one side and between theories describing man’s relationship to information phenomena on the other side.

Otten and Debons (1970) conclude that the metascience of information “0 0 0 is studying and describing the fundamental concepts of information and operation on information regardless of its main function—its theories must be equally applicable to computers, biological systems, man, social systems, and man-made IS.” (p. 93). The characteristics and potential benefits of a metascience of information have changed little between 1970 and today. A unified metascience simply has never been implemented as IS, Information Science, CS, and IT were each blown along a course set by their own disciplinary winds. The original metascience considerations exist today pointing us toward a process of unification and specialization similar to what was followed by the discipline of medicine.

Parenthetically, it is interesting to note that Metascience—a review journal that focuses on books in the fields of history and philosophy of science, saw fit to review Weisz’s (2006) most recent book Divide and Conquer: A Comparative History of Medical Specialization (Westmore 2007). In a comment that draws on one of the core concepts of decision support and IS analysis, Westmore (2007) states that “The title partially captures some aspects of specialisation. Dividing and conquering implies an intellectual strategy for problem solving—breaking down complex systems into smaller components—and a political strategy used initially by small numbers of doctors who ended up transforming medical practice.” (p. 573).

While in 1973 Stamper was still of the view that “we should not imagine that our knowledge of information and IS can become an additional science” (Stamper 1973, p. 341). He did call for the establishment of the role of “professional information specialist whose knowledge spans the whole of semiotics” (p. 341).

Galliers’ (2003) focus on transdiscplinarity reconciles well with the metascience view, advocating acceptance and pluralism instead of a narrow disciplinary approach. We observe these desirable transdisciplinary characteristics in the history of the medical field, which provides a viable approach to achieving the same within the DI without sacrificing a strong core.

## Discipline Formation

Bryant (2008) provides food for thought in his discussion of how disciplines are constituted. Claiming that academic IS is a flawed discourse, he argues that we must spend less time debating the IS core and instead encourage critical thought—thinking informatically— rather than seeking to confirm and legitimize the observe information behavior of individuals and organizations. Bryant, quoting Bauman and May (2001), places great emphasis on thinking about the world in different ways. We would argue that the medical field’s move toward unification and specialization had this inherent “new world view” characteristic at heart. The drive to view human anatomy and “subsystems” in new ways; the drive to focus on more specialized issues through the development and use of new technologies; the creation of new or modified institutions to support a new view; all of these share the spark of a “new world view.” The suggestion that IS as a discipline can be rescued by “thinking informatically” can be interpreted in many ways. We suggest that Bryant’s (2008) core elements of information, communication, and technology can be brought together through a process of unification alongside other concepts that have been forth as essential to the IS core, but doing so is a first (and formidable) step to be followed by specialization.

Recognizing the growth and variety of IS core concepts, as well as the emergence of subspecialties, Davis (2000) provides relevant direction, welcoming the level of intersection with other disciplines. He argues that the diversity of research methods and core topics do not demonstrate an immature field, but rather show how a maturing field can incorporate the necessary richness to function in and contribute to a complicated world. Davis’ (2000) evolutionary view in which the core is repeatedly grown with concepts from other disciplines, branched into specialties, and then pruned, describes a pattern not unlike the unification and specialization process, which have characterized the medical discipline for the past 150 years.

## Professionalization

Somers (2010) sets an important course toward professionalization resting on the three legs of academic IS, IS education, and IS practice. In a sense, this article presents a concrete example of how following a professionalization model might work. As Somers (2010, p. 386) notes, “Perhaps the most important advantage of viewing IS as a profession is a focus on process rather than on outcomes; that is, emphasis is placed on understanding the processes by which professions evolve rather than on focusing on where they should be.” While the stages proposed by Somers, consensus building; forging and identity; and socialization are different from those we have derived from the medical discipline, we concur with his conclusions that a professionalization process must take place. IFIP (Hughes 2007) has initiated a program to promote professionalization through the IFIP Professional Task Force, yet as Iivari et al. (2008) comment, “Whilst these goals are laudable, IFIP is somewhat silent on how to effect such professionalization” (p. 16). In fact, they argue in favor of analyzing IS as knowledge work, enhancing the expertise of an IT specialist, creating a specialist body of knowledge (BoK), and thus promoting increase professionalization of IT. They further elaborate upon the establishment of five BoK first discussed in Iivari et al. (2004). Creating such a broad BoK and its ensuing curriculum is essential and our study indicates that the medical model and its wealth of historical experience can lead us to a process of unification and specialization in which professionalization is inherent and a BoK emerges.

## Conceptual Unification

Work on unification of IS concepts has been limited and generally focused on narrow goals rather than the field as a whole. Vessey et al. (2005) present a unified classification system for research that combines the disciplines of CS, software engineering, and IS, motivated by the observation that “the three disciplines share substantial bodies of knowledge and that existing classification systems do not adequately address their combined needs” (p. 254).

Our work is consistent with, and advances the arguments for IS as a reference discipline as articulated by Baskerville and Myers (2002). They present a strong argument based on the state of IS research literature, showing both a research tradition and creation of value, suggesting two ways in which IS can become a reference discipline: a change in mindset regarding the audience for IS research; and making IS research more accessible to researchers in other fields. The factors and preconditions found prevalent in the transformation of medicine support these arguments and can significantly extend that line of thought by advocating a broad yet clearly delineated reference discipline.

## Competing Institutional Logics

Institutional logics are cultural beliefs and rules that shape the thinking and behaviors of actors in organizations, and as a result, shape the policies of the institution and guide the actions of its individual members (Friedland and Alford 1991, Lounsbury 2007, Thornton 2004). Such logics embody the essential workings of the organization and can change over time; though having one dominant institutional logic makes such change difficult resulting in relative permanence (Friedland and Alford 1991, Thornton 2004). Far from having a single dominant institutional logic, the DI appear to fall into a group of institutional environments that are fragmented with conflicting demands (D’Aunno et al. 1991) embracing multiple logics that make agreement difficult and consensus seem impossible. Rather than such conflict and pluralism being taken as a sign that institutional change is not possible, many researchers have found that this often becomes the motivating force for institutional change to occur (Bucher and Strauss 1961, Jones 2001, Seo and Creed 2002, Lounsbury 2007).

Medicine has had its own faculties since medieval times—it is indeed one of the classical seven faculties in universities with strong practical ties to institutionalized professions. In contrast, the DI are historically distributed along numerous schools with different professional interests and traditions including natural sciences, engineering, information or library science, some areas of social science (public administration), and business schools. The resultant separation of the DI within these schools and faculties and their somewhat hostile and ignorant relationships are more likely due to the institutional power structures and related institutional logics, which could (actively or passively) prevent similar processes of unification and separation. Yet, processes to manage competing institutional logics exist—and medicine itself has not been spared the need to reconcile disparate logics and institutional pluralism (Dunn and Jones 2010).

## iSchools and Institution Formation

An important aspect in guiding the development of a discipline, be it toward unification and specialization or in any other direction, is the existence and/or formation of a supporting institutional structure.

One key differentiator of the medical discipline is its unique institutional structure. The importance of medical institutions in the unification and specialization process is cited by Weisz (2003, 2006) as a precondition to many of the changes that took place in the 18th century. It is also emphasized as a dominant influence in present day (Castellani and Hafferty 2006, Schrewe 2013, Warner 2013), and reviewed comprehensively through a Freidsonian lens by Halpern and Anspach (1993). The DI cannot simply copy the institutional structure of medicine—and it is not at all clear that we would if we could. However, what is clear is that no move toward unification and specialization can succeed without an institutional focus.

In fact, it has already proven difficult to restructure IS in academia as the iSchool experience shows. On a practical level, the iSchools organization (“iSchools” 2011) was founded in 2005 to promote what is termed the “information field” serving as a conceptual vehicle that combines elements of CS, librarianship, education, psychology, anthropology, business, and journalism— focusing on the intersection between information, technology, and people. Member schools offer a wide range of information-centric degree programs. The iSchools organization has the declared goal of leading and promoting the information field. In discussing the iSchools, Avison and Ein-Dor (2007) highlight the importance of independent Schools of Information that have the ability to offer specific degrees in IS, raise the status of the discipline, and produce a broader scope of IS research. The member schools, as a group (referred to as the iCaucus), recognize the importance of the issues raised in our discussion—particularly, the need for a broader, more inclusive definition of our field, but each member pursues this goal through baskets of overlapping degree programs.

As the iSchool.org website states: “Each independent iSchool has its own strengths and specializations; together they span the full spectrum of the Information Field”; “Degree programs at iSchools include course offerings in areas such as information architecture, design, policy, and economics; knowledge management, user experience design, and usability; preservation and conservation; librarianship and library administration; the sociology of information; and human-computer interaction and CS.”<sup>3</sup> Many, however, offer only graduate degree programs. 4

A central iSchool goal is to bring all of the DI under one roof resulting in research and teaching synergies as well as the creating critical mass necessary for discipline recognition and significant research funding. Yet, there is a fundamental difference between bringing multiple related degrees under one roof, and creating a unified field. One proven path to a unified field is in an archetypal degree such as that offered by medicine suggesting that this may serve as the basis for professional activity and further specialization. A clear example of the difference between an iSchool granting multiple i-related degrees and a medical school can be seen in the curricular guidelines published by the World Federation for Medical Education (Lindgren 2012). Founding more iSchools in which all of the DI become housed under a single academic roof is perhaps a necessary first step but not sufficient to create a unified field.

The term disciplines of computing (so defined by the Joint Task Force for Computing Curriculum 2005) has been used to refer to the related disciplines of computer engineering, CS, IS, IT, and software engineering. To that end said committee and its subsequent subcommittees embarked on a course of action meant to delineate each component field by identifying their areas of uniqueness and overlap. The approach taken has had many positive results, including the development of independent curricula for each field. However, this approach may have precluded the emergence of a more unified field from which specialties and subspecialties can emerge and thrive.

Perhaps the lessons learned from our study will provide both impetus and guidance for iCaucus members to go even further in redefining and delineating the DI based on the model of unification and specialization.

## 7. Limitations

There are a number of clear differences between the field of medicine and DI ranging from their operating environments to the nature of practice which must be recognized. We are aware that these may influence or mitigate the ways in which IS follows the medical model: the centrality of hospitals; the direct impact on human life; western or social welfare views on the right to medical care; western views on malpractice and litigation; and the focus on individual patients rather than an organizational environment; all must be considered. In addition, there exist differences between medicine and IS regarding both the object of study and objective of study. The central artifact of medicine, complex though it may be, is fairly well defined and bounded and as Iivari et al. (2008, p. 23) point out, “has remained essentially the same for hundreds of thousands of years.” So too, the goals of medicine have been fairly well defined since the days of Hippocrates (460–370 BC). The IT artifact and objectives of IS research and practice, span multiple objectives, environments, systems, and instantiations—and it can be safely said that we have yet to see or even conceptualize all the possibilities in this regard.

Overspecialization in which the common core has been lost is a risk to consider, as Johnston (1957) warns: “There is but one surgery. It has one common heritage. It cannot become irrevocably segmented. The nine surgeries must become one to whatever extent the benefit of the patient dictates. 0 0 0 Surely the background and training of the surgical specialist ought to be equal to, if not better, than that of the general surgeon. It was once, and it seems a reasonable goal to attain again.” (p. 666). This warning can be applied equally to the DI and any specialization agenda that develops must not lose sight of the centrality of a unified core.

We also must not assume that the process of creating a unified and specialized disciplinary structure is a one-time task. The field of medicine continues to show that the process is dynamic and ongoing. In surgery, for example, after 150 years, the question of whether general surgery is a specialty in its own right or a required basis for any further surgical specialty continues to be an open question (Fernández-Cruz 2004). In addition, we recognize that the medical discipline is not without its shortcomings, inefficiencies, and flaws; and that the system of specialization as implemented in modern western medicine may be far from ideal. However, we emphasize that we are chiefly concerned with the conceptual leap that unification and specialization can provide, and that the manner in which the DI implement these lessons may differ from the path taken by medicine.

## 8. Conclusion and Future Directions

We have identified a new way to conceptualize and structure IS and related disciplines that has never been considered and has done so through a comparative sociohistoric analysis. We began by analyzing the history of medical specialization to identify structural change and the factors that were instrumental in the formation of the modern conceptualization of medicine and its specialization structure. We have then shown how four important specialization factors that followed the unification of the medical discipline can be identified within the IS milieu. We have further shown that the unification process, as seen in the history of medicine, is relevant with respect to IS and is worthy of further investigation both conceptually and pragmatically. Based on the parallels identified, we proposed a restructuring of the DI in which unification can unleash the full potential of specialization. Achieving strong results in even three of the factors discussed—academic legitimacy, research and practice synergies, and mastery through focus—would validate the adoption of the lessons of medicine.

Medical specialization was the result of a number of motivators and environmental factors. It was the pragmatic outgrowth of the evolution of the field and the impacts of environment and technology. One might even be tempted to say that it was a sociotechnical result. But it was also the result of a conceptual leap taken by John Morgan, founder of university medical education in the United States, in his discourse of 1765 (Gelfand 1976). It would take almost 100 years for the ideas crystallized by Morgan to significantly alter the medical landscape. We don’t have that long.

Despite some clear differences between the discipline of medicine and the DI at scientific, academic, and practical levels, there remains a strong and clear parallel between the factors that led to today’s conceptualization of the medical field. Following Freidson (1988) the identification and analysis of these factors makes it clear that we have much to learn from the history of medicine. Much work remains to be done in characterizing what will become the core and specialties of DI; what institutional ecology will result; and how specialization would be determined, accredited, and maintained. Likewise, there is much that still must be understood about additional factors and preconditions that may exist specific to our own disciplines.

Which fields must unify? How can divergent or complementary institutional logics be reconciled? Do we need to overhaul university structures to achieve unification and specialization—and if so, how would this be done? These questions and more are open for what is certain to be difficult, passionate, and a fruitful debate. Our hope is that the ideas drawn from the history of the medical discipline will stimulate disruptive thinking and systemic change. Beginning to think in terms of a duality of unification and specialization is a necessary first step. By adopting this mindset, we can begin to actively identify the relevant processes in which unification and specialization can promote more robust education; more meaningful research collaboration and practices; and even a more balanced approach to research relevance and impact assessment.

When, in 100 years’ time, historians document the formative years of IS, will they find the roots of a flourishing discipline as have the medical historians—or will they simply lament what might have been? If the DI as a group, and the academic field of IS, in particular, are to flourish, they must be afforded the necessary infrastructure with which to do so. A disciplinary infrastructure based on unification and specialization stands out as a prime lesson to consider.
