# What Leads to Prosocial Behaviors on Social Networking Services: A Tripartite Model

JUNGWON KUEM, SOUMYA RAY, MIKKO SIPONEN, AND SUNG S. KIM

Her research interests include information security, cyberloafing, smartphone addiction, and online communities. His research interests include IS security, IS development, computer ethics, and philosophical aspects of IS. His editorial board experience includes positions with Journal of the Association for Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information and Management, and Communications of the Association for Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: The major challenge for social networking services (SNS) has been in getting users to exhibit prosocial behavior by active participation in creating and sharing content. We seek to integrate and reconcile the varying, and sometimes conflicting, explanations of prosocial behaviors at SNS. Rooted in postadoption behavior and commitment theory, our study offers three distinct mechanisms that regulate how one’s experience at an SNS translates into commitment that leads to prosocial behavior. In particular, satisfaction, affective commitment, and active participation substantiate a dedication-based mechanism; past investments, continuance commitment, and resulting inattention to alternatives form a constraint-based mechanism; and social support, normative commitment and users’ intentions to moderate comments are a third, obligation-based mechanism. We empirically tested this tripartite model against data collected from actual Facebook users. The results of our analysis supported the proposed relationships between each mechanism’s experiential factor, mediating type of commitment, and prosocial outcome. Intermechanism effects were rare between commitment mediators and outcomes, they were common between experiential antecedents and commitment factors. Understanding these mechanisms allows SNS managers to fine-tune their service experience to promote specific prosocial behaviors. Meanwhile, researchers benefit from our overarching view of prosocial behavior at SNS that helps to combine and contrast emergent perspectives and theories.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: activity in social networks, SNS, social network content, social networking, social support, structural equation modeling, user commitment, user satisfaction.

Social networking services (SNS) have seen their collective fortunes rise as they became ubiquitous in our lives. Several major players emerged in this early gold rush, including Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, Google+, and MySpace. Facebook is now the leading SNS provider with over a billion active daily users [63]. The major challenge thus far for social websites has been in getting users to actively participate in creating and sharing content [4, 36, 39, 50]. But the SNS market has now matured to the point that users are carefully choosing among providers, and their active participation no longer guarantees their loyalty. Facebook has recently seen a drop in interest from its newer teenage users who are flocking to alternative services that allow them to share fewer of their private moments and see less drama from others [37]. This next generation of Internet users wants more carefully controlled content and is cognizant of alternatives when it comes to SNS. In the next chapter of the SNS story, providers must pivot away from focusing relentlessly on the quantity of participation to controlling the quality of contributions and keeping alternative services at bay.

SNS providers seek effective ways to encourage users to commit to a broader set of behaviors that help sustain the growth of their websites [55]. For example, active participation by members certainly remains an important factor for successful SNS [2, 16, 34]. But providers must also counter the draw of alternative services. Getting users to pay less attention to alternatives is a critical outcome that has gained increasing attention in information systems (IS) research [33]. Furthermore, as social networks grow and cover disparate social spheres in people’s lives (friends, family, colleagues, and acquaintances), the act of moderating conflicting views and attitudes among SNS members should be facilitated. Thus, SNS providers can simultaneously encourage their users to participate actively, pay less attention to their alternatives, and moderate messages themselves.

In an attempt to explain individuals’ reactions to online services, Kim and Son [33] developed a dual model of postadoption phenomena. This dual model indicates that two mechanisms, namely, dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms, differently regulate behavioral outcomes such as usage intention, word-of-mouth, inattention to alternatives, and willingness to pay. In particular, it suggests that one’s emotional attachment to an online service (i.e., dedication) influences usage intention and word-of-mouth, but the degree to which a person’s assets are locked into a particular service (i.e., constraint) determines inattention to alternatives and willingness to pay. By drawing on a conceptual model by Bendapudi and Berry [6] in the marketing discipline, this dual model has been shown to be a powerful force for explaining online users’ reactions to online portals [33]. But there is recent interest in extending the explanatory power of the dual model to highly social online contexts such as virtual world services [62]. However, despite its merit as a theoretical tool for online behavior, the dual model is essentially an explanation of individualistic behavior at services such as online news, music, portals, and transactions. It is questionable how completely this theoretical framework explains behavior in socially oriented online contexts where social customs and norms bear heavily on users, such as online communities and social networking.

Bateman et al. [4] developed an online commitment model to explain individual members’ behaviors. Specifically, this commitment model proposes that one’s commitment to a community website significantly affects posting, reading, and moderating behaviors. It further suggests that affective commitment, continuance commitment, and normative commitment influence posting, reading, and moderating behaviors, respectively. Drawing on the notion of organizational commitment in the management area [42], this commitment model originally was intended to explain individuals’ reactions to socially oriented online services. Specifically, the commitment model incorporates an individual’s sense of obligation, or normative commitment, which seems an essential component to properly account for interpersonal relationships. Because it represents a variety of personal commitment toward social groups, the commitment model by Bateman et al. [4] was expected to serve as a useful conceptual tool for explaining social behavior on the Internet. However, this model did not identify specific antecedents of commitment, although these antecedents are implied to some extent within the larger theoretical framework. Moreover, it did not include some of the outcome variables that we now realize have significant implications for SNS providers—for example, individual members’ interest in alternative services.

The dual model by Kim and Son [33] suggested in general the antecedents and outcomes of commitment related to online behavior. Accordingly, it was expected to yield insights into which type of commitment leads to a specific SNS-related behavior (e.g., inattention to alternatives) and the antecedent variables of such commitment. Recently, Zhou et al. [62] successfully adapted the dual model to the socially oriented context of virtual worlds. However, as mentioned previously, the dual model largely does not consider the social obligations that press on SNS users. To address such limitations, the dual model must be extended to incorporate the full range of social ties implied by the commitment theory.

The main objective of this study is to propose and empirically test a model of postadoption phenomena in the context of SNS. This model is specifically intended to show the underlying mechanisms of SNS members’ behaviors that are managerially relevant and critical, namely, active participation, inattention to alternatives, and moderating messages. Consistent with the dual model by Kim and Son [33], our model posits that the dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms regulate online members’ behaviors at the postadoption stage. Furthermore, we extended the dual model by adding the obligation-based mechanism so as to take into account the social aspect of online behavior in the context of SNS. In the tripartite model we propose, the three types of commitment discussed in Bateman et al. [4] serve as mediating variables. In particular, affective commitment, continuance commitment, and normative commitment are expected to play central roles in, respectively, the dedication-, constraint-, and obligation-based mechanisms.

Our tripartite model proposes that each mechanism generally differs in its relationships between intervening variables (i.e., affective commitment, continuance commitment, and normative commitment) and behavioral outcomes (i.e., active participation, inattention to alternatives, and moderating messages). Accordingly, within this framework, intramechanism relationships between commitment and outcomes are expected to be stronger than those of intermechanism relationships. However, the tripartite model posits that because of specific SNS characteristics, the complexity of the effects of the antecedents (i.e., satisfaction, past investments, and social support) on commitment exceed what the mechanisms imply. As a result, each antecedent is expected to have both intramechanism and intermechanism effects on commitment. Overall, we expect to show the applicability of the dual and commitment models to the context of SNS and, more important, highlight the enormous research potential in integrating their two distinctive perspectives and research traditions.

## Theoretical Background

As with Web portals, the contemporary style of SNS provides one-point access to a variety of online resources such as e-mails, news, commercial deals, games, photos, music, and videos. People are initially attracted to SNS because of their potential benefits, but over time, they become locked in because of high switching costs [33, 62]. These characteristics of SNS use that are driven by perceived benefits and switching costs are similar to those of other individually oriented online services. However, although broadly categorized as a type of online service, SNS are unique; unlike other individually oriented online services, SNS are intended for communicating with other users and maintaining relationships with them [20, 47]. SNS members update their friends on personal events to bolster social relationships. In addition, SNS members often form online communities in which they share common interests, information, resources, and activities with other members [46]. Thus, this social aspect (e.g., how members acquire the norms of interacting with others) should be taken into account for a complete assessment of SNS-related phenomena [7].

To explain individuals’ reactions to individually oriented online services, Kim and Son’s [33] model suggested that its dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms explain individuals’ reactions to individually oriented online services. These mechanisms are noticeably distinct in their antecedents, mediators, and outcomes. As depicted in Figure 1, this model proposes that in the dedication-based mechanism, loyalty mediates the impact of perceived benefits (e.g., satisfaction and perceived usefulness) on dedication-based outcomes (e.g., usage intention and word-ofmouth). In the constraint-based mechanism, switching cost mediates the effect of past investments (e.g., learning and personalization) on constraint-based outcomes (e.g., willingness to pay and inattention to alternatives). Kim and Son [33] found, based on 510 users of customizable web portals, that the two mechanisms are quite independent and thus exhibit highly discernible patterns in their causal relationships. That is, the factors in one mechanism generally exhibit significant relationships among themselves, but not necessarily with the factors in the other mechanism. Originally tested in a web portal setting, this model has recently been shown to be successful in explaining online behavior in other types of online services, such as virtual world services [62]. As such, the dual model is likely to yield important insights into individuals’ perceptions and behavior with other online services such as SNS. Nevertheless, the original focus of the dual model was to explain individualistic behavior patterns, and has not yet been adapted to explain the larger set of social commitments that constrain users in highly social online contexts.

In an attempt to describe the mechanisms regulating social dynamics in the online environment, Bateman et al. [4] proposed an online commitment model. This model posits that commitment, which refers to individuals’ psychological attachment to a community website, significantly affects their behavior at the postadoption stage. Within the framework of this model, three types of commitment—affective, continuance, and normative—exist. Figure 2 shows this commitment model, which suggests that in the context of online communities the three types of commitment differ systematically in influencing postadoption outcomes such as reading, posting, and moderating messages. Specifically, Bateman et al. [4] in a study of 192 users of an online discussion website showed that affective, continuance, and normative commitment influence, respectively, posting, reading, and moderating messages. In addition to its application to online communities, the notion of commitment has also been applied successfully to socially oriented online services [62]. Thus, the commitment model is expected to be valuable in shedding new light on the social aspect of online behavior in the context of SNS.

It is important to note that the commitment model closely resembles the dual model; in particular, whereas affective commitment reflects loyalty, switching costs represent continuance commitment [62]. However, unlike the dual model, the commitment model incorporates normative commitment to better reflect the nature of social interactions and norms that play an important role in online social media such as SNS. In contrast, the dual model permits valuable insights into a variety of antecedents and outcomes of commitment related to online behavior in general. ## Research Model and Hypotheses

Figure 3 depicts a tripartite model that consists of the three distinct dedication-, constraint-, and obligation-based mechanisms expected at play in the context of SNS. The model we propose explicitly theorizes that each of the three mechanisms will be mediated by a particular type of commitment, each of which has its own antecedent and behavioral outcome. The three antecedent factors largely cover the forces that pull SNS users in different ways: general satisfaction that endears users to their service, a history of past investments that constrains future choice of service, and the weighty gift of social support that obliges users to participate as good citizens. Drawing on the dual model by Kim and Son [33], we proposed that the dedication-based mechanism centers on affective commitment, which mediates the effect of satisfaction on active participation. Similarly, our understanding of the dual model leads us to propose that the constraint-based mechanism centers on continuance commitment, which mediates the impact of past investments on inattention to alternatives. Finally, we integrated Bateman et al.’s [4] commitment theory to suggest that normative commitment plays a central role in the obligation-based mechanism that results from social support and leads to moderating messages. Although our model is not meant to be comprehensive in every detail, it still aims to powerfully capture the presence of the three mechanisms that differently regulate postadoption phenomena in the context of SNS.

## Dedication-Based Mechanism

The dedication-based mechanism implies that people with favorable experiences with an online service will form an emotional attachment to it and eventually act to promote it [33]. Within the framework of the dual model, this affection-oriented process begins with a person’s recognition that the benefits of an online service outweigh its costs. There are many such positive attributes that attract and endear users, and these attributes could be either general to information technology (IT) services or specific to SNS. But overall satisfaction is a powerful and parsimonious measure of how users perceive the benefits of an online service. It is considered a major driver of the dedication-based mechanism [62]. Prior postadoption studies similarly use satisfaction to holistically capture attractive qualities that keep users attached to IT services [33, 49].

In this study, satisfaction is defined as one’s favorable evaluation of being a member of an SNS [36]. Consistent with the proposition implied by the dual model, a large number of IS studies suggest that an individual’s satisfaction with using an IT application exerts a substantial effect on subsequent perceptions and behavior [18, 36, 45, 54]. A current level of satisfaction serves as a cue for inference of a future level of satisfaction; as a result, satisfied members are likely to have an affective bond with others in their group.

Affective commitment represents an “emotional attachment to, identification with, and involvement in” a social group [41, p. 67]. In this study, we define it as the degree to which an individual is committed to maintaining membership because of an intense emotional attachment to the SNS [33]. Individuals with high affective commitment are known to feel a sense of belonging and consider membership emotionally rewarding [4, 36]. According to the dual model, affective commitment largely arises from satisfaction, which represents the positive benefits of using an SNS [33, 62]. Consistent with the dual model, Oliver’s [44] satisfaction theory indicated that satisfaction is the most important component in forming customers’ affective commitment to a service provider. Similarly, in a meta-analysis of organizational behavior research, Meyer et al. [42] showed that satisfaction is the most important determinant of affective commitment. Similarly, in a study of social virtual world services, Zhou et al. [62] also found that satisfaction mediates the impacts of other factors on affective commitment. These findings support the dual model, which suggests that positive experiences with an SNS will enhance feelings of emotional attachment to it. Thus, we propose that a member’s satisfaction with an SNS will be related positively to affective commitment.

Hypothesis 1: A member’s satisfaction with an SNS has a positive relationship with affective commitment.

The essence of the tripartite model lies in the claim that SNS users maintain their membership because they are emotionally attached (affective commitment), locked in (continuance commitment), or feel obligated to do so (normative commitment). Although the three different types of commitment are expected to lead to membership continuance, behavioral outcomes will differ, depending on why users continue their membership. First, according to the dual model, online users with high affective commitment are known to identify with, advocate for, and support others in their social network [6, 33]. Similarly, the commitment model by Bateman et al. suggested that members with a strong affective commitment are inclined to “invest their time and effort helping others” as a result of “their sense of attachment and belonging” [4, p. 845]. Consistent with their argument, Bateman et al. [4] found that affective commitment influenced posting behavior. In the context of online communities, Ray et al. [50] also showed that active engagement, which is similar to affective commitment, relates positively with knowledge contribution.

The discussion mentioned earlier pointed out that online members favorably disposed toward their SNS would show a desire to communicate with other members. In this study, active participation refers to the degree to which a member communicates with other members in the form of posting comments, replying to others’ posts, and exchanging messages [2, 16]. Active participation, as with posting [4] and knowledge contribution [50], requires members’ dedication to their peers in a voluntary, nonbinding, and nonobligatory setting. Thus, it differs qualitatively from a more passive participation like reading [4]. Furthermore, active participation differs from mere posting or knowledge contribution because it encompasses actively communicating with others, which is a key criterion in the context of SNS. As discussed previously, members with high affective commitment are likely to come to identify with their SNS and thus are concerned about its health and growth [12]. These users are naturally inclined to participate actively with other members. Therefore, we hypothesize that affective commitment will be positively related to active participation.

Hypothesis 2: Affective commitment has a positive relationship with a member’s active participation in communication among SNS members.

## Constraint-Based Mechanism

Whereas the dedication-based mechanism is formed mainly through satisfying experiences, the constraint-based mechanism operates because of the locked-in investments that would become useless with a switch to other services [6, 33]. In the case of constraint-based relationship maintenance, individuals cannot terminate their membership because the difficulty of switching has made them dependent on it. Prior studies that have examined switching constraints and their various causes have pinpointed a single factor that can affect all dimensions of constraint: the amount of time and energy that users have already invested in their service leads to a gamut of psychological and real costs that lock users in [49]. As an all-around driver of constraint-based relationships, past investments refer to the economic, social, or psychological investments devoted to an online service that are not easily transferred elsewhere [10, 30, 49]. In an SNS, people are expected to learn the norms and rules, get acquainted with other users, and post messages on the website. Such learning, effort, and resources constitute investments associated with membership. These nontransferable investments make people dependent on their incumbent service and eventually create “lock-in.”

Continuance commitment is “an awareness of the costs associated with leaving” a social group [41, p. 67]. Our study defines it as the degree of an individual’s commitment to maintaining SNS membership because of the costs of leaving. This commitment is also called calculative commitment in that it “is driven by doubts that an alternate organization could provide the same level of benefits for the same effort invested” [4, p. 843]. Within the framework of the dual model, a member’s willingness to maintain SNS membership will increase with an increase in locked-in investments (e.g., learning norms and rules, social relationships) [33]. Thus, we expect that the past investments associated with initiating and maintaining SNS membership will be positively associated with an individual’s level of continuance commitment [4, 27, 41, 49, 62].

Hypothesis 3: A member’s past investments in an SNS have a positive relationship with continuance commitment.

In the dual model by Kim and Son [33], the constraint-based mechanism was expected to lead to two types of outcomes—inattention to alternatives and willingness to pay an online service. Given that SNS rarely charge a fee, inattention to alternatives is arguably one of the most important constraint-based outcomes. In this study, inattention to alternatives refers to the degree to which an individual lacks interest in alternative SNS. Heide and Weiss [29] examined a buyer–seller relationship in high technology markets and found that a locked-in relationship discouraged buyers from seeking or trying new vendors. Simply put, when people know they have to stick with the current option, they explore fewer alternatives [57, 61]. Similarly, in the context of online services, Kim and Son [33] showed that online customers with a higher level of switching costs became less interested in exploring alternatives to their current service. Taken together, the literature leads us to expect that members with high continuance commitment will not be interested in searching for other SNS.<sup>2</sup> Thus, we propose that SNS users will consider fewer alternatives after an increase in their degree of continuance commitment.

Hypothesis 4: Continuance commitment has a positive relationship with an individual’s inattention to alternatives.

## Obligation-Based Mechanism

Whereas the dedication-based mechanism is rooted in a thorough evaluation of the pros and cons associated with SNS membership, the constraint-based mechanism results from awareness of the potential losses from switching to an alternative SNS. Thus, although distinct in nature, the dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms are both based on cost–benefit analysis. However, commitment theory further implies that online users do not always behave according to transactional economics; relational reasons also dictate people’s behavior [26, 41]. In the management literature, researchers who have examined numerous socioemotional factors in organizational contexts have found that the degree of support provided by others in an organization is a singularly powerful explanation for later perceptions of obligation [22]. Further research has consistently found organizational support to be perhaps the strongest social factor influencing employees in organizations [26, 42].

Adapting the well-established and broad concept of organizational support to our context, we defined perceived social support as an SNS member’s perception of the extent of support by the person’s social network on the website. Imagine a situation in which an SNS member receives advice or encouragement from other members. The recipient is likely to feel obliged to socially repay this support, although such payback is not explicitly required. In our study, an obligation-based mechanism refers to psychological phenomena caused by a sense of obligation or indebtedness to peers in one’s online social network. The dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms largely focus on individual-oriented and economic factors, but the obligation-based mechanism relates strongly to interpersonal, relational, and social issues.

The norm of reciprocity is the expectation that a person who receives something will return the favor to the giver [21]. Such a social norm is the basis of an implicit psychological contract widely present in a social exchange process. Based on the norm of reciprocity, perceived social support is likely to increase one’s sense of obligation toward one’s social network.

Normative commitment is “a feeling of obligation to continue” to be a member of a group [41, p. 67]. We define it in our SNS context as the degree to which an individual is committed to maintaining SNS membership because of his or her sense of obligation to the SNS. Commitment theory states that normative commitment arises from perceived support from others [26, 42]. Specifically, Bateman et al. [4] argued that community members who have benefited from others’ generosity are likely to feel a duty to maintain membership. Because SNS members generally have strong ties with others within their social network, we expect that the relationship between perceived social support and normative commitment will also be evident. Thus, we hypothesize that perceived social support would have a positive impact on normative commitment in the context of SNS.

Hypothesis 5: Perceived social support has a positive relationship with normative commitment.

Bateman et al. [4] demonstrated that individuals with high normative commitment tend to engage in prosocial behaviors such as moderating messages in their group. Moderating acts play an important part in improving social relationships because they promote a positive culture, discourage negative behavior, and socialize new users. According to the commitment model by Bateman et al., members with high normative commitment “act in selfless ways” and therefore, engage in moderating messages “even if doing so incurs costs to the member that are not offset by any other direct benefits” [4, p. 845]. In the context of SNS, conflicts are rare in situations in which a member’s social network includes only a few dozen close friends. Yet, in the case of a large network, SNS exchanges tend to lead to tension because of “the problem of conflicting social spheres” [7, p. 966]. In this situation, positive interactions (e.g., jokes) within one membership sphere could be interpreted as undesirable by others in the same network. Members willing to try to reconcile such conflicts could ease, to some extent, such tensions. As implied by Bateman et al. [4], members who feel obligated to the social network are likely to volunteer as moderators in their groups. Therefore, we propose that SNS members with high normative commitment will attempt to moderate conflicting messages to reduce tension in their social network.

Hypothesis 6: Normative commitment has a positive relationship with the extent to which one engages in moderating messages in that SNS.

## Intermechanism Effects

All the earlier hypotheses focused on intramechanism relationships in which two variables of interest belong to the same mechanism. However, it is important to note that our model does not necessarily preclude significant relationships across different mechanisms. Actually, we argue that the antecedent factors in this study have broader effects on individuals’ commitment than the intramechanism relationships suggest. Whereas people are highly conscious of economic costs and benefits in the context of online services, they tend to expect reciprocity in a social context [8]. Instead of weighing pros and cons, people in a socially oriented setting are known to make decisions according to “standard operating procedure,” which is based on reciprocity behavior [19]. Thus, decision making in the context of social networking is likely more complex than what is described in the dual model developed in the context of online services.

In H1, we predicted that satisfaction would influence affective commitment. We also expect satisfaction to have significant effects on continuance and normative commitment in the context of SNS. This is because those who benefit from others’ contributions are likely to feel obliged to repay what they received from others (i.e., normative commitment) and thus, are committed to staying in the same SNS (i.e., continuance commitment) [23]. In a study of virtual world services, Zhou et al. [62] reported a significant correlation between satisfaction and continuance commitment. In a meta-analysis of organizational commitment research, Meyer et al. [42] also found that employee satisfaction and normative commitment were significantly related. Thus, we expect that individuals’ satisfaction with an SNS will lead not only to affective commitment (H1) but also to continuance and normative commitment because of the norms of reciprocity.

Hypothesis 7: A member’s satisfaction with an SNS has a positive relationship with continuance commitment.

Hypothesis 8: A member’s satisfaction with an SNS has a positive relationship with normative commitment.

We predicted earlier that past investments would influence continuance commitment (H3). However, other studies suggest that the influence of past investments affects all three types of commitment [42], and we expect to see these effects in our SNS context as well. Initially, new SNS users might view their service relationship as opportunistic for both sides: users can freely use the SNS’s resources but abandon the SNS if it does not satisfy them, and the SNS can expropriate value by showing unwanted ads, selling user information, or more. But users who have devoted time and energy to develop a social network at their SNS will realize they cannot leverage these SNS-specific investments elsewhere and that their relationship with their SNS must necessarily extend into the foreseeable future. Such feelings of future extendedness from prior investments is known to alter the initial expropriation calculus of service relationships in two key ways: bonding and solidarity [52].

First, deeply invested users feel that they must trust their provider if they are to jointly share in the service’s future success, and this has a bonding effect that creates affective ties to providers [49]. Second, future extendedness makes users aware they are in an exchange relationship with a common purpose to maximize value for both sides. This sense of common purpose with an exchange partner triggers latent norms of solidarity wherein people inherently feel they are doing the right thing as a social actor by maintaining the relationship [52]. Thus, we expect that past investments, by virtue of their specificity to an SNS, will encourage users to develop affective and normative ties with their provider. These service-specific ties should enhance relevant aspects of their internal commitment, regardless of their degree of satisfaction or sense of social support.

Hypothesis 9: A member’s past investments in an SNS have a positive relationship with affective commitment.

Hypothesis 10: A member’s past investments in an SNS have a positive relationship with normative commitment.

We previously proposed that social support would influence normative commitment (H5). Much research indicates that social support has a wide-ranging influence on individuals’ perceptions and behavior that is not necessarily limited to normative commitment. As discussed earlier, decision making in a socially oriented context is not based solely on a cost–benefit calculation; instead, a decision will be based, at least partly, on the norms of reciprocity [8, 19]. Those who receive help from other members tend to feel a sense of belonging to the community, which increases their favorable feelings toward it as well as their commitment to stay and return the favor [13, 23].

Chow and Chan [17] showed that close relationships among members boost subjective norms in their group. Such norms were found to increase positive feelings about interacting with other members in the group, in turn promoting members dedication to continue to maintain the relationships in the group. Similarly, Zhou et al. [62] demonstrated that social support positively affected both affective commitment and continuance commitment. In an SNS context, social support can take numerous forms such as caring about others’ well-being, approving others’ opinions, sharing common goals, and offering favors to others [23]. SNS members who receive help from others are likely to have favorable feelings toward the SNS and want to stay in it. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that perceived social support would positively affect affective commitment and continuance commitment in the context of SNS.

Hypothesis 11: Perceived social support has a positive relationship with affective commitment.

Hypothesis 12: Perceived social support has a positive relationship with continuance commitment.

## Research Method

## Research Setting and Data Collection

We chose Facebook as the specific empirical setting for this study primarily for two reasons. First, Facebook is one of the most popular SNS in the world that is not limited to certain individuals, groups, or topics, but instead covers a range of personal and organizational interests [9]. As such, our findings are expected to be more generalizable than those from niche SNS. Second, Facebook gives its users opportunities to experience satisfaction, past investments, and social support. Thus, we considered it a desirable setting in which to examine simultaneously the dedication-, constraint- and obligation-based mechanisms.

We developed a web-based survey designed to measure research variables and other information related to individuals’ use of Facebook. To collect data, we worked with a market research firm that maintains a nationwide online panel of American adults 18 years old or older.<sup>3</sup> For this study, we e-mailed invitations to 1,500 randomly selected people in the firm’s panel pool. The 492 users who responded constituted a response rate of 32.8 percent. Of the 492, only 387, about 79 percent of the initial respondents, were found to actually use Facebook. In addition, we removed five incomplete responses. This resulted in 382 usable responses. The mean age in the final data set was 44, and 53 percent of the responders were female.<sup>4</sup> No significant differences in age and gender distributions were found between nonrespondents and respondents. Besides, we did not find any difference between the users and nonusers of Facebook in terms of age and gender.

## Measures

Online Appendix A contains the specific items included in this study. The scales we used were typically adapted from previous studies. The items for our three antecedents reflect recent conceptualizations in the literature. We measured satisfaction by using three items from Kim and Son [33], adapting them to relate specifically to the Facebook experience. We measured past investments with four items from Ray et al. [49] that we adapted to the Facebook setting. Ray et al. [49] demonstrated that this single scale correlates with, and strongly explains, a comprehensive six dimensions of perceived switching costs. To measure social support, we adapted the best items from Eisenberger et al. [22], omitting items they found to load below 0.70 in their confirmatory measurements. Our chosen items covered a range of support, including opinions, well-being, goals and values, and special needs.

We adapted the three mediating commitment factors from Bateman et al. [4], altering them only for the Facebook context. We used four items to measure participation on Facebook. These were based on a measurement scale relating to community participation used by Algesheimer et al. [2] and, based on the latter, Choi [16]. We measured inattentiveness to alternatives by using three items. Two of them related to trying an alternative service were borrowed from Kim and Son [33] and reversed. We added one more item related to not exploring another SNS so as to have at least three items. We measured intention to moderate by using three measures adapted from Bateman et al. [4].

Finally, as control variables, we used single-item measures of age, gender, years spent on Facebook, and total number of friends. We created a control factor for addiction that adapted a scale by Charlton [11], which Turel et al. [56] identified as a compact yet extant measurement for multidimensional addiction. We borrowed four items that show the most desirable psychometric characteristics based on the results of exploratory factor analysis in Turel et al. [56].

## Data Analysis

## Measurement Model

We tested and refined our measurement model by using a series of factor analyses. First, we used SPSS 17 to perform an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on 37 items of the 10 latent factors. The results indicated that the 10-factor solution explained 78.83 percent of variance. An item is said to be psychometrically sound if its factor loading exceeds 0.6 and cross loading is less than 0.4. We found that none of the items had cross loadings higher than 0.40, but four items (i.e., INV1, INV4, AC1, and CC3) had factor loadings of less than 0.6. As a result, we removed the four items from further analysis.

We reanalyzed our remaining 33 items in a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using LISREL 8 [31]. The measurement model consisted of 10 reflective constructs as well as 4 single-item variables. We ran our CFA and judged its fit based on five measures: the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the comparative fit index (CFI), the normed fit index (NFI), the nonnormed fit index (NNFI), the goodness of fit (GFI), and the adjusted goodness of fit (AGFI). The results showed a highly satisfactory fit between the measurement model and the data: $\chi ^ { 2 } \ ( 5 4 2 ) =$ 933.17 (p < 0.001), RMSEA = 0.044, CFI = 0.99, NFI= 0.98, NNFI = 0.99, GFI = 0.88, $\mathrm { A G F I } \ = \ 0 . 8 5$ . Table 1 shows the means, standard deviations, composite reliabilities, and the average variance extracted (AVE) of the 14 variables.

We examined the validity and reliability of the scales based on the results of the measurement model [3, 25]. Our multi-item scales displayed adequate convergent validity; the smallest item loading was 0.81, which exceeds the cutoff of 0.60 [15]. We also evaluated the discriminant validity of each construct by checking whether the amount of variance extracted from its items was larger than its relationship with other variables [14]. We found that the square root of each factor’s AVE exceeded its correlations with other constructs, which supports the discriminant validity of the scales. To further establish discriminant validity, we modeled each pair of factors by using two alternative confirmatory measurement models. One allowed the pair to correlate freely; the other restricted the correlation to unity. In each case, we found a significant chi-square difference between the alternatives, providing further evidence of discriminant validity. We also assessed the two types of reliability, that is, composite reliability and average variance extracted [3, 5]. As Table 1 shows, the composite reliability of all constructs exceeded 0.70, and the AVE was above 0.50, showing high reliability. Overall, with adequate levels of reliability, convergent, and discriminant validity, our measurement items were shown to be psychometrically sound.

We also checked the extent of common method variance to ensure our data did not suffer from this problem. According to Lindell and Whitney [35], the smallest correlation among the latent factors is a reasonable proxy for common method variance. As shown in Table 1, the lowest correlation—after excluding the singleitem variables such as age, gender, education, tenure, and personal network—is small $( r = 0 . 0 5 , p = \mathrm { n s } )$ . We also conducted a common method factor test [48] in which the major items of our major constructs were free to load on their proper constructs and a common method factor. The sum of squared loadings showed that the proper constructs of items accounted for 66.6 percent of all variance; measurement error accounted for 20.2 percent, and a common method accounted for 13.2 percent at most. This level of common method variance (13.2 percent) was smaller than the average amount of method variance in the IS discipline (16 percent) [38]. Taken together, our results suggest that common method bias does not pose a major threat to the validity of our findings.

## Post Hoc Analysis of Intermechanism Effects

Our model focuses on a pattern of relationships stemming from antecedent factors, mediating commitment, and outcomes that corresponds to dedication-based, constraint-based, and obligation-based mechanisms. We showed that the story of antecedents to commitment factors might be more complex than the dual model implies. However, our results also suggested that the three mechanisms come across quite distinctly between commitment and outcome factors. To strengthen this proposition through formal tests, we conducted a series of chi-square difference tests comparing within- and between-mechanism effects emerging from commitment. For example, our tripartite model suggests that an unhypothesized effect such as that of affective commitment on inattentiveness to alternatives would be significantly weaker than either affective commitment’s proposed effect on participation or continuance commitment’s proposed effect on inattentiveness to alternatives. This prediction requires two chi-square difference tests: The first involves comparing the effects of a certain commitment factor on different outcomes (P1, P2, and P3) as well as comparing the effects of different commitment factors on a certain outcome variable (P4, P5, and P6). We evaluated six such propositions using 12 chi-square difference tests. Table 3 shows the results of the chi-square difference tests and propositions.

We examined the relationships between types of commitment and outcomes. Of the 12 path comparisons in which we expected to find significant differences, only 3 failed to find such significance. All three instances of insignificance involved the path between normative commitment and moderating messages. This was so because the effect of normative commitment on moderating messages was highly significant but not overwhelmingly so (0.20, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Nevertheless, the effect of normative commitment on moderating messages still appeared larger than other intramechanisms effects, which is consistent with our earlier predictions.

In summary, the effects of commitment factors on outcomes were largely consistent with the premise of the tripartite model. These findings generally point out that, in the context of SNS, there are at least systematic patterns of relationships between commitment and its outcomes.

## Discussion

The objective of this study was to develop and test a tripartite model of postadoption phenomena in the context of SNS. Based on the dual model and commitment theory, the theoretical framework describes the nature of the dedication-, constraint-, and obligation-based mechanisms, including their antecedent, intervening, and outcome variables. The proposed model was tested with data from 382 actual users of Facebook. The results of structural equation modeling analysis empirically supported the tripartite model and our research hypotheses. First, the relationships between satisfaction, affective commitment, and active participation substantiated the dedication-based mechanism. Second, in the constraint-based mechanism, past investments affected continuance commitment, which in turn determined inattention to alternatives. Third, we found evidence of the obligation-based mechanism through the effect of social support on normative commitment as well as the effect of normative commitment on moderating comments. Finally, the existence of intermechanism relationships was asymmetrical; whereas intermechanism effects were rare between commitment and outcomes, they were common between antecedents and commitment. Overall, the present study contributes to IS research by combining two different perspectives into a coherent view of postadoption phenomena in the context of SNS, and shows the boundary condition of the dual model, especially when applied to a socially oriented context.

## Theoretical Contributions

A major contribution of this study to IS research is the first association of the obligation-based mechanism as a complement to the dedication- and constraintbased mechanisms already included in the original model of postadoption phenomena [33]. The basic assumption behind the dual model is that users maintain online relationships “either because they genuinely want to or because they believe that they have no option” [59, p. 86]. The obligation-based mechanism proposed in this study goes beyond the calculative cost–benefit analysis involved in the dedicationand constraint-based mechanisms and instead operates on the proposition of “should” or “what must be done.” Such normative pressure is not particularly relevant in most online services—for example, online news, online portals, and online retailing. Our findings indicate that prosocial behavior such as moderating messages is not explained by dedication or constraints but only by obligation, or more specifically, normative commitment, which in part is facilitated by perceived social support. More specifically, we found that moderating messages are not influenced at all by the dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms; rather, the act of moderating messages is found to be a function of normative commitment in the obligation-based mechanism. These findings collectively highlight an important limitation in the original dual model and a reason to take into account the obligationbased mechanism for a fuller account of SNS use.

One of the major contributions of this study to IS research is to show the asymmetric property of the three mechanisms in the context of SNS. We found that intermechanism effects were relatively strong between the antecedents and commitment, but such effects were weak or nonexistent between commitment and the outcomes. The dual model developed in individually oriented settings largely ignores the norms of reciprocity; as a result, the formation of commitment is predicted to follow a fairly straightforward cost–benefit analysis. In contrast, research on group and social behavior suggests that in socially oriented settings, people tend to follow standard operating procedure, and thus the formation of commitment is determined by a variety of relevant factors at the same time. However, once commitment is formed, it so greatly impacts behavioral outcomes that it leaves no room for intermechanism effects between commitment and the outcomes. Overall, our study extends the boundary of knowledge by revealing the asymmetric property of the three mechanisms among antecedents, commitment, and outcomes and shows the boundary condition of the dual model, especially when it is applied in socially oriented settings.

It is important to note that the true value of the tripartite model does not lie in simply combining factors of two existing models, but instead comes from the fresh realizations made possible by synthesizing two seemingly unrelated paradigms. For example, a conceptual framework by Bendapudi and Berry [6] suggested a broad set of antecedents (e.g., environment, interaction) and outcomes (e.g., acquiescence, advocacy) that were expected to be relevant to a variety of individually oriented online services. Thus, from this perspective, the specification of antecedents and outcomes is highly flexible. For example, inattentiveness to alternatives, which is one of the outcomes in this study, was not originally included in the commitment model by Bateman et al. [4], but it was still explicitly incorporated within the general framework by Kim and Son [33] and by Bendapudi and Berry [6]. Meanwhile, commitment theory is effective in clarifying interpersonal relationships and their corresponding dynamics [42]. In particular, this stream of research shows the close connection between normative commitment and social support, which is believed to be important in understanding the social aspect of online behavior. By combining these two paradigms, this study contributes to IS research by offering both a specific tripartite model and a new, flexible, and powerful way of thinking related to online behavior in general. We expect that our theoretical approach will offer valuable insights into not only SNS but also into other forms of online services involving interpersonal relationships such as online communities, virtual worlds, and online collaboration.

## Managerial Implications

This study offers practical insights for managing SNS services. A new finding of this study is that enhanced feelings of continuance commitment make users less attentive to their alternatives. Inattentiveness to alternatives is an outcome that earlier studies of commitment have not explicitly studied, but this form of inattentiveness is vital to a goal of keeping customers loyal. Customers who do not pay attention to alternatives are much less capable of, or interested in, switching in the future. However, affective commitment and normative commitment do not significantly increase inattentiveness. Thus, managers who desire this outcome must fine-tune their strategies to focus on enhancing continuance commitment. Our findings especially emphasize that member lock-in as induced by past investments is one of the most effective ways to boost continuance commitment. Past investments increase the sense of dependence that customers feel from the time and energy spent at their SNS service [49]. Thus, SNS providers could increase this sense of past investments by encouraging users to customize their experience and add more friends.

We found that not all types of commitment arise from all antecedent strategies. Specifically, the expected strategy of enhancing service quality to satisfy users may enhance affective and continuance commitment. However, it does not seem to yield normative commitment, which is the only determinant of moderating messages. Improving member satisfaction has been considered the best approach to attract members’ engagement and eventually facilitate prosocial behavior. However, our findings reveal that satisfaction does not necessarily lead to a sense of obligation or to normative commitment. Thus, to promote moderating messages, managers should avoid a single-minded focus on satisfaction but instead, among other measures, pay attention to facilitating interpersonal relationships [55]. In particular, SNS providers must ensure that their sites maximize opportunities for members to support and encourage each other [40]. For example, SNS services may reward users for leaving “like” notices, help them to remember anniversaries, and encourage users to reach out to those who seem to have withdrawn from others.

As a whole, creating commitment requires the careful balancing of provided benefits and support against barriers to exit. These two approaches may seem orthogonal, and at times at odds with each other, but this is a defining characteristic of commitment strategy for SNS.

## Limitations and Further Research

Several limitations of this study warrant attention. We relied on a nationwide online panel for the study, but of course, a better sampling frame of Facebook users could be realized if we could randomly sample from the entire Facebook user base. In a similar vein, we only examined users who are at least 18 years old in the United States. Thus, care should be taken in generalizing our findings to younger groups. Moreover, the antecedents, forms of commitment, and outcomes in our model may not be the only variables relevant to explaining SNS-related behavior. For example, we used satisfac tion, past investments, and social support as exemplars of the antecedent forces on our three mechanisms. Although these antecedents serve our story well by producing healthy $R ^ { 2 }$ values on the mediating commitment factors, we encourage researchers to investigate how other selections or configurations of antecedents might contribute to our understanding of intermechanism effects. Likewise, additional behavioral outcomes that correspond to one or more of the three mechanisms could broaden our understanding of commitment in SNS. Thus, it should be noted that the proposed model is not necessarily the most comprehensive one. In our study, we found addiction to be relatively highly correlated with normative commitment $( r = 0 . 5 7 )$ and moderating messages $( r = 0 . 7 4 )$ . Although these scales were found to be statistically distinguishable in this study, caution should be taken in interpreting our findings until the psychometric properties of the scales are further validated. Finally, because of the cross-sectional nature of our study, its findings could be susceptible to common method biases [48]. We found that common method variance in this study, if any, is not substantial enough to distort the results. Yet proceeding cautiously will be wise until our results are corroborated by longitudinal studies.

Wherever possible, this study used measurement items of key constructs established in prior studies. However, two items of past investments and a single item of continuance commitment did not load well on their proper constructs and had to be omitted from our measurement model. We ascertained that the items of past investments that were deleted relate to developing a relationship with the service provider and to customizing service settings. But Facebook does not directly engage with customers by using frontline employees the way subscription services do, and it does not allow for extensive customization of its interface as certain other social networks do. We caution researchers using these constructs to consider the relevance of their context-specific items.

This research offers interesting avenues for further research. Our model suggests unidirectional causal flows from antecedents to behavior, via types of commitment. However, as the integrative framework of technology use by Kim [32] implies, individuals’ behavior could also influence their beliefs and perceptions. In this sense, it would be interesting to investigate feedback loops between the effects of prior behavior on subsequent antecedents, commitment, and behaviors. An important issue here is whether the effects of previous behavior would follow the systematic patterns suggested by the tripartite model (e.g., active participation affects satisfaction but not switching costs and addiction). We suspect that behavioral outcomes would alter the prior goals and values of community users, which in turn could cause fundamental shifts in how they view their community. If this is the case, the effects of prior behavior on subsequent phenomena are likely to be more complex than the systematic structure implied by the tripartite model. We believe that a longitudinal investigation into such feedback loops would offer insights into the dynamic nature of postadoption phenomena in the context of online services.

We found that addiction is strongly related to normative commitment. Much research indicates that socially oriented activities in SNS often exhibit compulsive, obsessive, and pathological characteristics (e.g., receiving emotional support, meeting friends, and sharing the same interests) [43, 60]. Addiction is detached from a calculative value proposition in that it leads to a passionate attachment [44]. Such a passionate attachment is accompanied by self-sacrifice, devotion, and feelings of duty [1, 58]. For these reasons, we believe that addiction could be an antecedent of the obligation-based mechanism affecting normative commitment. Alternatively, addiction could be a moderating factor that changes the relationships between commitment and outcomes. Specifically, research suggests that addicted people are less controlled by their willpower (e.g., commitment) and regulated more by their unconscious desires [51]. Thus, the effects of commitment on behavioral outcomes are likely to decrease as the level of addiction increases. Addiction is increasingly an important topic in IS research [56]. Further research should be conducted to better understand the role of addiction in the SNS context and in other socially oriented settings.

## Conclusions

Social networking services are increasingly an essential part of everyday life as people spend more time socializing on the Internet. This study offers a tripartite model that suggests that three distinct mechanisms—dedication-, constraint-, and obligation-based mechanisms—regulate online behavior. In the context of SNS, these three mechanisms work together to influence the formation of commitment, but the mechanisms are largely distinct in their roles in regulating behavioral outcomes. We hope that our tripartite model will guide researchers in deciphering the complex causal structures that underlie postadoption phenomena in a variety of online services involving social relationships such as SNS, online communities, virtual worlds, and online collaboration.

## NOTES

1. In the dual model by Kim and Son [33], perceived usefulness is identified as another antecedent of affective commitment. However, satisfaction is generally identified as the main driver of affective commitment in the management and marketing literatures. Furthermore, IS research on online communities generally focuses on satisfaction while ignoring perceived usefulness [36, 50]. Consistent with these studies, our tripartite model includes satisfaction as a major factor affecting affective commitment. Nevertheless, we describe in the discussion section the potential limitation of our approach in which perceived usefulness is ignored.

2. Bendapudi and Berry [6] maintain that constraint-based relationship maintenance leads to an elevated level of interest in alternatives, which appears contrary to our expectation. In general, we agree with their claim that people will actively search for alternatives when they feel constrained. However, once they are committed to membership continuance, their interest in alternatives is expected to decrease [29, 33].

3. Online panel surveys have numerous advantages over traditional techniques of data collection [28]. For example, the online panel method helps to reduce the time and cost required for data collection because it can be executed quickly and efficiently. In addition, this method helps respondents answer questions at a convenient time and location. Moreover, compared with convenience samples (e.g., students), which are common in IS research, samples for online panel surveys are demographically more diverse and representative. Besides, social desirability bias is less of a concern because surveys take place on relatively neutral ground away from home and work. Nevertheless, the online panel method has some weaknesses. One disadvantage is the skewed attributes of the Internet population [24]. However, because Facebook is our target application, this is not a serious problem of this study. Another potential bias is panel conditioning [28]. If lucrative rewards are offered for study participation, respondents may pretend to fit into the target sample. This is not likely in our study because of the low level of rewards. However, the panel used in this study may fall short of representing the population of Facebook users, which is a potential limitation.

4. A Facebook demographic report shows that as of 2014, a majority of Facebook users were between 35 and 54 years old (31.1 percent) [53]. Considering that the mean age was 43 in this study, our sample seems a reasonable representation of the target population of Facebook users. Meanwhile, the same report indicates that ages younger than 18 made up only 5.1 percent of Facebook users and even this portion is quickly declining. Thus, our sample without the younger age group is believed to be generally acceptable.

5. We also checked the mediating role of commitment between the antecedents and the outcomes. The results indicated that the antecedents had significant effects on active participation $( p s < 0 . 0 5 )$ , but not on any of the other outcomes $( p s = \mathrm { n s } )$ . Interestingly, however, the impact of normative commitment on active participation became negative $\bar { ( p \textmd { < } 0 . 0 1 ) }$ in this alternative model, which implies overfitting. Thus, for the purposes of hypothesis testing, we used the proposed model, but confirmed that the results of the hypotheses stayed exactly the same even if the alternative model was used. Online Appendix B shows the results of this alternative model.
