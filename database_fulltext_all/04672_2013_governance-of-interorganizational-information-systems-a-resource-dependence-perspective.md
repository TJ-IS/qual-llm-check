# Governance of Interorganizational Information Systems: A Resource Dependence Perspective

Dipanjan Chatterjee

Faculty of Business, Brock University, St. Catherines, Ontario, dchatterjee@brocku.ca

T. Ravichandran

Lally School of Management and Technology, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, New York 12180, ravit@rpi.edu

n this paper we examine why firms seek to control and own interorganizational information systems, or IIOS. Practitioners have largely cited the issues related to control and ownership of IOS, referred to as IOS governance in this paper, as the key reason behind the failure of many IOS initiatives. We distinguish between two IOS governance modes, transactional and financial, and develop a mediated-moderation model to explain the factors influencing the governance choices. In our model, the key motivators of IOS governance are the criticality and the replaceability of the resources that firms procure, which affect IOS governance through thei influence on the degree of operational integration existing between partners. We hypothesize that while resource criticality will increase the needs for financial and transactional governance because of its positive impact on operational integration, resource replaceability will negatively influence governance needs because of its nega tive impact on operational integration. Furthermore, technological uncertainty associated with the resources is argued to negatively impact the extent of IOS governance exercised by firms by weakening the positive impact of resource criticality and strengthening the negative impact of resource replaceability on operational integration respectively. We empirically test our model using data gathered from a survey of 159 United States manufacturing firms. Results show that resource criticality positively affects the extent of financial and transactional IOS governance by increasing the need for operational integration, whereas resource replaceability negatively affects them by reducing the need for operational integration. Furthermore, technological uncertainty creates disincentives for IOS governance primarily by weakening the positive influence of resource criticality on operational integration, while no statistically significant effect of technological uncertainty on the relationship between resource replaceability and operational integration was discerned.

Key words: interorganizational information systems; IOS governance; inter-firm relationships; procurement systems; resource dependence theory

## Introduction

Interorganizational information systems, or IOS, are critical enablers of inter-firm commerce, and are acknowledged for their strategic and operational impact (Rai et al. 2006, Mayor 2007). While IOSgenerated benefits have received much attention, less research has been directed toward understanding the reasons for the successes and failures of these systems. While many factors can influence the success of IOS, decisions related to IOS ownership and control have always been crucial for the viability and survival of these systems. The collapse of many business-tobusiness (B2B) exchanges about a decade ago provides instructive examples of nonparticipation in IOS and subsequent IOS failures owing to issues of control and ownership. Nonparticipation in B2B exchanges often happened when online systems did not match the existing ways of doing business, and firms did not have adequate influence to mold the systems to their needs or appropriate value from them (Friesen 2003). Toyota eschewed the automotive exchange Covisint and stated it was “not going to change the way Toyota selects suppliers” (Chappell 2000). Toyota perceived that lack of control or ownership over the system prevented it from creating the features necessary for selecting, qualifying, and transacting with suppliers on its own terms.

Similarly, many electronic exchanges introduced in the Dutch flower auction market failed due to the nonparticipation of flower growers who perceived that electronic trading methods undermined their existing price discovery mechanisms, and lack of ownership and control prevented them from influencing the transactions (Kambil and Van Heck 1998). Data indicates that as many as 75% of all IOS initiatives culminate in nonparticipation by firms or achieve significantly lower utilization levels than originally intended (e-Procure Solutions 2005, Grover et al. 2008). As the potential benefits of an IOS are contingent on its viability and continued use, which is in turn tied to the concerns over IOS ownership and control, it is important to examine the factors that influence IOS ownership and control decisions.

In this paper we investigate why and how firms seek ownership and control of IOS, which we label as IOS governance choices. While governance of IT within individual firms has been researched extensively (Tiwana and Konsynski 2010, Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999), limited research has focused on the governance of IOS. Building on past studies (Kambil and Van Heck 1998, Choudhury 1997), we propose transactional and financial governance as two distinct facets of IOS governance. Transactional governance represents the operational oversight a firm can exercise over an IOS, allowing it to control various aspects of transactions on the IOS. Financial governance represents economic ownership of IOS, representing the extent to which firms can appropriate the value generated by an IOS.

To understand the motivations for IOS governance we draw from the resource dependence theory, which proposes that no firm is self-sufficient in terms of the resources it needs and depends on external firms to procure the deficient resources (Heide and John 1988, Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). To facilitate the procurement of the needed resources, firms devise specific operational processes linking external firms. Hence, firms have an intrinsic motivation to ensure that key operational features of external linkages are preserved and their ability to appropriate value from the linkages is not diminished when using an IOS. Thus, we examine how external dependencies created by resource deficiencies give rise to inter-firm operational integration, which in turn influences the IOS governance choices. We further examine how the uncertainties associated with the resources moderate the impact of external dependencies on operational integration and hence affect IOS governance. Our study fills a critical gap in the literature by operationalizing the constructs of transactional and financial IOS governance and examining the drivers that influence IOS governance choices, through a largescale empirical study.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We lay out our research framework and develop our constructs and hypotheses in the next section. In the subsequent section, we present our empirical study, and in the concluding section we review and discuss the empirical findings and highlight the principal contributions of our study.

## Research Model

Resource dependence theory provides the overarching theoretical framework for our research. Resource dependence theory postulates that firms are rarely self-sufficient in all the resources they need to remain productive, and hence, depend on and form relationships with external firms to procure the resources (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). The theory further identifies dependence and uncertainty as the key antecedents that combine to affect the external relationships of firms (Heide and John 1990, Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). While dependence characterizes the external dependencies on other firms that materialize due to resource deficiencies, uncertainty represents the unpredictability in the flow of resources as their production and transmission lie beyond the control of the firm. Firms structure operational relationships with external firms to ensure the procurement of deficient resources and to manage the conditions of dependence and uncertainty. We draw from resource dependence theory to build a mediated-moderation model to explain the drivers of IOS governance (Figure 1).

Resource dependence theory provides two critical insights for understanding IOS governance choices. First, it helps us understand the central and essential role of inter-firm relationships in enabling the resource procurement process. As firms invest in and design dedicated and integrated operations for resource procurement, they have an intrinsic motivation to preserve the key characteristics of such operations and retain their ability to appropriate value from them. We therefore examine how firms seek control and ownership of IOS to sustain the essential functionalities of external relationships, and to assert their right to appropriate value from them. Second, resource dependence theory explains the factors that affect the nature and extent of operational integration with external firms. Therefore, we examine how the external dependencies that arise due to resource deficiencies, represented by the criticality and replaceability of the resources, influence the extent of inter-firm operational integration. Moreover, we examine the role of technological uncertainty associated with the resources in moderating the effects of resource criticality and replaceability on operational integration. In essence, we examine how criticality and replaceability of resources on their own and also in the presence of technological uncertainty influence IOS governance choices through their impact on operational integration. We explain our constructs (Table 1) and their relationships in the following sections.

## IOS Governance

Research on governance of information systems has focused mostly on systems operating within a firm, and has examined various governance structures and their impact on IT alignment and change management (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999, Tiwana and Konsynski 2010, Kirsch et al. 2002). However, IOS governance poses distinct challenges as users are external firms that differ significantly from intra-organizational units in terms of goals, expectations, and abilities. While some studies have examined the motivations of firms to control IOS and thereby create competitive advantage (Choudhury 1997, Venkatraman and Zaheer 1990), only a limited amount of research has delineated the various facets of IOS governance and examined the factors that impact the governance choices of firms.

Studies in specific industries such as insurance and airlines have explored why firms seek private ownership of IOS instead of developing them cooperatively with other users (Choudhury 1997, Venkatraman and Zaheer 1990). The focus of these studies is the financial ownership of IOS, which is used as a means of appropriating economic value from a system. Ownership gives a firm complete claim over the value created by the system (Bakos and Nault 1997). Conversely, shared ownership or lack of ownership implies that firms lose a part of the value created by the system in the form of subscription fees or rents distributed to co-owning firms. For example, a firm can use the search capacities of an IOS to find better suppliers or exploit electronic integration features to send orders directly and expeditiously to suppliers and thus derive superior benefits. However, if the firm does not own the IOS, it loses a part of this value either as fees or as rents distributed to other owners.

While financial ownership represents an important means for governing an IOS, it does not represent the influence firms can exercise to mold the system to their needs. Studies in Dutch flower auction markets found that, when using electronic platforms, influential flower growers ensured that electronic auctions matched their traditional price discovery mechanism characterized by faster clock times and smaller lot sizes (Kambil and Van Heck 1998). Beyond price discovery mechanisms, firms seek to control various other aspects of transactions within an IOS. Quality control managers at Chrysler often interact with their counterparts in other firms to finalize quality specs for vehicle parts. Hence, Chrysler ensures that its dedicated quality control application, known as Powerway, is enabled within any IOS it uses (Mayor 2007). Furthermore, as the Toyota example highlighted, transactional control may also involve the ability to select, qualify, and award orders to specific partners. In essence, the ability to influence transactions within an IOS involves control of critical parameters such as price discovery mechanisms, selection of transaction participants, and participation norms and procedures, among others.

Building on and synthesizing the insights provided by past studies, we identify and define two distinct aspects of IOS governance. Formally, we define the extent of financial governance as the extent to which a firm is financially responsible for the development, implementation, maintenance, and future upgrades of an IOS and, the extent of transactional governance as the extent to which a firm can dictate the rules, policies, pricing mechanisms, and participation norms governing the transactions conducted within an IOS. We distinguish our characterization of IOS governance from the limited typologies used in past studies that focused on categorical IOS governance choices such as public or private IOS. Our conceptualization of IOS governance represents a continuum, rather than limited categories, which more accurately represents the diversity of options exercised by firms. For example, Elemica.com, an electronics procurement site, provides at least six different service alternatives, each of which requires varying degrees of IOS ownership. Furthermore, many firms use third party services such as Tradeplace.net to provide connectivity to disparate systems being used by their trading partners. In such cases, the subscribing firms retain ownership of their on-premise systems, but have no ownership over the linkages facilitated by Tradeplace.net. At the other end of the spectrum, firms such as Wal-Mart build and maintain their own IOS linking their suppliers. Practitioner experiences also suggest a similar continuum for transactional governance, ranging from minimal to very tight control over IOS-enabled transactions.

The two dimensions of IOS governance are complementary but not necessarily correlated. While financial governance implies ownership, transactional governance establishes authority in IOS-enabled transactions. Though it seems likely that financial governance is a pre-condition for transactional governance, it need not always be so. When an IOS is owned by a third party that is not involved in buying or selling, transactional governance can be exercised independently of financial ownership of the IOS. Many third party exchanges, such as GHX.com, allow firms to set up their own trading hubs for a fee, where they can govern transactions without needing to exercise financial governance.

## Operational Integration

We draw on the resource dependence theory to understand the antecedents of IOS governance choices. The theory suggests that the fundamental response of firms to the condition of inherent resource deficiencies is to craft and maintain relationships with external firms to ensure adequate resource availability (Heide and John 1990). The theory further proposes multiple dimensions of external relationships, with firms needing to manage various facets of relationships, including their political, operational, and economic aspects (Wathne and Heide 2004). A critical dimension of interorganizational relationships is the design and creation of dedicated operational linkages between firms. Such operational linkages specifically seek to coordinate and integrate the key operating policies, procedures, and planning methods across firms to better facilitate the procurement process (Subramani and Venkatraman 2003). For instance, firms can share short- and long-term forecasts, coordinate the production, transportation, and delivery of items, and create common inventory management policies. Beyond basic procurement processes, firms can integrate more sophisticated activities including product design and quality control. Additionally, firms can undertake activities to signal continued commitment to suppliers through reengineering and improvement of current operations or by initiating value-adding innovations to motivate supplier participation in integration initiatives (Lambert and Cooper 2000).

Various practices from the industry highlight the nature of integrated operations. Suppliers of Apple stock two weeks of inventory within a mile of Apple’s assembly plants, thus speeding up the material acquisition process (Satariano and Burrows 2011). However, Apple goes beyond such operational coordination to forge close supplier partnerships by permanently posting representatives at supplier facilities. Apple’s personnel coordinate with suppliers to solve problems, foster innovative initiatives, and provide a reliable communication link back to Apple (Satariano and Burrows 2011). Thus, operational integration represents a deliberate effort to streamline and integrate operations with external partners to increase the efficiency and effectiveness of the resource acquisition process. We define operational integration as the degree to which operational activities, including operating procedures, scheduling practices, inventory policies, and planning methods, are integrated across interacting firms.

Impact of Operational Integration on IOS Governance. Operational integration positively influences the extent of transactional IOS governance for two specific reasons. First, greater operational integration reflects the ongoing and significant efforts by a firm to tune the interlinking processes to its own needs, such that the processes become highly tailored and customized to the needs of the firm. Hence, when firms seek to enable such highly integrated operations using an IOS, it becomes important to preserve the operating features of its external linkages. Many automotive body shops have highly integrated operations with DuPont, which supplies them with specialty performance paints. One of the features that DuPont has created to strengthen the operational linkage with body-shops is the continuous transmission of updated material safety standards for paints, which provides information about various performance, quality, and environmental impacts of paints (InternetWeek 2002). Therefore, when choosing an IOS, DuPont was explicit about wanting to “control and customize more” to ensure that critical transactional elements, such as sharing of safety standards, were appropriately enabled within the IOS. The second factor influencing the extent of transactional governance is the scope of operational integration across firms. Chrysler, when integrating its operations with its suppliers, created nine different quality control sequences for approval of materials (Mayor 2007). As the scope of inter-firm operations increases with the increasing number and variety of processes, it becomes important for firms to comprehensively enable all relevant processes within the IOS. In summary, since higher operational integration entails greater customization and enhanced scope of inter-firm operations, it becomes increasingly necessary to exercise transactional governance to ensure the integrity and continuity of these integrated operations within the IOS. In the absence of adequate transactional control, a firm cannot mold the IOS to its unique needs, and hence, will be unable to benefit from exploiting the interlinking processes it had created and invested in.

Furthermore, operational integration positively influences the extent of financial IOS governance as firms are motivated to capture the enhanced value created through better integration. With higher operational integration, firms can acquire resources faster, find better resources, and reduce the resource acquisition costs. Such benefits translate into savings and added value for firms. Apple exploits its supply chain efficiencies to derive an operating profit margin of close to 25% on mobile devices, which is well above the industry standard (Satariano and Burrows 2011). Operational efficiencies also augment the speed and accuracy of product availability, allowing firms to serve customers better. Caterpillar, a leading industrial equipment manufacturer, has built its competitive reputation on accuracy and faster turnaround times for delivering equipment parts needed by owners (Flint 2007).

Essentially, integrated operations allow firms to create both operational efficiency and relational rents. When firms use IOS to enable such operations, there will be an inherent motivation to appropriate the current and potential value such operations can generate. Lack of IOS ownership or shared ownership will imply that portions of the value generated through such processes are lost as rents appropriated by other co-owning firms. Consequently, the higher the operational integration and the related value creation, the higher will be the motivation to retain the value by increasing the extent of financial governance. Moreover, the higher the operational integration, the higher will be the continuity expectations about the relationship. Since financial governance involves technology investments that might be specific to a relationship, firms are unlikely to seek higher financial governance when continuity expectations are low. Conversely, when such expectations are high because of the tight operational integration, firms might perceive it to be of value to invest in and own the IOS supporting such linkages.

## Resource Criticality

Operational integration is itself motivated by the need to manage the external dependencies that arise from resource deficiencies. Resource dependence theory posits that the way firms perceive and manage external dependencies is influenced by the “motivational investment” firms have in procuring specific recourses (Heide and John 1988, 1990). The motivation to procure a given resource is twofold, and is affected by the characteristics of the resource as well as the source from which it is obtained. When a resource is an important ingredient for the production of goods and services and is procured from the best available source, it becomes a critical input for the firm and there is a higher motivational investment in procuring the resource. Among other things, when a resource adds significant value or is necessary for differentiating a product, its criticality increases. The criticality of a resource also increases when it is procured from suppliers known for the quality of their products and processes or their reputation for innovation (Anderson and Narus 1984). Apple’s use of gyroscopes in iPhones provides a good example of resource criticality. Gyroscopes were positioned as one of the key differentiating features of iPhone. Apple relied on STMicroelectronics, a supplier with an established history of innovative products, to supply the gyroscopes (Keller 2010). A combination of the importance of the gyroscope and the performance record of STMicroelectronics made the specific external dependency critical for Apple. Thus, we define resource criticality as the extent to which a resource and its source of procurement contribute toward the value and differentiation of a firm’s products and services.

Impact of Resource Criticality. Resource criticality will positively impact operational integration for three specific reasons. While operational integration is generally beneficial for firms, research exploring interfirm partnerships suggests that firms face cost-benefit trade-offs when integrating operations (Gardner et al. 1994). Operational integration is resource intensive and requires firms to invest in relationship building, tailoring of inter-firm processes, and innovations to better synchronize external operations. As firms operate with a limited supply of investible resources, they are likely to be selective about operations to which they dedicate the resources. Moreover, integrated and dedicated operations are not easily replicated or transferred to alternative suppliers, and hence there are no scale benefits to be realized from supplier-specific investments for integration (Gosain et al. 2004). Hence, firms tend to critically assess the drivers for integration when creating close partnerships (Lambert et al. 1996, Lambert and Cooper 2000). Typically, firms are motivated to better integrate operations when there are significant anticipated impacts on profitability, prospects of improved market stature, or potential improvements in asset utilization.

Resource criticality significantly increases the prospects of realizing improved profitability, market stature, and asset utilization when integrating operations relevant to the critical resource, and thus creates the motivation to invest in such operations. Critical resources by definition involve suppliers of better repute, performance, and products, allowing firms to leverage the superiority of the inputs to differentiate their products in the market and boost their profit and competitive potential. Moreover, when firms use critical resources to add value to a product, the benefits are often multiplicative, or more than the sum of the benefits that can be derived by the firm or the supplier alone. For example, Asus has exploited the enhanced capabilities of quad core processors from NVIDIA in its latest generation of Transformer tablets to tap into the gaming market by allowing developers to create better gaming applications than competitors such as Apple and Samsung (Poeter 2011). Such synergistic benefits, which are difficult for the buyer or the supplier to realize on their own, provide added motivation to integrate operations. Additionally, as integrated operations imply continuity of relationships, the assets invested enjoy scale benefits of time, as they can be exploited over a longer time horizon. Hence, the need for dedicating resources to integrate operations gains validity as critical resources contribute toward better market performance, profits, and asset utilization.

We further hypothesize that the effect of resource criticality on transactional and financial IOS governance will be completely mediated by operational integration. The logic for mediation rests on two related arguments. First, independent of the degree of integration, there can be firm-specific variations in the methods and processes used to integrate operations. For example, vendor managed inventory (VMI) and just-in-time (JIT) delivery represent two different approaches to supplier integration. While VMI is a contractual obligation for suppliers to maintain inventory for customers at specified levels, JIT involves delivering products on an as-required basis involving cooperative planning and forecasting with the supplier (Kaipia et al. 2002). While both aid integrated operations, they employ different operational and transactional principles. As transactional governance primarily ensures the continuity of specific transactional processes, those choices cannot be made unless the processes are first defined and instituted. Second, the value that firms seek to capture through IOS governance is unlikely to materialize unless operations are integrated. Hence, the need for appropriating value through financial governance will not arise, unless operations are integrated in response to criticality of resources. Thus, resource criticality influences IOS governance through its impact on operational integration rather than directly.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A.</sup> Resource criticality increases the extent of transactional and financial IOS governance exercised by firms because it increases operational integration.

## Resource Replaceability

In addition to criticality, external dependencies are also assessed on the basis of resource replaceability. Resource replaceability represents the extent to which a resource or its suppliers can be readily substituted by a firm (Heide and John 1988, Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). Replaceability of a resource can depend on a number of factors. When there are multiple firms manufacturing a given resource it is easier to substitute a supplier due to availability of alternative sources of the resource. Additionally, replaceability is also enhanced when many firms can provide functionally similar substitutes of a resource. Finally, replaceability also depends on the specificity of the resource, implying the degree to which a resource is customized to the needs of a firm. For example, physical specificities may arise if a resource needs to conform to a specific shape, size, or other physical specifications (Malone et al. 1987). Additionally, geographic specificities can arise due to the physical proximity of a supplier (Malone et al. 1987). Less specific resources are more readily replaceable, as firms do not have to undertake any significant changes to accommodate and use alternative resources.

In essence, resource replaceability is enhanced with the availability of alternative sources for a resource or its functional substitutes, as well as the lower costs of substituting the sources for the resource. Thus, we define resource replaceability as the extent to which a resource or its source of procurement can be readily substituted by a firm. It must be noted that resource criticality and replaceability are distinct concepts and one does not imply the other. When there are no readily available alternative sources for a product, even noncritical resources can become irreplaceable. Conversely, with multiple available substitutes even critical resources can be replaced.

Impact of Resource Replaceability. When resource replaceability is high owing to the availability of alternatives or the use of nonspecific resources, firms will be reluctant to make the necessary investments in integrating operations. As explained before, operational integration requires devoting scarce managerial attention as well as significant resources toward the integration process. Since no specific relationship and related integrated processes are likely to sustain for long due to the constant search for the “best deal” from the available pool of alternatives, there will be less incentive to invest in integration with specific suppliers. In fact, deeper integration may produce lock-in effects preventing quick substitution of suppliers (Gossain et al. 2004). Given that integrated processes are specific and tuned to the needs of a given relationship, it will be difficult to adapt them to another firm quickly if a firm wants to change suppliers. Benefits that can result from having access to a large number of alternatives, such as exploitation of best spot prices, ability to change suppliers due to changes in demand, or ability to substitute nonperforming suppliers, can in fact be hampered in the presence of integrated operations (Gossain et al. 2004). Furthermore, the effect of resource replaceability on financial and transactional governance of IOS will be mediated by operational integration. As explained before, firm-specific variations in tools and methods used for operational integration, independent of the degree of integration, imply that IOS governance choices need to be in tune with the operational needs of the firm. Thus, replaceability primarily affects IOS governance through its negative impact on operational integration, wherein diminished need for operational integration reduces the motivation for IOS governance.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B.</sup> Resource replaceability decreases the extent of transactional and financial IOS governance exercised by firms because it decreases operational integration.

## Technological Uncertainty

Resource dependence theory further highlights how resource deficiencies subject firms to uncertainties, which affect their choice of external relationships (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). As both the resource and its sources lie beyond a firm’s boundary and control, fluctuations in the external environment can create disruptions in resource flows, and can impact a firm’s ability to anticipate and manage such disruptions. For example, external uncertainties can be associated with the volume requirements for a specific input. A cell phone manufacturer, for instance, might be concerned about the exact number of batteries it will need to order for the coming quarter. However, firms might also face uncertainties beyond volume unpredictability (Heide and John 1988). Disruptions might arise due to technological changes, creating uncertainties about the continued relevance and viability of specific inputs (Balakrishnan and Wernerfelt 1986). Owing to significant technological changes, certain inputs may become less economical or efficient to use, as improved or cheaper products are introduced or newer standards and compatibility requirements take effect. Many laptop manufacturers, for example, have become uncertain about the continued use of CD/DVD drives in their machines as network technologies have boosted the speed and richness of content delivery over the Internet.

We define technological uncertainty as the extent to which a firm is uncertain about the sustained and continued use of a product and the underlying technology in the future. We hypothesize that technological uncertainty will reduce the incentives for IOS ownership or control primarily by weakening the positive impact of resource criticality on operational integration. This will happen for two reasons. First, technological uncertainty will lead to a reassessment of the criticality of current exchange arrangements. While a firm will continue to value the role of a specific input or a given supplier, it will be aware that better options are likely to materialize when technological uncertainty is high. Hence, the criticality associated with existing arrangements will be diluted by the shadow of future expectations. Conversely, in situations of low technological uncertainty, firms are likely to feel more assured that current critical arrangements will continue to play their role in the value addition process on a sustained basis. Second, high technological uncertainty will also affect a firm’s response to conditions of criticality. Independent of how firms assess criticality, high technological uncertainty will lead to a prevailing sense of unpredictability whereby firms are discouraged from investing in relationships. Since operational integration requires significant resource investments, firms will be less inclined to commit such resources to relationships when they are less likely to be sustained under high technological uncertainty.

The impact of technological uncertainty is illustrated by the recent changes in the CPU industry, traditionally dominated by the x-86 family of microprocessors pioneered by Intel that are tuned to the Windows series of operating systems. The x-86 paradigm has been challenged by the emergence and rapid adoption of the competing ARM (Advanced RISC Machine) architecture that has gained ground due to its compatibility with mobile devices including tablets and smartphones (Snyder 2011). The emergence of ARM as an alternative platform has forced leading PC manufacturers such as Dell, which conventionally operated in the x-86 ecosystem, to reassess their technology platforms as well as their current supply chain partnerships. In the process of revamping its supplier base, Dell is creating alternative exploratory supply relationships with ARM chip manufacturers such as Marvell, TI, and Brodcom to enable a competitive transition to the ARM platform if needed (Merritt 2010, Matthew 2010). The shake-up in Dell’s supply chain illustrates how technological uncertainty leads to a reassessment of the criticality of resources, which weakens the relationships based on current resources. As the relationships are weakened in the presence of technological uncertainty, there is less incentive to invest in or manage the underlying IT applications supporting such relationships. Thus, technological uncertainty negatively impacts the extent of transactional and financial IOS governance primarily by attenuating the positive impact of criticality on operational integration.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A.</sup> Technological uncertainty decreases the extent of transactional and financial IOS governance exercised by firms because it weakens the positive influence of resource criticality on operational integration.

We further hypothesize that technological uncertainty will strengthen the negative impact of resource replaceability on operational integration, and thus diminish the need for IOS ownership and control. The knowledge of identified and accessible alternative sources of supply for specific inputs underlies the notion of replaceable arrangements. Under high technological uncertainty, above and beyond the identified sources of supply, firms will expect additional potential sources to materialize over time. As products and technologies change, it is conceivable that newer and more advanced sets of products and suppliers will emerge that will be compatible with a firm’s procurement needs. Hence, in effect, technological uncertainty will serve to expand the pool of sources a firm can use to replace its current arrangements. Technological uncertainty will also affect how a firm assesses the replaceability of its current arrangements. While a firm might perceive an arrangement to be irreplaceable due to scarcity of adequate alternative sources, technological uncertainty will weaken the perception due to the potential for alternative sources emerging in the future. By strengthening the negative impact of resource replaceability on operational integration, technological uncertainty will negatively influence the need for exercising financial and transactional IOS governance.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B.</sup> Technological uncertainty decreases the extent of transactional and financial IOS governance exercised by firms because it strengthens the negative influence of resource replaceability on operational integration.

## Control Variables

Consistent with the argument that larger firms are more likely to own and control IOS, we used firm size as a control. We include bargaining authority as a control to tap into the relative dyadic power and bargaining advantage of the focal firm. To account for the need to own and control IOS as a competitive response to actions of industry peers, we included competitive necessity as another control. Finally, consistent with the idea that firms will seek to control or own IOS when procuring products with high volume fluctuations, we included volume uncertainty as one of the controls.

## Empirical Study

Data for the study were collected through an online survey of U.S. manufacturing firms. The survey instrument was developed using standard procedures. In cases in which we found appropriate theoretical justification, we adapted the constructs and items from past studies. The instrument was tested for content validity by two IS faculty members and two Ph.D. students. Subsequently, the survey was administered to a few practitioners and seven responses were received, which helped to further refine our instrument. The online survey was administered by a professional organization with significant experience in conducting academic surveys and with a captive database of diverse firms. Using a set of online screening questions, the survey targeted manufacturing firms that were active IOS users, and had revenues exceeding \$50 million. We selected firms where respondents were from the procurement function as they were most likely to be informed about IOS-based procurement (Grover et al. 2002).

Data was collected at the level of an exchange arrangement represented by a specific supplier supplying a specific product. Each respondent provided answers for two different exchange arrangements, where they first identified a product procured electronically and then identified the top two suppliers. From a pool of 965 targeted respondents, 159 responses were collected. The response rate of 16.5% compares favorably with those reported in past studies (Grover et al. 2002, Teo et al. 2003). One hundred and thirty-one respondents provided information for two suppliers while 28 respondents provided information for a single supplier yielding a final set of 290 data points. We compared the responding firms with the nonresponders on criteria such as revenue, employees, and respondent position, and no systematic differences were detected. The responses were fairly diverse in terms of firm revenues, respondent ranks, and the type of IOS used (Table 2), while a variety of products were reported to be procured electronically (Table 3).

## Scale Validation

All our constructs were created using reflective items and hence validated accordingly (Petter et al. 2008). In the case of reflective scales, the items are influenced by the unobserved latent construct they represent, where all items are considered to be conceptual substitutes that are expected to co-vary with each other as well as with the overarching latent construct (Jarvis et al. 2003). We used the PLS program<sup>1</sup> to validate scale properties, which uses a bootstrapping technique and generates multiple samples of a fixed sample size. We ran a bootstrap of 1,000 samples with each sample having 289 data points. To test the scale properties, a PLS model was run with only the respective items loading on the constructs with hypothesized paths between the constructs omitted from the model (Chwelos et al. 2001). All constructs in our model had statistically significant item loadings with $" p ^ { \prime \prime }$ values of 0.0005 or less. International consistency ratio or ICR values showed that all the constructs had a high degree of unidimensionality (Table 4). Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the values of inter-construct correlations with the square root of average variance extracted for each construct. Diagonal elements in the shaded part of Table 4 represent the square root of the average variance of the items extracted by their underlying constructs. All diagonal values are greater than off-diagonal values, indicating adequate discriminant validity. All the parameters for the measurement model had low standard errors, ranging from 0.0049 to 0.0687, which were unlikely to weaken the reliability of our results (Marcoulides and Saunders 2006). The average standard deviation value for our constructs was around 1.50, indicating a fair amount of variability for all the constructs. We also report the Cronbach’s  values for all our constructs. As single informants were used for the study, we conducted Harman’s one factor test and the marker variable test to detect common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Harman’s one-factor test assesses if a single overarching factor emerges in a factor analysis of all the items, indicating the presence of common method bias. With all the variables entered into the factor analysis, no single overarching factor was discovered, with nine distinct factors emerging with Eigen values greater than 1.

We also assessed the bivariate correlations of the constructs against three marker variables: the length of employment of the respondent, the extent of webbased procurement, and the size of annual IS budget. None of the reported correlations were high enough to suspect common method bias, with the highest correlation value being only 0.15 between replaceability and the extent of web-based procurement.

Mediated-Moderation Results. Following Edwards and Lambert (2007), a four-step process was used to test the mediated-moderation Hypotheses 2A and 2B. The first of the four steps requires that the mediator significantly affect the outcome variable, implying a significant effect of operational integration on both transactional and financial governance. The second requirement is that the moderator significantly moderates the influence of the predictor on the mediating variable, implying that interactions of technological uncertainty with both criticality and replaceability have significant impact on operational integration. The third requirement is that the effect of the interaction terms on the outcome variable is completely mediated by the mediator. Finally, the magnitude of the impact of the predictors on the mediating variable must be affected by the moderator, implying that technological uncertainty should increase the negative effect of replaceability and decrease the positive effect of criticality on operational integration. The first of the four steps was already satisfied. We next report the results of the remaining three steps. The interaction between technological uncertainty and criticality had a significant negative impact on operational integration, and the introduction of the interaction term increased the R-square value, which was found to be statistically significant when assessed using the associated incremental F statistic (Table 5(a), Regressions 2 and 4). Tests of mediation indicate that the effects of the interaction term on both transactional and financial governance is completely mediated by operational integration, with calculated Sobel test statistic values of −20273 and −20289 respectively, which were significant at the 0.05 level. The interaction of technological uncertainty and criticality had no significant direct impact on either transactional or financial governance across all the regressions. Finally, as can be seen from the graph on the left in Figure 2, technological uncertainty significantly alters the relationship between criticality and operational integration. As indicated by the solid line in the graph, operational integration steadily increases with increasing criticality when technological uncertainty is low, and the effect is attenuated with no discernible impact when technological uncertainty is high. These results support Hypothesis 2A which states that technological uncertainty negatively impacts both the IOS governance choices by reducing the positive impact of criticality on operational integration.

We next report the results for Hypothesis 2B. The interaction between technological uncertainty and replaceability had no significant impact on operational integration, and the introduction of the interaction term did not significantly increase the R-square value (Table 5(a), Regressions 3 and 4). Mediation results indicate that there is no significant mediation of the effects of the interaction term on transactional or financial governance by operational integration.

## Discussion

We proposed and tested a mediated-moderation model to understand why firms seek to own and control an IOS. The results supported both our mediation hypotheses, wherein resource criticality increased the need for IOS governance by positively affecting operational integration, while resource replaceability diminished the need for IOS governance by negatively impacting operational integration. However, only one of the two mediated-moderation hypotheses was supported. Technological uncertainty negatively impacted the need for IOS governance by attenuating the positive effect of resource criticality on operational integration. However, results indicate that technological uncertainty failed to enhance the negative effect of resource replaceability on operational integration and hence failed to weaken the need for IOS governance as hypothesized. Finally, our proposed model plays a much larger role in explaining IOS governance choices than factors conventionally proposed as IOS governance drivers such as firm size, competitive compulsions, bargaining power, or demand uncertainties.

Mediation Hypotheses. The support for our mediation hypotheses establishes the importance of existing inter-firm relationships as well as the contingencies that influence them in shaping IOS governance choices. Of particular interest is the centrality of inter-firm relationships in dictating IOS governance choices. Researchers and practitioners have long argued that inter-firm relationships are sticky, and firms not only value the relationships but also tend to preserve them through various circumstances (Lorenzoni and Lipparini 1996). Our research explains why firms tend to stick with their relationships and why IOS governance choices are dictated by them. Features that characterize the operational relationships between two firms represent the cumulative effort of the firms in increasing the efficiency and effectiveness of the resource flows between them. Hence, firms try to preserve the needed features and their ability to appropriate value from the relationships by exercising transactional and financial control over the IOS used to enable the relationships. The role of inter-firm relationships in influencing IOS governance becomes apparent when examining the failure and evolution of B2B exchanges.

Research examining the failure of B2B exchanges has noted that the “greatest competition for B2B exchanges was not other B2B exchanges,” but rather it was the existing ways of doing business, and claimed that ignoring existing inter-firm arrangements while implementing B2B platforms was akin to “putting the cart before the horse” (Day et al. 2003, Sawhney 2002). More interestingly, surviving B2B exchanges such as Covisint and Elemica have done, has made them evolve their business models and they now provide services that accommodate and enable the existing collaborative needs of firms. While participation in Covisint previously required conformation to its rules and policies, firms can now purchase the needed services and applications and retain control over how they use the technology to suit their own needs.

Furthermore, support for our mediation hypotheses highlights the need for understanding the contingencies under which operational integration takes shape. A firm may be deficient in a number of resources, but not all of them will require similar levels of operational integration and hence create similar IOS governance needs. While critical resources will increase the need for operational integration, replaceable resources will diminish the need to integrate. This differential impact underlines why it is important to consider the contingencies while making IOS governance choices. When HP built its private IOS GetSupply to procure critical inputs such as memory chips, it also participated in the consortium-owned exchange Converge to exploit its spot buying features and auction capabilities for one-off procurement needs (Varon 2001). It is important to note that HP’s choice of IOS in response to criticality or replaceability of resources was ultimately dependent on the relationships it had in place for procuring such resources. While HP had the option to procure the memory chips from multiple suppliers using a consortium-owned IOS, it cited the dedicated arrangements it had with suppliers such as Celestica as the reason for using GetSupply to connect and transact with its preferred suppliers (Varon 2001).

The mediation results offer further insights when viewed alongside the results for the control variables in the model. Our results show that the need to suit current operations is a more important driver of IOS governance than the need to sustain power in dyadic relationships or the need to manage competitive necessities arising due to use of IOS by peer firms.<sup>2</sup> Our results help explain findings in earlier studies in the insurance industry that concluded that private ownership of IOS does not necessarily result in competitive benefits (Venkatraman and Zaheer 1990). We further found that while firm size affected transactional governance it had no significant effect on financial governance.<sup>3</sup> Finally, demand fluctuations for products had no influence on IOS governance. This demonstrates that firms do not seek to invest in systems just to manage demand uncertainties of products, and are motivated to control or own IOS only when such products are key for their operations, independent of their volume uncertainties.

Mediated Moderation Hypotheses. Important insights also emerge from the results of our mediated moderation hypotheses. Our results show that technological uncertainty will be a significant inhibitor for IOS governance decisions as it weakens the positive impact of criticality on operational integration. As we argue above, the weakening impact primarily materializes due to firms’ reassessment of criticality of resources and operational investments in light of technological unpredictability. Our findings are validated by the resurgence of B2B exchanges that support open sourcing in industries such as electronics, which are characterized by high innovation rates. A number of established market-makers and vendors such as Ariba and IBM are now creating B2B markets exploiting technologies like Cloud Computing (Radding 2010). The trend favoring public exchanges has grown as firms such as Dell have sought to redefine their supply chains and reach potential new suppliers to tap into emerging innovation pools. We emphasize that the negative effect in the presence of technological uncertainty does not necessarily negate the central and crucial role of inter-firm integration as discussed before. Essentially, technological uncertainty re-calibrates the requirements that necessitate operational integration rather than diminishing the role of operational integration itself. Levels of operational integration, independent of the factors that influence it, will continue to be the immediate and critical determinant of governance choices.

The results did not support the hypothesized role of technological uncertainty in negatively impacting IOS governance choices through its strengthening of the negative impact of replaceability on operational integration. The nonsignificant result can be explained by considering how firms tend to process the signals for integrating operations as opposed to terminating them. Comparing the coefficients of the causal paths leading from resource criticality and replaceability to operational integration, we find that the absolute value of the path-coefficient for criticality is much higher than that of replaceability. While both have significant, though opposing, effects on operational integration, the relative strengths of the path coefficients suggest that firms appear to more strongly respond to the needs for forming and sustaining relationships than to the signals for dissolving them. Since signals favoring loose coupling or dissolution of relationships have a relatively weaker impact to begin with, firms tend to discount any further signals, potentially generated by technological uncertainty, for decoupling of relationships.

## Limitations of the Study

One of the key limitations of our study is its lack of generalizability because we focused on manufacturing firms. IOS are routinely used by nonmanufacturing firms, including retailers and transportation and logistics firms. More studies are needed to examine whether similar sets of factors influence governance decisions across various contexts. Secondly, our study focused on the supplier’s side. As supply chains exhibit differing dynamics across the buyer and supplier sides, further studies are needed to examine the role of the proposed factors across various stages of the supply chain. Finally, we used multiple observations from the same firm. While we accounted for the lack of independence across observations by using the clustered-robust OLS technique, results can still be potentially confounded.

## Research Implications

The central contribution of our study is to fill a critical gap in research in terms of understanding the drivers of IOS governance choices made by firms. In spite of the practitioner relevance of IOS governance decisions and their role in determining the viability and use of IOS, IOS governance decisions remain poorly understood. Failures spanning successive generations of IOS technologies, from early EDI systems to recent B2B exchanges, have been generally attributed to concerns over stakeholder needs for control and ownership of IOS. However, there was a lack of theoretically grounded research focusing on the nomological network among factors influencing IOS governance choices. Moreover, there was also a lack of operational definition of IOS governance, with prior studies mostly relying on categorical choices such as private or public ownership of systems (Venkatraman and Zaheer 1990, Bakos and Nault 1997). Such categorization is restrictive and fails to adequately capture the nuances of IOS governance choices seen in practice. Our operationalization of IOS governance in terms of transactional and financial control creates an operational scale for measuring IOS governance and provides a more fine-grained approach to understanding governance decisions. We hope that the conceptualization of IOS governance and the scales enable future researchers to measure this important construct and examine its role in how firms create value through interorganizational systems.

Our study extends and complements the stream of research that has explored the role of IOS in fostering tighter buyer-supplier relationships (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1993, Bensaou 1997, Malhotra et al. 2005). Research in the area suggests that IT through its superior capabilities for storing, transmitting, and analyzing information provides a rich medium for collaboration between firms, where they can exploit electronic collaboration opportunities to undertake joint initiatives such as product innovation, quality improvement, and knowledge creation among others (Bensaou 1997, Malhotra et al. 2005). Our results demonstrate that the integrative effects of IT are in fact nuanced and not as universal as proposed. Firms will in fact prioritize how they want to exploit the integrative benefits of IT on the basis of the operational relationships they already have, and demonstrate their commitment to further strengthening the relationships by investing in ownership and management of the technology only in some specific contexts. We posited and found that resource deficiencies create the contingencies that dictate these choices through their effect on the operational arrangements that firms institute.

This study opens up several avenues for future research, a few of which are discussed below. First, future studies can examine the outcomes of governance choices. Studies can examine whether the implied fit between governance choices and existing operations in fact leads to significant differences in outcomes. Second, studies can explore whether the nature of outcomes at different levels of IOS governance are fundamentally different, and hence need to be examined separately. For example, firms using public exchanges might be more interested in cost savings than collaborative benefits that private IOS can generate. Third, a relevant factor that can be examined in greater detail is the role of IT capability in influencing IOS governance choices. The IOS governance choices that firms with significant IT capabilities such as Wal-Mart make might be premised on their ability to handle and create sophisticated IT systems. Hence, research can explore the impact of IT capabilities on IOS governance choices. Fourth, research can explore how IOS governance choices themselves impact the antecedents examined in this study. We assumed that organizational factors precede IOS choices and did not evaluate the contrary perspective. It is plausible that causality can flow in the opposite direction of what we propose, and the technology choices firms make can alter their current exchange arrangements and can even impact the criticality and replaceability of resources. The interplay between technology and organizational variables and the related directions of causality is extensively researched in the IS literature (Markus and Robey 1988), and it will be worthwhile to pursue this debate in the context of IOS.

## Implications for Practice

Our findings have important implications for IOS users as well as third party providers and IT vendors. As our results show, in general, users make IOS governance choices that match their operational arrangements. We caution however, that we do not test for performance implications of governance choices, and hence, cannot necessarily recommend that IOS governance decisions need to match current arrangements. Nevertheless, it is instructive to note the outcomes in the B2B exchange market, where the surviving exchanges adapted their business models to match the existing transaction norms of firms and industries or to the nature of the products transacted (Ravichandran et al. 2007, Friesen 2003). Hence, the overall pattern of IOS choice and use seems to be more in line with the findings of our study.

Given that IOS governance choices are generally matched to existing arrangements, third party providers and technology vendors will need to create appropriate offerings by understanding the exchange arrangements that need to be enabled. In cases in which firms are expected to control and own the IOS owing to integrated operations, third party providers will largely benefit from creating and enabling supporting services. Examples of such services include catalog technology, negotiation applications, and technology middleware among others. Such solutions allow users to continue using their own systems and vendor relationships, while they benefit from tools such as RFID inventory tracking or planning and forecasting applications that can plug into their existing

For firms seeking to leverage IOS for competitive benefits, our results suggest that managers closely examine the contingencies that influence their supplier relationships before investing in these systems. Historically, institutional pressures have played a role in influencing IOS adoption (Teo et al. 2003). While legitimacy considerations might be meaningful in some contexts, managers must be aware that the IOS investment decisions are more likely to be value enhancing when they complement the interfirm relationships they support. Since firms typically deal with a portfolio of relationships with varying degrees of operational overlap with their suppliers, a careful consideration is warranted when making choices about IOS.
