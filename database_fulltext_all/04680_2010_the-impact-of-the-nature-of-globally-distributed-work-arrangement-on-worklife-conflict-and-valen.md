# The impact of the nature of globally distributed work arrangement on work–life conflict and valence: the Indian GSD professionals’ perspective

Saonee Sarker<sup>1</sup>, Suprateek Sarker<sup>2</sup> and Debasish Jana<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information systems, Washington State University, Pullman, WA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Centre for Applied Information and Communication Technologies, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg, Denmark; <sup>3</sup>Simplex Infrastructures Ltd, Kolkata, India

Correspondence: Saonee Sarker, Department of Information Systems, College of Business, Washington State University, PO Box 644743, Todd Hall 440D, Pullman, WA 99164-4743, U.S.A. Tel: þ 1 (509) 335 1183

## Abstract

While there is a rich literature exploring the various facets of Globally Distributed Systems Development (GSD), there is scant attention given to the human resource issues such as work–life conflict (WLC) faced by GSD professionals and their valence toward globally distributed work. In this study, we attempt to identify factors that influence offshore GSD workers’ WLC and their valence toward globally distributed work. Specifically, we focus on factors related to the nature of the distributed work arrangements as experienced by offshore Indian workers involved in GSD. On the basis of survey data, we find support for the impact of a number of distribution-related variables on WLC and valence.

European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 209–222. doi:10.1057/ejis.2010.20; published online 30 March 2010

Keywords: work–life conflict; work–life balance; global software development; distributed teams; valence

## Introduction

Consider the following anecdote about an Information Technology (IT) professional’s life offered by Messersmith (2007):

Beep y beep y beep y The sound of a pager pierces the night as Chris reaches across his bed to inspect the message: ‘Bill cycle 24 is down – please call help line ASAP.’ Chris sighs as he pulls himself out of bed and glances at the 3:00 A.M. reading on the clock. As he grabs his cell phone and heads toward his laptop, he laments that this is the third time this week that his pager has interrupted him in the middle of the night.

To add to the demands of taking calls in the middle of the night, Chris and his team had been spending 12 h days for the past 5 weeks working on the development and testing of a new information system. These patterns may not be typical or persistent for IS development staff working in traditional (i.e., co-located) environments, except perhaps for those involved in the maintenance of critical systems. However, the pressure to be working or to be routinely available round the clock is familiar to most IS professionals involved in Global Software Development (GSD) (King & Torkzadeh, 2008; Sarker & Sarker, 2009). As we are all well aware, the landscape of software development has changed dramatically in recent times (Robinson & Kalakota, 2004; King & Torkzadeh, 2008). Much of today’s software development occurs in GSD settings, where development team members are located in different parts of the world, and collaboratively work on the same development project (e.g., Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Sarker & Sarker, 2009). For many organizations around the world, GSD has become an important option to gain competitive advantage (e.g., Carmel & Agarwal, 2002; Krishna et al., 2004). Recent estimates suggest that the demand for GSD is increasing, and will continue to grow at least until 2011 (http://www.offshoringtimes.com, 26 March 2009).

While software development in general is complex, time-consuming, and often stressful for those involved (as Chris’ example discussed above highlights), the problem is multiplied several times when such development is conducted in globally distributed settings, owing to the coordination and communication required (Prikladnicki et al., 2003; Sarker & Sarker, 2009). Given the complexities involved, and the increasing relevance of distributed teams used to accomplish systems development tasks, it is not surprising that IS researchers have given the arena of GSD considerable attention recently. However, our review of the literature suggests that much of the prior research has focused on: (a) the benefits/ disadvantages of this practice to the organizations closely examining the financial benefits, costs, and risks associated with this venture; (b) the factors driving an organization’s decision to engage in GSD; (c) developing/assessing strategies for effective management of GSD projects; and (d) the factors, including those associated with vendors and clients, that determine outcomes of projects (Aron et al., 2005; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Romano Jr. et al., 2007; Sakthivel, 2007; King & Torkzadeh, 2008).

Interestingly, while the motivation for GSD is often to harness appropriate human capital, there appears to be scant attention given to the human resource issues faced by systems development professionals who work in a globally distributed environment. Indeed, Niederman et al. (2006, p. 57) observe that there is an immediate need to investigate working conditions of the GSD workforce. In this study, we take a step in this direction, by examining work–life conflict (WLc) and related human resource issues faced by Indian IT professionals engaged in GSD.

It is now well known that India provides a significant proportion of IT workers involved in GSD work (Robinson & Kalakota, 2004). Recent literature has highlighted that turnover rates are very high in the Indian IT sector, somewhere between 30 and 80% (Lacity et al., 2008), which is a big concern for all organizations involved in GSD (Lacity et al., 2008, p. 226). Hurley & Costa (2001) argues that ‘there is most certainly a set of predictors and prescriptive actions that can minimize the likelihood of losing [these] high-calibre people’, with one of the top issues being the IT professionals’ work–life balance (WLB).

WLB refers to ‘helping employees to balance the demands of work with those of their lives beyond the workplace’ (Nord et al., 2002, p. 223). In today’s global and hyper-competitive work environments, increasing personal needs of individuals, heightened personal expectations, and infusion of certain technologies and work practices have changed the ‘dynamics of work-life balance’ (Greenblatt, 2002). This is particularly true for the IT workers (Quesenberry et al., 2006), especially those involved in GSD, who often need to deal with the uneven demands of IT work, and consequently experience high levels of WLC. What this suggests is that in order to retain GSD workers who contribute to the productivity of organizations in many of the developed nations, it is necessary to focus on their WLC (i.e., a lack of WLB), and eventually design ways to mitigate the imbalance in their lives. However, in spite of the recognition that attention to balancing work life of the GSD workforce can help in retaining personnel, and thereby avoid additional recruitment, training, and knowledge transfer costs, to our knowledge, no known research has examined the factors that might contribute to work–life imbalance or conflict of workers engaged in GSD, in general, or for the Indian IT workforce involved in GSD. In fact, our search of the ABI/Informs database using the search terms, ‘work-life balance and offshoring’, ‘work-life balance and distributed teams’, ‘work-life balance and globally distributed software development teams’, and ‘work-life conflict and offshoring’, resulted in no scholarly article being selected.

Our key objective in this study is thus to fill this void by studying some of the key factors affecting the WLC of the Indian IT professionals involved in GSD. Given the multitude of factors that can potentially be relevant, in this study, we focus on the nature of the distributed arrangement and the effect it has on the WLC. Our primary research question may be stated as:

## RQ1: What are the key distributed work arrangementrelated factors that lead to WLC for IT professionals involved in GSD projects?

We realize that it is important not only to focus on the short-term retention of the IT workforce, given the widespread use and increasing demand for GSD, but also to understand (and ensure) that the IT workforce’s general orientation (i.e., valence) toward working in distributed software development remains positive. It is only through such positive orientation of the IT workforce that the GSD model can be utilized for a longer period of time for achieving productivity gains – indeed, being able to sustain GSD is a concern raised by a panel of leading scholars (King & Torkzadeh, 2008). Thus, our second research question is:

## RQ2: What are the key distributed work arrangementrelated factors that affect the valence of IT workers involved in GSD projects?

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on WLC, valence, and GSD, and the key antecedents pertaining to the distributed arrangement that has been suggested in prior research on distributed work. Next, we discuss our research model and our hypotheses. Thereafter, we provide an overview of our research methodology, including our data collection, measures adopted, analysis, and the results of the study. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the results, the study’s contributions and limitations, and directions for future research.

## Review of the literature

## Work–life balance/conflict

WLB has been defined as ‘the relationship between the institutional and cultural times and spaces of work and non-work in societies where income is predominantly generated and distributed through labor markets’ (Felstead et al., 2002, p. 56). WLB has also been defined as the degree ‘to which an individual is simultaneously able to balance the temporal, emotional, and behavioral demands of both paid work and family responsibility’ (Hill et al., 2001, p. 49). Lack of a WLB increases the ‘work-family conflict’ or WLC, which is the ‘inter-(between) role conflict where the demands created by the job interfere with performing family-related responsibilities’ (Netemeyer et al., 2004, p. 50). On similar lines, the existence of WLB has been viewed as ‘the absence of unacceptable levels of conflict between work and nonwork demands’ (Greenblatt, 2002, p. 179).

Prior researchers argue that there are typically five different models on WLB/WLC (Zedeck & Mosier, 1990; O’Driscoll, 1996; Guest, 2002). First is the segmentation model, which argues that ‘work and non-work are two distinct domains of life that are lived quite separately and have no influence on each other’ (Guest, 2002, p. 258). The spillover model, on the other hand, rejects this segmentation view, and believes that there are ample spillovers between these two domains; researchers adhering to this view focus on understanding the nature of these spillovers. Spillover models hold that time during which a worker is employed ‘has boundaries’, and are ‘juxtaposed to non-work times and places’, and that it is important to understand the point of transitioning from one space to another (i.e., shift from the work space/time to the non-work space/time) and to manage the process of transitioning (Felstad et al., 2002). The compensation model argues that whatever an individual lacks in one domain in terms of satisfaction or demand is made up in the other domain, while the instrumental model argues that certain activities in one domain can actually be used to ‘facilitate success’ in the other domain, and attempts to understand how the facilitation can be made. Finally, the conflict model argues that the high demands of the modern globalized economy causes significant conflicts between one’s work and life domains, which are unavoidable. Researchers supporting the conflict model attempt to understand the types of conflict, the antecedents and effects of this conflict, and how they may be reduced (Greenhaus & Beutell, 1985; Guest, 2002). Kossek et al. (1999), for example, note that proponents of the conflict model typically assess ‘the sources, processes and types of conflict between work and family roles and their consequences for attitudinal outcomes’. In the GSD context, where IT professionals are distributed across time and place, we believe that the conflict model is most suitable. Given the temporal, spatial, and cultural distances with colleagues and clients that IT professionals engaged in software development have to continuously bridge (Sarker & Sahay, 2004; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Oshri et al., 2009), we would expect that they will face significant conflicts between their work and non-work domains.

The conflict model defines WLC as the ‘inter-(between) role conflict where the demands created by the job interfere with performing family-related responsibilities’ (Netemeyer et al., 2004, p. 50). Quick et al. (2004, p. 428) argue that two important types of conflict that hinder the balance in one’s work and life are time-based conflict and strain-based conflict. Time-based conflict refers to the conflict that arises when ‘the time devoted to work makes it difficult to fulfill the obligations and requirements of the family role’. Strain-based conflict, on the other hand, arises when the ‘pressures of the work role’ spill over and ‘affect interactions within the family domain’ (Quick et al., 2004, p. 428). In the context of GSD arrangements, we expect both these types of conflicts to be evident among many employees. In such arrangements, selected employees at one center are expected to regularly coordinate their activities (and collaborate) with employees at the overseas centers. Significant time differences between these centers mandate the need for employees in one (or both) locations to stretch their work times, and thus create conflicts with their family times. Similarly, working in distributed networks (across geographical and sometimes culture distances) can bring in its own set of frustrations due to lack of a shared frame of reference regarding the work, and differences in working styles, cultures, and priorities (e.g., Sarker & Sahay, 2004), all of which contribute to high levels of strain-based conflict.

Prior research has investigated WLC from either the organizational level or the individual-level perspectives (Felstead et al., 2002; Nord et al., 2002; Lyness & Kropf, 2005; Mesmer-Magnus & Viswesvaran, 2005). Among the individual-level variables, the nature of the family structure of the employee, and his/her gender has been argued (and empirically shown in certain cases) to have severe implications for his/her WLB. Individuals who have family, or have ‘family responsibilities’ tend to experience both higher time-based and strain-based conflicts (e.g., Lyness & Kropf, 2005, p. 43). On the other hand, the role of gender on WLB has received mixed support. While some researchers have suggested that gender does not have a significant effect on work conflict, others have argued that gender does play an important role, with female employees experiencing greater WLC than male employees (e.g., Lyness & Kropf, 2005). Finally, in recent research, ‘the predictive power of personality variables’ such as neuroticism on an individual’s WLC has also been established (e.g., Wayne et al., 2004, p. 109).

Among organizational-level variables, providing flexible work schedules to employees has a positive effect on their WLB (e.g., Thompson et al., 2004; Lyness & Kropf, 2005). In fact, most ‘leading organizations have begun offering flexible work arrangements’ with the belief that it lowers employees’ WLC (Spinks, 2004, p. 5). Some researchers suggest that organizational support in the form of flexibility in working hours does not necessarily mean that WLC would decrease, since employees may be reluctant or ‘afraid’ to take advantage of benefits such as flextime y because of possible negative career consequences’ (Thompson et al., 2004, p. 548). Thus, even more than flextime availability, according to Thomas & Ganster (1995), the existence of a supportive supervisor or a ‘good boss’ (Hurley & Costa, 2001) can help reduce employees’ WLC.

While we feel that the above discussed factors are potentially relevant to the GSD context, and can be validated through studies of globally distributed workers, in this study we wish to focus not on these general factors, but on the factors specific to distributed arrangements (in line with the focus of the Special Issue), that have understandably not been studied in WLB investigations in traditional (co-located) settings.

Next, we discuss the concept of valence, which is a core aspect of the second research question.

## Valence

The Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary (2009) defines valence as the ‘the degree of attractiveness an individual, activity, or thing possesses as a behavioral goal’. Similarly, Hoffman & Kleinman (1994) view valence as the degree of positive or negative feeling an individual has toward an option. Valence owes its origins to the Expectancy Theory (Vroom, 1964), and the concept has been argued to have implications for individual employees’ ‘motivational state’ and their turnover intentions (e.g., Oliver, 1977).

There are multiple views regarding the salient antecedents of valence, or on how valence develops. One group of researchers believes that ‘values are assumed to be one set of variables that influence these positive or negative valences’ (Feather et al., 1998, p. 641). Proponents of this value approach believe that valence develops because of individuals’ inherent beliefs about the ‘ways of behaving and about the desirable goals or end states’ (Feather et al., 1998, p. 641). An alternate view regarding valence is that they are ‘linked to a specific context and to a present timeframe’ (Feather, 1995, p. 1135). Proponents of this context-specific view of valence argue that valence is developed due to ‘perceived goal properties of potential actions and outcomes, or objects and events, at a particular moment in time’, and heavily depend on the ‘situation’ a person finds himself/ herself in (Feather et al., 1998, p. 641). Consistent with the situation-specific view of valence, Hoffman & Kleinman (1994) argue that valence develops through a weighing in of the positive and negative characteristics of a particular situation. Similarly, Oliver (1977) argues that valence towards a particular event is heavily dependent on the ‘degree of effort involved in its attainment’. In other words, if individuals perceive a certain event to be frustrating and requiring great deal of effort on their behalf, they are likely to form a negative valence toward it.

We believe that, in the context of our study, valence toward cross-national-distributed work similar to WLC is also likely to be affected by the person’s ‘distributionsituation’ variables.

## The uniqueness of the GSD environment

GSD is defined as a ‘collaborative software development internationally’ (Carmel & Agarwal, 2001). While there can be much variation in GSD arrangements and the nature of software being developed, broadly GSD projects can be viewed as ‘activities, methods, practices and technologies’, which include, but are not limited to, tasks such as the collection of requirements, the development process, software configuration, testing, user training, etc. (Prikladnicki et al., 2003). Typically, in many of these phases, members from multiple locations are involved (Sarker & Sahay, 2004; Sarker & Sarker, 2009). In a way, the tasks involved in GSD projects can be likened to the ‘Performance’ category of tasks in McGrath’s (1984) Task Circumplex model. McGrath (1984, p. 65) includes the ‘performance’ tasks under his ‘Execute’ category or Quadrant IV tasks, and argues that such types of tasks ‘involve complex sets of activities requiring coordination between members and over time’. The other issue associated with these types of ‘Performance’ tasks is that they often need to be ‘disassembled into their individual components, allocated to the individual members, and then assembled back (McGrath, 1984). This creates additional coordination complexities.

In the context of GSD, it must be noted that executing such performance tasks in a globally distributed environment poses its own unique challenges. Researchers in IT-related distributed work argue that time and space differences are the key challenges in such work (e.g., Carmel, 1999; Sarker & Sahay, 2004). For example, Carmel & Agarwal (2001) argue that communication, coordination, and control are the three primary issues facing GSD teams. Similarly, Evaristo et al. (2003) high light the level of dispersion and the level of synchronicity (or time overlaps) among the members as important aspects of GSD projects. Krishna et al. (2004) draw attention to the role that cultural differences play in such contexts.

To summarize, the GSD literature indicates that time differences, communication, coordination, and cultural differences among members are the key issues in GSD projects. Thus, the key distribution-specific variables that we sought to examine are (1) the extent of time differences between the individual IT worker and his/her other team members, and (2) the nature of the communication required for the project. Further, we also sought to understand the nature of coordination (Sarker & Sahay, 2004; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005) required by the GSD project on the WLB. Specifically, as the number of countries in which distributed team members are located increase, the coordination required and the difficulty of reaching the state of mutual understanding also increases, and this could not only negatively impact the WLB, but also increasingly frustrate the IT worker. This is likely to negatively impact his/her long-term attitude toward the globally distributed mode of working. Thus, in this study, we examine if the number of countries in which other distributed team members are located impacts offshore IT workers’ WLC as well as valence. Finally, consistent with the important role of the cultural differences/similarities between members in GSD contexts, and given that the similarities/differences are likely to have implications on the extent of communication and coordination required (Sarker & Sarker, 2009), we also study the role of perceived similarity.

## Hypotheses

## Time differences with remote colleagues

The organizational behavior literature frequently focuses on the important role of work hours of employees, and argues that ‘the number of hours people work y partially determines their ability to achieve [the] desired integration or work and family role demands’, and shapes their general attitude toward work (Valcour, 2007, p. 1512). Believers of this approach to work argue that ‘time is a finite resource that cannot be expanded through engagement in multiple roles, an hour devoted to one domain represents an hour that is not available to the other domain’. Work domain spilling over to family domain is specifically rampant in the context of offshoring and distributed work. According to Gambles et al. (2006, p. 47–48), while today’s technology enables telework across time and place, it ‘often results in people working longer and at a faster pace’; this is especially true when individuals work with colleagues located in other nations, and ‘across time zones’. In such cases, they ‘often feel that they have to be constantly available’ (Gambles et al., 2006, p. 48) or may have to work late into evenings (or in early mornings) to coordinate tasks with (or clarify issues for) geographically remote colleagues. These colleagues may be in time zones that have little or no overlap with the work hours of that individual, thereby negatively affecting each team member’s WLB. In the instance of Indian IT workers, they often need to work late into the night to coordinate activities or attend meetings with their remote colleagues/client representatives located in the United States. Working consistently until late hours (inconsistent with their physiological clocks) tends to cause frustrations for these IT workers (Sarker & Sahay, 2004), ultimately leading to their negative valence toward globally distributed IT work (Oliver, 1977).

H1a: IT workers involved in GSD with little or no work–time overlaps with distributed (remote) team members/ colleagues will experience higher WLC.

H1b: IT workers involved in GSD with little or no work–time overlaps with distributed (remote) team members/ colleagues will develop negative valence toward globally distributed mode of IT work.

## Nature of communication required in the project

Communication is extremely critical in distributed ISD contexts where the team needs to convey ideas, converge on ideas, manage their project, and frequently interact with clients and other team members for relationship development and maintenance (e.g., Nidumolu, 1995; Montoya et al., 2009). The frequency of communication required to fulfill the tasks in globally distributed software settings are likely to have an impact on the individuals (e.g., Espinosa et al., 2007). Others argue that it is not only the frequency of communication required but also the extent to which this communication and interaction comes in the form of synchronous communication, in which the IT worker is required to answer immediate questions (or provide clarifications) to remote members, and enable ‘convergence’ or shared understanding on issues with immediacy (Dennis et al.. 2008).

Messersmith (2007) further argues that in the current age, most ISD workers are expected to be available ‘anytime, anywhere’, and this usually results in tremendous strainbased conflict for the IT worker (Messersmith, 2007). This is even greater for GSD where coordination meetings occur at odd times, disrupting the physiological and social rhythms of IS professionals (Sarker & Sahay, 2004), and thereby negatively impacting their WLC and their valence towards distributed IT work.

H2a: IT workers, involved in GSD, who engage in frequent communication involving high levels of synchronous communication with their remote members will experience higher WLC.

H2b: IT workers, involved in GSD, who engage in frequent communication involving high levels of synchronous communication with their remote members will develop negative valence toward globally distributed mode of IT work.

## Number of distributed locations

Complexity of a team has often been linked to both team-related and member-related outcomes in the context of globally distributed teams (e.g., Espinosa et al., 2007). One of the major sources of complexity in such contexts is the coordination required among the team members, which depends greatly on how these team members ‘are configured geographically’ (Espinosa et al., 2007, p. 613). Such a view has also been expressed by other researchers, who argue that as the number of distributed locations in which an individual’s team members are located increases, the coordination of tasks, complexities associated with the transition of work from one place to another, especially when there are time-zone differences, also increases (e.g., Sarker & Sahay, 2004; Romano Jr. et al., 2007). Such problems result in much more strain as well as involvement on the part of the IT worker to ensure that things work smoothly, thus resulting in greater work–life imbalance. In addition, the additional, persistent, and inescapable stress of coordinating among multiple locations across national boundaries is also likely to increase their levels of frustration, and thereby have a negative impact on their valence towards distributed IT work in general.

H3a: IT workers in GSD project teams with members distributed across multiple cross-national locations will experience higher WLC.

H3b: IT workers in GSD project teams with members distributed across multiple locations will develop lower valence toward globally distributed mode of IT work.

The moderating role of perceived similarity It has been argued that the extent of similarity that an individual has with his/her remote team members/ colleagues tends to mitigate the challenges of working with colleagues in multiple distributed locations (Espinosa et al., 2007). Sarker & Sahay (2004) show that one of major challenges in GSD is that members may end up working with colleagues located in different countries, who prefer different types of technologies/techniques, communication styles, work practices, etc. Moreover, the complexity of maintaining communicative linkages across multiple locations increases substantially over the complexity of maintaining linkages within one location. Thus, in teams distributed across multiple locations, members have to put in extra effort to get to know each other and to facilitate relationship development (McKnight et al., 1998). Such initiatives need to be undertaken in addition to the already heavy ISD workload, spilling over into an employee’s family and leisure time (Messersmith, 2007). However, when individuals are similar to their remote colleagues, they ‘communicate more effectively because they have common ground and refer to the same technical terminology’ (Espinosa et al., 2007, p. 616). This reduces any inherent process losses within the team, and individuals ‘spend less time acquiring information about each other’. They are then likely to experience less work spillovers into their leisure time, and also be more positively orientated towards distributed ISD work.

H4a: IT workers in GSD project teams with members distributed across multiple cross-national locations, but sharing similarity with these colleagues, will experience lower WLC.

H4b: IT workers in GSD project teams with members distributed across multiple cross-national locations, but sharing similarity with these colleagues, will develop higher valence toward globally distributed mode of IT work.

## Research methodology

We adopted a survey methodology to empirically test our proposed model. The data utilized in this study satisfied two conditions: (a) the respondents were working in globally distributed software development environment, and (b) they were working out of offices in India.

A variation of the snowballing technique was used to identify potential respondents. Snowball sampling was first introduced by Coleman (1958) and is defined as a process in which first a ‘random sample of individuals is drawn from a finite population’ (Goodman, 1961, p. 148). Once these individuals complete a survey, they are asked to name additional individuals (Goodman, 1961), who could be ‘best friends’, or ‘individuals from whom one seeks advice the most’, etc. These different individuals next complete the survey, and in this way a chain of survey respondents are tapped into. Indeed, researchers argue that it could be viewed as a ‘formal sampling method’ (Snijders, 1992) with which to make statistical inferences (Goodman, 1961). A snowballing technique such as the one discussed above has several benefits (e.g., Myers & Ogunc, 2008): (1) it helps in identifying a useable and requisite sample size such that statistical power is achieved, (2), it helps in garnering a good sample size across multiple organizations, thereby removing organization-specific responses, and finally, (3) it is argued that respondents identified through such a technique tend to be similar in demographics and other individual-related variables, and thus, eliminate an effect of these extraneous variables on the relationships of interest. Further, snowball sampling is considered to be appropriate and suitable especially in contexts in which the respondents need to fulfill certain strict eligibility conditions (Mick, 1996). For example in the context of this study, our respondents needed to fulfill the condition that they are IT workers involved in GSD and working from India with distributed colleagues in other parts of the world. Further, we wanted to capture respondents from a range of different types of organizations, such that the WLC that the respondents experienced was not an artifact of their organization, but the nature of their work. Clearly, the objectives listed above would be very difficult to meet using a random sampling approach; thus we used an approach that was practical while trading off some of the formal statistical generalizability.

Consistent with the guidelines put forth by prior researchers using snowball sampling (e.g., Coleman, 1958; Goodman, 1961), we identified a number of IT workers involved in GSD (through personal connections, etc.) over email, and encouraged them to complete the electronic survey, and, in relevant cases, also to identify other individuals fulfilling our sampling criteria and to forward the electronic survey link to them. Through this, we developed a chain of respondents.

We note that the responses were anonymous, given the nature of the topic (especially the dependent variables), though we did ask the respondents to optionally list their organization.

## Sample

At least 300 individuals were contacted. However, after removing incomplete surveys, our final useable sample consisted of 141 respondents. Out of these respondents, 118 were males, and 23 were females. Further, 28 of the respondents were under the age of 25, while 114 respondents were between the age of 25 and 55 years. A majority of our respondents had between 2 and 10 years of experience within the IT industry, with a similar number of years experience in working in globally distributed software development contexts. Respondents included members of well-known organizations such as IBM, HP, Saudi Aramco, PricewaterhouseCoopers, Tata Consultancy Services, AOL, Infosys, Polaris, Cognizant, Accenture, Siemens, HSBC, etc. as well as several less known organizations. We summarize the demographic details of our sample in Table 1.

## Measures

We used established instruments (where relevant) for measuring our constructs. Our primary construct, WLC was measured using nine items adapted from Kopelman et al. (1983). This is a widely used scale, and its variations have been used in many prior studies.

The frequency of communication was assessed by asking individuals to respond to ‘how often have you needed to communicate with your (globally) distributed team members?’ on a Likert scale. On the other hand, extent of synchronous communication required in the project was assessed by an item that captured the extent (on a scale of 1 to 7) to which they needed to synchronously communicate with their globally distributed team members using technologies such as online chats, videoconferencing, or teleconferencing.

Respondents were asked to provide the largest time difference they had with their distributed team members. From this information, a categorical variable was created that captured the time difference with remote colleagues. Specifically, the absence of time difference was coded as 0, a time difference of 1–4 was coded as 1, a time difference of 5–8 was coded as 2, and a time difference of 9 h or more was coded as 3.

The number of cross-national locations in which one’s remote colleagues are located was assessed using a single item. Specifically, individuals were asked to respond to the following questions: ‘typically, how many different countries (including yours) have your (globally) distributed project team members been located in?’ with the options being 2, 3, 4, 5, and X6.

The extent of similarity among group members was measured using three items modified from items measuring perceived similarity in organizations and groups (Turban & Jones, 1988; Hobman et al., 2003). The items captured the extent to which individuals perceived that they shared the same values, perspectives, and outlook as their team members, and viewed things in similar ways.

## Analysis

We used PLS Graph 3.0 to analyze the data. The PLS analysis technique has been viewed as superior for medium sample sizes, and when the model has interaction variables, as in this study (e.g., Chin et al., 2003). Consistent with PLS guidelines (Chin et al., 2003), the first phase involved the instrument validation, followed by a test of the hypothesized relationships.

Instrument validation To assess the validity of the instrument, a confirmatory factor analysis was conducted. Adequate convergent validity was confirmed based on the following: (1) all items loaded significantly on their respective constructs, and none of the items had a loading below 0.50 (Hulland, 1999); (2) the composite reliabilities of each of the items were above 0.70 (Hulland, 1999); and finally, (3) the Average Variance Extracted (AVEs) of all the constructs were over the threshold value of 0.50. In assessing the discriminant validity, we ensured that the square root of the AVE of a construct exceeded all correlations between that factor and any other construct within the study (Gefen & Straub, 2005). Further, we also examined the correlation between the latent variable scores and the measurement items. This examination suggested that the measurement items loaded higher on their ‘assigned factor’ than on any other factor (see Table 5) (Gefen & Straub, 2005, p. 93) (please see Tables 2-4).

Hypothesis testing Once the psychometric properties were verified, the hypotheses were tested. To test the hypotheses predicting the moderating effects, interaction terms were created following the product-indicator approach suggested by Chin et al. (2003).

H1a and b suggested that IT workers involved in GSD with little or no work–time overlaps with distributed (remote) team members/colleagues will experience higher WLC and negative valence towards distributed IT work. H1a was supported (b ¼ 0.267, Po0.01), while H1b was not supported (b ¼ 0.099, p40.10). H2a and 2b argued that IT workers, involved in GSD, who engage in frequent communication involving higher levels of synchronous communication with their remote members will experience higher WLC and lower valence toward the globally distributed model for ISD. H2a was supported (b ¼ 0.168, P ¼ 0.05), while H2b was not supported (b ¼ -0.053, p40.10). H3a and b suggested that IT workers in GSD project teams with members distributed across multiple cross-national locations will experience higher WLC and develop lower valence toward globally distributed IT work. H3a was not supported (b ¼ -0.171, p40.10), while H3b was supported (b ¼ -0.708, Po0.01). Finally, H4a and b suggested that IT workers in GSD project teams with members distributed across multiple cross-national locations, but sharing similarity with these colleagues will experience lower WLC, and develop higher valence toward GSD. H4a was not supported $( b = 0 . 0 4 4 , p > 0 . 1 0 ) _ { . }$ but H4b was supported (b ¼ 0.773, Po0.01). Finally, the independent variables explained 12.3% of the variance in WLC, and 12.4% of the variance in valence toward distributed ISD, showing that the distribution-related variables do indeed have some level of impact on the two dependent variables chosen. Much of the results summarized above can be explained through the ‘resource drain’ approach to work–family balance (Valcour, 2007, p. 1514). WLB or WLC is seen as the balance (or lack thereof) that individuals experience on a daily basis. According to the ‘resource drain’ theory, time is seen as an important personal resource (Valcour, 2007). Resource drain, then, is viewed as the daily ‘transfer of finite personal resources such as time, attention, and energy from one domain [e.g., work] to another [e.g., family]’ (Edwards & Rothbard, 2000, p. 181). As these resources are finite, when one domain demands more of these resources on a daily basis. a person may be left with little or no resource to spend in the other domain. For example, when one works evening shifts, it takes away a person’s time resource away from the family (Edwards & Rothbard, 2000). Similarly, when a person works very long hours, his/her energy and attention resources may be too drained to expend in the family domain (Valcour, 2007). We believe that a similar effect is seen in the context of the Indian IT workers involved in GSD where they share limited time-zone overlaps (because of significant time differences) with their remote team members, and need to engage in frequent and high level of synchronous communication. Both these abovementioned constructs demand an important personal resource from the IT workers studied, that is, time. When the workers do not share time overlaps, they need to work beyond their normal hours to coordinate activities with their remote team members, or to ask/answer relevant questions related to the project. Similarly, when frequent communication (that too, synchronous) is required, the GSD participants need to be present at their work domain for longer hours, which eventually ends up causing a huge drainage on their time and energy, such that they have very little left to spend on their family domain. This in turn results in high-levels of WLC.

On the other hand. the number of cross-national locations (e.g. India with Malaysia, Thailand, Singapore, and Japan), while may increase the complexities of the project, does not necessarily cause daily drainage of one’s personal resources (e.g., Edwards & Rothbard, 2000), and thus, was not found to have an effect on the WLC of the Indian IT workers. However, the number of cross-national locations involved as well as the perceived similarity the Indian IT workers felt with their remote team members had an effect on their valence toward future distributed ISD. These results can be explained by drawing on prior research as well. Krishna et al. (2004, p. 64) conclude from their years of study of GSD that ‘cross-border collaboration’ is ‘not a trouble-free process’, especially when the collaboration involves multiple cross-border locations. Differences in working styles, in addition to ‘cultural norms of social behavior, attitudes towards authority, and language issues’ all pose significant challenges, and the more cross-border locations involved, the greater is the challenge (Krishna et al., 2004, p. 64). For example, it was found that Indian IT workers needed to ‘approach communication with U.S. and Japanese’ collaborators differently, with U.S. members preferring ‘extensive written agreements and explicit documentation, reinforced with frequent and informal telephone and email contact’, while the Japanese preferring ‘verbal communication, more tacit and continuously negotiated agreements, and less frequent but more formal use of electronic media’ (Krishna et al., 2004, p. 64). Similarly, Sarker & Sahay (2004), from their empirical study of distributed cross-national ISD teams, found that diversity in terms of national cultures leads to several problems such as members switching to their native languages from time to time, making the remote members feel excluded, or in some cases, members being caught unaware about different festivals and holidays celebrated in the remote members’ country. These caused added frustrations in terms of scheduling project activities and meeting deadlines, and often ended up making certain team members feeling that their remote members were ‘being irresponsible and lacking commitment’ (Sarker & Sahay, 2004, p. 12).

We believe that the above discussion helps interpret the results of this study. For example, if a project involves Indian and Japanese team members (known to have different communication styles and preferences compared to Indian IT workers who may have been trained in a U.S.-based environment), the Indian GSD workers would need to keep Japanese collaborators satisfied by catering to their very different working and communication styles. While this may not mean ‘resource drainage’ on a daily basis, keeping up with such disparate styles and cultural norms can prove to be taxing, and likely to cause a dent in their valence towards future distributed ISD.

However, when there is similarity between the crossborder locations, these frustrations are likely to be much less, since there will be less difference in working styles and values. For example, it was found that Norwegian organizations often preferred to outsource to ‘Russian software suppliers rather than Asian companies’, because they felt they shared ‘similarity of the so-called European mindset’ (Krishna et al., 2004, p. 64). Similarly, Japanese and Chinese ISD workers prefer to work together, because even though they do not share a language, they have ‘compatible ways of working’ (Krishna et al., 2004, p. 64). Thus, in the context of our study, even if multiple crossborder locations are involved, if there is significant similarity between these personnel in these locations and Indian ISD workers, then the level of frustration would be low. They are more likely to enjoy working with their remote team members, and consequently have higher valence towards future distributed ISD.

We would like to note that the fact that the above distribution-related variables explain approximately 12% of the variance in the two dependent variables is encouraging and significant. The facets of distributed arrangements investigated in this study are specific to the GSD context, and while the literature suggests that the distributed team characteristics may be relevant to the study of human issues, we are not aware of any empirical investigation on this front. Of course, we expect that individual factors such as personality characteristics and organizational factors such as family-friendly policies, long identified by organizational behavior researchers, would explain part of the variance of the dependent variables in the GSD context as well. However, we submit that this study, in identifying and (partially) validating the distribution-related variables, offers something new for the distributed ISD environment.

While the study offers some useful knowledge, like any other study, it does have certain limitations, which we discuss below.

One of the primary limitations of the study arises from the sampling strategy used. Snijders (1992, pp. 59–60) suggests that snowball sampling involves some ‘inherent bias’. He goes on to quote Berg (1988) who has argued that ‘snowball sample will be strongly biased towards inclusions of those who have many interrelationships with, or are coupled to, a large number of other individuals. In the absence of knowledge of individual inclusion probabilities in different waves of the snowball sample, unbiased estimation is not possible’. However, Snijders suggests (1992, p. 68) that this concern can be mitigated by ensuring that one draws ‘the respondents, as much as possible, from independent sources’. Consequently, in this study, we tried to ensure that there was very limited linkage among our initial identified set of respondents (e.g., most were from different organizations, they were working with clients in different parts of the world, they were involved in different projects), which we believe lessens much of the concerns related to snowball sampling. Further, Coleman (1958) argues that snowball sampling is similar to regular samples, the only difference being that while ‘the population in ordinary samples is a population of individuals’, in the case of snowball sampling, ‘it is two populations: one of the individuals and one of relations among individuals’. In summary, while we agree with the positive arguments presented by advocates of snowball sampling, we do believe that in the interest of greater confidence regarding the generalizability of results, future research involving more systematic approach (e.g., random sampling) needs to be conducted. However, as we noted earlier, such data are very difficult to collect or gain access to.

Second, this study provides the perspective of Indian ISD workers only. While India has been a major player in the GSD arena, several other nations are relevant, and need to be studied. Given the substantial cultural differences, especially with respect to working styles, working attitudes, and preferences between the Indians and the people of the other countries where significant amount of GSD is undertaken, studies involving different regions/ countries in the world such as Europe (e.g., Russia, Ireland), and Asia (e.g., China, Vietnam) need to be conducted before the results of this study can be generalized to all GSD workers. There is also a need to study the same phenomenon from an onshore perspective, that is, the work–life imbalances and valence of those ISD professionals in the developed countries (e.g., U.S., Canada, U.K.) from where some (or all) of the ISD work is offshored.

Third, this study focused on the role of key distribution-specific variables only. Prior research on WLB has highlighted several individual-related variables (e.g., gender, neuroticism, family structure) and organizationspecific variables (e.g., the presence of family friendly policies within the organization) that can potentially influence the WLB of employees (Wayne et al., 2004; Lyness & Kropf, 2005). Further, in the context of ISD, the nature of the project (e.g., requirements fluctuation, the type of ISD methodology being used) can also affect the attitudes of the ISD worker (e.g., Nidumolu, 1995; Fowler, 2003). We believe that all of these abovementioned categories of variables are likely to affect the distributed ISD worker as well, but given the complexity of the testing, several categories of variables within one study, we chose to focus only on the nature of the distribution-related variables that we found to be most relevant to the topic. Future research needs to examine the individual, organizational, and project-related antecedents in an effort to gain a comprehensive understanding of the antecedents of WLC and valence of globally distributed ISD workers.

Finally, in this study, we focus on GSD tasks that are similar to ‘Performance’ tasks in the Task Circumplex Model, and the implications of performing such tasks on the WLB of the IS professionals. Even though GSD is one of the most common activities performed in a distributed environment, several other types of organizational processes, including, but not limited to legal processing, assessments of medical reports, etc., are also performed in distributed settings. These processes involve different sets of tasks (pertaining to the other categories within the Task Circumplex Model), requiring varying levels of coordination and communication. Thus, future research needs to be conducted using professionals involved in such tasks/processes to get a more comprehensive understanding of the impacts of distribution-related factors on the WLB of professionals working in a distributed environment in general, not just GSD.

## Conclusions

## Theoretical/research implications

While GSD has increased exponentially over the last few years, and has consequently received much attention from researchers, to our knowledge, little or no attention has been paid to understanding the human resource issues related to this phenomenon. This study addresses this gap in the literature by specifically examining the antecedents of WLC and valence toward future distributed ISD work. To our knowledge, WLC and valence of distributed ISD workers (or their antecedent factors) have not been empirically examined before, and we believe that this is the key contribution of the current study.

WLC has received considerable attention within the management and human resource literature in which different sets of individual-specific, organization-specific factors can be important antecedents of WLC. Instead, by focusing on certain distribution-related variables, the findings of this study become particularly important to the context of cross-border distributed work. Our results indicate that time overlaps between the members, and the nature of communication required (i.e., the frequency and extent of synchronous communication) have significant negative effects on the WLB. The identification of distribution-related variables, we believe, is an important contribution of the paper.

The study also contributes to the existing body of knowledge by proposing and then empirically validating a new construct within the domain of GSD, that is, valence toward future distributed work. While valence has been used previously within the organizational literature, its use within the IS discipline, especially within the GSD context has been limited.

## Practical implications

Hurley & Costa (2001) argues that organizational managers often view human resource issues to be ‘soft’ and ‘difficult to define and manage’, and thus ‘it is not unusual for these issues to be shunted aside’, but that constitutes ‘a big mistake’. One such human resource issue is the WLB of the employees. In fact, Greenblatt (2002) suggests that meeting ‘employee work/life balance concerns is now a strategic imperative for many organizations’ [emphasis added], especially since work–life imbalance has been seen to have significant effects on workers’ health, general psychological well-being, productivity, turnover rates, and long-term engagement with the profession (Felstead et al., 2002). This issue is of even more concern in the context of IT professionals involved in GSD. This study identifies some key factors that reduce the WLC of GSD professionals, such that managers can derive specific tactics to mitigate these issues. Specifically, the results of the study highlight that organizations need to focus considerable attention on their choice of the distributed locations and communication requirements for software development projects.

Further, our study not only helps understand the antecedents of WLC, but also highlights the specific distribution-related factors that affect the valence of the IT professionals toward future distributed software development. If IT workers are positively oriented toward GSD work, even if these IT workers leave their present organization, they are likely to join another organization engaging in this mode of software development. We would like to note that such orientation or valence plays a key role in career decisions. Studies suggest that individuals who develop negative valence toward GSD will end up working for organizations where they did not have to work in a globally distributed environment (Kilian, 2008). Our study is based on the premise that if companies based in developed nations intend to continue to derive productivity gains by engaging in GSD utilizing human resources from countries such as India, China, Vietnam, etc. (Robinson & Kalakota, 2004), they need to ensure that these workers have a high WLB and positive valence toward such environments.

We would like to conclude by stating that while work– life tradeoffs and employees’ emotional reactions and dispositions related to WLC concerns have been receiving some attention from practitioners, surprisingly, there has not been much research on WLC-related issues of GSD workers, who work in very challenging circumstances, given that they bring varied levels of knowledge, and are separated by significant time, distance, and cultural assumptions. Our study aspires to bring this important arena into the IS research community’s focus.
