# Using information systems to achieve complementarity in SME innovation networks

Sven-Volker Rehm<sup>a,</sup>\*, Lakshmi Goel<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> WHU – Otto Beisheim School of Management, Burgplatz 2, D-56179 Vallendar, Germany <sup>b</sup> University of North Florida, 1 UNF Drive, Jacksonville, FL 32224, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 October 2015 Received in revised form 27 September 2016 Accepted 9 October 2016 Available online 12 October 2016

Keywords: Complementarity Synergy SME innovation network Interorganizational system

## A B S T R A C T

Within dynamic innovation networks of small and medium enterprises (SMEs), complementarity between innovation partners is central to extend <sup>fi</sup>rm-level resources and capabilities and create synergies at the network level. Few guidelines exist for how information systems (IS) can be used to achieve <sup>fi</sup>t between innovation partners. We conduct qualitative case studies of three innovation networks and investigate information technology (IT) affordances that undergird complementary activities. We provide evidence for three areas of <sup>fi</sup>t and identify IT-based affordances, which foster synergy creation. These areas provide stimulus for developing IS strategies to networked innovation. We offer a conceptual model of how complementarity in SME innovation networks can be supported with the help of interorganizational systems.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations often try to innovate with the help of interorganizational partnerships. Through multiple partnerships within business ecosystems, <sup>fi</sup>rms combine complementary contributions by forming innovation networks [40,10,13,2,30]. Innovation networks are constellations of partnering <sup>fi</sup>rms that together develop distinct new products, processes, and services within cooperative new product development (NPD) projects. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of leveraging complementarity between partners in such networks has been emphasized in the previous literature [55,63,7]. Resource complementarity [34] and knowledge complementarity [66] are integral to achieving synergies that drive network performance.

Information technology (IT) systems are central to these networks as they act as integrators and facilitators [44,45]. Usually, innovation networks comprise various IT systems, application software, and information repositories collectively forming an IT system landscape as an innovation infrastructure that provides information and communication services to the partners involved in an NPD project. Structured IT support for networks comprises interorganizational systems (IOS), such as electronic data interchange, product lifecycle management (PLM), or supply chain management systems. However, IT-supported networks are usually long-term alliances or collaborations with partners that have resources to invest in particular IOS.

The context of innovation networks for small and medium enterprises (SMEs) is somewhat unique. SMEs need to adapt, improve, and extend their own resource base by developing and linking complementary contributions and resources in an agile manner [11,14]. An SME may be a part of multiple networks at a time, often without formal agreements [54]. Such networks work under the assumption that the participating partners individually (or in shared ownership) create competitive products and services [20,49]. At the end of the NPD project, the network might sustain as a production or service delivery network, building its competitive advantage on the developed complementary processes and resources. Alternatively, and as is more often the case, the network dissolves. Either scenario results in improved capability of the SME for creating resource and knowledge complementarities in networked partnerships, supporting the SME to build new competencies with the subsequent projects. We use the term “SME innovation networks” to denote such ad hoc, temporary, and dynamic partnerships between SMEs.

The idea of reusing (parts of) an innovation infrastructure’s IT system landscape in succeeding NPD projects or in multiple networks is regularly neglected in the context of SME innovation networks. Previous research proposes that unique resource bases can be created in networks that generate sustained competitive advantage [62,6]. These resource bases can potentially create synergies beyond the NPD project, which arise from resource complementarities [21]. Even though the approach of SME networked innovation is a growing trend, there is only little insight into how complementarity, i.e., alignment of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s resource base with the network to derive synergies, can be supported by the right information system (IS). Hence, we know very little on the IT affordances that help support complementarity in the case of SMEs and there is little guidance for how SMEs can effectively leverage IS in order to extend their resource base for engaging in multiple innovation networks.

Given this background, our research question is “How do IS help achieve complementarity in SME innovation networks?” We address this issue by providing insights from qualitative case studies of three SME innovation networks. First, we identify the sources of complementarity that allow the <sup>fi</sup>rms to successfully integrate with each other for innovation. Subsequently, we identify IT-based affordances that support the sources of complementarity. Finally, we provide insights on how IT-based affordances help achieve complementarity through the creation of speci<sup>fi</sup>c synergies in the networks. Our results can guide the development of an IS strategy for innovation networks, because they illustrate key directions for implementation of network-speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT system landscapes that facilitate integration of resources between innovation partners. We suggest related IT functionalities and IT-based activities to achieve complementarities and unique resource constellations. We draw conclusions for IS strategies for networked innovation, and formulate several insights for research and practice.

## 2. Related research

## 2.1. The role of complementarity

As suggested by the relational view, the engagement of SMEs in innovation networks, or as part of larger business ecosystems, can bring about substantial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [17]. Research has argued that alliances are required in particular to gain access to complementary resources, which allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop new and valuable capabilities [21:687,23,24,26,66]. Creating and sustaining complementary relationships allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to leverage each other’s knowledge, technological know-how, resources, and processes that could not be developed by a <sup>fi</sup>rm on its own [28,36].

The notion of complementarity is often traced back to Milgrom and Roberts [37,38]. They consider the relation between activities, which are coordinated in view of a common objective. The bene<sup>fi</sup>t achieved in view of this objective is characterized as supermodular, when the related activities are complementary. This is the case when aligning activities brings about more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than when doing more of each activity separately [37:516]. This idea, which is often referred to as synergy, also implies that when one of several complementary activities is enforced, it will be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the common objective to also increase the related, complementary activities [37:516]. In other words, “doing more of one thing increases the returns to doing more of another” [38:181], (emphasis in original). Supermodularity is an important notion when two or more business partners engage in shared activities, because it provides the motivation to contribute to a shared effort with the expectancy of receiving increased bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and even if at <sup>fi</sup>rst sight direct bene<sup>fi</sup>t is not involved for oneself.

Although the literature is replete with discussions of complementarity and its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the explicit tie-in to activities and resources as precursors to complementarity has not been made. The considerations necessitate that <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>rst become aware of the relatedness of resources in view of a common objective, and second, integrate or coordinate activities in a way that materialize their complementarity. Such activities are not independent from the applied resources, the knowledge brought into the activities, and the processes that coordinate them. All these factors serve as inputs and are eventually adapted to bring about complementarities.

Thus, to the notion of complementarity as prevalent in the literature, we add a perspective on the resources that are being used and adapted through coordinated (and potentially complementary) activities (see Fig. 1).

In this view, complementarities become visible when they achieve materiality through coordinated action. This action in turn requires some material basis, a shared task or resource, which it is adapting or adapted to. Thus, when looking for candidates for achieving complementarities, concrete task assignments or resources need to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and their interrelations explicated. In this context, past research has hinted at the fact that complementary resources are not necessarily identical, but heterogeneous, as well as existing in different corporate contexts [24,25].

## 2.2. The significance of complementarity in SME innovation networks

Partnerships between <sup>fi</sup>rms do not exist in isolation, but within a business ecosystem. Business ecosystems can be conceptualized as the “economic community involving many companies working together to gain comparative advantages as a result of their symbiotic relationships” [27]. They permit <sup>fi</sup>rms to create value beyond what one <sup>fi</sup>rm could achieve on its own, as well as bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for an increased range of business partners [1]. For <sup>fi</sup>rms, gathering knowledge about the ecosystem is a prerequisite to strategically position them within the business ecosystem, which is a precursor to the formation of networks. This includes not only understanding the own core business but also the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of interrelated entities, processes, and knowledge [2,27:155].

Recent research has suggested characterization of ecosystems based on their contextual conditions, involving the aspects of knowledge intensity and environmental velocity [27]. In this view, innovation networks of SMEs are characterized by high knowledge intensity and high velocity, which favor the use of IOS, such as content and knowledge management systems (KMS), and collaborative work systems (CWS); [7,27,45]. Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of promising partner constellations within their business ecosystem enables SMEs to form ad hoc, temporary, innovation networks, which in view of their environmental velocity can be termed “dynamic.”

Within such dynamic innovation networks of SMEs, complementary resources exemplify “intricately tangled” assets that form speci<sup>fi</sup>c interorganizational relationships [56,57]; cited by Mesquita et al. [36:918]. These assets provide a unique competitive advantage because they are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate for outsiders, and are the basis to achieve additional effects through realization of synergies [21]. Such synergies are prerequisites to reach, or even create new markets, or to develop disruptive innovations [14].

The involvement in innovation networks also entails speci<sup>fi</sup>c approaches for joint business development and market strategies [39,14]. Often, the outcomes pursued in an innovation network of SMEs comprise a number of new product and service developments carried forward individually by each network partner – which, however, are linked through the network’s NPD project – together targeting the creation of a bundle of products and services. Such bundles associate and deliver aligned, complementary products, services, and processes, which together allow focusing on markets, a network partner on its own could not reach – while each product or service on its own represents marketable output for the contributing <sup>fi</sup>rm. In our study for instance, SMEs formed innovation networks in order to produce products and services for markets, which were dominated by large <sup>fi</sup>rms, such as automotive markets or markets for medical devices that would have prevented market success if the SMEs had pursued an NPD on their own.

Popular models such as the open innovation model suggest that a range of innovative ideas brought up at the front end of innovation can be leveraged either through inclusion of knowledge and contributions brought into the <sup>fi</sup>rm from outside, through provision or selling of intermediate results to another company, or through joint business developments by licensing out/in, venturing or acquisitions, etc. [8,10]. This “inside-out” view proliferating largely disjoined business partnerships is supplemented in innovation networks by the network of partnerships that is often created around a product or service idea.

In contrast to these models, our research approach adopts an “outside-in” view, inclusive of external partners that may serve as service providers, suppliers of raw materials, business customers, or providers of products and services, which complement a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s output in such a way that an innovative bundle of products and services is created. In other words, forming bundles of products and services requires joining as a network of partners, integrating contributions, and setting up cooperative innovation processes. Innovation networks can thus generate partnership links and conjoint resources, which are hard to imitate or to substitute and can provide a basis for long-term partnerships and strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [35].

## 2.3. Challenges in achieving complementarity

The task of achieving complementarity involves the need to establish “particular symbiotic relationship[s]” [36]. In other words, forming such relationships requires establishing a “<sup>fi</sup>t” between partners, involving the capacity of partners to adapt to each other. This includes factors such as trust, commitment, dependency, and compatibility [53], as well as pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency in managing collaborations [16]. These aspects in<sup>fl</sup>uence the critical coupling of resources, which involves signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts, because it entails that unique constellations of objectives, resources, and activities need to be developed in an innovation network in order to serve as sources of complementarity.

In order to sustain such complementarities and to achieve additional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the potential originating from this critical coupling of resources needs to be transferred to new situations. As has been conceptualized in previous research [21], such persistent pairing of resources, i.e., complementary resources persistently integrated and managed toward new objectives, can bring about synergies.

Past research has indicated that capabilities acquired during particular collaborations are indeed transferable to new situations [16]. However, how such capabilities of critical coupling and persistent pairing of resources are leveraged to create synergies has not been described in the literature so far.

On the basis of the relational view literature, we draw on three key aspects in order to analyze interorganizational relationships: (a) the <sup>fi</sup>t of knowledge, culture, and partnerships; (b) the <sup>fi</sup>t of technologies, physical resources, and value contributions; and (c) the <sup>fi</sup>t of processes, operations, and (concurrent) activities (see Table 1). In our study, we use these aspects as three lenses to observe how dynamic innovation networks create complementarities, as each aspect creates a particular challenge to achieve <sup>fi</sup>t.

Each innovation network needs to arrive at a “<sup>fi</sup>t” in each of these three areas in its particular context to achieve complementarity. How to achieve this <sup>fi</sup>t is yet an open question. Research has discussed the role of IS in interorganizational relationships, but primarily for long-term, stable alliances. The role of IS to <sup>fi</sup>nd and leverage complementarity in ad hoc, dynamic innovation networks of SMEs is relatively unknown.

## 2.4. IS and complementarity: the role of affordances

Recent research has characterized the strategic potential of resource constellations as “activity systems” that foster value creation when enabled by IT [50,45]. IT systems act as integrators and facilitators by representing, reproducing, and enabling the particular resource and activity constellations [44,45]. Various authors have emphasized that IT system deployment needs to be complemented by speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational activities in order to create improved business effect or value [31,46,47,18]. In this way, IT systems have been shown to contribute to competitive advantage through the “con<sup>fi</sup>guration of an activity system, dependent on IT at its core” [50:748]. Among other factors, IT systems can improve interactions in cross-functional as well as geographically dispersed teams [33,9] and can also improve operational capabilities of knowledge workers in complex process settings [64]. Hence, the role of IT systems has been suggested as crucial for activity systems in NPD [48,29].

The concept of affordances – or action possibilities Gibson, 1986 – helps de<sup>fi</sup>ne speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which IT can facilitate complementarity in SMEs. A more recent de<sup>fi</sup>nition of affordances is “the potential for behaviors associated with achieving an immediate concrete outcome and arising from the relations between an object (e.g., an IT artefact) and a goal-oriented actor or actors” [70]. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition suggests that having relevant activity systems offers affordances as a “space of possibilities” [61] for complementarity; however, these have to be appropriated by network partners to achieve complementarity.

In SME innovation networks, usually several different IT systems and application software are used in parallel, often during a limited time frame, to support speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks or phases along the innovation process. These systems and software form heterogeneous IT system landscapes that provide essential information processing and communication services to the involved partners during – and eventually after – an NPD project. Previous studies suggest speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of systems, such as decision support systems (DSS), PLM systems, or virtual simulation software for supporting distinct NPD activities, collaboration software for product design and development [5], project management systems, resource management systems, knowledge management systems, and cooperative work systems [48]; see also the overview in [45:218]. However, most of these tools work well in a structured context, where long-term strategic alliances are supported by IOS, often deployed by large companies with adequate resources to invest in such systems. This usually does not apply to our context of SMEs. Hence, SMEs provide a unique context for IT-based affordances for innovation networks [41].

In contrast to studies that target at suggesting concrete types of IS, our research addresses the question of how IT systems enable complementarities within IT system landscapes of dynamic innovation networks of SMEs. IT system landscapes for dynamic innovation networks need to mirror the emerging innovation process and be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently <sup>fl</sup>exible to accommodate a variety of innovation management techniques and tools [22] as well as related application software required within each phase of the process. During an NPD project, a portfolio of application software might evolve, which can support future phases or projects as well, in particular with regard to the knowledge about their use. For instance, [68] reports strong positive interaction effects, i.e., synergies, between IT infrastructure and e-commerce capability, being more effective in producing business value when complementing each other. He suggests integration of resources as a major factor to achieve in<sup>fl</sup>uence through IT systems.

The IT system landscape established at the end of an innovation process is the primary source for supporting the production phases following the NPD project. This can happen for instance by providing information about production process parameters or material properties. However, not all projects necessarily lead to production or service provision by the involved partners of an innovation network themselves, but the original partners of an innovation network might offer licensing options for achieved results to third parties. In such cases, the data and information generated and captured within IT systems can represent the offered solution itself.

During the innovation process, <sup>fi</sup>rms learn about cooperative use of software, and can take away bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for establishing future cooperation and innovation processes; adoption barriers of software solutions can be lowered. Thus, IT system landscapes can provide a relatively stable, shared resource base on network level, even beyond the particular cooperation in an innovation network within a business ecosystem [27].

## 3. Research setting and approach

## 3.1. Research context: three dynamic innovation networks

Over a 3-year period, we closely observed three innovation networks of SMEs. Each of the networks pursued a radical innovation goal in the sense that for the intended outcomes, substantial materials, associated production processes, machinery, and related services had to be developed anew, and the products and services featured signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly new functionalities [12,51,19]. This necessitated considerable efforts from the partners to align with each other in terms of partnerships, their value contributions, and innovation processes. The three networks were part of a cooperative research project within the framework of the European Commission’s Directorate General for Research and Innovation. Each of them was provided with a shared web-based cooperative work environment (CWE), which featured wiki-based editing and project management functionalities. The CWE was provided to the networks by a software service provider and was supplemented by application software for business process modeling, for communication (telephony, chatting) and by further software of the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms. The CWE enabled con<sup>fi</sup>guration of virtual team workspaces that included work<sup>fl</sup>ow functionality, and provided an option for enduser development (EUD) in order to create “apps” for particular use cases, such as task assignments and approval or speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation management techniques.

## 3.3. Analysis scheme

Our analysis focused on IT systems and their uses with respect to creation of complementarities and synergies, following our research question: how IS undergird complementarity in our research context. Our approach and interpretation were exploratory insofar as we did not operationalize “complementarity” upfront as quanti<sup>fi</sup>able measures, but instead tried to interpret, based on the achieved project outcomes, whether critical couplings of resources and activities had materialized. We proceeded with our analysis as follows:

First, we looked for activities that established a coupling between <sup>fi</sup>rms, as such activities are candidates for complementarities. In other words, we tried to identify related activities of two or more <sup>fi</sup>rms, which mutually resonated with each other. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed such activities based on the three areas of “<sup>fi</sup>t” identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Table 1. The role of IT systems at this stage was visible in the use of shared information repositories, or when the ways of communication were established through particular, traceable IT functionalities, which represented or materialized the relatedness of actions. Thus, the situation had to be symmetric in the sense that at least two <sup>fi</sup>rms had to engage for a common objective during a shared episode, which represented the critical coupling. In our analysis, we considered the efforts of the partners to establish cooperation with each other and the resulting communication processes. These aspects comprise their efforts to gain knowledge about their new partners and to mutually align their activities, as well as eventual challenges and changes partners experienced with regard to their business strategy or the overall project strategy. It is through the cooperative balancing of the partners’ diverse contexts, work arrangements, expectations, or through unexpected incidents that the partners managed to achieve complementarities on several occasions.

Second, we looked for evidence of complementarity in the synergies that resulted [52,59]. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed synergies by analyzing episodic narrations, when unintended bene<sup>fi</sup>ts became obvious as consequences from actions that had originally been directed to other purposes.

We analyzed the involved IT functionalities after identifying relevant episodes, because <sup>fi</sup>rst, the created <sup>fi</sup>t or synergy had to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and only after that could the development through potentially complementary actions be retraced. As suggested by the previous literature, “affordances offer an analytical bridge between observed events and causal structure of mechanisms” [69]. Hence, by looking at relevant IT functionality, we could make sense of certain actions as contributing to complementarity.

We derived our insights about complementarities through a comparative juxtaposition of the three cases. Similar activities in all the three networks led to comparable complementarities, but not all of them led to unexpected additional consequences, which could be framed as synergies for the respective network partners. This setting allowed us (a) to analyze the generation of complementarity relationships within each of the three key aspects of interorganizational relationships (see Table 1), as well as (b) to identify several (potential) sources of synergies. Our overall analysis scheme focuses on the conception and the effects of complementarities (synergies) as suggested by the previous literature (see Table 5).

## 4. Analysis and <sup>fi</sup>ndings

In the following, we present evidence from our analysis of three innovation networks taking three perspectives, concerning the <sup>fi</sup>t of (a) knowledge, culture, and partnerships; (b) technologies, physical resources, and value contributions; and (c) processes, operations, and (concurrent) activities (see Table 1). By looking for evidence of the types of <sup>fi</sup>t, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three episodes – one episode for each key aspect of complementarity, which showcase critical couplings and synergies arising from complementary innovation activities. Although several kinds of activities were pursued similarly in all the three studied networks, we observed the appearance of synergies only with respect to speci<sup>fi</sup>c trigger events that created distinct circumstances in a particular network. The episodes describe various IT-based systems and application software used or created, thus providing evidence of the IT affordances that enabled complementary activities and critical resource couplings.

## 4.1. Identifying opportunities to build innovation networks

In the early phases of the NPD projects, partners in all the three networks used business process modeling tools to model their own value chain and activities as knowledge maps. These models could be linked to the partners’ activities in scope of planning the overall innovation process. Fig. 2 gives an example of such a map displaying the sample development process of the helmet’s inner shell. In order to collect information about the network and the task ahead – which was termed “ecosystem knowledge” in the project – different types of maps were created covering NPD processes (sequential steps and milestones in product/service development), resources required or available in the network, or informational aspects (repositories, important documents, data bases, etc.).

In this way, the partners could learn about each other and could easily position themselves in the network with regard to the <sup>fi</sup>t of activities. They were able to explicate important aspects and domains of their expertise, which was supplemented by further descriptions of their planned principle value contributions within the project.

“ . . . why do we have to use (this technology)? Thanks to advances in nanotechnologies . . . the future at this moment are polymers. We write down the logic of these approaches here inside a knowledge map. I share it with my network, a mapping procedure to build ideas, collecting and linking. (It helps) designing future projects, scheduling activities and so on . . . ” (Product Manager, Helmet Producer/MHN)

The activity to create knowledge maps helped both the partners who created the maps, because they became more self-aware of their own capabilities and the network partners, because they had an easily accessible repository of information about the partners’ capabilities.

“What is firstly changed in terms of weight is basically awareness about real skills, real capabilities, real knowledge of our own structure, split into all key aspects of the company life, that means real knowledge, real competences, and which degree of each parameter, how. And this helped the company to well–structure the internal process, in terms of methods, in terms of information flows, management, in terms of awareness, and as well the relationships with potential external partners being advisors or suppliers or partners. So, first of all, the ecosystem knowledge is the most important, is the basement, . . . on which we base the further evolutions thanks to the tools, thanks to all the analysis methods, and so on. So, first, the ecosystem knowledge. The model knowledge for us is the first and the most important tool.” (Product Manager, Helmet Producer/MHN)

The maps were accessible through the CWE and allowed for displaying and transmitting image and capabilities of each partner, and were used regularly during and after the project. They also motivated the change of internal processes in order to align to partners, thus lowering efforts for coordinating activities.

“When you analyze your ecosystem as an SME for the first time, you really analyze and check every point of your company with a different point of view . . . ‘Check this in that way – who does what and why?’ It is not always well specified inside the company and clear. Everybody tries to do everything that is useful. And every time this method changes. Having a clear ecosystem you can systematically apply your best . . . – the real capability of your company – on that specific task.” (Product Manager, Helmet Producer/MHN)

In the motorbike helmet network, the partners vividly used modeling as a tool to represent themselves as part of the network. The project idea involved the use of a new dampening material in the design of a motorbike safety helmet. All network partners provided their parts of the evolving network model as complementary activities. With the progressing project, the availability of the knowledge maps in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the ways NPD team members approached their tasks. One team member expresses how he became aware of a learning curve:

“And now I understand the possibility to extend the models, the system to exchange information, to a network. For me it is a step ahead, because I have to change my approach to exchange knowledge. Because I have to speak with different people and (now) (I need) to organize all the information in a different way that I organize (it) in my mind . . . ” (Owner, Material Supplier/ MHN)

During the progressing project, a kind of “symbiotic” relationship emerged, which allowed to extend the project-related partnership into discussions about future activities – enlarging the project scope. Now, as SMEs, knowing that an ecosystem exists, allows us to systematically apply it on our internal process and our relationships with the others.” (Product Manager, Helmet Producer/MHN)

“Because if you are speaking about a new product, you have to call a specific profile that you already identified during the ecosystem analysis of your company.” (Product Manager, Helmet Producer/ MHN)

The light-bulb moment of this episode with respect to synergy was reached through the reciprocal combination of the map-based representations of the partnering <sup>fi</sup>rms. In this case, complementary activities of modeling create knowledge maps as new resources, which are available to be used in the future. In the motorbike helmet network, they trigger the uptake of new activities, projects, and partnerships. While at <sup>fi</sup>rst, the network had targeted only the development of a motorbike helmet, now they understood their base materials and engineering skills could also be applied to rotors of wind turbines; and subsequently the network reoriented toward a respective ecosystem.

In summary, the use of IT-based, shared knowledge maps allowed <sup>fi</sup>rms to learn about themselves and each other, and build the motorbike helmet network around shared objectives, creating a “<sup>fi</sup>t” among the knowledge brought into the network, their partnerships, and the created cooperation culture. The maprelated IT affordance enabled identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of symbiotic complementary activities and critical resource couplings during the network’s progress. With continued use of and re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the maps, this affordance persisted and led into new application <sup>fi</sup>elds for their cooperation, resulting in the synergy of the discovery of new ways in which the dampening material of the helmet could be used.

## 4.2. Developing new strategic capabilities through mutual adaptation

The introduction of the CWE as central work environment signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stimulated the partners’ interactions in all the three networks. The participating team members were generally eager to learn about the software, and eventually approached the software provider to enhance several functionalities and applications con<sup>fi</sup>gured on top of the CWE. The NPD teams of all the three networks after some time managed to establish an agile working mode enabling the team to react quickly on situational demands.

In the medical device network, the partners had to discuss complicated technical issues, because as part of the product idea materials were to be used that had not been applied in this context so far: the targeted stent-graft should use an innovative hybrid material for the graft, composed of the nickel–titanium composite Nitinol, and covered with polyester. Firms and research organizations from various <sup>fi</sup>elds were involved into the network, in order to bring in speci<sup>fi</sup>c expertise to the NPD, such as areas of medical devices, textile processing and machinery, and a university’s institute of physics. The innovation network proved accordingly heterogeneous:

“We more or less understand each other when we meet. We meet, but, after the meeting we go back and everyone more or less continues into his own direction.” (Principal Researcher, Research Service Provider/MDN)

Consequently, the network partners had to take signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts to integrate their knowledge and approaches.

“We need to teach them our (technical) language, because we just did not understand each other, because each of us spoke another language. (( . . . )) And we had to put pressure on them to become interested in our problems . . . They do research on their own issues, not on those we want or need to address.” (R&D Manager 1, Medical Device Producer/MDN; translated)

One particular boundary condition for the success of this NPD project was provided by the legal quality requirements for medical device products, their development, and production. Each party involved in such processes needed to ascertain speci<sup>fi</sup>c standards for processes and documentations. This however constituted a new <sup>fi</sup>eld for some partners, which consequently had to adapt.

“We told the Machinery Company’s CEO, ‘You need to control your internal structures to become compatible as supplier for medical devices’. Now we look into their processes . . . we need retraceability.” (Process Consultant; Service Provider B/MDN; translated)

The role of “moderators” emerged who learned how to actively advance and promote important issues throughout the network. The moderators usually already had some broadened expertise across several expert domains, industrial <sup>fi</sup>elds, and applications. They managed for reaching a <sup>fi</sup>t of the partners’ activities with respect to achieving compatible structures.

“It is not just about merging documentation structures. The Design History File is a collection of files with references to other IT systems and materials. It is rather creative tinkering. All that needs to be transformed into a (compliant) structure ready for production if we are talking about admission procedures for medical devices.” (Process Consultant; Service Provider B/MDN; translated)

The moderators at the same time took a wider view on the strategic importance of their work:

“They want to become an automotive supplier, too. Thus we need to leverage structural synergies. This is the issue. ( . . . ) They realized it at the right time, like, they now make use of the CWE to get their Design History File right.” (Process Consultant; Service Provider B/ MDN; translated)

Through the moderators’ efforts, an operative alignment was reached when the network partners moved their activities into a common direction. This was facilitated particularly through introduction of techniques and tools, which systematically supported integration of activities in view of legal regulations. The network partners implemented failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) to this end, in order to reach risk management compliance, and cooperatively con<sup>fi</sup>gured respective application software on the basis of the CWE. This enabled the systematic documentation and supervision of developments.

“I am merging their contributions to build a combined product. Then I have to name these intermediate products. I need to invent descriptive structures. There are no data structures to describe them.” (Process Consultant; Service Provider B/MDN; translated)

Network partners who formerly had not documented their R&D efforts in a systematic way were now able to perform tasks that complied with the high standards of medical device production.

“All the steps defined by the FDA (United States Food and Drug Administration agency) have already been included (into the system). . . . We couldn’t do that before, but this year we had the audit . . . and if we want to cooperate (further), we have to incorporate all these (steps) into our design control. And we did it.” (R&D Manager 2, Medical Device Producer/MDN)

On the basis of their experience in the medical device network, several network partners became more <sup>fl</sup>exible in shifting between emergent knowledge-sharing routines and more rigid operational processes, e.g., governed through FMEA. This newly developed capability for mutual adaptation remained as a strategic asset beyond the project, making them eligible also as suppliers for industry sectors with high demands on process and quality standards such as automotive and health care.

In summary, the use of the CWE to coordinate contributions and to solve technical issues by con<sup>fi</sup>guring innovation management techniques as work<sup>fl</sup>ows in the CWE allowed <sup>fi</sup>rms to adapt processes within the medical device network. This IT affordance of process coordination and adaptation enabled achievement of complementary activities and critical resource couplings. Because this affordance could be maintained through “app” templates on the CWE and new skills to apply them, a synergy resulted as the strategic capability of adapting to partner <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 4.3. Creating resilience through cross-fertilization

In order to keep up with the technical developments in their projects, the partners in all the three networks created a central workspace on the basis of the CWE. This workspace contained project overviews and plans, as well as relevant technical descriptions, articles, and other project management information. Over time, this helped the team members to develop shared modes of working.

“Yes, we created some pages about different subjects . . . Now with the CWE, it is better to inform other people. When we change a page, we send a link. I think that with the CWE it's really better to work and it's really easy.” (Textile Researcher, Service Provider A/ TCN)

“When we have a meeting, we create the meeting in the CWE and we put all information discussed in the meeting in there . . . So it's easy to . . . if we have to find some information about different meetings or other things, we put everything in the CWE and it's really nice to work with this tool because I think it's really easy to find the information and to exchange it with all partners.” (R&D Engineer, Interior Textile Producer/TCN)

In addition, in the textile coating network, the network partners had set up a shared repository with technical articles about topics related to their developments. This allowed them to exchange knowledge about recent market trends and to develop a kind of market intelligence. The network had originally targeted a novel sol-gel <sup>fi</sup>nishing technique to be used with a large customer in the furniture industry. At one point in the project, this key customer dropped interest in the project. After the original key customer of the textile coating network backed away, the network partners decided to proceed in two directions. One was to look for a new customer and another was to use the established IT system landscape, processes, and routines to continue their cooperation. Originally, the key customer had been the sole contact of the textile producer; however, through the advanced relationship, backed up by the project experience, the partners decided to join their efforts in looking for new options.

“(The Coating Producer) immediately started looking for further potential customers for the final product. (The Interior Textiles Producer) agreed that (the Coating Producer) could contact other partners to promote the jointly developed technology.” (Process Consultant; Service Provider A/TCN; translated from report)

A potential new customer was found after a few months.

“We can totally use all information that we have now. . . . Yes, we will use it and already we started to work with another customer to develop some tests to see where we can go . . . ” (Textile Engineer, Coating Producer/TCN)

The decision to also pursue the second direction, i.e., continue their cooperation to look for new product ideas, stemmed from how far, along with the innovation process, the network had come: the previously created integrated information base enabled the partners to mutually share market intelligence. The partners also became well aware of the technical expertise domains of the partners. This enabled them to principally act as representatives of the network as a whole. In consequence to the loss of the key customer, this capability for cross-fertilization enabled them to continue the project, which had been put in jeopardy through unfortunate circumstances. They targeted the development of an “easy-clean” coating that would prevent fabrics from staining and which promised multiple application scenarios, e.g., in home textile use.

“The CWE was used to formulate the new boundary conditions for the easy-clean project, the respective meetings and first tasks were documented. They also used the CWE to carry out the (innovation management techniques).” (Process Consultant; Service Provider A/TCN; translated from written report)

The partners transported their developed workspace structures into a new project framework, using them as blueprints, which eased coordination in this critical situation.

“I see it as a logical evolution . . . If you can prepare a couple of templates, it makes it easier to use this type of system. . . . The way it works with the wiki is very convenient. . . . you collect all the little pieces of information there and it is already shared . . . .” (Textile Researcher, Research Service Provider/TCN)

In this situation, the partners recognized that their use of the CWE had created a new resource, which could be mutually shared and carried forward into new directions. Their previous efforts to create a content base for their cooperation as a side effect also had generated viable structures (templates) that were now available after the initial cooperation attempt, together with aligned working modes leading toward a shared “architecture” for the network.

In summary, the use of the CWE to document and share relevant technical contents and to develop shared modes of working allowed the <sup>fi</sup>rms to build resilience to a change in external conditions; in this case, the loss of the key customer of the textile coating network. This IT affordance enabled consolidation and repeatable alignment of complementary activities, and creation of a repository that made critical resource couplings retrievable. Transferring these affordances into a new context, i.e., setting itself a new NPD objective, resulted in the synergy of resilience to the market.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. A process view of complementarity enabled by IT-based affordances

In our exploratory study of three SME innovation networks, we have observed how partners within cooperative NPD projects engage in complementary activities in order to reach common objectives. We have structured our analysis perspective along three key aspects of interorganizational relationships. Within each of these aspects, we have detected instances of critical resource couplings, which became signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in consequence to outstanding trigger events that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the NPD project. Following such trigger events, synergies appeared, which could be traced back to the critical resource couplings – or complementarities – obtained in the history of the innovation network. The meaning of trigger events is that they bring about “extreme” cases, situations, or conditions in the sense that they disrupt the ordinary or expected <sup>fl</sup>ow of activities in the network. In our study, this was happening for instance through discovery of new uses for a material (motorbike helmet network), through compliance requirements, which were new to some network partners (medical device network), or the exit of a network partner (textile coating network). Table 6 provides a summary of the overview of the evidence obtained from the innovation networks of our study. On the basis of this evidence, we can draw several conclusions on the role of IT-enabled activities and resources for creating potentially critical resource couplings, and on the related appearance of synergies in innovation networks.

In the context of the need to create a <sup>fi</sup>t of partnerships, network partners engaged into modeling knowledge maps that indicate their overall capabilities and expertise. The maps’ origina intention was to mutually learn about the cooperating partners and to improve alignment between the partners within the scope of the project. These maps, and the related ability to use business process modeling software, provide a distinctive potential. In our study, the maps were made available to the network partners in the CWE, and their use was principally uncoupled from the primary goal. Eventually, when the partners were reaching out to new application opportunities for the material they applied, the partners used the information from the maps, their new ability to model an ecosystem, and the CWE to identify new options and enlarge the project scope. In our study, we observed, for example, contents, process structures (apps), or functionalities of the CWE reused in different contexts or by different users. It is in such situations that we recognized apparent synergies.

## 5.2. From synergies to organizational affordances

Based on our interpretation of the evidence collected in the three SME innovation networks of our study, we can attempt an explanation of how complementary activities can create synergies. We want to demonstrate this explanation by means of one example from our study.

If we generalize the synergy “Identifying opportunities” that was apparent in the motorbike helmet network, it denotes that the establishment of appropriate representations of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to the network, in combination with an adequate representation of the network and its partners, can lead to increased bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the network’s business case. In our study, this happened because through its representation in knowledge maps, relevant knowledge is made available that allowed for identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of novel business opportunities, for different or improved planning of cooperative activities, and for other purposes. In other words, activities related to representation such as reconsidering own competences and capabilities, learning about external expertise, cooperative planning, or preparing to share and integrate external knowledge – as common operational tasks in networked NPD – can substantialize synergies when they are combined with a partner’s corresponding (complementary) activities. IT resources, e.g., application software for business process modeling, support the capturing of respective knowledge and act as services to the representational activities (see also [46]).

In our study, we found the principle underlying this example as a recurring pattern to synergy creation: through the coordinated effort, which is required to accomplish complementary activities, new capabilities and resources are created. These capabilities and resources – which in our case site particularly relied on IT-enabled activities and IT resources – are persistent with respect to their constant and repetitive availability and accessibility of contents, functionalities, and related capabilities to use them. The underlying principle is given by the effect that executing complementary activities and forming complementary resources implies generating or using IT-enabled resources and activities; the latter bring about respective capabilities. Together, those elements substantiate affordances that are available to the involved actors and parties (and also presumably third parties with respective capabilities).

This is in line with the previous research that de<sup>fi</sup>nes organizational affordances as the combination of technological features with organizational arrangements and practices that support their use [67]. Such organizational affordances have been found to result in new forms of organizing. Particular affordances have also been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the context of innovation ecosystems [60]. Similarly, we can characterize organizational affordances with respect to the synergies we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our study (see Table 7). Our characterization involves a projection of IS functionalities and uses, which are common to our study’s context of cooperative NPD.

From Table 7, additional types of synergies can be projected when looking at the characterization of affordances. For the theme of resilience for instance, it can be assumed that professional social networking sites might hold promising options to support partner searches. Although we have seen the use of such sites in the three networks, it had not led to appearance of further synergies during the time of our study.

These considerations allow formulating a more general model of synergy creation, which explicates the role of IT-enabled activities and resources. In this narrow view on IT-enabled synergy creation, IT-enabled activities and resources play a distinctive role because they substantiate the persistent linkage between complementary activities and resources to generate an action potential or affordance. If this affordance is leveraged within new contexts, i.e., for new objectives, the creation of (supermodular) bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be conceived, which are higher or of a different quality than those intended before the coordinated (complementary) activities. In other words, the newly generated affordance becomes the source of synergy.

We want to propose the relation between IT-based activities and resources and the set of complementary activities and resources to the extant conception of supermodularity: their relation resembles the idea of “constructive interference” that leads to the action potential or affordance as the source of synergy.

From the evidence collected in our study and on the basis of this explanatory model, we can infer that the intentional creation of synergies can be attempted. A key role in this respect will be played by the affordances that are brought about (or are conceivable) in relation to the implemented IT resources when accomplishing complementary activities. Further affordances might contribute to synergy creation, which are carried forward by other (IT-enabled) activities and resources. These analyses lead us to conceptualize the role of IS in the context of synergy creation on a broader scale.

## 5.3. The dual role of IS in networked innovation

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that IS ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a dual role as elements of an infrastructure for innovation. This duality involves <sup>fi</sup>rst, their role as information processing resource and second, as relationshipembedding mediator. The latter can be derived from our explanatory model of synergy creation. Awareness of this distinct role might inform management with regard to the question, which IT systems of a system landscape could prove essential with regard to synergy creation: on the one hand, IT systems can be considered in their primary role as enablers of technical expert tasks and as integrators of information, in view of their information processing and exchange capacity, etc. On the other hand, this – traditional – role is complemented by a second one, which emphasizes IS’ capacity to represent and reproduce relatedness between activities. It is through this relational or mediating capacity that IS embed, explicate, and operationalize relationships between actions, which are required to install complementarities and to leverage synergies. Our model offers a way to explicate this relatedness, and in turn to identify resources and activities that support complementarity and synergy creation.

## 5.4. Implications for practice: setting up an IS strategy for networked innovation

One particularly interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding of our study is the observation that not so much of the physical resource, e.g., a certain type of IS, provides the key to complementarity and eventually synergy creation, but that the intricate ways involved in how actors learn to use available tools, as well as the mutual relationships established between <sup>fi</sup>rms, are of paramount importance. In other words, challenges arising for establishing complementarity are not met by establishing “must-have” resources, but through step-by-step, mutual, or re<sup>fl</sup>ective development and evolution of competences and relationships. In our study, we could observe this relevance of a transformational process along all the three lines of our analysis, or areas of “<sup>fi</sup>t,” with respect to the <sup>fi</sup>t of knowledge, culture and partnerships, of technologies, physical resources and value contributions, and of processes, operations, and (concurrent) activities. This observation is also plausible considering the fact that effective use of IT resources involves acquiring respective skill sets.

The latter consideration provides insights into management practice. Managing a network’s IT system landscape from a strategy perspective includes two perspectives: <sup>fi</sup>rst, to create a persistent innovation infrastructure that can be reused in upcoming projects, and maybe together with other partners; and second, targeting at maintaining relevant IT resources for the own enterprise, and nurturing relevant skill sets for using them. Both perspectives imply safeguarding complementary relationships between partners. This goal can be achieved through ensuring that mutual arrangements within the three areas of <sup>fi</sup>t are adequately translated into the network-wide IT system implementation, usage, and support – reproducing and representing respective individually de<sup>fi</sup>ned relationships.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the mere use of certain types of IT systems, as has often been suggested in the IS literature, is not an effectual prerequisite to reach complementarity. As has been implied by recent IT business value research on complementarity, investing into several complements is required [18]. Moreover, it can be helpful to consider the areas of <sup>fi</sup>t to establish complementarities, such as the ones we have analyzed in our study. Equally, we assert that further areas might exist that foster complementarities.

The synergies identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our study and the related affordances can provide stimulus for practicing managers. For instance, regarding synergy (1) identifying opportunities, questions such as “How do I represent my <sup>fi</sup>rm to the network? How can the partners access this knowledge? How do they use it? What do I know about my partners? What about other potential partners?” could become starting points for discussing issues of mutual interest and for developing appropriate skill sets, as we have seen it happen in our study.

## 5.5. Implications for research

We offer an explanatory model on synergy creation to the IS literature on complementarity. We consider the role of IT-based activities, competences, and resources in creating complementarities and synergies, and explicate the effect of constructive interference that leads to generation of affordances. It is these affordances that become sources of synergies.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings accentuate the dual role of IS for innovation purposes, including the notion of IS acting as relationshipembedding mediator. This role is of strategic importance for management practice in networked innovation.

In view of this strategic relevance, future research might look closer at the process of how affordances are generated and leveraged considering the intricate relations between complementary resources and activities, their IT-enabled counterparts, and the requisite skill sets. Sociomateriality approaches might be a promising avenue in this direction [32].

IT business value research could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from including considerations on the processes behind materializing complementarities and synergies. These might extend the “classical” set of factors and measures considered with respect to governing IT system landscapes, in particular for the innovation network context. Another avenue for future research is the development of a framework of IT-based skills or competencies and the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of speci<sup>fi</sup>c competencies for complementarity.

Our analysis framework for this study has been derived from the relational view literature. Although in addition to the three perspectives used, further areas might be relevant to the creation of complementarities and synergies; it is also between these areas that such effects could appear. Future research might thus look in detail toward quantifying distinct complementarity or synergy potentialities.

## 6. Conclusion

Networked innovation is a complex undertaking, yet accounts for a good part of innovation at large. For SMEs, the implementation of IT-enabled activity systems together with network partners creates high obstacles, and sustaining the created IT system landscape to institutionalize the network or business ecosystem is often not accomplished. At the same time, these are prerequisites to effectively leverage network connections for innovation and to extend the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s resource base. Our research provides insights into how SMEs can approach an IS strategy to achieve the requisite <sup>fi</sup>t of resources in innovation networks. Further efforts are needed to transfer our <sup>fi</sup>ndings into management guidelines and business models for establishing viable interorganizational IS infrastructures allowing to tap unseized opportunities in networked innovation.
