# Competitive Environment and the Relationship Between IT and Vertical Integration

Gautam Ray

Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455, rayxx153@umn.edu

Dazhong Wu

School of Business and Economics, Indiana University-South Bend, South Bend, Indiana 46634, dazhwu@iusb.edu

Prabhudev Konana

The McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712, pkonana@mail.utexas.edu

he information systems (IS) literature suggests that by lowering coordination costs, information technol ogy (IT) will lead to an overall shift towards more use of markets. Empirical work in this area provides evidence that IT is associated with a decrease in vertical integration (VI). Economy-wide data, however, sug gests that over the last 25 years the average level of VI has, in fact, increased. This paper studies this empirical anomaly by explicating the moderating impact of two measures of competitive environment, demand uncer tainty, and industry concentration, on the relationship between IT and VI. We examine firms included in 1995 to 1997 InformationWeek 500 and the COMPUSTAT database. Consistent with the IS literature, the analysis suggests that IT is associated with a decrease in VI when demand uncertainty is high or industry concentration is low. However, contrary to the IS literature, IT is found to be associated with an increase in VI when industry con centration is high or demand uncertainty is low. Furthermore, as demand uncertainty increases, less vertically integrated firms invest more in IT, while as industry concentration increases, more vertically integrated firms invest more in IT. The analysis also suggests that firms’ choice of the level of VI and IT investment, under different levels of demand uncertainty and industry concentration, are rational. When demand uncertainty is high or industry concentration is low, increase in VI may increase coordination and production costs. Thus, less VI is rational. However, when industry concentration is high or demand uncertainty is low, increase in VI may decrease coordination and production costs. Thus, firms choose more VI in such industries. The implications for research and practice are discussed.

Key words: information technology; vertical integration; coordination costs; production costs; demand uncertainty; industry concentration

## 1. Introduction

When compared to firms, markets generally have lower production cost because of economies of scale and specialization (Coase 1937, Williamson 1975). However, the coordination cost associated with a market exchange is usually higher than the coordination cost inside a firm. As information technology (IT) can reduce coordination costs, information systems (IS) researchers have argued that IT will lead to an overall shift towards more use of markets. This is often referred to as the electronic markets hypothesis (Malone et al. 1987, Gurbaxani and Whang

1991, Clemons and Row 1992, Clemons et al. 1993, Brynjolfsson et al. 1995). Thus, increasing use of IT in the economy may suggest decreasing levels of vertical integration (VI). Hitt (1999) provides empirical evidence that IT is associated with a decrease in VI. Similarly, Dewan et al. (1998) and Hitt (1999) find that less vertically integrated firms have higher demand for IT capital as these firms have a higher need for external coordination.

Despite the above theoretical arguments and empirical findings, the average level of VI in the U.S. economy appears to have increased in the last 25 years.

If we examine all the firms in the COMPUSTAT database, the average level of VI has not decreased, but has increased in the last 20 to 30 years. As shown in the figure, the pattern of the increase in VI has persisted. One plausible explanation for this phenomenon is the increase in vertical merger activity (McMillion 1999, Fan and Goyal 2006). Firms may also be integrating into downstream operations as value-add is shifting from manufacturing to service (Osegowitsch and Madhok 2003, Konana and Ray 2007).<sup>1</sup>

Given the evidence in the IS literature that IT investments are associated with a decrease in VI, the trend in VI is surprising. It is plausible that under specific circumstances IT is used more to reduce internal coordination costs than to reduce external coordination costs. Consider the following two scenarios. In the computers and electronics manufacturing industry— where firms face significant demand uncertainty— firms like Cisco, Ericsson, Phillips Electronics, etc., outsource manufacturing to contract manufacturers like Solectron and use IT to reduce external coordination costs (Hoyt and Lee 2001). Generally, the level of VI in this industry is quite low. In contrast, in a concentrated industry like petroleum and coal products manufacturing, firms such as BP, Exxon Mobil, Chevron, and Shell, organize production and distribution in vertically integrated organizations (Reinhardt et al. 2003). These firms operate tightly coordinated upstream (exploration, drilling, pumping, refining) and downstream activities (distribution from refineries, to terminals, to roadside gas stations), and use IT to reduce internal coordination costs (Worthen 2002). Thus, under different competitive environments, IT may be used differently (Choudhury 1997).

The management literature has examined the role of competitive environment on VI (e.g., Harrigan 1986, Balakrishnan and Wernerfelt 1986). This literature suggests that the competitive environment influences firms’ choice of their VI level. It is likely that IT has a different relationship with VI under different competitive environments, an issue not yet examined in the IS literature. In this paper, we examine (a) the impact of two measures of competitive environment— demand uncertainty and industry concentration—on the relationship between IT and VI, and (b) how the level of VI in different competitive environments impacts firms’ coordination and production costs.

We analyze firms included in 1995–1997 InformationWeek 500 and COMPUSTAT. The analysis suggests that the competitive environment moderates the relationship between IT and VI. The results show that when demand uncertainty is high or industry concentration is low, IT is associated with a decrease in VI. However, when industry concentration is high or demand uncertainty is low, IT is associated with an increase in VI. Also, as demand uncertainty increases, less vertically integrated firms invest more in IT, whereas as industry concentration increases, more vertically integrated firms invest more in IT. Furthermore, the analysis suggests that the endogenous choice of VI and IT under different levels of demand uncertainty and industry concentration are rational. Firms choose less VI when demand uncertainty is high or industry concentration is low because VI in such environments may increase coordination and production costs. However, firms choose to be more vertically integrated when industry concentration is high or demand uncertainty is low because VI in such environments may decrease coordination and production costs.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the theory and the hypotheses. Section 3 discusses the data and the variables, and §4 presents the empirical analysis. Section 5 concludes with a discussion of the results, and presents directions for future research.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

There is a rich literature on how the competitive environment influences the level of VI (e.g., Harrigan 1985, Balakrishnan and Wernerfelt 1986, Harrigan 1986, Tushman and Anderson 1986, Miller 1987, Walker and Weber 1987, Chatterjee et al. 1992, D’Aveni and Ilinitch 1992, Sutcliffe and Zaheer 1998). In this literature, a firm’s competitive environment is often characterized by its demand uncertainty and industry concentration (Dess and Beard 1984, Keats and Hitt 1988, Dess et al. 1990). Demand uncertainty refers to the uncertainty about, and unpredictability of, customer tastes and preferences, and production and service technologies (Miller and Frisen 1983, John and Weitz 1988, Bergh 1998). Industry concentration,<sup>2</sup> on the other hand, influences the nature of the competitive interaction in an industry (Tushman and Anderson 1986, D’Aveni 1994, Thomas 1996, Harrigan 1986). For example, Porter (1980, p. 18) argues that in a concentrated industry its “leader or leaders can impose discipline and play a coordinative role.” Therefore, a high concentration ratio may imply a stable competitive environment as a small number of firms dominate the industry (Porter 1980, Harrigan 1986). Below we first examine how the competitive environment (i.e., demand uncertainty and industry concentration) influences the relationship between IT and VI. We then examine the performance (i.e., the coordination and production cost) implications of the level of VI in different competitive environments. The performance analysis allows us to examine whether the choice of VI and IT in different competitive environments is rational.

## 2.1. Competitive Environment and the Choice of VI Level

2.1.1. Demand Uncertainty. Demand uncertainty is salient in industries where product lifecycles are short and technologies change rapidly (Fisher 1997). When demand is very uncertain, no one firm is likely to have all the assets needed to bring products to market in a timely fashion (Robertson and Gatignon 1998). In such environments, firms need to maintain flexibility as they require a changing set of assets to compete (Tushman and Anderson 1986, Thomas 1996). Furthermore, the likelihood of insufficient capacity or excess specialized capacity without alternative use increases (Williamson 1975, 1991). Thus, VI under conditions of demand uncertainty subjects firms to significant risks. For example, D’Aveni and Ilinitch (1992) find that in uncertain demand environments vertically integrated firms face higher risk as they are less adaptive. These firms have many vertical links between their different units, leading to higher interdependence among them. Such interdependence may make it harder to adapt to demand fluctuations because of the difficulties in changing strategies, abandoning obsolete technologies, or filling unevenly balanced upstream and downstream capacity. Thus, when demand uncertainty is high, firms may be better off reducing their VI, and instead coordinate more activities with external partners with the necessary assets (Baldwin and Clark 1997). Afuah (2001), for instance, found that when technology changed from CISC to RISC, workstation manufacturers that were vertically integrated into the old (CISC) technology performed worse than the incumbents who were not vertically integrated in that manner.<sup>3-</sup> <sup>4</sup>

As firms rely more on suppliers who have the necessary assets, the need for coordination with external entities increases (e.g., Choudhury 1997). IT can lower such coordination costs. Lee (2002) discusses how firms in computer and electronics industries, where technologies change rapidly and demand is very unpredictable, use electronic exchanges to collaborate with suppliers and customers. These electronic exchanges allow firms to share product content information that support product changes and reduce the cycle time for new product introductions. In this industry, contract manufacturers like Solectron use Web-enabled extranet for information sharing with customers (Hoyt and Lee 2001). Thus, the so-called “fabless” firms make reduced commitments in manufacturing capacity and instead use IT to coordinate production with contract manufacturers like Solectron. This allows the original equipment manufacturers to serve fluctuating demand by offering more innovative designs and shorter delivery times than more integrated firms can offer. Hence, when demand uncertainty is high, firms prefer a lower level of VI and instead use IT to coordinate more activities with external partners. IT can also be used to reduce the cost of coordinating with external suppliers in other ways. Many firms are managing their demand uncertainty by relying on collaborative planning, forecasting, and replenishment tools (Lee 2002). These systems reduce the bullwhip effect by sharing demand information across the supply chain and have led to lower inventory levels as well as reduction in stock outs (Lee et al. 1997). Similarly, firms such as Dell have become successful by implementing IS that allow them to build products on order by sharing demand information across the supply chain. Thus, when demand uncertainty is high, firms may use IT to coordinate activities in less vertically integrated structures.

literature on this issue is in Mahoney (1992). The argument is that specialized assets and uncertainty make vertical contracting difficult and are associated with an increase in VI. Therefore, for a given level of asset specificity, when uncertainty leads to an increase in external coordination costs, firms prefer to perform more activities within the firm. However, when customers’ preferences and production and service technologies change rapidly (sometimes referred to as technological uncertainty), firms require access to different specialized assets. In such environments, increase in uncertainty may lead to lower levels of VI (Mahoney 1992). Sutcliffe and Zaheer (1998) also present similar arguments.

In contrast, when demand uncertainty is low, firms may prefer a higher level of VI. As demand is predictable, firms can invest in specialized assets and bring more activities inside the boundaries of the firm (Harrigan 1985). Higher levels of VI in such environments allow firms to improve coordination inside the firm, achieve scale economies, and capture more of the value-add and margins (Harrigan 1985). As demand is predictable, firms can invest in specialized assets (Williamson 1975, 1991) to produce more of the inputs in-house as the risk of excess capacity is reduced. Such VI may also allow the firm to reach efficient scale. Also, by performing more activities internally, the firm keeps a higher proportion of the value add and margin inside the firm. In this context, IT may enable superior internal coordination through information sharing inside the firm. IT may enable production and distribution schedules to be optimized by improving the visibility and transparency of demand, inventory, and capacity information across an integrated value chain (Lee 2002). In this regard, firms with IT capabilities to share information to coordinate the value chain have observed superior performance (Barua et al. 2004). Thus, when demand uncertainty is low, firms may use IT to coordinate activities in more vertically integrated organizations. The above arguments lead to the following hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> When demand uncertainty is high, an increase in IT will be associated with a decrease in VI.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> When demand uncertainty is low, an increase in IT will be associated with an increase in VI.

2.1.2. Industry Concentration. Industry concentration is a key measure of competitive stability. An industry is competitively stable when it has high entry but low exit barriers and competitors are not inclined to use aggressive price-cutting to increase market share (Porter 1980, Harrigan 1986, D’Aveni 1994). Stable industries (e.g., petroleum and coal products manufacturing) have a few well-established players. Each player knows who their competitors are and how they may behave (like symmetric oligopolies), so there is less uncertainty about the nature of the competitive interaction (Keats and Hitt 1988, Anderson and Tushman 2001, Castrogionanni 2002). In concentrated environments, specialized assets (Williamson 1975, 1991) are more enduring and operational efficiency and market power are key (Eisenhardt and Sull 2001). Thus, firms can increase revenue and capture more of the value-add and margins by integrating vertically (Harrigan 1985). Increasing VI can also increase entry barriers (Scherer 1980), enhance market power (i.e., control input and output prices), provide differentiation based advantage (through forward integration) (Fronmueller and Reed 1996), offer opportunities for creating one-stop shopping that can increase switching costs (Chen and Hitt 2002), and provide experience curve advantages (Aaker and Day 1986). Hence, in concentrated industries VI offers different advantages (Teece 1988).

In concentrated environments, IT-enabled information sharing can reduce coordination costs in vertically integrated firms (e.g., D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). Brews and Tucci (2004) report that IT can reduce coordination costs related to the information gathering, decision making, and compliance monitoring needed when producing internally. Similarly, Lee and Billington (1992) suggest that firms can share production plans, capacity, demand, and inventory information to coordinate their value chains. For example, the adoption of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems for real-time information sharing and coordination in vertically integrated firms is associated with improvement in coordination performance (Hitt et al. 2002). Similarly, in the chemical industry, electronic hubs such as Elemica enable ERP-to-ERP connectivity between industry majors and their business partners. Such “integrated end-to-end systems” provide the industry leaders with VI like benefits that reinforces existing industry structures (Christiaanse 2005). Thus, in more concentrated industries, VI offers many advantages and firms may use IT to coordinate activities within more vertically integrated structures.

In contrast to concentrated industries, environments with low entry barriers may lead to a more crowded competitive environment that engenders hypercompetition (D’Aveni 1994). In such environments it is also hard for firms to maintain higher prices as there are a very large number of competitors (Porter 1980). There may also exist players with very different specialized assets that compete aggressively to increase their market share. The variety of players’ assets also gives rise to heterogeneity that may increase competition in ways that often require greater flexibility. Such unstable industry structures increase the risk of VI as the higher overhead cost and asset commitments associated with VI are exacerbated by the aggressive price competition that characterizes such industries (Harrigan 1986, D’Aveni and Ilinitch 1992). VI should be avoided in these settings as it may restrict a firm’s options (Sutcliffe and Zaheer 1998). VI also reduces access to outside innovation in an environment where flexibility is crucial (Harrigan 1986).

In less concentrated industries, firms are better off specializing in a narrow range of activities and using IT to coordinate more with external specialists. Specialization allows firms the flexibility to respond to any competitive moves, whereas VI entails commitments that may impair the ability to respond quickly. Clemons and Row (1992) and Clemons et al. (1993) argue that IT can reduce the cost of searching for, and monitoring other participants in the value chain. Similarly, Gosain et al. (2004) find that through modular design of interconnected processes and structured data connectivity, IT-enabled supply chains provide the flexibility to support changes in orders (offering flexibility) as well as the ability to partner with different supply chain players (partnering flexibility). Thus, when industry concentration is low, firms may use IT to coordinate activities in less integrated organizations. The above arguments lead to the following hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> When industry concentration is high, an increase in IT will be associated with an increase in VI.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> When industry concentration is low, an increase in IT will be associated with a decrease in VI.

## 2.2. Performance Implications of Vertical Integration

The level of VI has implications for firms’ coordination and production cost (Malone et al. 1987, Gurbaxani and Whang 1991). Coordination cost is the cost of finding suppliers and partners, negotiating and specifying contractual terms, monitoring execution of contracts, and taking corrective actions when required. Production cost includes the cost of material and labor involved in producing goods and services. As discussed above (i.e., in H1 and H2), firms may choose different levels of VI and IT in different competitive environments. In this section, we examine the impact of the level of VI chosen in different competitive environments, on firms’ coordination and production cost.<sup>5</sup> Thus, in this section we examine whether the choice of VI and IT in different competitive environments (as hypothesized in H1 and H2) is rational.

2.2.1. Coordination Cost. If a firm performs activities in a more vertically integrated structure, it may exploit opportunities for coordinating activities internally and lower its coordination cost. For example, in steel manufacturing very high temperatures are required for several stages of production, so an integrated plant can save coordination cost by heating steel once and performing many steps (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). VI can also guarantee supply of inputs and reduce cost by avoiding the transaction cost (e.g., finding, contracting, resolving disputes) associated with procuring from external suppliers. VI can also reduce coordination cost as selling and advertising expenses are reduced as sharing of product information and order taking are done through internal hierarchies, rather than through markets.

However, the impact of VI on coordination cost is contingent on the nature of the competitive environment. A firm may choose to respond to high demand uncertainty by increasing its level of VI.<sup>6</sup> This may happen when managers suffer from the “illusion of control” that they can manage the uncertainty by conducting more activities internally.<sup>7</sup> However, coordination of production and information flows is very complex in such environments. Subunits in vertically integrated firms have to adapt to demand fluctuations by extensively coordinating transfers from one business unit to another (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft

1994). High demand uncertainty will also require more administrative effort to cope with unused capacity in the value chain whenever there is a downturn in demand and to solve shortage problems during periods of high demand (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). Thus, uncertain demand conditions may require vertically integrated firms to have more elaborate hierarchies to monitor and synthesize demand information to coordinate production between different business units (Jensen and Meckling 1976, Fama 1980, Jones and Hill 1988, D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). Therefore, VI in uncertain demand environments may increase coordination cost. However, when demand uncertainty is low (i.e., demand is more predictable) vertically integrated firms can more easily coordinate production across different subunits using smaller hierarchies, and thus higher VI may decrease coordination cost.

In contrast to high demand uncertainty environments, in concentrated industries VI may have a different impact on coordination cost. Here VI can improve a firm’s bargaining power over its suppliers by providing information about the processes used to produce an input and thus make the threat of backward integration more credible (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). Similarly, forward integration can reduce coordination cost by guaranteeing adequate outlets for a firm’s outputs and by reducing the price distortions caused by powerful distributors (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). However, when industry concentration is low, VI may increase coordination cost as the unstable competitive environment requires greater flexibility. In this environment the hierarchies needed to coordinate production and other strategic decisions across different sub units of vertically integrated firms may increase coordination costs. These arguments lead to the following hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3A</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> When demand uncertainty is high, an increase in VI will be associated with an increase in coordination cost.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3B</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> When demand uncertainty is low, an increase in VI will be associated with a decrease in coordination cost.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4A</sup> <sup>(H4A).</sup> When industry concentration is high, an increase in VI will be associated with a decrease in coordination cost.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4B</sup> <sup>(H4B).</sup> When industry concentration is low, an increase in VI will be associated with an increase in coordination cost.

2.2.2. Production Cost. If a firm performs activities in a more vertically integrated structure, it may increase production costs. Internal production may lead to a cost disadvantage because of the inability to achieve efficient scale (Stigler 1968). Having in-house captive demand may also give rise to agency problems (Jensen and Meckling 1976, Eisenhardt 1989) that can increase production costs because in-house units often do not have the incentives to be as efficient as market suppliers. Markets enforce discipline and efficiency on suppliers, which may be absent under internal production. Thus, VI can increase production costs as it may link firms to inefficient adjacent subunits (Harrigan 1986).

However, the impact of VI on production cost is contingent on the nature of the competitive environment. Firms often have to make production and pricing decisions before actual demand is observed (Mahoney 1992). Thus, if demand uncertainty is high, there is a risk of supply failure to the customer (e.g., when there is stock out because of underproduction) or the risk of overproduction for the firm (Mahoney 1992). In response to this demand uncertainty, a firm may choose to perform activities in a more vertically integrated organization. However, if the firm invests in many specialized assets, it is likely to be saddled with assets that are not useful when conditions (e.g., consumers’ tastes and preferences, technologies) change (Williamson 1975, 1991; D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994). Also, the unpredictability of demand may result in procurement of material at a premium, or shipment through expensive mode (e.g., air shipping). These uncertain demand conditions may also require many change orders that lead to more rework. Similarly, rush orders may require overtime labor, increasing production costs. Thus, when demand uncertainty is high, production costs can increase with VI. However, when demand is more predictable, there is less risk of excess capacity, and firms can build and operate plants at more efficient scale. Thus, if demand uncertainty is low, VI can reduce production costs.

In contrast to uncertain demand environments, VI in concentrated industries may have a different impact on production cost. Firms in concentrated industries may have the market share to achieve economies of scale, thus VI can reduce production costs. VI can also provide learning and experience curve advantages (Aaker and Day 1986). Though the large captive demand may exacerbate the agency problems (Jensen and Meckling 1976, Eisenhardt 1989), on balance, VI in concentrated environments may lead to a decrease in production cost. However, in less concentrated environments, VI may be associated with an increase in production cost. In these unstable competitive environments, firms need to retain the flexibility to change strategies frequently. Thus, vertically integrated firms are often stuck with obsolete assets when they need to pursue new strategies with different technologies. These arguments lead to the following hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5A</sup> <sup>(H5A).</sup> When demand uncertainty is high, an increase in VI will be associated with an increase in production cost.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5B</sup> <sup>(H5B).</sup> When demand uncertainty is low, an increase in VI will be associated with a decrease in production cost.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6A</sup> <sup>(H6A).</sup> When industry concentration is high, an increase in VI will be associated with a decrease in production cost.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6B</sup> <sup>(H6B).</sup> When industry concentration is low, an increase in VI will be associated with an increase in production cost.

## 3. Data and Variable Measures

## 3.1. Data

This study uses data from multiple sources. Firm level IT spending data from 1995 to 1997 are drawn from InformationWeek 500. This list was compiled annually by InformationWeek. These data have been used in prior research in IS (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999). We match the IT spending data for the firms listed in InformationWeek with data from COMPUSTAT. We also use data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) to estimate the level of VI.

## 3.2. Variable Measures

3.2.1. IT Spending. We use annual IT budget as a measure of IT spending. The IT budget data was adjusted using the price and inflation index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

3.2.2. Vertical Integration. VI refers to the extent to which a firm carries production processes from raw materials to the final product within its boundaries (Singer 1968). We use the following three-step process to assess VI (Fan and Lang 2000). First, we investigate each firm’s segment information in COMPUSTAT’s segment database. For each firm, its primary segment (the four-digit standard industrial classification (SIC) segment with the highest sales) is identified, and all the other segments are considered as secondary segments. Second, for each secondary segment, we calculate the vertical relatedness between this secondary segment and the primary segment, based on the 1997 Input-Output (IO) Table from $\mathrm { B E A } . ^ { 8 }$ The value of the vertical relatedness between the two segments is determined by two factors: (i) the dollar value of the secondary segment’s output required to produce the primary segment’s output, and (ii) the dollar value of the primary segment’s output required to produce the secondary segment’s output. If two segments have strong make-buy relationship according to the material flow data in the IO Table, they will have a high value of vertical relatedness. Third, based on the calculation from the first two steps, we assess each firm’s VI level using the following formula:

$$
V = \sum_ {j} W _ {j} V _ {j}.
$$

In this formula, $W _ { j }$ is the ratio of the jth secondary segment’s sales to the total sales of all the secondary segments, and $V _ { j }$ is the vertical relatedness between the $j \mathrm { t h }$ secondary segment and the primary segment. If $^ { \mathrm { a } }$ firm’s largest secondary segment(s) has (have) strong vertical relatedness with its primary segment, then the firm will have a high VI index and will be regarded as being highly vertical integrated.

3.2.3. Competitive Environment. Demand uncertainty reflects the instability/variance in sales in an industry (John and Weitz 1988, Bergh 1998, Bergh and Lawless 1998). For each industry, past five years’ data on industry sales is used to calculate the demand uncertainty for each year. More specifically, demand uncertainty is calculated using the following regression equation (Keats and Hitt 1988):

$$
\ln (y _ {t}) = a + b x _ {t} + e _ {t},
$$

where $y _ { t }$ is the yearly sales of a particular industry, and $x _ { t }$ is the year. Growth rate is the antilog of the estimate of the regression slope b. Demand uncertainty is the antilog of the standard error of the regression slope b.

The four-firm concentration ratio is the sum of the market shares of the top four market share leaders in an industry. A high four-firm concentration ratio implies a stable competitive environment (Harrigan 1985, Harrigan 1986, Chatterjee et al. 1992, Majumadar and Ramaswamy 1995). One advantage of the demand uncertainty and four-firm concentration ratio combination of measures for the competitive environment is that they complement each other. As demand uncertainty is related to the variance in industry sales over time, it measures uncertainty on the demand (consumer) side of a firm. Four-firm concentration ratio, on the other hand, is a simple measure of uncertainty on the supply (competition) side of the firm. When calculating the demand uncertainty (or four-firm concentration ratio) for a firm that operates in multiple industries, we weight the demand uncertainty in each industry the firm participates in, with the proportion of the firm’s sales from that industry. Demand uncertainty and four-firm concentration ratio are calculated using the COMPUSTAT Segment database.

3.2.4. Performance Measures. We are interested in the impact of firms’ VI level on coordination and production costs. We use selling, general, and administrative (SGA) cost divided by sales as a measure for coordination cost. SGA reflects the selling and administrative costs incurred to coordinate activities inside the firm and with suppliers and customers, and thus is an aggregate measure of coordination cost (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994, Mitra and Chaya 1996, Bharadwaj 2000). We use cost of goods sold divided by sales as the measure of production cost (D’Aveni and Ravenscraft 1994, Mitra and Chaya 1996, Bharadwaj 2000). The data for the performance measures is drawn from COMPUSTAT. As this is a cross-industry study and performance varies from industry to industry, we normalize performance by dividing a firm’s, say, production cost, by the average production cost of all the firms in the same industry.

3.2.5. Industry Capital Intensity. Industry capital intensity is an alternative explanation for the level of VI as firms in capital intensive industries are expected to be more vertically integrated (Brynjolfsson et al. 1994). Thus, industry capital intensity is included as a control variable. This variable is calculated from COMPUSTAT segment database. For each segment (identified as a SIC/NAICS code) i, we calculate its total assets AT  and its total sales $( S A L E _ { i } )$ based on the segment data from all the firms that participate in segment i. If a firm participates in n segments, the firm’s industry capital intensity is calculated as: $\begin{array} { r } { C A P = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } ( s _ { i } / S ) ( A T _ { i } / S A L E _ { i } ) } \end{array}$ where $s _ { i }$ is the sales of this firm in segment i and $S = \sum ^ { n } s _ { i }$ . In other words, the industry capital intensity of a firm is the capital intensity of all the industries the firm participates in weighted by its sales in those industries.

The other control variables in the study include capital structure—ratio of total liabilities to sales (Hitt et al. 1997), and debt to equity ratio (Hitt 1999). The level of VI and coordination and production costs may also be influenced by the scale of operations and the number of employees involved in operations. So sales and the number of employees are also included as control variables. Similarly, firms with larger market share and firms in growing industries may be more vertically integrated. Thus, market share and industry growth rate are also used as control variables for VI (Hitt 1999). Also, manufacturing firms may have a different level of VI compared to service, and agriculture and mining firms. Therefore, industry type is also used as a control variable. The level of IT investments may be influenced by the alternative investment opportunities a firm has, so growth options—the ratio of book value of assets to market value of the firm (Dewan et al. 1998)—is used as a control variable for $\Pi . ^ { 9 }$ Table 1 presents sample characteristics and Table 2 presents the bivariate correlations between the key variables. ## 4. Empirical Analysis

## 4.1. The Models

We are interested in two questions. (1) How does the competitive environment moderate the relationship between IT and VI? (2) How does the level of VI in different competitive environments influence firms’ coordination and production costs, i.e., is the choice of VI and IT in different competitive environments (as found in (i)) indeed rational? Prior studies (Dewan et al. 1998, Hitt 1999) have shown that ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation will suffer from simultaneity bias because a firm will choose its IT investment given its VI level, and vice-a-versa. Hausman tests on our data set also show that VI and IT budget are endogenous, thus rejecting the OLS formulation in favor of two-stage least squares (2SLS). Therefore, we use a 2-equation model for the first research question as causality may run not only from IT to VI, but also from VI to IT. The model is shown below.

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} V I _ {i} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} I T _ {i} + \alpha_ {2} U N C _ {i} + \alpha_ {3} I T _ {i} \cdot U N C _ {i} + \alpha_ {4} F O U R _ {i} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {5} I T _ {i} \cdot F O U R _ {i} + \alpha_ {6} C A P _ {i} + \alpha_ {7} M S _ {i} + \alpha_ {8} C S T _ {i} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {9} D E Q _ {i} + \alpha_ {1 0} S a l e s _ {i} + \alpha_ {1 1} E M P _ {i} + \alpha_ {1 2} G R R _ {i} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {1 3} T _ {1 i} + \alpha_ {1 4} T _ {2 i} + \alpha_ {1 5} T _ {3 i} + u _ {v} \end{array} \right. (\mathrm{VI})
$$

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c} I T _ {i} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} V I _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} U N C _ {i} + \gamma_ {3} F O U R _ {i} + \gamma_ {4} V I _ {i} \cdot U N C _ {i} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {5} V I _ {i} \cdot F O U R _ {i} + \gamma_ {6} C A P _ {i} + \gamma_ {7} S a l e s _ {i} + \gamma_ {8} D E Q _ {i} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {9} C S T _ {i} + \gamma_ {1 0} G R O P _ {i} + u _ {z}, \end{array} \right. \tag {IT}
$$

where VI (VI ), IT (IT ), demand uncertainty $( U N C _ { i } )$ four-firm concentration ratio (FOUR<sub>i</sub>, industry capital intensity (CAP , market share (MS , capital structure (CST , debt to equity (DEQ , sales (Sales , the number of employees (EMP<sub>i</sub>, growth rate $( G R R _ { i } )$ and growth options (GROP , represent the characteristic of firm $i ,$ respectively.<sup>10-</sup> <sup>11</sup> We also use three dummy variables as control variables for VI. $T _ { 1 }$ is 1 if firm i has segment(s) in agriculture or mining industries, $T _ { 2 }$ is 1 if firm i participates in manufacturing industries, and $T _ { 3 }$ is 1 if firm i participates in service industries.

For the second research question, we use the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} P F _ {i} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} V I _ {i} + \beta_ {2} I T _ {i} + \beta_ {3} V I _ {i} \cdot U N C _ {i} + \beta_ {4} V I _ {i} \cdot F O U R _ {i} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {5} U N C _ {i} + \beta_ {6} F O U R _ {i} + \beta_ {7} M S _ {i} + \beta_ {8} S a l e s _ {i} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {9} E M P _ {i} + u _ {R}, \end{array} \tag {PF}
$$

where the dependent variable PF is performance (coordination cost or production cost).

The VI, IT, and PF models also use interaction terms. For variables that are involved in the interaction (e.g., IT, UNC, and FOUR in the VI model), we center them by subtracting the mean from each variable. Centering can reduce multicollinearity among these variables and make the regression coefficients more meaningful. For example, after we center IT, UNC, and FOUR in the VI model, $\alpha _ { 1 }$ is the impact of IT when UNC and FOUR are at their average level.

Two-stage least squares method (2SLS) to assess each model. Instrumental variables include two-digit SIC segment and year dummy variables. Each twodigit SIC code instrument variable takes a value of 1 if the firm participates in this two-digit industry and 0 otherwise. These exogenous variables capture industry specific effects (Wernerfelt and Montgomery 1988). We also use two-year dummy variables, Y1995 and Y1996. For example, if a data point is for the year 1995, then $\Upsilon 1 9 9 5 = 1$ . The year dummy variables capture any variances that are related to time, such as IT prices, interest rates, price of oil, level of defense expenditures, etc. (Hitt 1999). We tested for multicollineraity. The variance inflation factor values in all the models are well below the normal threshold of 10 (Cohen et al. 2003).

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

Before discussing the implications of the findings, it is important to recognize the limitations of this study. The analysis was performed using IT investment data collected by InformationWeek. The InformationWeek 500 purportedly includes leading users of IT in the US. To that extent, the sample is not purely random. However, if we can show the differences in the relationship between IT and VI under different competitive environments using this sample, it can actually bolster our claim. Furthermore, this data set has been used in prior research to examine the economic impact of IT investments (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Anderson et al. 2004). Also, because these firms are leading users of IT, they are likely to make more judicious use of IT that other firms can learn from. The data may also be considered old. However, because the data predates the Internet boom and bust periods, it may be free of the overreactions commonly associated with that period.

The ability to use IT to cost-effectively coordinate with suppliers and business partners has received much attention in the IS literature. This stream of research presents theoretical arguments and empirical evidence that IT investments are associated with a decrease in VI. However, economy-wide data suggests that over the last 25 years the average level of VI has, in fact, increased. This paper studies this empirical anomaly and examines the moderating impact of competitive environment to provide a more refined understanding of the relationship between IT and VI. The analysis suggests that in uncertain demand and in unstable competitive environments, IT may be associated with a decrease in VI as firms try to maintain flexibility by reducing commitments in specialized assets. Here IT provides the agility to coordinate with different external partners and specialists (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). The analysis also suggests that in concentrated and in more predictable demand environments, IT may be associated with an increase in VI as firms use IT to coordinate more activities inside the firm to increase revenue, and capture value-add and margin.

The findings of this research have important implications for the diffusion of IT-enabled services such as business-to-business (B2B) electronic exchanges, and can help in understanding why B2B exchanges may take different forms in different environments. For example, when demand uncertainty is high, firms are more likely to adopt B2B electronic exchanges to search for and to coordinate with suppliers and customers. This is consistent with the developments in B2B exchanges (Hoffman et al. 2002). For example, in the electronics industry, B2B exchanges such as Converge and e2open are used to search and coordinate activities with external suppliers with specific assets and capabilities. However, in more concentrated industry environments, such as in the chemical industry, B2B exchanges (e.g., ChemConnect, Elemica) are used for close coordination of activities with business partners with whom there exist well established ongoing relationships (Lee 2002, Hoffman et al. 2002). Therefore, B2B exchanges may take different forms in different industry environments.

The difference in the use of IT across different competitive environments is also consistent with Fisher (1997) and Lee (2002)’s arguments that supply chains take different forms depending on demand and supply uncertainties. Firms selling innovative products that often have uncertain demand and supply, need agile supply chains; whereas firms selling functional products with relatively more stable demand and supply, need efficient supply chains. For example, firms in the networking industry (like Cisco, Nortel Networks, and 3Com) that sell innovative products, have vertically deintegrated and rely on agile supply chains and use IT for global virtual manufacturing (e.g., Dedrick and Kraemer 2005, Barua et al. 2004). However, firms in the relatively more stable retail industry, use technologies such as such as telemetry for vendor managed inventory (VMI). VMI provides suppliers visibility into the demand and consumption patterns, and enables retailers to benefit from quasiintegration with suppliers.

In any industry, the relationship between technology and VI is also changing over time. For example, firms in the telecommunications (e.g., AT&T) and the photographic (e.g., Kodak) industries that were once highly vertically integrated are seeing significant restructuring because of technological changes such as digital technology and the Internet. Similarly, in the 1990s many pharmaceutical majors were vertically integrated into pharmacy benefit managers (Levy 1999). However, as demand uncertainty increased and concentration decreased, the level of VI also decreased. This suggests that changes in the competitive environment and technology interact continuously to influence the level of VI. In this regard, this paper indicates that the changes in the nature of the competitive environment can inform about what form the relationship between IT and VI will take in a given industry.

This research raises two issues for further analysis. First, this study suggests that IT may not be associated with a general decrease in VI, but that the impact of IT depends on the nature of the industry environment. Thus, one key implication of this research is that the level of VI may be chosen strategically, given the nature of the competitive environment. For example, an increasing proportion of the value-add is shifting from manufacturing to service (Osegowitsch and Madhok 2003). Firms like IBM and HP are moving from manufacturing to IT services. Firms are pushing downstream to capture this value-add by establishing direct relationships with customers using IT and embedded IT (e.g., Konana and Ray 2007). Direct relationships allow firms to understand customer preferences, build switching costs, and sell more products by cross-selling and up-selling. For example, Fronmueller and Reed (1996) find that forward integration is associated with differentiation advantage. Thus, as the service component of the economy increases, and firms integrate into downstream markets for strategic reasons, it is important to take a more integrative approach to study the relationship between IT and VI. This integrated perspective is also consistent with the call by Santos and Eisenhardt (2005) to take a broader look at firm boundaries.

One strategic motive for VI is to use it as an entry barrier. A vertically integrated firm can raise costs for competitors by reducing the price of outputs or by increasing the price of inputs. Thus, the threat that a large, vertically integrated firm can engage in price squeezing, deters entry of less integrated firms (Mahoney 1992, Osegowitsch and Madhok 2003). For example, as discussed earlier, it may be argued that large firms in the petroleum industry use IT to coordinate operations in vertically integrated structures, not only for efficiency reasons, but also to deter the entry of specialist players in different markets. Antill and Arnott (2004) suggest that with the growth in the depth and liquidity of crude and product markets, the case for vertically integrated operations in the oil industry has become less convincing. They provide an analysis of the barriers to competition and efficiency that vertically integrated “super majors” can exploit.

The fashion industry provides another interesting example of the strategic use of VI. In this industry product life cycles are short and differentiation advantages built on product styling can be quickly imitated. Also, low cost global manufacturing has become the norm where producers exploit lower labor costs in developing countries. However, even in this environment some vertically integrated firms (e.g., Zara) have achieved competitive advantage by shifting competition towards speed and timing (Richardson 1996). Here, IT facilitates the integration of customer demand information with manufacturing to enable rapid learning and flexible and fast cycle manufacturing, a strategy not available to the more efficient but vertically disaggregated firm (Kasra et al. 2004).

Second, IS research has considered coordination and production costs as independent of each other. However, in practice the nature of coordination can affect production costs. For instance, improved coordination may lower production costs through better use of resources (e.g., machine and labor), and lower procurement costs (e.g., by avoiding last minute procurement at higher rates or shipping using expensive modes). Accounting statements also present coordination costs such as selling and administrative expenses, independent of the production costs (i.e., cost of goods sold). It is likely that coordination efficiency and effectiveness affects production costs. Future research must explore in greater detail how technology driven coordination and production activities interact, and the implications of this interaction for the level of VI.
