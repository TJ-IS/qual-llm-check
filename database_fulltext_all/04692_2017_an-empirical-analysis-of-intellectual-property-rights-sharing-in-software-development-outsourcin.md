# AN EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS OF INTELLECTUALPROPERTY RIGHTS SHARING IN SOFTWAREDEVELOPMENT OUTSOURCING

Yuanyuan Chen Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117417, REPUBLIC OF SINGAPORE {discy@nus.edu.sg}

Anandhi Bharadwaj Goizueta Business School, Emory University, Atlanta, GA 30322 U.S.A. {abharad@emory.edu}

Khim-Yong Goh Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117417, REPUBLIC OF SINGAPORE {gohky@comp.nus.edu.sg}

Software development outsourcing (SDO) contracts are plagued with ex post opportunism and underinvestment problems. Property rights theory (PRT) argues that appropriate property rights allocation between vendors and clients can reduce opportunism and incentivize relation-specific investments. We conduct an in-depth content analysis of 171 real SDO contracts and empirically examine how project attributes and contract parties’ bargaining power affect the allocation of intellectual property rights (IPR). We find that clients retained more IPR when software development was modularized whereas they shared more IPR with vendors in contracts that incorporated greater use of a vendor’s proprietary software. Greater levels of task complexity were associated with more IPR sharing with vendors. We also find that the responsiveness of IPR to project attributes varied across the different types of intellectual assets. For example, vendors were more likely to obtain redeployment rights of know-how if they were contracted for novel software development projects. However, clients were less likely to cede ownership of data and confidential information embedded in software customization projects. We control for a variety of firm and transaction characteristics and the results we obtain here are robust to concerns of endogeneity bias.

Keywords: Software development, outsourcing, contract design, intellectual property rights allocation, property rights theory, incomplete contracts

## Introduction

Software development outsourcing (SDO) is a knowledgeintensive process where the contracting parties create a bundle of intellectual assets such as functional requirement specifications, business process designs, technology and data architectural plans, algorithms, and source code (Gefen et al. 2008; Walden 2005). These intellectual assets are created for the core purposes of the client’s business activities and in accordance with specific instructions by the client. They often embody the client’s confidential information such as business practices and pricing analytics. The intellectual assets generated in the SDO process are generally protected as intellectual property (IP) (de Laat 2005; Harison 2008).

IP laws grant asset owners a bundle of IP rights (IPR) including ownership and usage rights to exploit the intellectual properties. In SDO, it is generally the case that clients hire vendor firms under the work-made-for-hire principle, where the default norm is for clients to retain all of the new IP created in the project. However in practice, it is striking to observe that clients are often willing to share or cede some of the ownership and usage rights to the vendors. For example, Carson and John (2013) reported that clients share at least some IPR with vendors when they outsource their research development and engineering projects. This is also observed in SDO contracts when clients grant vendors the right to use or produce derivative software products for future use (Susarla et al. 2010; Walden 2005). However, in the case of software contracts, there is no comprehensive understanding of (1) the intellectual assets generated in SDO and the related IPR involved and (2) the contingency factors that determine when vendors are more likely to obtain IPR from clients. Understanding the first question is essential to securing the value generated in SDO as the means to create and exploit such value can vary considerably depending on the strategic and complementary use of different types of intellectual assets. We do not know enough about the appropriate approaches to protect different types of IPR in software and the ways to exploit created values. Studying the determinants of the allocation of IPR is a central issue in the negotiation of SDO contracts. IPR allocation matters because it can help reduce ex post opportunism and ensure that the contracting parties are incentivized to invest relation-specific efforts (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart and Moore 1990; Milgrom and Roberts 1992).

The purpose of this paper is to analyze the types of intellectual properties generated in the SDO process and the determinants of IPR allocation. Although IPR in software development has been studied in the IS literature, the focus has centered largely on the relative efficacy of various IP protection mechanisms such as copyrights and patents to protect against unauthorized use (Arora 1996; Leiponen 2008; Simon 1996). The literature on risks in software and IT outsourcing has focused on the typical project management risks dealing with cost, quality, and schedule of the projects, with the concomitant approaches to better planning, monitoring, and control (Gefen et al. 2008; Keil et al. 2000; Krishnan et al. 2000; Tiwana 2008b, 2010; Tiwana and Keil 2009), but has remained largely silent on IP risks. Despite limited theoretical work on allocation of IP rights in SDO (Walden 2005), the software contract design literature has mainly focused on compensation mechanisms such as pricing structure and monetary incentives (Dey et al. 2010; Gopal et al. 2003; Lichtenthaler and Ernst 2012) but has ignored the incentive opportunities from IP rights.

The limited empirical research on how IPR is allocated to reduce ex post opportunism has either focus on a specific type of IPR (Anand and Khanna 2000; Elfenbein and Lerner 2003; Susarla 2012) or has treated IPR as a monolithic and indivisible bundle of rights (Whang 1992). Given that the responsiveness of contract parties’ investment efforts to the allocation of IPR may depend on the value and the degree of appropriability of individual IPR (Lerner and Merges 1998; Walden 2005), the allocation of individual IPR or combinations of IPR subsets may fulfill different contractual governance functions. Therefore, previous research focusing on a specific dimension or lump sum effects of IPR has not been able to differentiate their value and effects clearly and has led to some ambiguous results (Adegbesan and Higgins 2011).

The only prior studies that have analyzed IPR framework and allocations in contracts are Lerner and Merges (1998), Adegbesan and Higgins (2011) and Carson and John (2013), but these studies have not examined SDO contracts. The first two papers examined IPR allocation in a biotechnology development alliance and focused on the impacts of biotechnology companies’ bargaining power (measured as the firm’s financial status) on the allocation of patent ownership. Carson and John studied the role of IPR allocation as a dependencebalancing mechanism to change ex post bargaining power of contract parties in R&D engineering outsourcing. Their findings may not be fully generalizable to the SDO context for the following two reasons. First, the primary exchange hazard in software development outsourcing not only arises from contract parties’ bargaining power but also assetspecificity and uncertainty in software development projects. Software development often intertwines with other operation and production activities within the client company (Gefen et al. 2008; Tiwana 2008a, 2008b). The linkage between different functional activities and the interaction between client and vendors make the software development process complicated and difficult for parties to foresee and articulate contingencies in contracts, thereby increasing the hazard of ex post opportunism such as hold-up. Second, the contract party’s response to the incentive opportunity of IPR varies across industries and sectors. In industries where lead time or secrecy is crucial, such as the case in the software industry, the incentive effect of property rights allocation could be different from that in biotechnology and engineering R&D contracts. In addition, the above three studies all focus on the allocation of patent ownership. Patent rights are not so prominent in SDO contracts because software can be protected not only under patent law but also under copyright and trade secret law. The value and the appropriability of copyright or trade secret are quite different from patent ownership given that these IPR are governed by different rules. Against this backdrop and to the best of our knowledge, our study represents the first attempt at categorizing different types of IPR that are specified in SDO contracts and the determinants of these IPR allocations using actual contracts signed between vendors and clients.

We begin by examining what IPR are generated in the SDO process. We refer to law textbooks (Bainbridge 2007) and the literature in economics (Milgrom and Roberts 1992) and software engineering (Kurbel 2008; Larman 2004; Pressman 2005) to identify key intellectual assets generated in the SDO process. We then conduct a comprehensive literature review of research in IPR to identify different types of ownership and usage rights that have been examined in the prior literature. Combining these streams of literature, we derive eight types of IPR that are critical for SDO contracts. Next, we build a coding scheme to analyze the content in contract provisions in terms of how these IP rights were distributed between contract parties. Drawing on prior theoretical and empirical work on property rights and transaction cost economics, we then develop specific hypotheses to show how the relative vulnerability of the contracting parties to ex post opportunism drives the need for IPR sharing in SDO contracts. The content analysis data coded from 171 actual SDO contracts are used to test the hypotheses.

This study makes three significant contributions to the literature on SDO and contract design. First, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of IPR in SDO and develop a framework that specifies ownership rights of five valuable intellectual assets (namely, preparatory materials, computer programs, databases, derivatives, and other works) and the usage rights of these assets. This allows us to examine the value and characteristics of different types of intellectual assets and how the property rights of these assets are negotiated and allocated independently or as a bundle. Second, our research contributes to the literature on contract design by empirically examining how IPR allocations serve as a governance mechanism. SDO contracts are unique because software development requires relation-specific investments from vendors and the outputs are mostly customer-specific. We develop specific hypotheses that link the antecedent factors based on the indispensability of the contracting parties and the degree of uncertainty in the contracting context to the propensity of the clients to share or cede specific IPR. Our analysis and findings enable us to advance the literatures in both incomplete contract design and software outsourcing risk management while providing strategic implications for software outsourcing risk management. Finally, we develop a coding scheme that can be used to codify contract clauses in other IT service or technology development outsourcing contracts. Contract design research is restricted by the availability of contracting data. Most prior studies are either based on analytical modeling or survey data. As Carson and John suggest in their paper,

IPR might be better measured by observing explicit contractual clauses rather than by questionnaire scales. This approach might also allow better empirical (and theoretical) discrimination between the various aspects of IPR and their functioning (p.1082).

Subsequent related research can use our coding scheme and content analysis methods to collect and analyze data from explicit contractual clauses. Collectively, these contributions extend and deepen our understanding of the research on IPR allocation in SDO contracts in a novel and distinctive way.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The following section discusses prior work on software develop ment and IPR allocation; we then move on to develop our hypotheses on the determinants of IPR sharing. Next, we describe our data, data analysis methods, and variables, which are followed by the results, discussion, and conclusion.

## Software Development and Intellectual Property Rights

## Intellectual Assets Involved in Software Development Processes

Different intellectual assets are created in the different software development stages. A typical software development process comprises several generic phases: requirements gathering and analysis, design, construction, testing, debugging, and deployment (Kurbel 2008; Larman 2004; Pressman 2005). Figure 1 depicts the stages of software development and the types of intellectual assets generated in each stage. Software development often starts with requirements gathering and analysis which identify a program’s functions and business investment requirements. The developer will then design software internal structures such as subroutines, flow charts, task divisions of modules, and definition of database structure. The requirement and the design stages often require the most up-front time and investment in creating preparatory materials. Once the program design is complete, the developer starts transposing the program’s structural blueprint into the source code. The construction stage often requires the developer to be creative in conveying the blueprint into source code. The source code is subsequently translated or “compiled” into machine-readable object code. When the coding is completed, the developer will use the client’s business data to test the program in order to find and correct any logical and syntactical errors. The testing and debugging process generates multiple testing plans and reports, as well as release documentation. After the successful completion of testing and debugging, the program is deployed. The developer is expected to deliver the computer program which consists of the source code, object code, compilation of the programs, and final product documentation.

Conventional IP law suggests that the intellectual assets generated in the SDO process can be classified into five types of properties: preparatory materials, databases, derivatives, other works, and software programs (Bainbridge 2007; Keplinger et al. 1984). The classification of these five types of works is based, first, on their roles in the input–output sequence of software development and, second, on the value of each type of intellectual assets which is different due to their embedded knowledge, efforts spent, criticality, and appropriability level. Preparatory materials refer to the inputs for software development, including requirement specifications, algorithms, input/output formats, reports, and protocols, whereas derivative works are the works based on one or more preexisting software program in which a work may be transformed, adapted, extended or modified (Bainbridge 2007) to facilitate development of the final product. For example, translating an English version of a work to a Japanese version is counted as making a derivative of the work. The derivative is also a protected intellectual asset. Databases comprise client’s business data for software testing, database structure, data contents, and database displays, etc. Other works such as logos and images are often produced in software development processes. Source code, object code, compilation of programs, screen display, and alpha and beta release documentation are in the software program category which is the final output of the SDO process.

## Types of IP Rights in SDO

The input and output works created in the SDO process are subject to the protection of IP rights which are the exclusive rights granted by law over the “use of creations of the minds” for a certain period of time.<sup>2</sup> IP owners have exclusive control over a collection of individual rights inherent within the property (Klein and Robinson 2011), including control over the use of a property as well as its returns (Milgrom and Roberts 1992), the right to grant others use of the property, as well as the right to exclude anyone else from using the property without consent (Bainbridge 2007). Furthermore, IP owners are deemed to have the control of residual rights that are not explicitly specified in contracts (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 1988; Hart and Moore 1990; Milgrom and Roberts 1992). For instance, under the IP laws of most countries, the client is the default property owner of the work-in-progress software based on the work-made-for-hire principle. Property owners can keep the entire ownership for themselves or transfer it wholly to another party (sole ownership). Alternatively, they can share the IP ownership (joint ownership) or selectively grant IP usage rights to others (Bainbridge 2007; Carson and John 2013).<sup>3</sup> A few IS studies have examined IPR in IT contracts. Elfenbein and Lerner (2003) studied ownership of servers, customer data, and URL in e-commerce website development contracts. Leiponen (2008) examined ownership of service outputs such as design, process plan, and technology in consulting service agreements.

Usage rights are economic rights consisting of reproduction and distribution of intellectual property (Bainbridge 2007). IP owners can authorize others to use, reproduce, or distribute a part or the entirety of an intellectual asset and share the proceeds from the IP usage. The usage rights can be granted in a limited way, for a limited time, and in a limited geographical scope or purpose of use. Prior IS research has examined several types of usage right in IT contracts. For example, Anand and Khanna (2000) studied the license to use in licensing agreements and Susarla (2012) investigated the redeployment right of know-how and intellectual assets in IT outsourcing contracts. In the Carson and John (2013) study, distribution right was included as an important usage right for R&D outsourcing relationships. These usage rights are also valuable for software developers since they can use these intellectual assets and know-how for other clients or use them to further develop their proprietary technologies.

Prior contracting studies (in IS and non-IS) often consider IPR as a subset of control rights to govern contractual relationships (Elfenbein and Lerner 2003; Lerner and Merges 1998). As summarized in Table 1, in addition to IPR, previous research has also examined the right to terminate the contract at will (Lerner and Malmendier 2010; Ryall and Sampson 2009; Susarla 2012), source code escrow (Susarla 2012), non-compete clauses restricting vendors from competing with clients ex post (Leiponen 2008; Mayer and Salomon 2006), and business restrictions on clients or vendors (Elfenbein and Lerner 2003; Leiponen 2008). These control rights are distinct from IPR since control rights are allocated for control over the development and relationship management processes whereas property rights are granted for the outputs of software development (Lerner and Merges 1998). Empirical research also suggests that the allocation of IPR and control rights respond to different contractual risks and criteria (Elfenbein and Lerner 2003). As our paper focuses on IPR as a form of incentive to ensure the commitment to tasks, studying other forms of control rights are outside the scope of this research.<sup>4</sup>

The value of the intellectual properties may differ across firms and contracting contexts in which knowledge, innovation, and appropriability play a key role (Arora and Merges 2004; Teece 1986). Individual IPR may be granted and shared depending on the transactional environment and context (Carson and John 2013). Hence, it is necessary to understand the IPR system holistically. Our study focuses on the ownership of five types of intellectual assets and three usage rights that are economically important in SDO relationships and that have been examined in prior literature, namely

(1) Right to use the developed software and source code for a specific purpose after the contract ends, but not modify, reproduce, or sell the software. This right does not allow the party to use the developed software, to sell, or to distribute the software program to the public.

(2) Right to redeploy the residual knowledge asset such as information relating to ideas, concepts and techniques which are gained in the SDO process into projects for other parties. Different from the right to use which grants the right to use the output of the SDO project, the redeployment right allows the rights holder to use the know-how and information gained in the SDO process, and the residual intellectual assets that may not have been clearly specified in SDO contracts.

(3) Right to sell the developed software to third parties in certain geographical regions and industries.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Intellectual Property Rights Allocation

SDO contracts are inevitably incomplete because contracting parties are usually unable to exactly prescribe the system requirements, architecture, prototype designs, and deliverables ex ante (Boehm 1989; Zmud 1980). Such contract incompleteness is exacerbated by unobservability and unverifiability of vendor’s actions and output quality in software development projects (Boehm 1989; Dey et al. 2010; Mayer and Salomon 2006). Given each party’s fear of hold-up at the renegotiation stage, SDO contracts are often plagued with severe underinvestments (Klein et al. 1978; Susarla 2012). The prior literature on contract design for software and IT outsourcing has drawn heavily on incomplete contracting based on transaction cost economics (TCE) and the property rights theory (PRT). These theories assume that due to bounded rationality, contract parties cannot foresee all possible future states of the world, thereby failing to specify a complete contract at the time of signing the contract (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 1988; Hart and Moore 1990; Williamson 1979, 1985). Contract parties who are strategic and self-interest seeking will behave opportunistically to

The contract party that invests more relation-specific assets in the production process is therefore likely to encounter opportunistic behaviors from the other party (i.e., hold-up). The investing party, anticipating this ex post hazard of opportunism, will therefore refrain from investments in relationspecific assets, leading to less optimal contracting performance. To counter the opportunism and under-investment problems, the PRT literature proposes that assigning property rights to the investing parties can induce incentives to invest critical resources in the relationship (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 1988; Hart and Moore 1990). Property rights owners have ownership of the intellectual assets and also the control rights to determine how the assets are to be used in circumstances not specified by contracts or laws (i.e., residual control rights) (Grossman and Hart 1986; Milgrom and Roberts 1992). Brynjolfsson (1994) extended the PRT approach to the study of information assets and examined the conditions under which ownership of information assets and complementary physical assets should be transferred or owned by the party investing in non-contractible assets. The IT outsourcing literature has used the PRT approach to address the primary concerns of underinvestment and hold-up (Walden 2005). The literature has examined the monetary aspect of contractual governance (i.e., the pricing structure) (Banerjee and Duflo 2000), and the optimal allocation of ex post contractual planning and decision making (Susarla 2012; Susarla et al. 2010).

PRT posits that when both contract parties expend relationspecific investments, property rights of both relation-specific assets invested in the production process and the output assets must be assigned to the party whose relation-specific investment has relatively higher marginal effects on contract performance (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart and Moore

1990). Property rights to development output and intermediate assets in SDO are prescribed to clients because they provide the investment needed to fund the development efforts. However, developers’ effort and investment on projects are critical and oftentimes indispensable for the success of the software development project. This is particularly true when the client’s source for a vendor’s specific expertise or for the vendor’s proprietary technologies. To reduce hold-up opportunism and overcome underinvestment problems, the PRT literature proposes that clients should cede some IPR to vendors to induce behavior that optimizes project returns (Carson and John 2013; Leiponen 2008; Susarla 2012; Walden 2005). Since software development creates a bundle of intellectual properties, clients can choose to share the whole or a part of the bundle of properties with vendors (i.e., ownership sharing) or retain property ownership for themselves while granting individual usage right to vendors (i.e., usage right sharing) (Arora 1996; Arora and Merges 2004; Walden 2005). In this study, we refer to these types of IPR ownership and usage distribution choices collectively as IPR sharing.

Deciding the extent of IPR sharing is crucial in contract design literature. Prior literature on contract design combines transaction cost theory with the PRT approach and identifies factors that influence the degree of opportunism and thereby increase the pressure for IPR sharing. Ex post opportunism is exacerbated by the degree of uncertainty in the contract (Williamson 1985), the extent of asset specificity in the relationship (Hart 1988; Williamson 1979), and the degree of appropriability of IPR (Oxley 1997; Teece 1986). Uncertainty is defined as a lack of knowledge of future contingencies (Arrow 1974). A high level of environmental and task uncertainty as well as the contract parties’ behavioral uncertainties can make it impossible to prescribe contingencies that may occur in the future, thereby increasing the hazard of opportunism. Asset specificity refers to “the degree to which an asset can be redeployed to alternative uses and by alternative users without sacrifice of productive value” (Williamson 1989, p. 142). Relationship-specific investments have low values outside of the contractual relationship. When a transaction requires specific investments in the production process, an ex post quasi-rent is generated amounting to the differences in values between the intended use of the specific investment and the next best use of it. The firm that does not invest the specific assets may request a larger share of the quasi-rent from the investing party that has expended the specific investments by threatening termination of the relationship (Klein et al. 1978). Anticipating this hold-up threat, the investing party would bargain for a large share of quasirents in ex ante contract negotiations to secure their stake. Appropriability concerns also affect the extent of contractual safeguards in place, especially when parties have proprietary assets at stake that could be appropriated by the contract partner for other uses in the relationship. The greater the magnitude of such concerns, the greater the extent to which the parties will retain IPR to protect the assets involved (Oxley 1997; Teece 1986).

Although contracting hazards play a key role in IPR allocation, there are additional factors that can influence IPR distribution between contract parties. Aghion and Tirole (1994) extend the PRT approach to study IPR allocation in a situation of resource restriction. They suggest that resource indispensibility and relative bargaining power, in addition to opportunism and underinvestment, also play a role in the allocation of property rights. Resourse indispensibility refers to the degree of substituability of rival products. In the absense of alternative resources, contract parties who control the indispensible assets will have relatively stronger bargaining power. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that they will use their dominant positions to negotiate for as many rights as possible for themselves.

The TCE and PRT theoretical propositions are supported by prior empirical literature which finds that allocation of property rights are subject to the contract party’s indispensability and bargaining power (Carson and John 2013; Elfenbein and Lerner 2003, 2012; Kaplan and Strömberg 2004; Leiponen 2008; Lerner and Malmendier 2010; Lerner and Merges 1998; Ryall and Sampson 2009). For example, Elfenbein and Lerner (2003, 2012) found that the ownership of three important assets—servers, URL, and customer data—was assigned to alliance partners whose contributions to Internet portal development were relatively more indispensable. Kaplan and Strömberg (2004) and Lerner and Merges (1998) tested the bargaining power due to financial strength and found that contract parties with more financial resources retained more residual control rights. Leiponen (2008) studied another source of indespensibility and bargaining power—service providers’ innovation capabilities—and found that more innovative service providers retained more control over intellectual assets. Walden (2005) formulated five types of intellectual property ownership structures and posited that three factors—usefulness of software, resale value of the software, and relative importance of specific investments— affect the design of ownership structures in SDO contracts, while Carson and John (2013) found that vendors had more IPR when there were greater environmental uncertainty and asset specificity in the projects.

In summary, a comprehensive review of the theoretical and empirical literature based on TCE and PRT shows that contract parties’ asset indispensability and bargaining power, along with the degree of uncertainty inherent in incomplete contracting, drive the need for IPR sharing as a useful governance mechanism (beyond simply relying on the pricing mechanism to align parties’ interests). Dependence on a vendor’s proprietary technology and the degree of modularity in software design have served as useful mechanisms to examine the level of asset indispensability and contract parties’ bargaining power respectively (Baldwin and Henkel 2014; Bush et al. 2010; Dourish and Edwards 2000; Pressman 2005). To get to the question of project-related uncertainty, we turn to the software engineering literature which has carefully examined software project characteristics and associated risks (Boehm 1989; Cataldo and Herbsleb 2013; Na et al. 2004; Nidumolu 1995; Pressman 2005). Specifically, the degree of project complexity has been widely identified as a key amplifier of project uncertainty and thereby tends to drive up project-specific risks (Gopal and Koka 2010; Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan 2008; Mooi and Ghosh 2010; Susarla et al. 2010; Tiwana 2010). Additionally, when clients require software customization and newness in the project, vendors have to make relationship-specific commitments and this in turn induces hold-up and misappropriation opportunities, consequently driving up the overall risks associated with the project (Haines 2009; Hasan and Lokan 2013; Kurbel 2008; Wang 2002). Taken together, the above five factors determine the degree of relative vulnerability of the contracting parties to ex post opportunism and therefore we argue that these factors are explicitly considered by the client in determining the IPR sharing and allocation mechanisms. For expositional clarity, we take the client’s perspective and develop hypotheses relating these five factors to IPR sharing between clients and vendors.

## Project Newness

In SDO, vendors are commonly hired to develop new software systems for the client, which could mean either developing a program from scratch or adding new functionalities to an existing program. New software projects often pose additional challenges to vendors, stemming from a lack of understanding about the project requirements as well as the needs of potential end users (Humphrey 1995; Nidumolu 1995; Pressman 2005). Hence, new software development projects demand a high level of relation-specific investments from vendors. Requirements analysis is the most important stage in the software development process, especially for applications that are novel and involve unstructured tasks (Zmud 1980). Vendors have to develop a deep understanding of the client’s business processes, and spend hours interviewing end users to elicit their requirements for information display. Vendors are also required to invest upfront efforts in translating these requirements into detailed design specifications before the coding phase can begin (Kurbel 2008). These are among the most time-consuming and challenging outsourcing projects with regard to communication requirements between the client’s and the vendor’s employees (Liao et al. 2010). However, for modification of existing systems, it is relatively easier for the clients to perform the requirements analysis and develop precise systems specifications. In such cases, the vendors’ investment on relation-specific development is relatively smaller compared to that of totally new systems.

Once vendors begin the requirements analysis phase, their relation-specific assets are subject to the risk of hold-up by clients (Susarla et al. 2010). Clients may try to renegotiate to derive more value from the project or they may even renege on the contracts if they discover the development project to be infeasible. For instance, the client can abandon the project completely or take the requirements specifications to contract with a new partner. Anticipating this, vendors are likely to be reluctant to invest upfront efforts. Based on PRT (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 1988; Hart and Moore 1990), such holdup problems can be mitigated by allocating IPR to the vendor so as to generate the best possible incentives for motivating the vendor to expend maximum efforts in developing the new software system. Therefore, from an incentive perspective, we hypothesize that

H1: Vendors that are contracted for new software development projects are more likely to be assigned individual IPR or gain more IPR in SDO contracts.

## Task Complexity

A complex task consists of multiple and possibly unknown dimensions that are associated with project and performance uncertainties (Nidumolu 1995). In a complex software project, not only are the functionalities and specifications of the final output difficult to define, but development costs and delivery dates are difficult to estimate at the contracting stage (Kalnins and Mayer 2004; Susarla et al. 2010; Wang 2002). Moreover, complex software development often requires frequent changes on the agreed-upon requirements and architectural design (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991; Prendergast 2002). Such project uncertainties raise the difficulty and costs of contracting (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991; Prendergast 2002). For example, in a study of IT contracts, Anderson and Dekker (2005) found that the complexity of IT products and ex ante uncertainties about the quality and performance specifications of the IT transaction were associated with hold-up problems and higher contracting costs.

Task complexity also increases performance uncertainty. In complex development projects, the outcome of the project is difficult to estimate and measure regardless of the estimation techniques used (Kurbel 2008; Nidumolu 1995). Frequent adaptation of requirements analysis in the software development process makes it impossible to predict SDO performance outcomes such as actual costs, completion time, system benefits, the software’s compatibility with the client’s existing systems, and the software’s technical performance such as functional errors and bugs (Gefen et al. 2008; Kalnins and Mayer 2004; Nidumolu 1995). Such performance uncertainties give contractors an incentive to shirk and cut corners on a project. In addition, incomplete requirements specification and ambiguous design inevitably result in higher coordination costs. For these reasons, the rate of failure is higher for complex software projects. Complex projects, therefore, require higher relation-specific investments from the vendor, such as greater investments in communication and closer coordination with the client (Clemons and Hitt 2004). When these projects fail or when the clients end the contracts prematurely, the relation-specific investments will become sunk costs for vendors. Vendors may thus have a lower incentive to commit effort to the project, especially when their efforts are noncontractible.

To mitigate the risk of underinvestment, PRT holds that when contracts are incomplete, the party that owns or controls an asset will collect the residual returns on its own noncontractible investment and will therefore be motivated to make these investments (Hart and Moore 1990). Thus, by granting certain ownership or usage rights to the vendor, the client firm is likely to induce higher motivation on the vendor’s part to put in the requisite efforts, leading to our next hypothesis:

H2: Vendors that are contracted in software development projects with a higher level of task complexity are more likely to be assigned individual IPR or gain more IPR in SDO contracts.

## Customization

Customization is the tailoring of software for a specific user (organization or business unit) and differs from the development of generic software (off-the-shelf software packages) that is designed for the mass market (Dittrich et al. 2009; Haines 2009; Hasan and Lokan 2013). Customized software typically incorporates knowledge components and business rules that are specialized to the end users. Such personalization has been shown to improve both usage and productivity of computer systems and research suggests that it even increases the sense of end-user empowerment (Dittrich et al. 2009; Fischer and Scharff 2000). In fact, research in software engineering emphasizes the importance of user goals, preferences, and skills in designing customized software (Hui et al. 2003).

When clients choose to have customized software developed, it is an effective signal of the uniqueness of the firm’s IT and business context. Customized software embodies firmspecific knowledge that clients normally would not want their competitors to access. However in SDO, the clients will have to share with vendors their specific business requirements, practices, and trade secrets that might be significant sources of competitive advantage to them (Dittrich et al. 2009; Haines 2009; Hasan and Lokan 2013; Walter 2003). The knowledge assets created and exchanged during the SDO processes represent valuable IP and are subject to potential expropriation (Arora and Merges 2004; Oxley 1997). In transactions where appropriation problems are greater, explicit provision for property rights are used to protect vulnerable parties (Teece 1986; Williamson 1991). Client firms are therefore more likely to be motivated to retain the full IPR to the final product as well as to all the intermediate knowledge assets if they feel appropriation issues are critical.

Viewed from the vendor’s perspective, developing custom software is labor intensive (Dibbern et al. 2004) and vendors often need to acquire unique skills and business knowledge in order to complete the particular customization (Wang 2002). Such investments are highly client-specific and not readily transferable to alternative uses without losing substantial value, thereby reducing the vendor’s bargaining position in ex post renegotiations. Moreover, vendors often need to coordinate closely with clients’ personnel in order to complete custom development tasks. The interdependencies between clients and vendors create a potential hold-up problem for vendors because vendors are responsible for completing projects within time-bound deadlines, but in cases of interdependence, the vendor has to rely on the client’s personnel to make progress on the necessary tasks (Mayer 2006; Mayer and Salomon 2006). The vendor anticipating this vulnerability could potentially under invest unless sufficient safeguards are provided. A call to PRT would suggest that vendors should be allocated IPR as a safeguard against potential opportunistic behaviors.

The above discussion suggests that clients are likely to be pulled in both directions, toward greater sharing of IPR to motivate vendors against underinvestment of efforts, and toward little or no sharing of IPR to protect their own IP assets. However, to balance the dependence on vendors in customized software projects, substitutive incentives such as time and materials pricing and other monetary incentives (e.g., bonus payments for vendor performance) could be used to sufficiently motivate the vendors and to counter the risk of underinvestment. On the other hand, it is difficult to come up with alternative mechanisms to protect the client’s specific investments and intellectual properties other than through explicit IPR embedded in the contracts. With increasing investments in specific assets, the client’s quasi-rents increase and, anticipating this, clients would expect more ex ante contractual safeguards in the form of higher IPR allocated to themselves. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H3: Vendors that are contracted in customized software development projects are less likely to be assigned individual IPR or gain less IPR in SDO contracts.

## Vendor’s Proprietary Technology

Although cost reduction is still the most common motivation for software outsourcing (Lacity et al. 2009), many companies have outsourced for more strategic reasons which include gaining access to leading-edge technologies and know-how to improve business or process performance (DiRomauldo and Gurbaxani 1998). For example, even though Microsoft has strong in-house software development capabilities, it hired Inktomi to develop supporting software for Microsoft’s search engine. The main purpose of the contract was to access Inktomi’s proprietary coupled cluster technology that enables the connection of multiple computers to operate as a single computing system.

When vendors are hired to develop software using their proprietary technology, their preference, unless explicitly controlled by the client, would be to make architectural design choices that exploit their proprietary designs and technologies (which could include design methodology, development tools, and other proprietary code needed to develop the new software). Software development knowledge tends to be complex and involves a large number of different individuals and interacting technologies and routines. The connections between these elements are often highly context-specific. Therefore, the vendor’s proprietary technology and contextual knowledge become indispensable resources and clients will not be able to continue the focal software development project without the vendor’s cooperation. In some circumstances, vendors have accumulated innovation capabilities that exploit their proprietary systems and can make unique intellectual contributions to the client’s project. Thus, vendors can derive bargaining power from the valuable resources they bring to the software development process and negotiate for more ownership or usage rights of the generated intellectual assets (Emerson 1962; Heiskanen et al. 2008; Mayer and Salomon 2006; Mesquita et al. 2008). As vendors’ proprietary technologies becomes indispensable to the success of SDO projects, clients will be more likely to share ownership or usage rights with vendors as a form of incentive mechanism to encourage valuable resource deployment. At the same time, this can overcome opportunistic bargaining by vendors threatening to withhold resources. Under such conditions, the tenets of the PRT predict a greater degree of IPR allocated to the party with the better bargaining position (Aghion and Tirole 1994). Therefore, we hypothesize that

H4: Vendors that are contracted in software development projects based on vendors’ proprietary technology are more likely to be assigned individual IPR or gain more IPR in SDO contracts.

## Software Modularity

We next turn our attention to software design modularity and examine its implications for the extent of IPR shared with vendors. Modularity has emerged as a general set of principles for software design and refers to the manner in which a high level design is decomposed into constituent subsystems (modules) that can communicate through standardized interfaces (Baldwin and Clark 2000). By adopting a more modular design, one avoids what would otherwise be an unmanageable spaghetti tangle of systemic interconnections (Jordan and Lowe 2004). Work in the strategic management literature has examined the effect of modularity on alliance control (Bush et al. 2010; Sanchez and Mahoney 1996; Tiwana 2008a, 2008b) and has shown that that the design principles of loose coupling and standardized interfaces provide a form of embedded coordination that renders redundant overt managerial supervision (Sanchez and Mahoney 1996).

In software outsourcing, design modularity can be an important consideration both in the boundary between the outsourced system and the client’s base systems as well as, among the different components of the new system. Modularity insulates the client from new and unfamiliar underlying technologies used by the vendor and affords the client the ability to plug its existing (base) systems into the new system without changing its base applications (Kurbel 2008). Clients can apportion development tasks to multiple vendors with different skill sets. Multi-vendor sourcing helps to protect clients from vendor opportunism by reducing the lock-in risk that is inherent when working with a single vendor. Moreover, modular systems are easier to change and replace, thereby reducing the overall control that a vendor can exercise both in the current project as well as in future work (e.g., through maintenance contracts). Therefore, modularity reduces resource-dependence of the client and thereby weakens ex post bargaining positions of the vendor. Furthermore, modularity in software design helps protect the client’s knowledge base from risks of misappropriation because no single vendor is likely to gain access to the entire domain of knowledge possessed by the client. Since the client will only need to share with each vendor the knowledge needed for their particular subsystem as well as the interface knowledge common to all vendors, this can significantly lower appropriability concerns (Baldwin and Henkel 2014).

Modularity of products has also been shown to increase the level of standardization within an industry. For example, in manufactured products such as bicycles (Galvin and Morkel 2001), it has been shown that increasing modularity in design fragmented the industry into a series of largely independent subcontractors hired primarily through market-based contracts. The fragmentation of the industry based on specialized capabilities led to economic efficiencies and lower barriers to entry. Even though designing software remains a more complex activity than bicycle manufacturing, it is likely that the effects of greater modularity will have a similar impact on at least some segments of the software industry. The overall effect would be to reduce the client’s dependence on the vendor and thereby to lower bargaining power for the vendors and lower willingness on the part of the clients to share or cede IPR to the vendors. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H5: Vendors that are contracted in software development projects to develop modular systems (i.e., outsourced software has well-defined interfaces with the client’s existing systems) are less likely to be assigned individual IPR or gain less IPR in SDO contracts.

## Empirical Analysis

## Data Collection and Measures

Data for our study comes from software development contracts culled from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and further augmented with data from ICC fulltext annual reports for international company profiles.<sup>5</sup> The SEC regulations (17 C.F.R. Part 229) require publicly-listed companies to disclose “material contracts”<sup>6</sup> in their financial filings and software development contracts are frequently included in the filings of companies registered in the United States. We initially collected 215 SDO contracts signed between the years 1992 and 2007 (both years inclusive). To maintain generality in our analysis, we excluded transactions where (1) the contract was a renegotiation or restatement of a previous agreement between the two firms, (2) one firm had a controlling interest in the other firm (greater than 50%), and (3) multiple vendors were specified in the same contract. We also dropped 43 contracts due to substantial missing information. Our final sample comprised 171 SDO contracts spanning 13 industries, of which 163 agreements were signed by U.S. companies and 8 by UK companies. 121 agreements were with U.S. vendors and the remaining 50 were signed with vendors from 20 different countries. About 59% of the clients were from high technology industries. The frequency distribution of the sample across industries was found to be similar to those of other licensing agreements and alliance contracts which have been studied in prior literature (Anand and Khanna 2000; Oxley 1997). Of the 171 contracts in the final sample, 55 contracts released contract values, ranging from a maximum exceeding \$650 million to a minimum of \$20,000, with an average value of \$5.66 million. A total of 54 contracts were for simple enhancements or for adaptive or perfective changes to an existing system; 76 other contracts involved reengineering an existing system; another 41 required vendors to develop new systems, interfaces, or applications. We were able to collect contract length information for 169 agreements from the contracts, the firms’ 10-K reports, and press releases. The length of the contracts ranged from 3 months to 300 months with a mean length of 29.8 months and a standard deviation of 29.1. Overall, about 71% of the contracts used a flexible pricing structure (T&M and cost-plus). About 39% of the contracts in the sample were signed after the year 2000, with 20% of the clients having had prior business interactions with the vendors.

The data obtained from software contracts were supplemented with additional variables that served as controls for the empirical analysis. For each contract, we searched the Factiva News archive using client and vendor company names to find contract-related press releases. In addition, we collected dominant client information from COMPUSTAT, CRSP, and the firms’ financial statements. Statement of Financial Accounting Standards (FASB) No. 14 requires firms to disclose the identity of any customer representing more than 10% of the total sales of a firm. We collected the customer abbreviations from the COMPUSTAT industry segment files and followed the matching procedure used by Fee and Thomas (2004) to get the full company names from CRSP. When vendors were not publicly traded companies, we collected information on dominant clients from the “Business Relationship” and “Risk Factors” sections of the client’s financial statements and further searched contract information from other press releases. For nonpublicly listed firms, data about their revenue and the number of employees were culled from company websites and Bloomberg Businessweek database for small and medium size companies. We downloaded the lists of IT technology companies from three online resources: the Fortune 1000 technology firms, the Information Week list of 500 technology companies, and the International Association of Outsourcing Professionals (IAOP) Global Outsourcing 100 and sub-lists. Vendors’ reputation was coded based on whether the vendor was included in any of these lists. Country-level software piracy rates were collected from the Business Software Alliance’s (BSA) annual piracy studies (BSA 2008; IPRC 2003) and were used as indicators of the legal protection environment for IPR.

## Dependent Variable

We coded two dependent variables: the propensity for sharing individual IPR with vendors and the extent of IPR allocated to vendors. Referring to the economics and IT contract design literature (e.g., Carson and John 2013; Elfeibein and Lerner 2003; Leiponen 2008; Lerner and Malmendier 2010; Milgrow and Roberts 1992; Susarla 2010), we developed a coding scheme to code the five types of ownership rights and the three usage rights in each of the 171 contracts (see Table 2).

Ownership refers to the rights for full control over the property and to the proceeds generated by the property. Each of the five types of assets—software program, database, preparative material, derivatives, and others—was coded as 1 if ownership was assigned to the client, 2 if there was joint ownership, and 3 if it was assigned to the vendor (Elfeibein and Lerner 2003). Panel A of Table 3 provides the frequencies and cross frequencies of the five ownerships for all of the contracts in the sample. As expected, most of the SDO contracts assigned IPR ownership to clients. Overall, less than 37% of the sample contracts allocated sole ownership of any of the five assets to vendors. For example, about 37% of the sample assigned ownership of developed software to vendors, with only 28% giving vendors ownership of the database created during the software development processes. Cross tabulation shows that at least 70% of contracts assign bundles of ownership rights to vendors. For instance, ownership of software program clustered with ownership of preparative materials at a rate of 95%. Owning both assets gives vendors the traceability to link software development requirements to corresponding design artifacts, code, and test cases to support numerous software engineering activities and software modifications (Pressman 2005).

Usage Rights. Clients, as property owners, can choose to retain property ownership and grant usage rights to vendors or share the proceeds from the property. Three usage rights— right to use the developed software, redeployment right of know-how, and right to sell developed software—are critical economic rights in software development relationships. Each of the usage rights was coded as 1 if the client retained the sole ownership of the software program (i.e., vendors did not have joint or sole ownership) and granted the usage right to the vendor, and coded as 0 if the vendor did not have property ownership and the focal usage right (Adegbesan and Higgins 2011; Elfenbein and Lerner 2012; Lerner and Malmendier 2010; Susarla 2012). Panel B in Table 3 shows that out of 90 contracts where clients held sole ownership of software program, about 41% granted vendors the right to sell developed software and share proceeds with clients; only about 34% of the contracts allowed vendors to use or modify the software program and code after the contract ended; and less than 18% of vendors gained the right to redeploy residual information in other projects. Cross frequency analysis between the usage rights indicated that less than 60% of client owners authorized vendors both the right to use and the right to sell simultaneously and only about 40% of vendors who were granted right to use software also had the right to redeploy residual information and know-how gained in the SDO process.

Extent of Ownership/Usage Rights. The extent of vendor ownership and usage rights were also used as dependent variables to capture the total number of asset ownership (either joint or sole ownership) and usage rights assigned to vendors. The mean of the extent of ownership is 1.65 with a standard deviation of 2.14, whereas the mean of the extent of usage rights is 0.93 with a standard deviation of 0.90, demonstrating a different rights allocation strategy between ownership and usage rights and that asset ownerships were bundled but usage rights were often assigned separately.

## Controls

In order to control for alternative explanations and other potential confounds, we included several additional variables that served as important controls: (1) flexible pricing structure (Gopal and Koka 2010), (2) prior inter-firm interaction between contract parties (Goo et al. 2009; Rai et al. 2009),<sup>7</sup> (3) dominant client (Banerjee and Duflo 2000; Fee et al. 2006; Fee and Thomas 2004; Susarla et al. 2010), (4) vendor’s reputation (Banerjee and Duflo 2000; McGuire et al. 1988; Preston and O’Bannon 1997; Susarla et al. 2010), (5) software piracy level in the vendor’s operating countries (Marron and Steel 2000; Moores and Chang 2006), (6) log(vendor’s age) indicating vendor’s relative experience, (7) high-tech client indicating whether the client is in a hightech industry<sup>8</sup> (Francis and Schipper 1999), (8) log(client’s age), (9) client’s size, (10) log(contract length) (Susarla et al. 2010), and (11) year dummy to capture other unaccounted time-period effects by using the year 2000 as the cut-off point. In some circumstances, the client may have granted the vendor the right to sell but simultaneously used a non-compete clause to restrict the vendor from competing with the client’s business, for example, by selling software directly to the client’s customers. This type of non-compete restriction will reduce the value of the vendor’s usage rights. Hence, we included a non-compete control dummy to indicate whether the contract specifies a non-compete clause.<sup>9</sup> Table 4 provides a description of the variables and measurements.

To ensure coding consistency and reliability, two undergraduate law degree students independently carried out the coding of all the contracts and the supplementary data (control variables). Examples of sample provisions from actual contracts were provided to the coders who were trained extensively with a few comprehensive contracts. ## Model Estimation and Results

We propose an econometric model that explains a vendor’s ownerships and usage rights as a function of the variables featured in the hypotheses, along with all the controls described above. The first set of dependent variables—the propensity of individual ownership and usage rights sharing—are ordinal and binary variables respectively; and the second set of dependent variables—the extent of ownerships and usage rights sharing—are ordinal.

Care was taken to address potential endogeneity concerns in our econometric specification. First, IPR assigned to the vendor is typically negotiated during the contracting process and may be co-determined with other governance mechanisms such as pricing structure (i.e., simultaneity and self-selection issues). As depicted in Figure 1, the typical arrangement in SDO is for the client to search and identify a vendor company to develop and implement a software system with stated functionalities (Gefen et al. 2008). The client and the vendor will then negotiate the specific terms such as what is to be developed, how the work will proceed, and the payment terms ex ante and come to agreement on the most important aspects which will then be written into the contracts before the development work starts. Second, additional and potentially relevant characteristics that influence IPR allocations may be unobserved in the contracts. Unobserved factors, such as the client’s software development capabilities and its risk preferences, might influence the client’s inclination to share IPR with the vendor. Failure to account for these possibilities could lead to biased estimates of the effects of our focal explanatory variables. The prior literature on outsourcing suggests that pricing structure is endogenous to the contractual governance structure because clients often select pricing structure as a major incentive mechanism to control the risks and costs in contractual relationships (Banerjee and Duflo 2000; Gefen et al. 2008; Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan 2008). Therefore, in order to correct for potential biases that could arise as a consequence of endogeneity, we adopt the two-stage control functions approach (Heckman and Robb 1985; Wooldridge 2007) for our empirical analysis. In the two-stage estimation, we estimate an equilibrium model of pricing structure that predicts the probability of choosing flexible pricing in the first stage.

We use three instruments—public vendor, distance dummy, and labor cost—to satisfy the requirement of the exclusion restriction for the two-step control function estimation approach. Public vendor is measured as 1 if the vendor was a publicly listed firm. Distance dummy is an indicator for geographical distance (of more than 500 km apart) between the client’s and vendor’s operating addresses, and labor cost is measured as 1 if the average wage index (AWI) was higher than U.S. GDP growth rate, indicating a high labor cost business environment. Most of the clients in our sample were located in the United States. We used U.S. AWI data published on the USA Social Security Official Website and collected GDP growth rate from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis website<sup>10</sup> to calculate labor cost. These three variables may affect a firm’s selection of flexible pricing but are less likely to influence the IPR sharing.<sup>11</sup> In the second stage, we use estimates from the pricing structure model to derive the correction terms lambda, which is the inverse Mill’s ratio (IMR)—the error correction term generated in the first stage equation. Lambda is then included in the regression of IPR allocations in the second stage equation to control for the correlation between the error terms of the two equations. In sum, our econometric model is structured as follows:

First Stage: Pricing Structure Model

$$
\operatorname{PR} (\text { FlexiblePricing } = 1 | X) = \Phi (X ^ {\prime} \beta);
$$

$$
\operatorname{PR} (\text { FlexiblePricing } = 0 | X) = 1 = \Phi (X ^ {\prime} \beta);
$$

where, Pr denotes probability, Φ is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution, and X denotes a vector of regressors including five explanatory variables, all controls and three instruments.

Second Stage: Individual IPR Sharing Propensity and Extent of IPR Sharing Model

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { PR(IPR   Sharing) / Extent   of   IPR   Sharing } = \\ M (x _ {o} \beta_ {o} + \beta_ {e} P r i c i n g + \rho_ {1} L a m d a + \varepsilon_ {2}) \end{array}
$$

where, M(@) is a probit or ordered probit function for Pr(IPR Sharing) and extent of IPR sharing, respectively, $x _ { o }$ is a vector of variables including the five explanatory variables and all controls, and Lambda is the error correction term generated from the first stage model estimation.

We report the marginal effects of explanatory variables and controls in Tables 6 and 7. Column (1) in Table 6 presents the first-stage probit results of the pricing model. Task complexity, year dummy, client age, and non-compete dummy were significantly associated with pricing structure. It shows that complex software development contracts were more likely to use flexible pricing and experienced clients preferred fixed pricing to avoid moral hazard and shirking problems, which is in line with earlier findings (Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan 2008). Contracts signed after Year 2000 preferred to use flexible pricing and contracts with a non-compete clause were more likely to choose flexible pricing. Two out of three instruments—public vendor and labor cost—were significantly related to pricing structure. From correlation statistics, we also see that these two controls were not significantly correlated with IPR allocations, demonstrating that these two instruments were indeed exogenous variables.

We first run the two-stage control function model estimations to analyze the extent of IP ownership and usage rights sharing. The extent of ownership sharing was measured as the total number of IP ownerships (either joint or sole ownership but not double counted) that vendors have. The extent of usage rights was measured as the total number of usage rights granted to the vendor under the circumstances that the vendor was not awarded any type (i.e., joint or sole) of ownership rights to the software. In our sample, 90 out of 171 contracts did not grant vendors any type of ownership. The estimation results reported in Columns (2) and (3) in Table 6 provide good support for the hypotheses. We found project newness to be significantly associated with the extent of usage rights sharing but not the ownership, thus partially supporting H1. Task complexity was positively associated with the extent of vendor’s ownership and usage rights, thus supporting H2. The marginal effect of task complexity indicates that task complexity leads to 0.34 more ownership and 0.49 more usage rights assigned to the vendor. Customization was negatively and significantly related to the extent of the ownership and usage rights, supporting H3. The marginal effect of customization was -0.48 for ownership and -0.88 for usage rights. Consistent with our prediction in H4, the results demonstrate that a vendor’s use of proprietary technology was positively associated with more IPR assigned to the vendor with a marginal effect of 0.47 for ownership and 0.77 for usage rights. The estimated marginal effects associated with modularity were -0.71 for ownership and -0.70 for usage rights, suggesting that increasing software modularity reduces the client’s proclivity to share ownership and usage rights of end products with vendors, fully supporting H5.

We also use the same estimation method to analyze the propensity of sharing of individual IPR. As seen from the results reported in Columns (1) through (8) in Table 7, project newness was only positive and significant for redeployment right, whereas customization was negatively associated with the ownership of data, right to use, and right to sell. We observe that task complexity was positive and significant for many of the IPR except for the ownership of data, right to use, and right to redeploy. As hypothesized, proprietary was also positive and significant for the majority of IP rights except for the right of derivative. Vendors do not need to negotiate for the ownership of derivative because they own the proprietary technology that is the key for the software development. Instead, they might negotiate for the ownership of the software program to derive financial benefits from the developed software without restrictions. Modularity was negative and significant for most of the IPR except for the ownership of database, right to use, and right to sell. This finding is consistent with one of the major benefits of modularity: modular design reduces the interdependence among modules and minimizes the need for the sharing of proceeds. ## Robustness Checks

Additional robustness checks, beyond the two-stage control functions estimation described above, were also carried out. One concern is related to the exclusion restriction of the three instruments used in our models. We performed a two-stage least square estimation on the extent of vendor ownership by including the three instrumental variables. The Sargan statistic (for test of over-identification) was 4.93 at p-value = 0.085 under the null hypothesis that the all instruments are valid instruments. Therefore, we failed to reject the null hypothesis at the p < 0.05 level, demonstrating that the three instruments are indeed good instruments to control for the potential endogeneity of pricing structure.

We also used the two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI) method to estimate the extent of IPR sharing and the propensity of individual rights sharing. Instead of using the error correction term (i.e., Lambda), we included the response residual in the first-stage probit analysis as an additional regressor in the second-stage estimation. Prior econometric literature has proved that 2SRI estimation is consistent for nonlinear regression models (Terza et al. 2008). The results of the extent of IPR sharing were reported in Columns (4) and (5) in Table 6. We report the 2SRI estimation of individual IPR sharing propensity models in Table 9. The 2SRI results for the main explanatory variables were mainly consistent with the results of using error correction terms in the control function estimation.

Another concern relevant to our study is to examine whether vendors preferred additional ownership when compared to usage rights. In estimating the vendor’s aggregate number of IPR as the dependent variable, our results do not provide any information about the relative importance of these two sets of rights. To examine this possibility, we used the Chow test after a seemingly unrelated regression (SURE) (Green 2003) of 90 contracts to examine whether there exist significant differences in preferences between ownership and usage rights (Columns (6) and (7) in Table 6). The Chow test revealed significant differences between the estimated coefficients of all five main explanatory variables in the regressions of the extent of ownership and usage rights at the p < 0.05 level. The marginal effects of four main variables—project newness, customization, proprietary, and modularity—on usage rights are larger than those on ownership, demonstrating that clients preferred to cede vendors usage rights rather than grant ownership when they outsourced for development projects of high asset-specificity and when the vendor’s bargaining power was relatively strong.

The pricing structure is potentially endogenous to contractual IPR allocation as mentioned above. The selection of the pricing structure may influence the IPR allocations and these effects may differ across the selected pricing model. To assess the consequences for self-selection on vendor IPR allocation, in the second stage of the control function estimation, we divide the dataset into two subgroups, flexible pricing and fixed pricing contracts, and run two separate ordered probit estimations to estimate the parameters across the two pricing structures. As shown in Table 10, the estimated parameters varied across the two pricing structures. Project newness, customization, and proprietary technology were significant only for flexible pricing contracts but not for fixed pricing ones. Clients’ expectations about the higher risk of flexible pricing coupled with appropriation concerns and dependence on vendor’s technology may have led to IPR sharing as a particularly useful mechanism to reduce ex post opportunism. Task complexity was positive and significant for the extent of ownership sharing in fixed pricing contracts but not for flexible price contracts, indicating that the effect of IPR allocation on addressing the uncertainty-caused ex post opportunism issues is more prominent in fixed pricing contracts. Modularity was negative and significant for both fixed and flexible pricing contracts in terms of ownership sharing but not for usage rights, demonstrating that modular design decreased the need for ownership-based incentive mechanisms. The different results across two types of pricing contracts provide a more insightful understanding of how IPR-based incentives substitute for pricing incentives in different kinds of outsourcing tasks.

Another concern relates to the high percentage of flexible pricing contracts in our sample (71%). One of the possible explanations might be that some contracts combined software development and maintenance services in one agreement and used both fixed and flexible pricing methods for different tasks. We tried to address this issue by dropping contracts with long durations (more than 60 months) from the sample and reran the two-stage control function analysis of individual IPR sharing. When dropping long contracts, we assumed that such contracts were hybrid contracts. As a result, 24 contracts were dropped from the sample. Among 147 contracts left in the sample, 101 contracts (68%) were flexible pricing ones and the other 46 were fixed pricing ones. The main results of this alternative sample were consistent with the results using our full sample. This set of robustness check results is reported in Table 11. Although the ratio of flexible pricing contracts is still high compared to other studies, the consistent results obtained indicate that the potential oversampling of flexible pricing should not pose a significant concern to the results reported here.

## Discussion and Conclusion

We started this paper by highlighting two research gaps in our understanding of the IPR and the determinants of IPR allocation in SDO. Bridging these gaps is important for developing a consistent theoretical framework for analyzing IPR allocation in SDO contract design, especially as intellectual property has become more important to the competitive positioning of firms in the digital economy (Leiponen 2008; Lerner and Malmendier 2010). Our paper is among the first to comprehensively examine the different types of IPR involved in software development processes. Modern property rights theory (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 1988; Hart and Moore 1990) defines ownership as the control rights over the assets. This simplified notion of ownership implicitly assumes a monolithic nature of property rights and fails to account for a multifaceted concept of property rights as asset ownership is actually a bundle of partitions of property rights (Alchian and Demsetz 1972, 1973). Our study overcomes this limitation through a comprehensive content analysis of 171 SDO contracts. We find that IPR embodied in software contracts is not monolithic. Instead it is a hybrid bundle of ownerships and usage rights and the ways that these rights are allocated are quite diverse across software contracts. For example, about 53% of the contracts in our sample offered sole ownership of the software program to the client; less than 37% granted any rights to the vendor, and the remaining 10% provided for joint ownership between the two contract parties. This ratio in our sample is consistent with the SDO practice that clients own all the IPR of deliverables based on the default work-made-for-hire rule and it also hints that the value of different types of IPR are heterogeneous and contract specific. The empirical finding on the diversity of IPR framework sheds light on the multifaceted nature of IPR in SDO contracts and responds to the calls for correcting the PRT theory for “the lack of accounting for partitioning of ownership” (Kim and Mahoney 2005, p. 236). As a significant step in this direction, our study suggests that IPR allocation in incomplete contracts should take into account different types of IPR and their independent values in contractual relationships. Hence our research enriches the modern property rights theory and adds to the growing body of literature that examines contractual governance of SDO contracts (Chen and Bharadwaj 2009; Susarla et al. 2010; Walden 2005; Whang 1992).

The results show that IPR sharing depends on the vendor’s bargaining power, asset-specificity, and the degree of uncertainty in SDO projects. But different types of intellectual property rights have different values and functions to motivate the contract parties and safeguard against contractor opportunism. All of the ownerships, with the exception of derivative ownership, seem quite sensitive to the relative bargaining power of the parties measured by proprietary technology. When a software development project relied more on the vendor’s proprietary technology, the predicted sharing of ownership increased, a finding that is consistent with prior theory (Aghion and Tirole 1994; Argyres and Liebeskind 1999; Kim and Mahoney 2005). Project level uncertainty is another important determinant to the division of IP rights. Many software development projects are replete with uncertainty and performance risk. We find that this is explicitly taken into account in the contract parties’ investment decisions and addressed up front in contract negotiation through IPR sharing. We find that for the majority of intellectual assets, the vendor’s ownership increased with the task complexity of outsourced projects. The finding suggests that clients can effectively use IP ownership as an incentive mechanism to ensure vendor’s investment efforts in tasks with a high degree of uncertainty. Derivative ownership is useful in SDOs that are replete with project and performance uncertainties but may not work in projects requiring a high level of asset specificity. Our finding of negative impacts of modularity on IP ownership sharing indicates that client firms can use software modularity to offset the need to share ownership with vendors. A strategic implication of this finding is that investment on developing product and process modularization capabilities can be very valuable for client companies.

Beyond ownership rights, clients can also grant usage rights to encourage vendors to invest in relationship-specific assets. However, when significant uncertainties exist, usage and redeployment rights do not appear to be useful mechanisms for incentivizing vendors. Instead, clients may consider granting vendors the right to sell the developed software if they do not want to cede any property ownership to vendors. IP owners have full control over future income because ownership confers the ability to make decisions affecting the distribution of an income stream whose magnitude and even existence are uncertain ex ante. A significant managerial implication of these results for SDO client firms is that sharing of individual IPR can be used as incentives for noncontractible investments and to safeguard from ex post opportunism. When and how to use IPR sharing will however depend on the relative bargaining position of vendors, the complexity level of the SDO projects, and the asset specificity of the underlying investments.

Naturally, there are some limitations to our study. First, the data were limited to information disclosed in SEC filings and complementary data sources. These sources provided limited background information about the vendors and the clients. Our choice of variables was subject to the availability of the contract data. Although our results are suggestive of the effects of software characteristics such as task complexity, proprietary technology, and modularity on the decisions of IPR allocation, our measures are somewhat blunt and may not perfectly capture or fully reflect the breadth of the underlying project and technology characteristics. The results obtained here are also subject to potential sample selection bias, in the sense that we only observe contracts for publicly listed firms and only those contracts that the firms choose to file with the SEC.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the results obtained here facilitate a greater understanding of SDO contracts. Our coding scheme for IPR can be extended to studies of other types of technology development agreements. It will be interesting to examine IPR provisions across a diversity of technology agreements in different industries. Another direction for future research is to investigate the effects of modular design on outsourcing choices. For example, our results suggest interesting linkages among modularity, task complexity, and proprietary technology in the design of contracts. An interesting extension of our study would be to explore how modular design influences the type of sourcing in the sense of multi-vendor versus single-vendor and the levels of task complexity and customization therein.

The pricing structure in SDO contracts is another interesting variable worthy of further exploration, especially in conjunction with IPR assignments. Pricing is negatively and significantly associated with all types of ownership but not for usage rights. The negative marginal effects suggest that IPR sharing substitutes for pricing structure when it functions to motivate relation-specific investments. The results in Table 10 also indicate that clients allocate more IPR to vendors in fixed-price contracts and for complex tasks. Past studies have shown that clients favor fixed price contracts over time and material contracts (Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan 2008) because they can transfer risks to vendors via fixed pricing. We also find that clients use IPR sharing in flexible pricing contracts that require vendors to invest a high level of relation-specific assets or when vendors have relatively strong bargaining power. These findings suggest that IPR allocation can be used to address residual contractual hazards and hence complement pricing incentives. This result has important strategic implications for incomplete contracting and requires further examination.

Finally, it is also interesting to note that the vendor’s share of IPR in SDO contracts seems to have decreased in recent years. The results based on a binary distribution of the sample into two time periods (before and after year 2000), shows less vendor IPR allocation in the latter period. Additional regression analyses by calendar year show that IPR sharing arrangements have been less frequently used over time and that the extent of IPR sharing (depth of use) has decreased over the years. One possible explanation for this trend is that writing IPR clauses in contracts requires adequate specification of property rights and therefore the cost of negotiation for IPR is higher than that for other contractual governance mechanisms such as pricing and revenue sharing. Whether or not the reduced usage of IPR sharing in later years is due to a learning effect is left for future research.
