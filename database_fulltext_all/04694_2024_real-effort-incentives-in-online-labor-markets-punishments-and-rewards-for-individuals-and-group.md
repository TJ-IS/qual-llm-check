# REAL-EFFORT INCENTIVES IN ONLINE LABOR MARKETS: PUNISHMENTS AND REWARDS FOR INDIVIDUALS AND GROUPS<sup>1</sup>

Matthew J. Hashim Department of Management Information Systems, Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, U.S.A. {mhashim@arizona.edu}

Jesse C. Bockstedt Information Systems and Operations Management Department, Goizueta Business School, Emory University, Atlanta, GA, U.S.A. {bockstedt@emory.edu}

Online labor markets and the humans that power them serve a critical role in the advancement of artificial intelligence and supervised machine learning via the creation of useful training datasets. The use of human effort in online labor markets is not enough, however, as a key factor is understanding the possible interventions that market operators can leverage to incentivize human effort among their labor force. We propose that platforms could implement mechanisms such as rewards or punishments at individual or group levels to incentivize real-effort and output. We apply our interventions using a collaborative image tagging experiment—a folksonomy—and the results provide interesting insights and nonobvious consequences. On average, interventions applied at the group level outperformed interventions applied at the individual level. Punishing the group provided the most controversial incentive strategy and provided a nonobvious significant improvement in effort. Rewarding or sanctioning an individual had similar effects on average, with both treatments leading to significant increases in effort post-intervention. In contrast to predictions, sanctioning appears to have significantly motivated those that were punished. Overall, the interventions applied in our real-effort collaborative image tagging experiment had a significant impact on behavior, which provides guidance for online labor market operators and the use of incentives in the creation of labeled machine learning training datasets.

Keywords: Online labor market, free riding, incentive mechanism, economic experiment, economics of IS, collaborative image tagging

## Introduction

Supervised machine learning (ML) relies upon the creation, curation, annotation, and use of accurate training datasets. The importance of human-powered annotation is so great that industry analysts have suggested that more than 80% of the time spent on AI-related projects is in creating these training datasets (Schmelzer, 2020). As expected, the absence of accurate and high-quality training datasets leads to poor artificial intelligence (AI) reliability, quality, and predictions (Hao, 2021; Northcutt et al., 2021). Therefore, it should not be surprising that online labor markets and the humans that power these markets serve a critical role in the advancement of AI and supervised ML. The general use of human effort in online labor markets is not enough, however, as the key factor is understanding the effects of interventions that online labor market operators can leverage to incentivize human effort among their labor force.

Fortunately, online labor markets are fundamentally built as information systems (IS). Accordingly, controls and/or interventions may be implemented by the information system as necessary to promote a desired outcome such as encouraging effort in creating accurate image annotations. In fact, an important characteristic of online labor markets is that intervention mechanisms can be administered, monitored, and tested through software and the analysis of output, network communications, and platform usage. Alternatively, it is also possible that the human evaluation of online labor market outcomes (e.g., monitoring of image annotation output used for training data) can be used to provide feedback to the information system. It is therefore possible for a moderator, legal body, or labor market operator to monitor the effort and accuracy of human output and exogenously introduce interventions and incentives to combat problems. For example, influencing user behaviors that would otherwise have led to decreased labor effort and therefore decreased or inaccurate training data and lower predictive value.

Image annotation (i.e., tagging) is a core feature of many online platforms (Zuckerberg et al., 2011) and is commonly used for supervised ML as well as other purposes. These text-image training datasets are used to build image classification and object detection machine learning models and are also critical for training state-of-the-art AI systems such as the widely popular OpenAI DALL-E<sup>2</sup> and Midjourney models, which create computer-generated images and art (Radford et al., 2021). Accurately labeled training data is of paramount importance to machine learning and AI model performance, and inaccurately tagged images can lead to biased predicted outcomes and output. Besides the creation of training datasets, image tags can give rise to organically developed classification systems known as folksonomies, also known as collaborative tagging or social classification, which have become a commonplace tool that helps users and machines find and organize online content. Image tagging also plays an important role in enhancing the user experience for many popular online social platforms such as Instagram, Flickr, Facebook, Twitter, and Pinterest, and for content moderation. Tags are not only useful to the user who generated them (e.g., by helping them organize and locate content) but also to training datasets and all users of a platform through the creation of classification systems (e.g., the Flickr YFCC100M dataset). Thus, image annotation provides a valid context for testing possible online labor market interventions in a realistic collaborative image annotation experiment.

We explore the use of exogenous platform interventions to incentivize real-effort<sup>3</sup> in a collaborative image annotation task by human users. We draw primarily upon the experimental economics and online labor market literatures in that we look at the role of direct incentives through rewards and sanctions (punishments) at both the individual user level and group level. Several studies have examined the effects of sanctions on correcting behavior generally; however, there has been little research testing the effects of rewards on incentivizing behavior (see the next section for a review of the related literature). Furthermore, we are unaware of any research that compares the differences in outcomes for rewards and sanctions, as they are enforced by an exogenous system (e.g., a platform) to different groups of market players and in direct response to players behavior. The ability to monitor labor output and exogenously apply incentive mechanisms to shape behavior is unique to the IS context, especially for online labor markets, image collaboration platforms, and other IS that require effort to be expended by participants in exchange for a payoff.<sup>4</sup> As such, this approach to encouraging value-creating behavior has generally not been studied in the prior economics literature. In this paper, we fill this gap in the literature while leveraging the unique characteristics of information systems, behavioral economics, and collective intelligence (Goes, 2013). We address the following research questions: Which exogenously applied intervention improves effort more effectively, rewards or sanctions? To be most effective in improving effort, should exogenously applied rewards and/or sanctions be applied at the individual or group level?

We conducted a controlled online labor market experiment implemented as a collaborative image tagging task to answer these questions. First, we established theoretical predictions regarding the effects of the various types of rewards and sanctions by drawing upon the experimental economics literature. Second, we developed a collaborative image annotation experiment to test interventions and to determine the effectiveness of each intervention. We modified a public goods framework for the experiment, as it presents an ideal and wellunderstood foundation for modeling effort-driven tasks, especially collaborative tasks where some expend minimal effort and earn a payoff based upon the efforts of their peers. These types of behaviors have been widely recognized and studied in an extensive experimental literature on public goods (see e.g., Davis & Holt, 1993, ch. 3, for an overview). Because our design proxies a real-effort online labor task, it allowed us to study the causal effects of exogenously enforced interventions in any online labor market while also leveraging the randomization of assignment of participants to treatments. Accordingly, in our experiment, we implemented individual- and group-level interventions designed to represent the potential actions of an online labor market operator. Individual-level interventions included sanctioning chronic low effort and rewarding those that expend high effort (e.g., withholding payment or issuing a bonus). Alternatively, sanctions and/or rewards can be applied to an entire group rather than an individual.

The results of our experiment provide interesting and, in some cases, surprising insights into the use of negative or positive incentives to improve outcomes in online labor markets. Specifically, we found that group-level incentives outperformed individual-level incentives and that any intervention is better at impacting effort as opposed to doing nothing at all. Rewarding all workers was, not surprisingly, the highest-performing incentive. Sanctioning all workers in a group surprisingly led to a significant increase in effort, on average, across all group members, countering our theoretical predictions. Interestingly, applying interventions at the individual level had nonobvious consequences. Sanctioning only the worst individual in the group resulted in a significant increase in effort for the worst performer and marginal increases in effort for all other group members. Rewarding only the highest performer in the group resulted in a similar level of performance, on average, to sanctioning an individual. Overall, these results have significant implications for the design and implementation of interventions and incentive schemes in online labor markets.

The remainder of our paper is organized as follows. The Related Literature section describes the relevant real-effort and freeriding literature in economics and information systems. Following that, we establish the theoretical background and hypotheses. Next, the experimental design, procedures, and treatments of our collaborative image tagging experiment are detailed. We then present analyses and results, followed by a discussion of implications and policy recommendations.

## Related Literature

To properly ground our research, we first consider the existing work on incentives used in the literature for real-effort tasks such as online labor markets, public goods, and the free-rider problem in experimental economics. Additional related literature is discussed in the Theoretical Background and Hypotheses sections, where we establish economic predictions and motivate our treatment design in comparison to prior works.

## Real-Effort Tasks and Online Labor Markets

There is a growing literature in information systems and other fields that consider incentive design in online labor markets, with the stream largely focused on crowdsourcing and online reviews. Many of these works have discussed the challenge of worker motivation and output, as many of the online labor market interactions are seen as short-term gigs or HITs (human intelligence tasks), while fewer have considered employer reputation for recruiting workers (Benson et al.,

2020). As motivated earlier, human workers are critical for AI and ML (Northcutt et al., 2021, among other uses) and researchers have sought to explicitly identify and filter out unreliable workers in labeling tasks (Jagabathula et al., 2017). Others have shown that worker training and establishing a gold standard can be helpful in addressing variable worker effort (Hettiachchi et al., 2021). In online labor markets such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, researchers have started to consider both extrinsic incentives such as performance pay as well as intrinsic incentives such as praise (Fest et al., 2020). Not surprisingly, research has shown that explicit performance pay increases output while implicit praise does not. To address the challenges of worker motivation and realeffort in online labor markets and other similar contexts, we next describe the free-rider problem.

## Public Goods and the Free-Rider Problem

Diminished real-effort leading to a free-rider problem occurs when those who receive positive utility from resources, goods, and services do not contribute sufficiently to maintain them, which can result in the underprovisioning, overuse, or degradation of the resource, good, or service. Effort degradation and/or free riding is commonly observed in public goods contexts, which are goods that are both nonexcludable and nonrivalrous. In other words, individuals cannot be effectively excluded from using a public good (e.g., drawing value from others’ efforts to annotate images), and the use of the public good by one individual does not directly reduce its availability to other individuals.

The idea of applying sanctions (or rewards) as a mechanism to encourage effort and mitigate free-riding behavior is not a new one (e.g., Ostrom et al., 1992; Yamagishi, 1986). A seminal paper by Fehr and Gächter (2000) found that endogenously (peer) enforced punishment is particularly effective at increasing contributions (i.e., players punishing other players). Faillo et al. (2013) replicated Fehr and Gächter (2000) but with a refinement that only allowed prosocial players to punish antisocial players (i.e., “legitimate” punishment). Many other experimental economics papers followed, which studied these various sanctioning mechanisms involving players punishing other players (see e.g., Bochet et al., 2006; Carpenter, 2007a,b; Masclet et al., 2003; Nikiforakis, 2008; Ozono et al., 2016). An interesting result from this body of work is that players usually choose to incur a cost to themselves to endogenously sanction another player for free riding on others’ efforts/contributions. Rewards have also been introduced in similar settings but have generally been less effective than sanctions at mitigating problematic behavior (Andreoni et al., 2003; Sefton et al., 2007; Walker & Halloran, 2004).

Although online labor, information goods, and other services are not generally considered to be true public goods, there are many examples that have characteristics of a public good (Varian, 1998). Prior research has presented various IS-related contexts such as Wikipedia (Zhang & Zhu, 2011), open source software development (Baldwin & Clark, 2006; Bessen, 2006; Johnson, 2002), online discussion forums (Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Rafaeli et al., 2004), peer-to-peer file sharing networks and piracy (Hashim et al., 2014; Krishnan et al., 2003), crowdfunding (Hashim et al., 2017), and even the internet itself (Huberman & Lukose, 1997; Introna & Nissenbaum, 2000) as public goods. Further, the literature has also established individual contributions as a proxy for “work effort” toward the provision of a good (see e.g., Dickinson, 2001; Dickinson & Isaac, 1998), similar to the underlying efforts required to implement and contribute to an online labor task. So, as would be expected, online labor markets, specifically collaborative tasks, are vulnerable to the free-rider problem, especially when real-effort is required.

## Real-Effort Tasks and the Free-Rider Problem in Experimental Economics

As regards the research on the economics of real-effort and extrinsic incentives in the information systems literature, most studies have not made use of the experimental economics methodology. A recent research commentary in MIS Quarterly (Gupta et al., 2018) highlighted the benefits of experimental economics methods and approaches and called for their use in IS research contexts. Our paper hopes to answer that call by applying an economic framework to the study of real-effort and the extrinsic incentives in online labor markets and how real-effort incentives affect outcomes in a collaborative image tagging experiment. We also seek to contribute to the real-effort and online labor market literatures, as various instantiations of free riding that occur in these contexts have not been studied using experimental economics, especially in collaborative tasks.

A few IS studies have utilized a free-riding perspective from the information and public goods literature, but their focus was to explore the economics of piracy and those that engage in piracy behaviors. We know of one paper using a framed lab experiment to gain insights into the digital piracy context and another abstract lab experiment motivated by piracy and other behaviors such as crowdfunding. Regarding the former, Hashim et al. (2014) used a modified volunteer’s dilemma game (i.e., the piracy game) to study the role of the source of advice on piracy levels. In the latter paper, Hashim et al. (2017) implemented an abstract multi-threshold public goods game to understand the role of targeted information on contribution decisions. Both papers used collaborative contexts but are different from ours in terms of the experimental game as well as the intent of the research, as they primarily focused on piracy rather than other contexts, online labor markets, or platforms. Furthermore, neither of these prior studies considered sanctions or rewards for the players but instead were concerned with the provision of information to individuals that referred to contribution behaviors.

To summarize how our work is different from the related literature, we emphasize that much of the existing economic contexts are concerned with peer sanctions and less concerned with rewards and other types of exogenous interventions. While there is a growing literature on IS contexts that address real-effort and free riding, there is still significant tension to be resolved due to the various contexts, motivations, and reliance on observational or field studies. Unlike many of the prior studies, our research draws upon both IS contexts and the experimental economics literatures to implement a controlled experiment that allowed us to test incentive mechanisms at individual and group levels using a collaborative task. Accordingly, we draw upon the literature related to the free-rider problem to establish the basis for our experiment and treatment design.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

The primary mechanism described by the literature for empirically studying the free-rider problem is the public goods game (see e.g., Andreoni, 1988; Grossman & Hart, 1980; Groves & Ledyard, 1977; Samuelson, 1954).<sup>5</sup> We draw upon this literature because it is a clear foundation for understanding free riding and has been previously applied in other real-effort contexts. In the standard public goods game implemented by many experimental economics studies, the players independently choose how many tokens in their private endowment to contribute to a public account (i.e., to fund the public good). The sum of tokens in the public account is multiplied by a factor and the new total is then treated as the payoff that is split among the players (i.e., as positive utility derived from the public good). The game is designed to test the free-riding problem because it provides no incentive to contribute to the public account, assuming rational self-interested players. That is, players are strictly better off by deviating to a lower contribution level than the other players in their group.

The total utility for the group is maximized when all players in the group contribute all of their endowment to the best of their ability to the public account, which is costly for the individual. However, the standard prediction of Nash equilibrium play requires that we assume that individuals are rational and selfish and care only about their own monetary payoff. An individual under these assumptions is strictly better off by free riding on the effort of other players and will always contribute nothing to the public good (Fehr & Gächter, 2000). In traditional public goods games concerned with contributing tokens, the free-riding prediction remains valid any time the individual benefits from holding their endowment as a private investment rather than contributing to the group account.

Pure Nash equilibrium play is rarely observed in a standard single-period public goods experiment, as players tend to contribute something to the good. That is, free riding is typically minimal, and players tend to contribute to the good at levels halfway between the Pareto efficient level and the free-riding level (Marwell & Ames, 1981). However, in iterated or repeat-play games, it is typical to observe a decay in cooperation as the contribution to the good declines over time; thus, a gradual decline in contributions toward Nash equilibrium play is observed (e.g., Andreoni, 1995; Chaudhuri, 2011; Isaac et al., 1985; Isaac & Walker, 1988; Levitt & List, 2007). Furthermore, the introduction of endogenously determined punishments (i.e., sanctions) and rewards to players has been shown to increase cooperation and reduce free-riding behavior in certain conditions (e.g., Andreoni et al., 2003; Sefton et al., 2007).

## Hypotheses

The early research on free-riding in experiments found that decreasing (increasing) the payoff for players resulted in more (less) free-riding (Isaac & Walker, 1988). Follow-up work has manipulated payoff functions in a heterogeneous manner within groups of players and shown similar results. To be precise, those players with lower potential payoffs contribute less and those with higher potential payoffs contribute more (Fisher et al., 1995). Both studies assigned payoff functions to players at the start of the experiment, rather than having the payoff functions assigned as per an exogenously enforced reward/sanction in response to player behavior. Fischbacher et al. (2014) conducted a public goods experiment with heterogeneous payoff functions and a possible uncertainty in payoff. The authors predicted and found that treatments with higher potential payoffs should result in higher contributions in comparison to lower potential payoffs resulting in lower contributions. They also found that heterogeneous payoff functions between players do not necessarily affect conditional cooperation. Therefore, on aggregate, contributions are similar to those for players in groups with homogeneous payoff functions. In contrast to our paper, Fischbacher et al. (2014) assigned heterogeneity of payoff functions based upon a treatment selection and/or by the roll of a die after playing the game (i.e., uncertainty), rather than using any kind of carryover of behavior from one stage to another.

As regards our collaborative image tagging experiment, we implemented sanctions and rewards exogenously, which is significantly different than the prior literature. The design choice allowed us to model the features of online labor markets specifically, and information systems in general, by providing the ability of the platform to monitor and exogenously apply interventions. We used free-riding behavior (i.e., effort) in the first stage of our experiment to determine which player(s) the system would sanction or reward in a future stage of the experiment. For example, imagine a user free riding on others’ performance on a collaborative task in an online labor market or contributing low-quality effort that decreases the overall value derived from the collaboration. The online labor market operators can monitor the user and then punish the user with surprise loss in payoff due to their prior performance.

To summarize the findings of prior literature on free riding and interventions: under the presence of heterogeneous payoff functions within a group, the overall contribution behavior to a good or task should be similar to the contribution behavior for those groups with homogeneous payoff functions. That is, the predicted result of increasing a particular player’s payoff (i.e., rewarding a user) is an increase in the contribution of effort by that player (e.g., giving an online labor market participant a reward should result in increased utility for the contributor). Accordingly, the sum of the groups’ effort would also increase. Conversely, reducing a player’s payoff is expected to reduce that player’s contribution of effort (e.g., sanctioning a free rider from the online labor market should result in a reduction in effort by that free rider) and also reduce the sum of the groups’ effort. Therefore, the standard prediction of equilibrium play and the findings from the prior literature can be extrapolated to the scenario where sanctions and rewards are applied at either the individual or group level. Specifically, this prediction leads to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: In a group of online labor market workers, punishing (rewarding) an individual worker will result in a lower (higher) level of effort by that worker.

Hypothesis 2: In a group of online labor market workers, punishing (rewarding), the entire group will result in a lower (higher) level of effort by each worker in the group.

In addition to the hypotheses derived from the economics literature, we also considered the effects of context on behavioral outcomes. Note that we are only concerned with observing treatment effects that result from extrinsic factors. Our experiment does not account for or directly manipulate intrinsic factors. To test the predicted hypotheses and explore these issues, we introduced a payoff intervention that combined the two factors of interest (i.e., sanction/reward; individual/group) midway through the multi-round experiment.

## Experiment Design, Procedures, and Treatments

Our adaptation of the public goods game framework allowed us to explore how incentives (sanctions and rewards) can be used to influence effort in a collaborative online labor market. Our experiment differs from prior real-effort and public goods game implementations through the use of exogenously applied sanctions and rewards at the individual or group level (i.e., symmetric versus asymmetric payoff functions). These exogenous manipulations proxied the potential policies and actions that online labor market operators may implement using their information system to incentivize workers. To the best of our knowledge, this is also the first public goods-type experiment that has been extended to a collaborative online labor market context and also the first to study the combination of two factors: (1) type of intervention (sanctions vs. rewards), and (2) asymmetric vs. symmetric payoff functions (i.e., manipulation at individual vs. group level). For an exposition on the setup of the traditional public good game, please see Davis and Holt (1993, ch. 3).

Our experiment was designed as a real-effort collaborative image tagging folksonomy. Groups of four participants were randomly and anonymously assigned by a computer using a partner-matching protocol established before the start of the first round (Andreoni & Croson, 2008; Croson, 1996). Therefore, participants remained in the same group for the entire session. The use of partners matching was required because of the two-stage design of our experiment. That is, the consequences of behavior from the first stage carried over to the second stage. The rematching of participants using a “strangers” protocol would not allow for sanctioning (rewarding) of low (high) effort, as the members of the groups would change over time. However, participants are never informed about who is in their group nor are they informed about the individual efforts of their group members.

Each individual player in our experiment, i, was given the task of tagging images with descriptive words. They worked in groups of four players but were not aware of their group member’s contributions. Individuals earned a payoff for their contribution to the group’s performance in tagging images. The goal was to tag images as compared to a well-established and objective benchmark. The closer a group’s tags matched the benchmark (i.e., similarity), the higher their potential payoff. Thus, the participants were incentivized to contribute value-adding effort to the group goal while also having ample opportunity for problematic, non-value-adding, free-riding behavior—vis-à-vis a public goods-type game. A worker’s payoff function can be represented as

$$
u _ {i} = a _ {i} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} g _ {j},
$$

where $0 \leq a _ { i } \leq 1$ is the payoff multiplier and g<sub>j</sub> is a contribution measurement for each worker in the group. An individual’s earnings are determined by summing the total contributed effort by all individuals in the group and multiplying the summed contributions by $a _ { i } ,$ which can be thought of as the marginal per capita return (MPCR; hereafter multiplier) from a traditional public goods game. Although our design of the payoff function follows from the prior literature (e.g., Faillo et al., 2013; Fehr & Gächter, 2000), our game differs in the manner in which a<sub>i</sub> is manipulated in each treatment during the latter part of the experiment to proxy online labor market interventions. Although our values for a<sub>i</sub> and $g _ { j }$ deviate from the standard game for the purposes of applying the game to a real context (see Table 2), the values are always constrained such that the standard prediction and therefore equilibrium play of the public goods game are not affected. Note that our treatment manipulations (i.e., possible heterogeneous values of a<sub>i</sub> under certain treatments) can result in asymmetric payoff functions for the players.

## Treatment Design

In our experiment, there were two factors of interest (1) the selection of the intervention as either a sanction (i.e., decrease in a<sub>i</sub>) or reward (i.e., increase in a<sub>i</sub>) and (2) the application of the intervention (sanction or reward) to either an individual or an entire group. The combination of these two factors and their corresponding levels created four treatment manipulations for our experiment. The four treatments proxied the interventions motivated in the Introduction: all increase, reward the entire group; all decrease, sanction the entire group; one increase, reward only an individual; and one decrease, sanction only an individual. Compared to the prior literature, our experiment and treatment design is most similar to Cadigan et al. (2011), and Dickinson (2001). Cadigan et al. (2011) also utilize a two-stage game where the first stage is conducted to establish a baseline of behavior. Subsequently, earnings from the first-stage carryover become either the endowment or the groups’ multiplier in the second stage, depending on the treatment condition. Like Cadigan et al. (2011), we also carryover the behavior from the first stage of our game to identify the highest and lowest performers and then apply sanctions or rewards in the second stage of the game. However, in contrast to their study, we considered individual-level and group-level incentives, and since we were looking at effort in a collaborative image tagging context, we did not consider or endogenously manipulate endowments. Therefore, our design more closely emulates realworld performance concerns and effort in online labor markets and platforms. Dickinson (2001) employs a within-subject design and participants are assigned heterogeneous endowments by the experimenter. From there, they focus on individual incentives (bonuses or deductions of tokens) but do not consider group-level impacts or manipulation of the payoff multiplier. Dickinson’s experimental design also provides immediate feedback regarding high and low contribution amounts and establishes undesirable focal points (i.e., anchors) and competition for contribution behavior. We further differentiate our paper from the others because our carryover of sanctions and rewards is unknown to the players. That is, the players do not explicitly know that their payoff multiplier may change in a future stage based upon individual behavior; thus the first-stage behaviors observed in our experimental design are likely to represent the participants’ true strategies. Therefore, the participants’ strategies in our experiment should be free of priming, endogenously enforced consequences, platform-determined heterogeneous endowments, effort focal points, and competition.

Therefore, we integrated the idea of “exogenously enforced” sanctions and rewards with the possibility for heterogeneous payoff multipliers within a group (unknown to the players). This unique aspect of our experiment is particularly relevant in the context of collaborative tasks in online labor markets and other environments where an information system plays a central role in the collaboration of labor. Information systems, information services, software, and platforms provide owners with new capabilities to act as virtual social planners and the option to apply exogenously enforced sanctions and rewards.

## Experiment Procedure

At the beginning of the experiment, the participants were informed they would work through 10 rounds of image tagging tasks. Pictures chosen for the tagging tasks were purposefully complex and ambiguous, such as cityscapes and nature scenes.<sup>7</sup> This was done for two reasons. First, complex images are harder to tag with descriptive words and thus effort could be more easily observed through the number of tags contributed. Second, ambiguous images have no clear correct tags, which makes the work task more complicated and thus realistic and open to free riding on effort. If the images had been simple pictures of known animals or objects, the effort required in tagging would be minimal and the potential for observing changes in free-riding behavior would be low.

Participants always knew their payoff function, payoff multipliers, a<sub>i</sub>, and the multiplier for the group. In each round, they were provided with an individual similarity score, group similarity score, and payoff for the round. Similarity scores measured how closely the tags created by the individuals and group matched a performance benchmark and are used as g<sub>j</sub> in the payoff function. The calculation of the similarity scores is discussed below. Following five rounds of image tagging and feedback on similarity scores, we randomly and exogenously introduced sanctions or rewards at the group or individual levels, as defined by our treatments, and measured changes in effort for an additional five rounds of image tagging. At the conclusion of the experiment, the computer running the experiment randomly chose two of the 10 rounds and credited each participant with their average payoff over these rounds using a conversion rate of 20 points per US\$1.00 (participants were aware of this ahead of time, as it was covered in the instructions). Randomly selecting rounds for payment avoids wealth effects because the earnings from each round are valued independently of each other. As such, participants are incentivized to make economically rational decisions throughout the entire experiment, reducing the possibility for accumulated wealth to affect decisions in later rounds.

Prior to the start of each session, image order was randomized by the computer for each group (i.e., individuals in a group saw the same 10 images in the same order, but the order was randomized across groups). In each round, a single image was presented to a group and each participant was asked to individually tag images with descriptive words. There was a minimum of one tag required and no upper limit to the number of tags a participant could assign to an image. There was no time limit for tagging images. Similar to what might be expected in a real, collaborative, online labor market where individuals provide work effort toward a common goal, participants in the experimental groups viewed the same images at the same time but were not allowed to communicate or cooperate in any manner. The word tags from each participant in a group were then combined into a single master group tag list for each image, with duplicate tags removed (i.e., if two participants in a group tagged an image with the same word, only one instance of the word was added to the group tag list). Each individual worker’s tag list and the group’s combined tag list for each image were compared to the set of image labels (tags) generated for the image by the pretrained Google Cloud Vision API.<sup>8</sup>

The Google Cloud Vision API provides image labels along with confidence estimates from 0-100% that represent the accuracy of the labels. We used 70% as a stopping point for label confidence generated by the API to keep consistency between the labels for the various images used in the experiment. The group’s potential payoff for each image round was calculated based on the similarity of their group tag list to the Google Cloud Vision API tag list. The more similar the group’s tags for an image were to the Google API tags, the higher their payoff for that image. Accordingly, our design incentivized group members to contribute useful tags toward a common group goal while still allowing for free riding to occur. Free riding was possible because the payoff was based on combined similarity of the group’s total tag list in comparison to the Google API tag list, and poor individual tagging effort could be offset by the tagging effort of the group. In other words, an individual could reduce their effort (i.e., free ride) but still receive an acceptable payoff due to the group’s effort.

We measured the similarity between a group’s tag list and the Google Cloud Vision API image labels using pretrained word vectors from the Stanford Global Vectors for Word Representation (GloVe) project (Pennington et al., 2014). Word embeddings are natural language processing techniques used to map words and phrases to vectors of real numbers. Real number vector representations enable automated comparisons of words and phrases for tasks such as similarity calculation, similar word identification, sentiment analysis, and syntactic parsing (e.g., Socher et al., 2013). GloVe is one of the most used word embedding techniques and uses unsupervised learning to obtain vector representations for words. GloVe models are trained on aggregated global word-word co-occurrence statistics from a corpus, which results in representations that showcase interesting linear substructures of the word vector space (Pennington et al., 2014). We used a model of GloVe word vectors that were pretrained on the Wikipedia 2014 Corpus and the English Gigaword (5th ed.) with the gensim Python package for natural language processing.<sup>9</sup>

To calculate similarity, each word tag in a tag list for an image was converted into a fifty-dimension vector of numerical embedding values using GloVe. Then an average (mean of the individual word vectors) was taken of all words in the tag list for the image. This resulted in a single fifty-dimension vector of the average word embedding values for the set of words in the tag list. For each image, a vector of average embedding values was calculated for the group’s combined tag list, each individual’s tag list, and the corresponding image tags generated from Google Cloud Vision API. We calculated a cosine similarity score between the groups’ average tag vector and the Google Cloud Vision API average tag vector for each image (Mikolov et al., 2013).

Figure 1 provides a graphical representation of the process we used to calculate similarity. In Step 1a, individuals independently tagged an image, which was then added to a group tag list. Step 1b had already been completed using the Google Cloud Vision API by the experiment administrator to create the system’s benchmark word list. Next, Step 2a created 50-dimension word vectors for each group’s tags as well as the Cloud Vision tag list, with averages of the vectors calculated in Step 2b. Finally, in Step 3, cosine similarity was calculated for each group to provide a word vector similarity score. The group’s similarity vector was then used to calculate group payoffs, as described next.

This similarity score for each group $( 0 \leq s _ { j } \leq 1 )$ was the basis for the group $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ potential payoff for each image. Note that we use the group similarity score as the aggregate performance measure of the group, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { s _ { j } = \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } g _ { j } } \end{array}$ . The actual payoff for an individual worker for an image is their payoff multiplier for that image, $a _ { i } .$ , multiplied by their group’s similarity score, $s _ { j } \colon \pi _ { i } = a _ { i } s _ { j }$

Higher similarity scores thus resulted in higher payoffs (presented as points), which incentivized participants to contribute to the group by providing accurate, descriptive tags for images while also allowing for free riding on the efforts of the rest of the group. We implemented an initial baseline multiplier, $a _ { i } ^ { 0 } = 9 0 \forall i ,$ , before any treatment manipulations were introduced. Although the payoff function is simplified, the product for the term $a _ { i } s _ { j }$ is consistent with the requirements of the multiplier and group account as defined by the public goods game after the conversion rate from points to cash is included. That is, individuals can maximize their utility by simply free riding on the efforts of others while expending little effort.

We tested five treatments that differed by the multiplier assigned to the participants during the second stage of the experiment, as detailed in Table 1. Treatment manipulations allowed for the heterogenous setting of $a _ { i } = \{ 6 5 , 9 0 , 1 1 5 \}$ , depending on treatment assignment.

At the beginning of Round 6, all participants in all treatments (including the control group) were unexpectedly halted by the computer and shown a message corresponding to their treatment. The message notified the participants of an exogenously applied intervention in their payoff functions, implemented as a multiplier change due to the behaviors of one of their group members. That is, the multiplier change was a consequence of behavior and was interpreted as a reward or punishment by those affected (i.e., the computer ranked the similarity scores by individual for the prior five rounds to identify the best or worst individual at image tagging in each group). The participants were never ex ante told if or when the multiplier would change prior to receiving the message, or if it would change again during the experiment.

## Sample and Process for Treatment Assignment

The experiment was conducted at Emory University in the United States during the fall of 2020. The experiment was computerized using the oTree open-source software platform (Chen et al., 2016). oTree offers a Django and Python webbased application framework and allows for simple integration with GloVe and gensim. Participants were recruited from an undergraduate behavioral lab in the business school and a total of 184 individuals participated in our experimental sessions. In addition to lab credit for their participation, students earned an additional cash payment ranging from \$0-\$5 US dollars, as described earlier.<sup>10</sup> The payoff function discussed above was used to determine the amount earned by students for participating in the study.

Each session consisted of a single group of four participants, with each participant accessing the web-based experimental system from a remote location (due to COVID-19 restrictions). Note, the decision to limit sessions to a single group was a practical one to ensure the reliability of our study. The participants moved simultaneously through each round of the experiment; thus, any technical issue experienced by a single participant (e.g., internet connectivity, dead laptop battery) could lead to a failed session for all participants.

To ensure a random assignment of participants to sessions, we used the following process. (1) Participants signed in using an online form immediately prior to the start of the experiment. (2) The sign-in process implemented a computer-based randomizer to shuffle the signed-in participants and assign each of them to a session. (3) Any participants who were not selected for a session were invited to return to a later sign-in. (4) Individuals participated in only one session (and therefore treatment) for this study. (5) Treatment assignment was randomly determined by the oTree program, based upon a random draw done by the software. Overall, our procedures ensured that it was impossible for the experiment administrator to bias the session assigned to each participant, or bias the treatment assigned to each session.

It is also important to note that participants were aware of the integrity standards required by the behavioral lab and were required by their code of conduct to take the study seriously. As will be discussed in the results, we are confident that the procedures we implemented ensured complete randomization and control of the experiment. The breakdown of participants to treatments is as follows: 44 participants in all increase, 44 participants in all decrease, 36 participants in one increase, 40 participants in one decrease, and 20 participants in control. Copies of the experiment instructions were provided electronically to each participant.<sup>11</sup> If participants had questions, they were able to reach the experiment administrator at any time during the experiment. Each session lasted approximately 45 minutes and participants earned \$3.69 dollars, on average, in addition to lab credit. All participants were shown their earnings privately and individually at the conclusion of the experiment. Participants were privately paid using electronic funds transfer by the coordinator of the behavioral lab.

## Results

We validated the random assignment of participants to treatments by comparing contributions prior to our manipulation of the earnings multiplier. We first discuss our aggregate results and then present our between-subject analyses to identify treatment effects before and after the interventions were applied. Next, we present our withinsubject analyses to determine the impact of the message and the associated changes to the multiplier on contribution behavior. Finally, we present subsample within-subject analyses to understand the effects of sanctions and rewards on individuals who were and were not affected by a change in the multiplier.

## Aggregate Results

Both individual similarity scores (as compared to the Google Vision API) and effort, measured as number of tags contributed per image, are statistically indistinguishable between any of the treatments in Round 1 (Kruskal-Wallis tests: quality [word vector similarity], χ<sup>2</sup> = 5.17, p = 0.27; effort [tag count], $\chi ^ { 2 } = 7 . 3 2 , p = 0 . 1 2 )$ . Regarding participant demographics, we did not find a statistical difference between any of the treatments for either age or gender<sup>12</sup> (Kruskal-Wallis tests: age, χ<sup>2</sup> = 5.27, p = 0.26; gender, χ<sup>2</sup> = 2.29, p = 0.68). Therefore, we are confident in the control established by the random assignment of participants to each treatment and the overall setup of our experiment because Round 1 measures and participant demographics are statistically indistinguishable between each of the treatments. Aggregate results for demographics and tag count are shown in Table 3.

In line with our hypotheses, we focused our analysis on the changes in effort contributed by each worker because of treatment interventions. We measured effort by counting the number of tags that individuals used to describe each image.

By design, we incentivized workers with a payoff based on similarity to the Google Vision API and not on the number of tags contributed. We used this approach because a focus on similarity requires the participant to expend effort through individual thought, especially because each additional tag is created by the participant. Conversely, had we only incentivized tag count, the payoff function would have been easily manipulated by the workers, with little expended effort in terms of individual thought. For example, workers could have manipulated the output by entering many nonsensical words as tags, which would have resulted in higher payoffs. Therefore, by using tag similarity as the incentive, effort can be more easily measured in terms of the number of tags contributed without introducing an endogenous incentive to game the system.

We also note that the control treatment appeared to be affected, on average, by path dependency in the early rounds (as shown in Figure 2); therefore, the average effort is not directly comparable pre-intervention to the other treatments in the between-subject analysis. However, we do believe that control still serves as a useful point of comparison for the within-subject analysis, as will be discussed later in the section.

We conducted random effects panel data regressions to explore the results of our experiment, as shown in Table 4. Random effects models are appropriate for our analysis due to the panel nature of our data (i.e., multiple participants with multiple observations per participant over time). Also, random effects are appropriate for our analysis because the participants were randomly selected from the population and randomly assigned to treatments, and our controlled laboratory experiment design limits the impact of omitted variables. In other words, the individual-specific effects are uncorrelated with the predictor variables in our regression model (i.e., treatment assignment), which warrants a random effects model. Our use of a between-subject design resulted in treatment effects being perfectly collinear with participant fixed effects. Furthermore, given the controlled experiment methodology used, our data did not suffer from those types of biases of concern common in many secondary data analyses. The regressions also included a round fixed effect to account for time effects such as learning. The robustness of the result comparing effort (tag count) between treatments for Rounds 1-5 were confirmed by the random effects model (with the exception of control as mentioned earlier), and we could not distinguish pre-treatment contribution levels between treatments for Rounds 1-5.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Overall, our real-effort collaborative image tagging experiment provides an interesting perspective on the implications of the interventions suggested in our research. Interventions exogenously applied on either the group or individual levels, improved effort in our experiment, in comparison to doing nothing at all. On average, the interventions applied at the group level outperformed interventions applied at the individual level. Not surprisingly, rewarding all (all increase) was the best motivator and resulted in significant increases in effort postintervention in comparison to the other treatments. In contrast to the first result, we were quite surprised that sanctioning all (all decrease) would be a successful motivator for participants. Countering the theoretical predictions of reduced effort due to reduced incentives, participants in the all decrease treatment significantly increased their effort post-intervention. Regarding the individual interventions, rewarding/sanctioning an individual had a similar effect, on average, in both treatments, leading to significant increases in effort postintervention. Looking deeper into the effects of the individual interventions, sanctioning appeared to significantly motivate the manipulated participants, with individuals in the other subsamples only slightly increasing their effort, on average. The last result contradicts what was predicted because an overall lower multiplier (one decrease) should have resulted in lower effort in comparison to an overall higher multiplier (one increase).

## General Implications for Policy and Practice

Among all the conditions, the all decrease treatment provided the most controversial incentive strategy for affecting behavior in a real-effort, collaborative online labor market. It provided a nonobvious result leading to a significant improvement in effort (although the response is tempered in comparison to the all increase treatment) and thus a reduction in potential free riding on others’ effort. Nash equilibrium behavior would predict that with no intervention, all players would eventually free ride, expending minimal effort in all treatments (we did observe in our control treatment that individuals did not increase their effort over time). Similarly, in the one decrease treatment, we observed sanctioned individuals significantly increasing their effort postintervention. Additionally, the rest of the group, on average, did not significantly decrease their behavior compared to the first five rounds. In other words, punishing only the worst individual appears to correct their behavior and also sends a signal to the rest of the group.

Punishing only the worst individuals may be the best strategy for reducing value-harming behaviors in online labor markets, especially in those cases when such individuals can be identified and dealt with by the information system. Further, public awareness of these sanctions also seems to be a good deterrent to preventing other users from decreasing their efforts. This result has interesting policy implications for the management of information goods, information services, and online platforms that suffer from problematic contribution behaviors. Indeed, incorporating mechanisms that monitor performance and behaviors in information systems and online labor markets would seem to be a straightforward task.

The all increase condition also provides evidence that rewarding everyone for their effort leads to further increases in effort over time, which aligns with the results from prior literature on incentivizing online reviews. However, our results suggest that it is better to apply rewards to all group members and not to individual participants. Interestingly, the one increase condition had counterintuitive implications. Although, on average, we observed an increase in effort, the mechanism of only rewarding the best effort may signal to the receiver of the reward that they are doing too much, leading them to maintain rather than continue to improve their efforts.

In general, information systems owners should be aware that participant effort can be affected through proper interventions, akin to how government regulation and taxation are often used to curb undesirable behavior. It should be noted, however, that not all incentives are applicable to all organizations or contexts, and the incentives may not be cost neutral. Additional potential consequences should be considered depending on the specific situation. Given the nature of information systems and online labor markets, system owners have the ability to act as social planners in these environments by monitoring undesirable behavior and dynamically implementing regulations via user rewards and sanctions as required to improve outcomes.

## Limitations and Future Research

A potential limitation of this work is that we utilized a controlled randomized experiment, which is a simplification of a real-effort, collaborative online labor market. Further, because the game is an application of a particular type of online labor market, not all interventions may be applicable to other contexts, and some contexts may be driven by intrinsic and extrinsic factors. However, the simplified model allowed us to draw upon economic theory and identify the potential underlying mechanisms driving behavior, free of potentially confounding factors. Thus, simplification allowed us to directly assign experimental manipulations to the associated results, thus preventing potential unobserved biases.

Since we observed different behaviors between the sanctioned and rewarded individuals and their nonsanctioned and nonrewarded group counterparts, the expected change in total group welfare is nonobvious. Therefore, one potential extension of this work would be to explore how group size impacts the effects of sanctions and rewards. Additionally, platform operators should consider the trade-offs between monetary worker costs and dataset value as they seek to maximize their return on investment (ROI) when hiring workers in the labor market. Given that we used a controlled lab experiment, it is impossible for us to make specific recommendations in that regard, but we acknowledge that some incentive schemes may not be ideal due to ROI concerns. Prior research has shown that the group size does not typically change contribution behavior, except for in very large groups in laboratory experiments (Isaac et al., 1994). However, prior research has not considered sanctions and rewards applied asymmetrically within the group. We fixed the group size at four in this study; larger or smaller groups may have different behavioral dynamics with asymmetric sanctions and rewards. Similarly, the sizes of the sanction and reward, in terms of both absolute size and their size relative to payoffs, are likely to impact changes in nondesirable behavior. A future experiment could utilize treatments that vary group, sanction, and reward sizes as well as payoff synchronicity.

Another extension would be to identify the behavioral mechanisms driving decisions when sanctioned or rewarded, such as spite, reciprocity, warm glow, and others.

## Conclusion

The general implication of the study is that how and to whom an intervention aimed at affecting value-decreasing behavior is applied has a significant impact on the resulting behavior. Our paper provides initial insights into how exogenously applied sanctions and rewards impact contribution behavior at the individual and group levels in the context of collaborative image tagging. The application of interventions to a contextspecific online labor market also illustrates that interventions are successful at influencing behavior in nonobvious ways, thereby assisting in the mitigation of the free-rider problem. Accordingly, online labor market operators could utilize our insights to positively affect the growing need for accurate and reliable datasets for training AI, ML, and other types of social classification systems. Overall, information systems and online platforms such as online labor markets provide unique capabilities for monitoring behavior and exogenously implementing policies and interventions to address problems.

## Image Tagging Experiment Instructions

Thank you for participating in this experiment. You will earn credit for your participation. In addition to credit, you may also earn a variable cash bonus of up to US\$5.75. The amount of cash you earn depends on the decisions that you and other participants make in individua rounds. All cash earned in this experiment will be presented to you as points. Your final points amount will be determined by two random draws done by the computer at the conclusion of the experiment. Each draw will correspond to one round of the experimental session. The average points amount over these two randomly selected rounds will be used to calculate your final earned cash. The conversion rate of points to cash is 20 points = US\$1.00.

You will never be asked to reveal your identity to anyone during the experiment. You should never offer to reveal your identity to anyone during the experiment. Your name will never be associated with any of your decisions. In order to keep your decisions private, please do not reveal your choices or your identity to any other participant.

You are welcome to ask questions at any time by emailing {email address}. While the experiment is in progress, please do not communicate with other participants. Please do not communicate with others after the experiment. This is important to the validity of the study.

## Specific Guidelines

You will participate in 10 rounds in a group with three other participants (i.e., groups of 4). You will not know who is in your group. In each round you and your group members will be presented with the same image on your screens. You will then describe the image using words, with each word separated by a space. You and every member in your group will individually decide how to describe the image. All decisions are made simultaneously and without communication. No other group member will ever know what you specifically wrote as your image description. They will only know the combined image description for your group.

Your points earnings in each round are determined by comparing the usefulness of the combined image descriptions for your group in comparison with image descriptions provided by Google's automated image analysis software. Image description usefulness is calculated by the computer using automated machine-learning statistical analysis. The earnings equation is presented below.

## Your Earnings this Round = Your Earnings Multiplier \* Your GROUP Usefulness Score

The points earned depend on your group's combined image usefulness, having a calculated range from 0 to 1. The points are determined by multiplying the image usefulness for your group by an earnings multiplier. So, if the earnings multiplier is 90, and your group usefulness score is 0.85, you will have earned 90 \* 0.85 = 76.5 points in that round.

You will always know the earnings multiplier before describing the image presented to you and your group. Unless otherwise specified, all players in your group will always have the same earnings multiplier as you. Please stay on task during the experiment. You and your group are working together, in real time.

## Examples:

• If your group usefulness score for a round is 0.60 and the earnings multiplier is 90, then you will have earned 54 points.

• If your average points earned for the two randomly chosen rounds is 80 points, you will divide that amount of points by 20 to earn \$4.00 of bonus cash plus credit for your participation in the experiment.

• If I am presented with an image of a golden retriever dog sitting with their family, I might describe that image with words such as: dog family human golden pet. There is a minimum of one word and no maximum number of useful words that you can choose to describe the image.
