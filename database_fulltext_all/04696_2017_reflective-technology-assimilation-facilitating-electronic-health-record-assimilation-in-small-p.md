# Reflective Technology Assimilation: Facilitating Electronic Health Record Assimilation in Small Physician Practices

AARON BAIRD, ELIZABETH DAVIDSON, AND LARS MATHIASSEN

AARON BAIRD (abaird@gsu.edu; corresponding author) is an Assistant Professor in the Robinson College of Business at Georgia State University with a primary appointment to the Institute of Health Administration and a secondary appointment to the Department of CIS. His research primarily focuses on health information technology adoption, assimilation, use, and related outcomes. He has served on the Arizona and Georgia health-related Chapter Boards, has collaborated with a variety of industry partners including the Mayo Clinic, and has published in Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Research, European Journal of Information Systems, and others.

ELIZABETH DAVIDSON (edavidso@hawaii.edu) is W. Ruel Johnson Professor of Information Technology Management at the Shidler College of Business, University of Hawai‘i. She has studied the diffusion and assimilation of health information technologies such as electronic health-record systems and personal health records using field study, action research, survey and discourse analysis methods. Her research work has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Information and Organization, and elsewhere. She currently serves as Editor in Chief of Information & Organization, and has served as Senior Editor for JAIS and Senior Associate Editor for EJIS in the past.

LARS MATHIASSEN (lmathiassen@ceprin.org) is Georgia Research Alliance Eminent Scholar, Professor at the Computer Information Systems Department, and cofounder of the Center for Process Innovation at Georgia State University. His research focuses on development of software and information services, on IT-enabled innovation of business processes, and on management and facilitation of organizational change processes. He has published extensively in major information systems and software engineering journals and has coauthored several books. He has served as senior editor for MIS Quarterly, and serves as senior editor for Information and Organization and for the Journal of Information Technology.

ABSTRACT: Effectively incorporating complex technology into organizational practices requires moving beyond initial plateaus of postadoption usage. However, achieving this is especially challenging for small businesses because competing priorities in addition to resource and attention constraints may lead to technology assimilation satisficing. With this context in mind, we conducted a four-year action research project in which we supported electronic health record (EHR) assimilation in 10 small physician practices by facilitating reflections to help these small organizations move beyond an initial plateau of postadoption EHR usage. Drawing from these results, we theorize reflective technology assimilation (RTA) as an emergent process of facilitating deeper learning and reflection within and across small resource- and attention-constrained organizations to enable ongoing technology assimilation. In addition, we articulate the foundational principles for reflective action research (RAR) as an approach in which researchers facilitate reflective problem solving while advancing theoretical knowledge.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: action research, community-of-practice, electronic health record (EHR), learning, reflective practice, technology assimilation.

To effectively assimilate information systems (IS) in organizational contexts, practitioners must integrate feature use into revised work practices in ways that enhance outcomes for themselves and related beneficiaries. This process requires dedicating resources and attention to deliberate learning in order to effectively balance system capabilities, individual needs, and contextual requirements (e.g., [7, 27, 28, 53]). What often happens, however, is that practitioners assimilate such technologies simply to the point of “good enough,” or satisficing [75] when learning new capabilities [80, 85]. Prior assimilation research suggests that assimilating beyond this plateau, so as to more fully realize benefits, requires significant organizational resources and attention, particularly through strong leadership, champions, end-user training, and support resources (e.g., [7, 18, 27]). In resource- and attention-constrained organizations, day-to-day operations that keep the business running typically take precedence over fully assimilating technology, however. This is especially true for small organizations, which have few staff or financial resources to dedicate to the learning and experimentation needed to move beyond an assimilation plateau [18]. In this study, we ask: When resource and attention constraints lead organizations to satisficing behavior, can reflections on available features, work practice revisions, and their implications for values that govern technology assimilation help move practitioners beyond an assimilation plateau?

We examine this question in the context of assimilation of electronic health records (EHRs) by small physician practices. Health-care policymakers have long advocated for the widespread use of EHRs to benefit health-care delivery systems (e.g., [14, 38, 49, 67]). However, assimilation of EHRs into small physician practices is challenging, especially when resources and attention are scarce [19, 45, 48, 62]. Large health-care organizations might overcome such challenges by taking advantage of network resources, administrative staff, training, management support, and incentives to mediate EHR assimilation [8, 18, 20, 67]. However, small physician practices by their very nature are constrained by staff, attention, and time, limiting their ability to commit the resources needed to effectively assimilate an EHR [19, 71]. EHRs, designed to document patient care information and to enable data sharing between clinicians [1, 54, 65], can disrupt work practices to such a degree that productivity drops significantly with initial use [45, 62]. In the United States, the lack of resources in small physician practices is a primary constraint in EHR assimilation [36, 37]. Therefore, EHR use in these practices has historically been low [30, 41], and assimilation of EHRs by smaller practices does not often lead to full use of EHRs [60]. Even with the provision of external resources, such as funding or training, the physicians themselves must devote their own time and attention needed to assimilate these complex technologies.

To address the challenges of EHR assimilation in small health-care practices, we drew on reflective learning theory [4, 5, 73, 74] to conduct an action research project [12, 13, 47, 61] in which we designed and facilitated social contexts for physicians and their staffs to address learning-related EHR assimilation barriers. The action research project incorporated the dual goals of assisting small primary care physician practices with assimilating EHRs beyond their initial plateau of use, while also generating contributions to knowledge on technology assimilation and action research through our sustained engagement with these practitioners. To do so, we examine how reflection can advance technology assimilation in resource- and attention-constrained organizations and articulate how reflective action research can address problem solving and theory development in such settings.

## The Problem Setting

The action research project Bridging the IT Adoption Gap for Small Physician Practices was funded and chartered by a charitable foundation in 2008, and interventions were conducted from October 2009 until December 2013. This project focused on designing and facilitating social contexts in which physicians could actively learn about and reflect on their EHR use in order to more effectively integrate EHR capabilities into their clinical practices and office workflows. The research team recruited a group of 10 physician owners of small practice organizations that had adopted and were using an EHR software package. Participants included the physicians, their office staffs, an EHR consultant, and three researchers over the course of the project. Physicians participated in bimonthly joint workshop meetings as well as training and consulting sessions with the EHR consultant in their own offices. The second author served as the principal investigator for the grant, project director, and researcher throughout the interventions. The first and third authors joined the research project to advance data analysis and theory development.

The action research project operated in parallel with, but independently from, the U.S. government’s Meaningful Use (MU) program, which was intended to incentivize physicians to adopt EHRs [14]. The federal MU program began providing financial incentives in 2011 to physicians who adopted a certified EHR and used the EHR in specified ways (see Online Appendix A for details). The MU program incentives heightened interest among the participating physicians in advancing use of their own EHRs. However, in contrast to the MU program, the action research project [12, 13, 47, 61] was aimed broadly at assisting these small organizations to advance assimilation beyond their initial EHR postadoption usage plateaus through systematic reflections within and across their practices. The MU program afforded the action researchers an opportunity to examine the role of regulation and incentives on physicians’ assimilation of EHRs, through their reflections and assimilation actions related to MU program criteria and thus to theorize how such regulations can contribute to illusionary technology diffusion [29] and “click-based” EHR adoption benchmarks [45] (see Online Appendixes A and B for further details).

## Theoretical Background

## Technology Assimilation

Technology assimilation relates to the process in which organizations become aware of, adopt, deploy, and incorporate organizational technologies into their practices [27]. IS scholars are increasingly examining factors that influence organizational adoption of health information technology (IT) (e.g., [10, 66]). Beyond initial adoption and deployment stages, assimilation requires a deliberate and ongoing learning and experimentation process for an organization to fully realize the anticipated benefits of an IS [56]. In health-care settings, this process has implications for effective communication among clinicians and thus patient outcomes [1, 54, 65]. Assimilation is therefore closely associated with organizational learning theories and constructs including absorptive capacity [54, 72], sensemaking [81], practice theory [25, 63], and mindfulness [52]. Such learning-based research has shown that initial assimilation learning processes typically lead to routine technology use, sometimes followed by shorter periods of exploration, which then lead to further routine use (e.g., [80]).

Assimilation research conducted within large organizations, such as multinational firms (e.g., [63] and [42]) and companies with hundreds or thousands of employees (e.g., [7, 28, 53]), has demonstrated that organizations with significant resources can leverage deliberate learning approaches in order to facilitate IS assimilation. However, such technology assimilation studies are premised on the availability of sufficient organizational resources and attention to commit to understanding system features and modifying organizational practices. Following context theorists [39, 44, 68], we argue that technology assimilation in the context of small organizations presents different and highly salient challenges. In particular, small organizations face trade-offs that limit their ability to dedicate or reallocate resources and attention specifically to technology assimilation, for example, conducting leadership commitment and oversight, hiring consultants, attending extensive training, or achieving economies of scale and scope by standardizing across organizational activities [28, 79, 82]. Further, small organizations often have a centralized governance structure, whereby one proprietor or a few partners make all major decisions while also running day-to-day business operations [76, 82]. Trading time and attention needed to operate the business and generate revenue in order to attend to technology assimilation is risky, as the latter does not always immediately result in positive financial or productive outcomes [80]. Even if financial resources are available to delegate assimilation tasks to outside consultants, this approach may not be feasible, especially when the technology is core to revenue-generating operational activities [19, 71]. Small physician practices face this dilemma with EHR assimilation in that physicians are essential to determining how EHRs will be used in their own and their staff’s activities. However, spending time on assimilation can be costly as the physicians generate the majority of their organization’s revenue on time spent seeing and treating patients.

Given these contextual constraints, assimilation processes in small organizations often move rapidly toward the goal of good enough [43, 85] or satisficing [75] when incorporating technology features into existing practices. A small organization may simply integrate the technology as quickly as possible with limited changes to practices [43, 60]. Such levels of use may be considered effective [16] if they meet a basic goal of getting the technology up and running, or enhanced [9] if additional useful features are incorporated over time in organizational activities. However, further learning and assimilation often become constrained by habitual practices, even when an organization is faced with new or different stimuli [31, 43, 69], as individuals “tend to engage less frequently in reflective consideration of [the novel] behavior and rely instead on previous patterns of behavior to direct future behaviors” [43, p. 542].

## Reflective Learning

Facilitating technology assimilation beyond a postadoption usage plateau requires encouraging organization members to spend time evaluating their prior technology use assumptions and revising their related cognitions [43]. Accordingly, our development of RTA draws on: (1) Argyris and Schön’s theory of how action strategies and governing values influence work practices [4, 5], and (2) Schön’s reflective practitioner theory [73, 74].

Argyris and Schön suggest that action strategies reflect deeply rooted governing values embedded into each practitioner’s belief system and organizational context [4, 5]. In situations where an action strategy does not work, practitioners may substitute a new action strategy to address the breakdown in practice. Argyris and Schön argue that such instrumental substitution of action strategies arises from single-loop “problem-correction” learning, which provides an immediate solution without challenging the underlying values that govern actions. If governing values conflict with or undermine strategic goals, actions resulting from single-loop learning generally have limited effect on organizational outcomes. Accordingly, practitioners need to periodically question, and possibly revise, action strategies and governing values through double-loop learning. That is, practitioners must not only consider revised action strategies but also reflect on the underlying values that govern actions [2, p. 206]. This distinction between single-loop and double-loop learning has been applied to different professional practices, such as software development [59, 77] and nursing [34].

Schön further contrasts instrumental problem solving with reflection-in-action to address problematic situations that involve complexity, uncertainty, uniqueness, and value conflicts [73, 74]. Instrumental problem solving might apply to well-defined problems and highly routinized practices, but this approach often leads practitioners to manipulate situations of practice in ways that reinforce their existing repertoires of concepts and techniques. For instance, research has shown that IS use practices are often plagued by cycles of unproductive problem solving (e.g., [23]); therefore, practitioners may need to engage in problem setting before problem solving when assimilating complex organizational technologies. Problem setting entails explicit consideration of the implications of governing values on action, in that “we select what we will treat as the things of the situation, we set the boundaries of our attention to it, and we impose on it a coherence which allows us to say what is wrong and in what directions the situation needs to be changed” [74, p. 40]. Schön’s theory of the reflective practitioner has also been adopted in various disciplines such as IS development [51, 58] and nursing [70].

Drawing from these theoretical foundations, we investigate RTA as a process that: (1) supports and enables situated learning within a community of practitioners, (2) engages practitioners in reflection on conflicts and synergies in values governing technology assimilation, and (3) engenders ongoing emergent organizational capacity to incorporate useful technology features into existing or modified work practices.

## Research Design

## Reflective Action Research

Action research [12, 61] is particularly well-suited for addressing our project’s dual goals of helping physicians assimilate EHRs into their primary care practices and advancing scientific knowledge on technology assimilation in resource-constrained contexts. Therefore, we drew from extant literature to develop reflective action research as an approach to help the project’s participating physicians address the learning barriers created by resource and attention constraints within their small primary care practices.

Considering the diversity in action research approaches within the IS discipline, Baskerville and Wood-Harper [12] distinguish between three types of IS action research process models: linear, iterative, and reflective. Linear models offer a single sequence of activities, for example, engage, diagnose, unfreeze, change, freeze, and disengage. Iterative models emphasize repeating interactions between activities, typically between diagnosing problems and taking action. While also iterative, reflective models typically focus on the specific differences between the action theories that practitioners claim to be following (i.e., espoused theory) and the theories that can be inferred from their actions (i.e., theory-in-use) [4, 5].

Reflection is considered as an activity within a number of well-established action research approaches. For instance, Davison et al. [21, 22] elaborated on Susman and Evered’s [78] classical approach into canonical action research (CAR) with the principle of learning through reflection as one of five basic activities. Emphasizing the need “to think and act more deeply and more reflectively” in action research, McKay and Marshall [61] suggest organizing action research into two interacting cycles of problem solving and research, each with goals and activities and with data and theory flowing between them. Similarly, Mathiassen [57] promotes collaborative practice research as a way for groups of researchers and practitioners to work together to understand, support, and improve professional practices. Emphasizing reflection [5, 74] among researchers and practitioners, Mathiassen used this approach to contribute reflective systems development [58] to IS development theory.

Despite the emphasis on reflection as an important activity within specific IS action research approaches [21, 22, 57], there is no action research approach dedicated to comprehensively leverage and develop reflective practices per se. Therefore, with our goal of theorizing RTA in the context of small resourceconstrained organizations, we developed reflective action research as a comprehensive approach to advance knowledge based on problem solving in such contexts and articulated the foundational principles for reflective action research based on Baskerville and Wood-Harper’s four dimensions of action research approaches [12] as summarized in Table 1.

Recognizing that small physician practices operate under severe resource constraints and competing priorities, our engagement was based on the idea that participating physicians could advance technology assimilation within their respective organizations if our collaboration helped them to effectively and continuously reflect on the opportunities and challenges afforded by their EHR [19]. Consistent with Argyris and Schön’s view of organizational learning as a particular form of participatory action research [6], we centered our interventions around the idea of the reflective practitioner [73, 74] to emphasize how the physicians could advance their assimilation of EHRs by reflecting on their action strategies and the values that governed them [4, 5]. Following Baskerville and Wood-Harper [12], we articulate the process model dimension of reflective action research as the principle of reflective practices.

The primary goal of our action research project was to support organizational development within each small physician practice. We aimed to help each practice dedicate time and attention to learn how to better use its EHR in order to improve work practices beyond their initial use plateau. Because the participating physicians worked in similar small practices and used the same EHR, the project interventions allowed the physicians to observe, share, and learn from each other’s experiences [11]. The project design also acknowledged the differences in the physician practices––for example, history, patient panel, and configuration of office staff. It was therefore important to acknowledge how these contextual variations afforded different conditions of EHR assimilation [17, 68]. Hence, we relied on the principle of situated learning, in which reflections are anchored in the specific practice context of each participant.

To foster situated learning across these small physician practices, we brought the physicians together to stimulate reflection and knowledge sharing. We relied on community of practice (CoP) theory [83, 84] to understand how physicians from similar contexts could help each other reflect on using EHRs in their practices. A CoP is defined as a group “of people who share a concern or a passion for something they do and learn how to do it better as they interact regularly” [83, p. 1]. We organized regularly scheduled workshops in which physicians came together to discuss their EHR use, and we also offered consulting support in their offices to help analyze EHR problems and solutions. We capture this fluid collaboration structure in reflective action research through the principle of shared community. This structure allows practitioners to engage in joint reflection and problem solving with each other and the researchers.

The action researchers played an important role in setting up and cultivating the shared community for learning. In the problem-solving cycle [61], the researchers organized workshops for physicians, provided in-office consultation services, and periodically visited each physician’s office to follow up on ideas from the shared workshops. The researchers supported the community with knowledge about their EHRs, the evolving MU program, and technology assimilation. In their interactions with the physicians and their staffs, the researchers methodically collected data on EHR assimilation. Initially, the emphasis was on the problem-solving cycle, with data collection and preliminary analysis supporting practical goals. As the collaboration with the physicians ended, the emphasis shifted to the research cycle by developing formal knowledge contributing to the project’s theoretical goals. We capture this approach to researcher involvement in reflective action research through the principle of research-based facilitation.

## Data Collection and Analysis

Based on these four principles of reflective action research, the research team recruited physicians from 10 small practice organizations for participation in 18 workshop sessions held over four years––see Online Appendix B, Tables 1–2, for a description of the physician’s practices and workshop topics. Workshop topics were determined based on physicians’ interests, new EHR versions with extended capabilities, and the rollout of the government’s MU incentive program. Each meeting included a “Tips and Tricks” segment, in which physicians shared their experiences using the EHR. The EHR consultant met bimonthly with physicians and staffs in their offices to discuss questions, new features, and ways to extend use of the EHR.

Data were collected throughout the project to support the ongoing planning, conduct, and evaluation of interventions as well as for systematic analysis associated with theory-generating activities [61]. Data sources included: (1) interviews with the physicians at the onset of the project, midway through the project, and during project wrap-up, (2) interviews and group meetings with staff members, (3) field notes on in-office consultations, and (4) field notes from the workshop sessions (see Online Appendix B, Table 2). The analysis also included materials that the EHR consultant used during the problem-solving cycle, such as MU program regulations, EHR training materials, and workflow manuals documenting each office’s EHR-related workflows. The research team developed annual grant status reports as well as presentations for academic workshops and conferences, which provided insights for the theoretical analysis.

To systematically review and organize these diverse data from multiple workshops, office consultations and observations, interviews, and written materials (such as reports and manuals), we identified 29 workflow activities that were salient to the physicians and their staffs. We then analyzed workflows across the 10 small physician practices, noting the variations across practices and over time. To concisely report findings, we grouped data on the 29 types of workflows into four categories that reflect the general informational purposes of EHRs, as outlined in Table 2. These categories reflect general informational purposes of EHRs, namely sharing patient information (by receiving and sending patient records to other clinical settings), storing patient records for future use, and documenting patient encounter information while providing health-care services.

Second-level analysis involved assessing physicians’ reflections on EHR use and assimilation. Data for this analysis were evident in observations of physicians discussions in joint meetings, interactions with the EHR consultant and research team during in-office visits, and research interviews. We were interested in whether the physicians’ reflections indicated explicit consideration of values governing actions [5, 73, 74], and if so, what values were salient and whether physicians perceived conflicts or synergies among values related to EHR use. Through iterative data analysis, we identified four governing values relevant to EHR assimilation action strategies: maintaining practice efficiency, maintaining standards of care, modernizing patient data management, and meeting regulatory requirements (Table 3). These values have face validity. Physician owners of small practices must be efficient to remain financially viable and they are held to professional and regulatory clinical standards. Because EHR use is tightly integrated with physicians professional practices, these values similarly guided assimilation action strategies. EHRs also present opportunities to update decades-old methods for managing patient data by embracing modern health-care information technology (HIT).

Governing values may support or contradict organizational goals and thus lead to changes in values [4, 5]. Our third-level analysis revealed instances of conflict as well as synergy among these four governing values in relation to EHR assimilation evident in physicians’ reflections about their EHR use practices and in three patterns of how reflection influenced governing values: confirming, adjusting, and revising. Findings on how these patterns are related to EHR assimilation action strategies follow. For analytical emphasis, these patterns and the salient governing values are highlighted using italics in the following section (for further details, see Online Appendix C).

One physician (Dr. Y) commented ironically on her initial decision to adopt an EHR: “December 27th [‘go live’]. The day my life changed forever! . . . I knew it was not going to be fun, but I decided, I’ve got to do my part.”

Physicians and their office staffs had received initial training, had begun using the system for at least some patient encounters and documentation, and had established baseline uses of the EHR in their workflows. ## Managing Incoming Information

The physicians routinely received a variety of information about their patients from external sources, including laboratory test results, results of imaging studies, referral records from other physician practices, and discharge summary reports from hospitals. This information arrived in various formats, such as paper, fax, or electronic files. The EHR’s “patient hub” could be used to accumulate patient information from these sources in an electronic folder, analogous to a paper folder. However, office staff had to ensure that the information was digitized successfully and linked to the proper patient record, requiring new workflows or adjustments to existing work flows. Ms. D, an office practice manager, explained:

We’re just scanning a lot. . . . Because when labs come in, notes from other docs, because we’re primary [care], we get everything. So everything is scanned in, and that takes us a while to prep a chart. Versus prepping a manual paper chart, we just gathered the notes that came in, clipped it to the chart and gave it to him [physician].

Particularly vexing was managing lab test results from external testing laboratories (“labs”). Some lab reports arrived via fax or mail, requiring that the document be scanned, but it was also possible to receive many lab results as structured data via an electronic feed, which could automatically populate data fields in the EHR. This saved staff time when the lab feeds worked; however, sometimes the lab feeds simply failed. The labs supplied an in-office printer to print test results in addition to the electronic feed, which then had to be reconciled, as Dr. H explained: “The [medical assistants] check to see if the lab is in the patient record when they get the printout, and if it is, they shred the paper . . . some never come through electronically, so we can’t rely on the system 100 percent.” The electronic lab feeds often had small differences in patient names or birthdates. Because the electronic results could not automatically populate the EHR patient hub when patient identification data were not a 100 percent match, these lab results were sent to a “to be reconciled” file in the EHR. In addition, the labs sometimes used their own unique codes for diagnostic tests, which differed from codes used in the EHR, creating further differences to be reconciled.

Reflecting on these difficulties, Dr. H. began working with the labs to correct inconsistencies, so that patient lab data could populate correctly into her EHR. In doing so, she initially adjusted the values governing her actions by balancing modernizing patient data management with maintaining practice efficiency. Working with the labs consumed her own valuable time as the sole revenue generator for her small practice but also promised more effective patient data in the EHR. She related her approach to the other physicians in the project’s joint meetings, but the other physicians did not take up this time-consuming action. Even Dr. H eventually gave up her efforts as data discrepancies recurred.

The physicians found workarounds––such as manually reconciling the printed and electronic sources, or scanning all lab reports as an added safety measure––which ensured reliability in processing lab data, confirming the value of maintaining standards of care with respect to managing patients’ lab data. While lab feed breakdowns were not completely eliminated, breakdowns became less frequent as technical issues such as transmission failures were resolved. These action strategies did not address deeper issues with interorganizational lab data exchange, but the physicians lacked the market influence to pressure the labs to modernize their systems and fix the lab feeds. Thus, the EHR consultant reinforced the need to crosstrain medical assistants (MAs) to ensure they kept up with the lab reconciliation tasks in office sessions and workshops.

Each day, physicians reviewed the incoming lab results to either sign off on the lab or determine whether follow-up action with the patient was necessary. This could be a time-consuming physician workflow. In one joint workshop meeting, Dr. C and Dr. M described how they could quickly sign off on labs electronically using the EHR’s lab sign-off screen. However, at that meeting Dr. Y rued the lack of context on this screen: What were the patient’s prior results? What were the numeric lab values versus the color-coded normal or abnormal indicators the EHR displayed? Dr. Y also complained that the multiple “clicks” she had to go through to open and view earlier lab results in the patient hub were too time consuming. Confirming the value of maintaining standards of care, she then described a workflow adjustment, in which she transcribed new lab test values into a free-text comments field on the lab summary screen. In this way, she could later confidently assess the patient’s most recent labs results along with prior results, but without having to click through multiple screens. Dr. C subsequently commented in an interview that she had adapted Dr. Y’s approach by having her MAs enter the numerical values prior to her review. Dr. C’s action strategy suggested an adjustment to values that balanced maintaining practice efficiency with maintaining standards of care. This small workflow enhancement, shared between two physicians, illustrated how through these facilitated reflections they could alter their habitual EHR uses to align with others’ EHR practices.

## Managing Outgoing Information

Physicians also produced a variety of information that would be used outside of their offices, such as patient records sent to specialists. Sharing information between offices typically relied on faxing, either through the office fax machine or the EHR itself. For example, the physician could share relevant patient records by printing selected items from the EHR patient hub for the office staff to fax to the specialist. This action strategy achieved the immediate need to exchange patient data easily, hence confirming the value of maintaining practice efficiency with a slightly altered workflow. While a far cry from electronic patient data exchange, this approach nonetheless enhanced the usability of the shared records, as Dr. B noted: “We’ve heard feedback from people who get faxed the records, ‘Wow, this is really nice, it’s easy to review. . . . It’s really nice to receive notes from people who have an EMR [electronic medical record], because it’s legible, there is so much more data.”

Physicians shared action strategies in the joint meetings that confirmed the value of maintaining practice efficiency in information sharing workflows. For instance, Dr. M realized that computer faxing of patient records directly from his EHR would be more efficient than printing and then faxing a copy; however, he could not get his EHR to generate the necessary reports as PDFs (portable document formats). Ms. D, the office manager for Dr. D and Dr. B, figured out how to generate the PDF file, while experimenting with new software features. Explaining her persistence in pursuing efficiency, she commented: “[I] don’t settle for mediocrity . . . it’s got to be efficient. There’s got to be an easy way. Or it’s a glitch.” Dr. D mentioned this solution in the “Tips and Tricks” portion of a workshop, and Dr. M quickly adopted this approach to improve his own office’s efficiency in patient referral workflows.

The action research team designed interventions to help physicians move beyond faxing. Physician-to-physician (P2P) messaging capabilities of the EHR were reviewed in joint workshop sessions. Since demonstrating the ability to electronically exchange clinical patient data, for instance, through the P2P capability, was one requirement to earn MU incentives, the EHR consultant also helped physicians set up and execute a P2P data exchange. All 10 physicians completed a onetime exchange, but they did not embrace P2P messaging beyond this onetime demonstration. Computer faxing remained the preferred workflow for interoffice patient file exchanges, as Dr. T commented in his exit interview: “If I refer a patient to somebody, I’ll just fax them my notes, and then, once they’re established with me and another physician, whenever I see them, I fax them my notes.”

That is, despite in-office training, discussions at joint workshops, and active reflection about P2P capabilities, physicians continued to rely on computer faxing. This action strategy (rejecting P2P features despite the MU requirements) confirmed the value of maintaining practice efficiency, as engaging fully with P2P features would have required significant physician attention, time, and organizational resources that were in short supply. This action strategy also confirmed the value of maintaining standards of care with more orderly patient records, whereas the governing value of modernizing patient data management beyond exchanging digital images of patient files by computer faxing PDF files had little influence.

Nonetheless, in her exit interview, Dr. C indicated her readiness to spend some time and effort to promote P2P exchanges among colleagues with whom she exchanged patient records.

I’d like to see us use better this year the P2P features in this EMR, because again, the same front desk person that’s faxing a lab result to this provider, I know that provider has the same EMR. We could be on P2P, and it could be sent electronically, without having to fax it at all, and it would go directly into that provider’s EMR. . . . I think it would be a better way to do it. But it’s a matter of getting all those other providers to also raise their comfort level with using P2P.

As department head at her hospital, Dr. C planned to promote P2P initiatives in her physician community. Through reflection in her own practice and in joint workshop meetings, she had recognized potential synergies among values and revised her own values governing her planned assimilation action strategy to incorporate modernizing patient data management.

The use of e-prescribing features is another example of information outflows that required reflection on appropriate assimilation action strategies. (E-prescribing refers to issuing prescriptions through an electronic network of pharmacies.) When the research project began, only two physicians were using the e-prescribing feature. At a joint workshop session in which Dr. M and Dr. Y described their use of e-prescribing, Dr. C commented she would have to pay significant additional monthly fees for e-prescribing and did not want to incur the extra expense. She was already computer-faxing prescriptions to the pharmacies and found that to be effective. In the ensuing discussion, she learned that computer faxing did not qualify as e-prescribing, a future MU program criterion. Dr. C soon incorporated e-prescribing into her office practice, suggesting that she revised values to incorporate meeting regulatory requirements immediately, rather than simply confirming the value of maintaining practice efficiency by avoiding extra fees. In an interview, Dr. B reflected that eventually he too would have to adjust his e-prescribing action strategy in order to meet regulatory requirements and avoid future payment penalties: “Medicare is going to cut my reimbursement 36% in the next so many years. I feel like a dinosaur, because I’m not doing electronic prescribing.”

All 10 physicians had assimilated e-prescribing before the action research interventions ended and MU incentives or penalties took effect. This indicated that heedful interaction and reflection in the joint workshop meetings contributed to physicians aligning action strategies in this area. Sharing in these sessions also helped them fine-tune their EHR use. For example, Dr. U observed that drugs not listed in the EHR formulary could not be e-prescribed; however, Dr. Y then explained her workaround to prescribe drugs not listed in the EHR, which facilitated transfer of a simple but useful action strategy developed in her office to other office practices.

An instance of revising values governing EHR assimilation also occurred in Dr. C’s office through the implementation of patient portal features. The EHR patient portal features were discussed extensively during office consultations and in joint workshops as a way to communicate information with patients electronically and to address MU program criteria for sharing data with patients electronically. Dr. C and staff undertook the months-long process of recruiting patients to enroll in the patient portal and then started posting lab results electronically. Initially expending staff resources to start using the portal, Dr. C anticipated synergy among the values of maintaining practice efficiency (by eliminating mailing routine test results), maintaining standards of care (by interacting with patients about labs), modernizing patient data management (via electronic notifications), and meeting regulatory requirements (of the MU program). At the final joint workshop, Dr. C related her experiences, noting some trials and errors but overall satisfaction. Despite these reflections, in-office consultations, and pilot projects to help Dr. Y and Dr. M implement the patient portal, the other physicians remained wary. They confirmed the value of maintaining practice efficiency with current workflows as their priority. For instance, in his exit interview, Dr. D indicated his reluctance to use a patient portal:

Not if I don’t have to . . . it’s one more thing that you need to stay on top of and keep refreshed. So I’m really interested to see what else people do with it, besides just having an avenue to say, “these are your lab results,” or “this was something I was going to call you about,” or “call us to make an appointment.”

While this comment suggests Dr. D’s reflective rejection of this EHR feature, at least until he perceived synergy between values governing its use, it also illustrates that physicians were actively heedful of what others were doing with their EHRs.

## Organizing Information Within the Office

To make fullest use of the EHR, physicians needed to ingest thousands of existing paper records into patients’ electronic files within the EHR. Physicians tackled this effort in various ways that reflected the enormity of the task and the way in which the physicians valued modernizing patient data management. Only Dr. T fully committed to the goal of eliminating paper files, being willing to expend the resources to do so. He organized existing paper files for scanning, required his MAs to scan files in any spare time, and paid to have paper records shredded after scanning. His comments highlight the reflection required to modernize EHR patient files:

For old charts and old records, and I can’t really emphasize that enough if people are starting an EMR and they have old records, they really have to separate it out into sections. They can’t just put the whole chart into one thing, or you’ll have the same problem, you never find stuff.

Thus, Dr. T confirmed the value of modernizing patient data management, which led him to first adopt an EHR, despite his substantial and sustained resource and attention commitment to do so for several years. In contrast, Dr. M’s action strategy for dealing with existing records was more gradual; he focused on digitizing files for active patients by reviewing, organizing, and scanning charts himself, as he had the time. In doing so, he identified some instances for follow-up patient care. Therefore, Dr. M adjusted values governing his assimilation action strategy for dealing with patients’ paper records by giving some priority to modernizing patient data management (by digitizing existing records) versus simply maintaining practice efficiency (by contributing his own time and effort to scanning records), while also incorporating maintaining standards of care (by reviewing older charts for possible follow up with patients).

On the other hand, Dr. D and Dr. U had decidedly different action strategies for merging paper and digital data files. They documented their patient encounters in the EHR and had their staff scan in new information, but staff also printed copies of the EHR records for inclusion in the patient’s paper file, creating duplicate records on paper and in the EHR. This action strategy was an amalgam of confirming the value of maintaining practice efficiency by conforming to the physician’s habits (at some cost in staff time) and maintaining standards of care during patient encounters (e.g., a physician’s preference for not having a computer in the exam room). Nonetheless, these physicians confirmed the value of modernizing patient data management by at least using the EHR system to document and maintain patient records.

Dr. B, a specialist, initially tried to scan all patient records, but later focused on scanning records only for patients he expected to see again. This action strategy reflected adjustment between the values of modernizing patient data management and maintaining practice efficiency, which nonetheless helped him “stem the tide of paper” flowing into his small, cramped office:

I’ve had to hire a weekend helper. . . . I used to have boxes and boxes of charts under my desk, and it’s gone from something like 12 boxes down to 2. . . . So it’s been very gradual, but I’ve been able to appreciate how much we’ve been able to stem the tide of paper.

In joint workshop meetings, physicians shared their approaches and were heedful of each other’s views, but variations in action strategies illustrated that learning, reflection, and action strategies for digitizing paper files were situated in the specific context and preferences of each small organization. Dr. T was particularly proud of his accomplishment of scanning and shredding all of his files, whereas Dr. D was unapologetic about his preference for paper. Dr. D explained the importance of the physician’s personal workflow preferences in his exit interview:

Sometimes, you’re trying to do something, and you ask someone else to see if they’ve actually found a way to do something, but for the most part when you talk to other doctors around, they just set their path. Unless they physically need to do something new in their system, they don’t really deviate from that too much.

Physicians and staff also had to organize information about patients in need of services such as periodic visits to check on a chronic condition or follow-up appointments after an abnormal test result. These recall systems were important to physicians for maintaining standards of care. Each practice had recall workflows involving computer spreadsheets, paper lists, index cards, or sometimes just a physician’s or staff member’s memory. EHR software features such as “alerts” and “actions” were designed for these recall purposes, so the EHR consultant provided in-office demonstrations and assistance setting up the features. Several joint workshops featured demonstrations and discussion of how to use these features to identify patients in need of preventative or chronic care attention. Yet despite considerable reflection, physicians generally retained their earlier methods of monitoring patients needing follow-up services, thus confirming the value of maintaining standards of care with their existing system and workflow. In her exit interview, Dr. C explained her cautious approach to using her system’s recall features: “We haven’t trusted that those [alerts] would work well for us, so we manually set those [patient] recalls [for follow-up testing] rather than using the kind of global alert system that I think is built into the EMR.” Dr. T also noted in his exit interview that he used these selfreminder features very little, although he had thought about their possible uses and agreed they might be more generally helpful than the way that he was currently using the EHR’s features: “I use the alerts really only for diabetes . . . I haven’t used [them] for anything else, but I know there’s a lot more effective ways to use them.”

Considering why physicians were reluctant to assimilate these features, the action research team surmised that despite reflection and training support, physicians remained skeptical that these EHR features would improve quality of care, and they were reluctant to experiment with features that might fail to work properly, thus adversely affecting quality care. Therefore, the physicians largely confirmed the value of maintaining standards of care with their tried-and-true recall systems rather than prioritizing modernizing patient data management with automated reminder features that were not yet trusted.

## Documenting and Using Information

In the process of assessing and diagnosing patients, physicians are required to “chart” (document) what the patient reports, what the physician observes and assesses, and what treatments are recommended. Using the EHR to document patient encounters greatly influenced the time it took to document each visit, and thus productivity of physicians, who were the primary revenue generators in these small physician practices. Thus, physicians were curious about how others in the project used the EHR in order to maximize efficiency. Dr. T commented:

I mean, let’s face it, we are really isolated, and we are doing things the way you evolve, you know, kind of like Galapagos Island, you don’t necessarily interbreed. So, it might be interesting to see how things have evolved differently by different offices, and there might be better ways of doing things than we’re doing them.

Physicians took great pride in and were exacting about how they documented patient encounters. They assimilated some EHR features, but they did so in ways that accommodated their own preferences and habits, and documentation action strategies varied greatly as a result. In a joint workshop discussion about using templates (i.e., predesigned outlines for documenting patient encounters), Dr. T and Dr. Y explained that their patient encounters were too varied to benefit from using predesigned templates. Dr. C limited her use of templates to some types of encounters. Drs. D, M, T, and U used voice-recognition software (a frequent discussion topic) to dictate their notes and treatment plans in unstructured “free” text. Other physicians used a combination of free text and “pick lists” (i.e., semistructured terminology in dropdown menus) to document patient encounters and treatment plans.

Through workshop presentations and in-office consultations, the action researchers stimulated physicians’ reflections on the utility of their EHR patient records when using unstructured free text rather than structured language features. For instance,

Dr. T was unable to use EHR reporting features to identify diabetic patients overdue for the diagnostic A1C test, because he dictated his treatment plans to order the test as free text, rather than creating a structured lab order that the EHR system could search for. Since Dr. T found dictation with voice recognition to be fast and easy, using free text in his EHR confirmed the value of maintaining practice efficiency (physician time) versus modernizing patient data management by using structured data, which would, in turn, enable use of EHR decision support and reporting features.

To encourage the physicians to consider efficiency and modernizing records as potentially synergistic rather than conflicting governing values, the action researchers introduced newly released software features in joint workshops. For instance, developing “order sets” of commonly used orders could make structured documentation easier and more standardized. During in-office consultations, the EHR consultant also helped interested physicians to set up order sets. However, designing useful order sets and maintaining them in the EHR required physicians to allocate their own time and attention to do so, and physicians generally focused on revenuegenerating patient encounters and mandatory documentation versus fine-tuning EHR features. Thus, the EHR consultant observed limited use of order sets in subsequent office visits.

Nonetheless, the action research interventions to stimulate physicians’ reflections about documentation practices did show some results. Indicating an adjustment to governing values that balanced maintaining practice efficiency with modernizing patient data management, Dr. T and Dr. M eventually began to enter lab orders from “pick lists” rather than dictating the orders into care plans as free text. By the end of the action research interventions, Dr. M also reported that he was considering using order sets more routinely.

Conflicts among governing values with implications for EHR action strategies were evident in physicians’ reactions to the MU incentive program, which started the rollout of incentives two years into the action research project. The MU program specified use criteria for the patient data that physicians had to record. The action researchers dedicated several workshop sessions to update physicians on these criteria and to stimulate reflection on their current EHR assimilation strategies related to MU criteria. While physicians were interested in earning incentive payments, their discussions illustrated perceived conflicts between the values of maintaining practice efficiency, particularly their own time documenting encounters, and meeting regulatory requirements. Reflecting on these conflicts, Dr. D complained: “With all the requirements that have come along. . . . There’s other things we need to do to make sure that we are doing what we are supposed to do to get our little brownie points, so definitely more work.”

Dr. C tended to perceive synergy between governing values, largely due to the MU financial incentives, and emphasized in her exit interview how her revised value of meeting regulatory requirements for MU now influenced her priorities for further EHR assimilation actions: “I’m starting to look through my stuff. What in “Meaningful Use” stage 2 is most likely to trip you up?” Other physicians expressed doubt about whether MU regulations supported maintaining standards of care or even modernizing patient data management, as Dr. M observed:

We have so much to do during the day already. Is it something that’s going to be helpful, number 1? Number 2, is it required? I guess if it’s required we’re going to have to do it. . . . We’ve printed a couple [of chronic care reports] just to qualify for Meaningful Use, but we’re not really using it in any great degree, because we have our recall system in place.

A final example concerns how physicians responded to the MU criteria that a certain percentage of patient files contain at least one item in the “problem list” or a “no problem” entry. Several physicians did not find using the problem list useful. In the joint workshop sessions, they discussed how to minimally meet the MU requirement so they could earn the financial incentives. Along with printing but not using reports or doing a onetime P2P demonstration, this minimal action strategy highlighted how revising governing values to include meeting regulatory (MU) requirements could result in instrumental EHR feature assimilation strategies.

## Theorizing Reflective Technology Assimilation

Through the action research project, researchers supplemented the physicians’ limited organizational resources for EHR assimilation by providing ongoing training, workflow consultations, and facilitated workshops, in order to help them advance beyond their initial postadoption (“satisficing”) plateau of EHR use. These interventions required physicians to dedicate time and attention in their busy schedules to actively reflect on their own and their staff’s EHR usage and to draw on the experiences and insights of other physicians facing similar issues. Our empirical analysis explicates three patterns in physicians’ reflections: (1) confirming, (2) adjusting, and (3) revising governing values, which influenced the assimilation action strategies they devised. Through these actions, physicians advanced assimilation of their EHRs to better manage their small practices and care for their patients, while contributing, albeit incrementally, to modernizing patient data management and meeting new regulations.

Drawing on the empirical analysis (see Online Appendix C for summary) and key elements of technology assimilation and reflective practitioner theory [2, 3, 4, 5], we now formalize a model for RTA. Figure 1 provides a visual depiction to guide this discussion.

First, we propose that RTA depends on learning and reflection in two interrelated contexts: (a) the actors’ use of technology within distinct practice settings [2, 3], and (b) the actors’ interactions within a community that spans across these practice settings [11, 83, 84]. Consistent with the principles of reflective action research, we facilitated physicians’ reflections in both contexts with in-office EHR consultations and joint workshops. According to the physicians’ reviews of the project, these interventions were useful to advance their EHR assimilation. In addition, we observed that by the end of the action research project, some physicians were taking on assimilation projects on their own and were sharing their EHR experiences with other physicians who were not members of the research collaboration. These outcomes are consistent with our primary goal of promoting organizational development related to technology assimilation and to highlighting the ongoing nature of RTA.

Figure 1. An Analytic Model for Reflective Technology Assimilation (RTA)

Following the literature [2, 3, 4, 5], we posit that RTA necessarily entails (c) consideration of the underlying values that govern action strategies for technology use, and that these reflections can reveal (d) both conflicts and synergies among governing values. In the action research project, physicians often perceived conflicts between maintaining practice efficiency and modernizing patient data management, as they considered whether to adopt a new feature or another physician’s approach to using the EHR. At other times, physicians perceived synergies among these and other governing values from assimilating features.

Following the literature [2, 3, 4, 5], we posit that (e) reflection affects governing values, and through these values, (f) the assimilation action strategies that practitioners devise. However, we diverge from a strict distinction between single- and double-loop learning [5] in terms of how governing values are implicated in action strategies. Our empirical analyses revealed that reflection does not necessarily result in a change in values, as physicians sometimes confirmed the values most salient to their use of specific EHR features, leading to action strategies that ranged from rejecting features to persisting with difficult assimilation efforts. For instance, when the physicians perceived conflicts with maintaining practice efficiency, they tended to confirm this value and to resist or minimize integrating use of new EHR features into their practices. This finding is consistent with the purposeful trade-offs that resource- and attention-constrained organizations face when assimilating new technologies. Yet, one physician (Dr. T) confirmed the value of modernizing patient data management despite the toll on his own time as he sustained this strategic commitment to eliminating paper records.

On the other hand, revising governing values at times led to incremental, even superficial assimilation actions, as we saw in the way that physicians adjusted their EHR use to meet MU regulations. We did observe significant, strategic assimilation action strategies (such as implementing a patient portal) when physicians perceived synergy among values and revised their values by incorporating new ones, whereas incremental action strategies were typically associated with more modest adjustment (balancing) of values.

To summarize, we propose that confirming, adjusting, or revising values all indicate (e) the practitioner’s reflection, even if (f) the resulting action strategies are to resist or reject assimilation of new features, that these patterns may each contribute to strategic as well as instrumental action strategies, and that what might appear as habitual or routinized use may result from rational, reflective choices, given the trade-offs that practitioners face in the allocation of time, attention, and resources required to advance technology assimilation.

Organizations continually encounter new opportunities and unexpected challenges in their technology use––from new software releases to workflow breakdowns to feedback from earlier assimilation actions. We therefore posit that reflective technology assimilation is an emergent process engendered by (g) ongoing situated learning occurring through technology use [54, 65]. Improvements that physicians and their staff devised to better manage inflows and outflows of patient data illustrated this ongoing and emergent assimilation process. On the other hand, their idiosyncratic habits and solutions such as relying on faxing, scanning, and free text dictation created a legacy of satisficing use that proved difficult to overcome. Facilitated reflections, such as the action research interventions with workshops and training, served to stimulate physicians to reconsider earlier choices, even if they did not adopt particular features.

A shared community is important for sustaining and energizing emergent assimilation processes because the community provides a broader context for (h) devising and relating innovative technology use practices. Assimilating technologies, such as EHRs, influence professional work practices as well as technology use per se [1, 20, 29, 35, 50, 54, 65]. Heedful interactions among practitioners allow them to maintain coherence, if not uniformity, of shared practices within the community, rather than drifting apart based solely on personal habits [11]. For instance, by comparing how they documented patient encounters using the EHR, physicians were reassured that their own idiosyncratic approaches were acceptable variations of shared professional practices. By reflecting both (a) within their situated practices and (b) within the shared community, physicians at times confirmed values governing their own EHR use, for example, Dr. D and Dr. U maintained duplicate EHR and paper patient files. They also at times adjusted or revised values, and thus their assimilation action strategies, for example, Dr. C embraced e-prescribing in alignment with other physicians involved in the project.

In theorizing RTA in resource- and attention-constrained contexts, we have presented analytical generalizations while remaining grounded in relevant literature, the empirical analysis, and reflective action research principles. However, the model has limitations that merit comment. First, the model portrays circumstances under which reflective technology assimilation might occur. This does not imply that ongoing assimilation of technology features is automatic or inevitable [26, 76], particularly beyond incremental action strategies required to adjust to changes such as software releases or new regulations. This action research project illustrated how, even with a high degree of intention among physicians and commitment of substantial facilitating resources, EHR assimilation advanced slowly and incrementally within and across these small organizations. Further studies will help determine when facilitating resources can develop naturally within a practitioner community versus when external facilitation is essential to advance assimilation of technologies like EHRs (e.g., [67]).

Second, the model does not explicitly address the complexity that might result if different values govern the action strategies among organizational users of the same technology. As is typical of small business owners, the physicians we worked with decided, for the most part, how they and their staff would use the EHR. This arrangement afforded a reasonable level of consistency in governing values within each small organization. In larger organizations, we would expect that differences in governing values among groups could contribute to divergent, rather than convergent, assimilation strategies and technology use patterns (see, e.g., [20, 29, 35, 50]).

Third, the model does not explicitly consider constraints to assimilation arising in the external sociotechnical environment. In this project, given the lack of an effective community infrastructure for interorganizational data exchange (e.g., with lab feeds or P2P exchange), the participants remained dependent on existing reliable infrastructure, such as faxing records. Developing action strategies to overcome community-level infrastructure limitations requires community-wide reflection and facilitation, as Dr. C. acknowledged in her plans to promote P2P exchange with colleagues. Developing this theoretical argument further will require a multilevel, multiple stakeholder view of RTA.

Finally, the patterns of conflicts and synergies in governing values that we identified are drawn from small organizations in a highly regulated industry. Efficiency, quality, data management, and regulatory compliance arguably govern technology assimilation in many contexts, though additional or contrasting values may be more salient in other settings. While confirming, adjusting, and revising values through reflection are analytically general concepts, additional patterns may be revealed in other settings.

## Contributions

## Technology Assimilation Theory

The RTA model contributes to theory in three important ways. First, the model integrates various theoretical streams related to organizational and community learning in technology assimilation [24, 26, 66], organizational leadership and resources needed to advance assimilation (e.g., [7, 28, 42, 43, 53, 63, 80]), and the legacy of habits and routines in postadoption use that influence assimilation (e.g., [9, 43, 80]). To achieve this integration, the model foregrounds and centers on reflection [2, 3, 4, 5] as a central learning mechanism for technology assimilation within situated practices and across practitioner communities.

Second, going beyond the dichotomy between single- and double-loop learning [2, 3, 4, 5], the model explicates the influence of governing values and how the conflict and synergy between these values influence assimilation action strategies. RTA involves a continuum from confirming to adjusting to revising values, where each entail some degree of reflection with implications for action strategies. This nuanced perspective highlights mechanisms underlying postadoption use [9, 33, 43, 46], particularly when conflicting values govern overall organizational performance. In such cases, practitioners’ postadoption use might be better viewed as rational tradeoffs and choices than merely as habits [54, 65].

Third, we elaborate this model in the context of small, resource- and attentionconstrained organizations, which are seldom considered in IS assimilation research. Small organizations are generally less likely to adopt innovations than bigger organizations [28], in large part because they lack sufficient resources to adequately support assimilation. The RTA model highlights reflection by key practitioners, such as owners, as an essential resource that is not easily outsourced, because of their role in addressing value conflicts and trade-offs in determining technology use practices. The model theorizes how a shared community for learning can extend or leverage limited organizational resources for reflection and learning.

## Action Research Methodology

Proponents of action research have long recognized reflection as one of several activities that can help practitioners and researchers collaborate to solve practical problems and advance theoretical knowledge [12, 21, 22, 57]. Our study builds on these insights to offer reflective action research (see Table 1) as a comprehensive approach that places practitioner reflection across similar professional contexts at the center of organizational development and change, and we demonstrate how we applied this approach to guide the reported research collaboration.

Our action research was designed and carried out based on a reflective process model [12], in which the participating physicians were supported to assess current use of their EHRs, to experiment with approaches and use of new features, and to share with each other their experiences with the EHRs (the principle of reflective practices). The primary goal of this project was to support organizational development [12] through improved EHR usage in each of the participating practices, based on the assumption that this technology assimilation could only be achieved through interventions into the specific context of each individual practice setting (the principle of situated learning). At the same time, the collaboration structure was fluid [12] affording opportunities for the participating physicians to move back and forth between their practice settings and shared workshops to exchange experiences and stimulate learning (the principle of shared community). Rather than acting only as research subjects, the fluid collaborative structure gave physicians an active voice in shaping research interventions and laying the empirical foundation for formal knowledge articulation.

Finally, the involvement of the researchers was facilitative [12], based on continuous interactions between problem-solving activities with the participating small physician practices and research activities aimed at empirical analysis and theory development (the principle of research-based facilitation). This principle was particularly important to accomplishing the dual goals of action research, as the action researchers generated instances of the phenomena of interest by designing and carrying out interventions within and across practice settings. These interventions created the time and place for practitioners to reflect and develop action strategies in a focused, explicit manner beyond what might otherwise have occurred in these small organizations. This involves specific actions on the part of researchers, but it does not privilege the researchers as the sole, or even primary, causal agent for change. Rather, this process acknowledges the essential role of the practitioners in crafting their own solutions and actions. Facilitating interventions also generated rich context-situated data that afforded articulation of general knowledge (formal theory). Such data are less accessible even in observational field studies, which depend on the phenomena of interest occurring naturally during the study period.

Our reflective action research approach has potential limitations. Regarding the primary goal of organizational development in each of the involved practice settings, the principle of research-based facilitation highlights issues with sustainability when problems are ongoing and substantive [15]. Although the participating physicians did realize, albeit to varying degrees, improved EHR usage in their primary care practices, assimilating EHRs (and other health IT) presents ongoing challenges [54, 65]. Providing long-term mediating resources [18, 64] may require institutional stakeholders, such as professional associations or government agencies [26], to assume the facilitative role on a permanent basis.

Overcoming subjectivity in theorization is also a challenge for researchers deeply involved in facilitating organization development [47]. One way to alleviate this limitation is to collaborate with other researchers in the research cycle, as such, the first and third authors joined with the second author to challenge her assumptions and help select the most appropriate theoretical lens, conduct formal analysis and clarify interpretations, improve articulation of the methods, and ensure contribution to scientific knowledge.

## Implications for Practice

Small physician practices face significant challenges in assimilating complex EHRs into their small office practices (e.g., [8, 32, 40, 55]). Incremental improvements such as learning how to computer fax records or to review lab results more quickly are far from the ambitious claims that widespread EHR use will fundamentally transform the health-care system (e.g., [14, 38, 49]). Nonetheless, these minor advances are important to these small physician practices on the front line of U.S. health-service delivery, being increasingly burdened with administrative and regulatory reporting requirements. Financial incentives such as the U.S. government’s MU program may motivate physicians’ EHR adoption of designated features, but assimilation action strategies may be superficial if they believe that EHR use challenges their practice’s efficiency or fails to convincingly support standards of care, as evidenced in this study.

If the societal goal is to advance physicians’ assimilation of EHRs to realize transformational improvements in health-service delivery (e.g., [38, 45, 66, 67]), physicians’ active reflection and learning in the postadoption phase is critical to move beyond satisficing or good-enough levels of use. An abundance of informational resources are available from EHR vendors, professional associations, and consultants about EHRs, but absorbing and acting on this information requires physicians’ time and attention. The RTA model is premised on practitioners themselves as central actors in technology assimilation. Moreover, the practical advice of a colleague with similar experiences and constraints may be more meaningful for spreading innovative ideas on efficient and effective EHR use. To advance EHR assimilation within and across small physician practices, stakeholders such as government agencies, vendors, and professional associations might dedicate resources to facilitate activities to encourage physicians to engage in RTA in the postadoption phase.

Although the setting and thus specific findings of this project reflects the U.S. health-care system, the RTA model holds widely applicable implications for practice, particularly in areas where health-care delivery depends on small, isolated clinics, and medical practitioners lack the resources necessary to assimilate technologies such as EHRs. In such contexts, facilitation and support will be needed, not only in terms of hardware, software, and network infrastructure, but also in terms of practical advice and hands-on help to assimilate technology features into practice, develop organizational capabilities to sustain technology assimilation, and to foster learning within shared communities.

## Conclusions

In this study, we articulate RTA as a critical process to enable practitioners in attention- and resource-constrained organizations to progress beyond technology assimilation satisficing (i.e., “good enough” use of complex technologies such as EHRs). Although our focus was on small organizations in the health-care setting, this model can be adapted to other industries and settings. For instance, when large organizations implement complex enterprise information systems, these technologies must be assimilated across organizational units with different contextual constraints and opportunities (e.g., [7, 53]). Smaller units within large organizations are likely to encounter similar issues with moving beyond satisficing plateaus and to benefit from situated learning and reflection among organization members in diverse units in order to facilitate and advance local assimilation. The reflective action research (RAR) approach outlined and demonstrated in this study provides methodological guidance for synergistically supporting practitioners in such settings and for advancing theoretical knowledge through such projects.
