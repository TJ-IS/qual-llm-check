# Parametric search engines: What makes them effective when shopping online for differentiated products?

Arnold A. Kamis <sup>a,\*</sup>, Edward A. Stohr <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Information & Process Management Department, 405 Smith Academic Technology Center, Bentley College, 175 Forest Street, Waltham, MA 02452-4705, United States

<sup>b</sup> Stevens Institute of Technology Morton Building, Room 327, Castle Point on the Hudson, Hoboken, NJ 07030, United States Received 3 August 2005; accepted 11 August 2006

## Abstract

Many online retailers try to assist consumers via parametric search engines (PSEs), i.e., attribute-based search engines. The objective of this study is to model the effectiveness of four PSEs in using search effort and domain knowledge to increase decision quality, decision confidence, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. Our model comprises a set of behavioral decision theory antecedents to the technology acceptance model. We tested users with four PSEs in a laboratory experiment and modeled the results with partial least squares (PLS) analysis. The main result of this paper is a PLS model showing that the effects of search effor and domain knowledge are mediated through decision quality and decision confidence to impact perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. The model explains the variance in decision quality (17.3%), decision confidence (28.3%) and perceived usefulness (27.0%). Overall, this study shows that input, process and outcome variables are important for predicting the effectiveness of PSEs. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

Keywords: Online shopping; Parametric search engine; Decision quality; Decision confidence; Domain knowledge; Search effort; Perceived ease of use; Perceived usefulness; Partial least squares

## 1. Introduction

Online shopping offers the customer a large search space, which can be attractive but overwhelming. When shopping for a differentiated rather than commodity product [3,11], the situation is amplified, because the online shopper considers several relevant dimensions, i.e., not only price, but also quality and other attributes. Despite advances in online shopping technology, some researchers have expressed doubts regarding the viability of online shopping for differentiated goods [8,63,81]. Some argue that the Web is simply too passive and indirect to engage shoppers [41].

One ambitious solution to online shopping challenges is to employ autonomous agents [38,44,55]. Such agents could be replicated in great number with slight variations in search criteria and endowed with the ability to execute a transaction. They could be knowledgeable, open-ended and able to have a dialogue with the user. Research shows that shopping agents are a possibility, but have thus far achieved mixed results [55,82,83]. As long as the agents are insufficiently informed and trusted, they are reduced to information gatherers; the user still has significant information filtering, weighing and integration to perform.

A more realistic solution than autonomous agents is the parametric search engine (PSE), which is based on attributes rather than Boolean combinations of keywords. The objective of this paper is to model the effectiveness of four PSEs in using search effort and domain knowledge to increase decision quality, decision confidence, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness.

In the next sections, we discuss the research background, model and hypotheses. We then describe the methods and present the data analysis. We conclude with a discussion of the results and the implications for theory and practice, as well as limitations and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Research background

Multiple attribute decision making has been deployed to solve complex problems, and multiple attribute decision support systems (DSS) have grown increasingly powerful [24]. In their review of multiple attribute DSS, Dyer et al. called for the development of easy-to-use, interactive software that supports decision makers. A system that advances the state-of-the-art in multiple attribute DSS and is easy-to-use, they argued, would be more likely to be effective. However, a system that is extremely easy-to-use, but lacking interactivity, i.e., a ‘‘black box’’ system that marginalizes the user, may not be advisable. One study noted that it may not be ‘‘possible or desirable for a multiple attribute DSS to have an automated system perform the entire decision process’’ [34]. An interactive multiple attribute DSS would be more effective by opening the ‘‘black box’’ and engaging the user in a dialogue to elicit user input.

The challenge of balancing multiple attribute DSS functionality, user involvement and ease of use is consistent with the main findings of the technology acceptance model [20,80]. Users want systems to be useful yet easy-to-use. A useful system that is hard-touse or that introduces decisional conflict can diminish user acceptance [49]. Engaging the user too closely with a multiple attribute DSS, however, can overly tax the user or introduce excessive ‘‘noise’’ into the system, e.g., by incorporating well-intentioned yet erroneous judgments. In sum, a purely ‘‘black box’’ approach or a purely ‘‘clear box’’ approach is unlikely to be an effective PSE.

One study assessed DSS research in light of the Internet revolution and found a number of challenges, including the deluge of information accessible to unsophisticated online customers and the inaccuracy of search engines [12]. The study called for a joint effort from a variety of disciplines to design online DSS, possibly with optimization-based models from mathematical programming, to advance DSS research. The current study is a combination of behavioral decision theory and information systems theory which addresses the call articulated in that study.

To determine PSE effectiveness, we adapt a framework [54], shown in Fig. 1, which is based on the IS Success research of DeLone and McLean [22]. The framework includes two categories of impact variables: the impact of a DSS on (1) the decision making inputs and process and (2) the decision making outcomes. For each of those two categories, the framework distinguishes between relatively objective measures and relatively subjective measures. This framework addresses some of the more under-researched areas of DSS research.

## 3. Hypotheses and model

We employed the framework in Fig. 1 to study the effectiveness of PSEs, i.e., attribute-based DSS for online shopping. In this section, we discuss the research model, starting with search effort and domain knowledge and their expected impact on decision quality. We then discuss the expected impact of decision quality on decision confidence. Finally, we discuss the expected impacts of decision confidence and perceived ease of use on perceived usefulness.

## 3.1. Search effort

Minimizing search effort is a strong guiding principle for most decision makers [20,74]. This tendency is so strong that decision makers have preferred retrieving information from memory over acquiring information from the task environment, even when the latter takes mere milliseconds and results in superior performance [35]. As one study noted, online shoppers are reluctant to exert more than a few mouse clicks and browser window scrolling quickly becomes intolerable [68].

Perhaps most distressing to DSS designers is the finding that decision makers using a DSS have been reluctant to reallocate saved effort to the pursuit of better decision outcomes, such as decision accuracy [75]. However, a more complex decision aid designed to achieve greater accuracy can be preferred over a simpler decision aid, as long as the former is equally easy to use [77]. In addition, PSEs can be sufficiently easy-to-use and useful, thus enabling users to simultaneously decrease effort and increase decision quality [37]. It seems that PSEs can enable the user to quickly filter out low quality alternatives in earlier stages and exert greater effort in later stages of decision making.

## 3.2. Domain knowledge

Domain knowledge, e.g., of printers or computers in general, can have a strong impact on search behavior. Prior research has found that search increases when prior knowledge is general, i.e., of the product category, but decreases when it is specific, i.e., case-based [62,71]. The mechanism seems to be that greater knowledge of specific products leads to the perception of fewer benefits from search [2], which leads to decreased search.

Studies of effort-accuracy tradeoffs have shown that people usually exchange accuracy for effort [60], but it can be a sequential process. After an individual examines a core set of attributes, the effort to acquire additional information decreases dramatically [64]. The user’s domain knowledge thereby serves as a checklist, after which search effort is likely to diminish.

## 3.3. Decision quality

We define decision quality as the degree to which a decision outcome (a choice) is optimal, both in an objective and subjective sense; that is, the best choice possible and the best choice for the decision maker. Assessing decision quality objectively and subjectively when shopping for a differentiated product is difficult, because the shopper needs to juggle a potentially large number of attributes. This challenge can be addressed by exerting greater search effort or by increasing one’s domain knowledge. Either tactic should result in increased decision quality, and the shopper should continue to search until the marginal benefit of an extra unit of search effort is negligible [72].

Greater domain knowledge improves the decision maker’s search by enabling him to focus on using the tool in the more fruitful areas of the search space. For example, the user is more likely to specify tighter attribute ranges in PS when domain knowledge is high and looser attribute ranges when domain knowledge is low. However, the user with greater domain knowledge could leverage his knowledge (exerting greater effort) or he could search more selectively (conserving effort) or he could become complacent (exerting less effort than is warranted) [5]. It is therefore difficult to predict the impact of domain knowledge on search effort, but it should have a positive impact on decision quality [69,73]. We therefore hypothesize the following.

H1. Greater search effort will be positively related to greater decision quality.

H2. Greater domain knowledge will be positively related to greater decision quality.

## 3.4. Decision confidence

Decision confidence has been conceptualized and defined primarily in two ways: as the objective confidence interval about a given value and as the subjective judgment of one’s decision. The first way of conceptualizing decision confidence has been as a probability of having the correct answer or of reducing uncertainty through information acquisition [31,39]. It tends to be measured statistically, as standard deviation or variance about some correct or estimated value. Similarly, it is defined as calibration, as the correspondence between the decision maker’s perceptions and the objectively correct reality [1,10,32,46]. In this approach, decision confidence is conceptualized as a statistically measurable interval of inaccuracy. The second way of conceptualizing decision confidence is as a self-reported level of confidence in one’s own decision. This second way defines confidence as a desirable and important factor to the decision maker, not as a deviation or miscalibration, but as a positive halo about the decision or choice. In this study, we adopt the second operational definition of decision confidence, because online customers often report that they need to feel confident in their decision before committing to it [35]. Even if that self-reported perception introduces measurement difficulties, i.e., noise, it is an important perception to attempt to capture.

Decision quality has a significant impact on decision confidence with the web as a shopping channel [23,57]. Because a high quality decision should foster the notion that a better choice would have been unlikely, greater decision quality should lead to greater decision confidence. In other words, higher decision quality should correspond positively to higher decision confidence. We therefore hypothesize the following.

H3. Greater decision quality will be positively related to greater decision confidence.

## 3.5. Perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness

The technology acceptance model (TAM) is a wellestablished model for characterizing a user’s subjective experience with an information technology for work purposes. The TAM variable of perceived ease of use (PEU) has also been applied successfully to many ecommerce contexts: predicting intention to return to a web site [50], trust in e-commerce [53], satisfaction with the web channel [23], trust in the e-vendor [28], social presence and trust in B2C e-services [29], intention to transact [58] and effort-accuracy tradeoffs in online shopping [42]. Most TAM research has found a positive impact of perceived ease of use on perceived usefulness [20,21], across gender [79] and across time in longitudinal field studies [78], regardless of other factors [80]. In this study, we use the two key variables of TAM, i.e., perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, to subjectively assess the effectiveness of parametric search engines in online shopping.

Greater perceived ease of use should correspond to greater perceived usefulness. Greater perceived ease of use should also have a positive impact on decision confidence, because the user feels efficacious in using the PSE. To the extent that there is alignment between user, technology and task, the user experiences a smooth, seamless process. Conversely, a struggle with the PSE would cast doubt on the user or the technology, making decision confidence unlikely. Finally, perceived usefulness of a PSE should be a function of both decision confidence and perceived ease of use. We therefore hypothesize the following.

H4. Greater perceived ease of use will be positively related to greater decision confidence.

H5. Greater perceived ease of use will be positively related to greater perceived usefulness.

H6. Greater decision confidence will be positively related to greater perceived usefulness.

## 4. Methods

We chose a laboratory experiment rather than a field study to rigorously test the above hypotheses and maximize internal validity. This controlled approach, testing subjects in a single laboratory room, enabled us to minimize the impact of confounds, e.g., user mortality, user-user contamination and task interference from other sources [35]. After a pretest, a laboratory experiment was conducted with subjects who volunteered to participate. We checked for authenticity of behavior in two ways: (1) user comments solicited at the end of the experiment and (2) statistical examination of all variable distributions for outliers. The subjects were 71 college students (58% female, 42% male) who were homogeneous with regard to age (mean 18.9, S.D. 0.55) and level of education. Age varied very little as the subjects were predominantly freshmen. There were slightly more females than males, but no statistically significant gender differences. The subjects were recruited from multiple sections of the same introductory computer course.

## 4.1. Task design

Each subject was to recommend a product for others to purchase. The purpose was to have the subject consider his own reaction to the final choice, but also the reaction of others. The task included an incentive designed to motivate subject performance. The better the performance, the greater the probability of winning a cash prize. The level of performance was defined by the combination of decision quality (the higher, the better) and search effort (the lower, the better). Thus the subjects were confronted with a trade off: greater search effort would likely result in greater decision quality, but too much search effort would likely decrease the probability of a reward.

## 4.2. Experimental treatments

We built an experimental Web store for testing subjects who shopped for printers (practice task) and computers (performance task) with PSEs components having different interface designs. Specifically, the PSE components were parametric search (PS) and elimination-by-aspects (EBA). PS and EBA have been shown to yield no significant differences in PSE effectiveness in a model of variables drawn from consumer behavior research [43]. The current study aims to verify and extend that study by developing a model of variables drawn from behavioral decision theory [76].

A parametric search (PS) enabled the user to define upper and lower bounds for one or more parameters (attributes) of products, while ignoring other dimensions [36]. Fig. 3a shows the flowchart for PS. An eliminationby-aspects (EBA) enabled the user to narrow the set of alternatives, one attribute at a time. The importance ranking of attributes was determined beforehand. The decision process started with the most important attribute, in which alternatives were eliminated if they did not satisfy the filter value [6,40]. Fig. 3b shows the flowchart for EBA. A comparison matrix (CM) was used to terminate the decision process. The shopper was presented a matrix of the remaining items (columns) by attributes (rows), in which each cell contained the attribute value for the intersecting product and attribute. This display gave the shopper a detailed, side-by-side tool for comparing products on an attribute-level basis. Fig. 3c shows the flowchart for CM.

The PSEs were the four possible combinations of PS and EBA (terminated with CM), to which subjects were randomly assigned. Table 1 shows the four combinations of PSE components, i.e., the experimental treatments.

For the practice task, subjects were randomly assigned to either treatment one or treatment four, so that they would experience both PS and EBA. For the performance task, subjects were randomly assigned to a treatment different from their practice treatment. Note that the output set of items from one stage was the input set of items in the next stage; e.g., in treatment one, the output from PS was the input to EBA.

## 4.3. Database contents

The shopping database was populated with unbranded printers for the practice task and unbranded computers for the performance task. The method of generating products from their attribute levels was the same regardless of the product. For each product attribute, we used current market data for high, medium and low levels on each of five attributes, including price, and used every combination to populate the database. This method of product construction ensured that our product database was preference indifferent; i.e., we did not skew the database toward any combination of attribute values. In addition, we removed the dominant product, the one with high levels on the first four attributes and a low level for the price, to prevent the task from being too easy. We also removed the five next most dominant products, as determined by data envelopment analysis (DEA) [4,19], which is described in Appendix A. This method of product database construction was similar to that used in another study, which manually constructed the database to contain only a few objectively dominant products [37].

## 4.4. Reward function

The subjects participated for a nominal incentive: \$10 plus a chance at winning one of three cash prizes: \$50, \$100 or \$200. The probability of winning a cash prize was based on a lottery system, in which decision quality and decision time were rewarded equally. Decision quality was calculated with DEA and with the weighted additive (WADD) method [7,61]. The first measure of decision quality was simply the degree of optimality calculated by DEA. The second measure of decision quality, calculated with WADD, multiplied the user’s self-reported attribute weights by the attribute levels of the chosen item to give it a score. That score was ranked relative to the score for all items to give the chosen item a utility rank. The PLS analysis generated weights for combining the DEA measure and the WADD measure of decision quality into a single score for decision quality. Finally, each subject’s score for decision quality was ranked relative to the scores of other subjects as above average (five tickets), average (three tickets) or below average (one ticket). Effort was ranked by elapsed time and subjects were given a score for faster than average (5), average (3) or slower than average (1). The ranks for decision quality and search effort were then averaged and rounded to give the subject his final number of lottery tickets. The reward function was explained and illustrated with examples in the instructions to the user before the practice task started (see Appendix B).

## 4.5. Experimental procedure

We conducted two pretests to ensure that the Web store simulation was bug-free and the instructions were clear. To proceed from start to finish, subjects simply clicked on hyperlinks and entered values in web browser forms. The page design minimized screen clutter and distractions by having few extraneous links or images. Subjects were allowed to progress from one stage to the next, whenever they wished to do so, and they could press the browser’s back button within any stage. Both the practice task and performance task started with an elicitation of attribute weights, followed by a randomly assigned PSE and a post-task survey. No significant problems were discovered by the experiment supervisor.

## 4.6. Instrumentation

The variables were measured with standard instruments whenever possible. For the survey instrument, see Table 2.

Search effort was measured as elapsed time for the task, after reading instructions, precise to the second. The objective and subjective measures of decision quality were operationalized as described in the reward function. Using both an objective and a subjective measure guarded against mono-measure bias.

## 4.7. Data analysis approach

After an ANOVA analysis, we pooled the data to test our hypotheses in the form of a structural equation model (SEM). We used SEM to simultaneously test the hypotheses as direct and indirect paths of the research model [15,48]. We chose partial least squares (PLS) rather than LISREL<sup>1</sup> to allow integration of variables from different disciplines with a relatively small number of subjects. The goal of PLS is similar to that of multiple regression: maximize variance explained while ensuring that all linkages are statistically significant [13,18,27]. Information systems researchers have used PLS to examine a wide variety of information system problems, including customer acceptance of ecommerce [58], environmental uncertainty and task characteristics impacts on management satisfaction with data [45], knowledge transfer from consultants to clients developing enterprise resource planning systems [47] and the impact of cognitive absorption on the acceptance of online learning [65]. (Gefen et al. present an excellent review of structural equation modeling, including the appropriateness of different techniques [30].)

In sum, we used multiple methods and multiple measures, which helped to guard against mono-method and mono-measure bias. The measures captured both objective and subjective components of decision quality, as well as perceptions of domain knowledge, decision confidence, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness.

## 5. Results

We first analyzed the data of all six variables in the model to see whether there were any differences attributable to the individual PSEs. There were no significant differences, as an ANOVA showed that the F Statistics were 0.60 $( p = 0 . 5 5 1 )$ for search effort, 0.16 $( p = 0 . 8 5 4 )$ for domain knowledge, 1.85 $( p = 0 . 1 6 5 )$ for decision quality, 1.09 $( p = 0 . 3 4 1 )$ for decision confidence, $0 . 0 2 \ ( p = 0 . 9 8 4 )$ for perceived ease of use and 0.17 $( p = 0 . 8 4 1 )$ for perceived usefulness. Consequently, we pooled the data from the four PSEs for subsequent analysis.

Five of the six hypotheses were supported, H1 at a level of $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , H2 at a level of $p = 0 . 0 4 5$ , H3 at a level of $p = 0 . 0 1 4$ and both H4 and H5 at a level of $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Decision confidence had 28.3% of its variance explained by decision quality $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 8 )$ and perceived ease of use $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 0 )$ , thus validating H3 and H4. Perceived usefulness had 27.0% of its variance explained solely by perceived ease of use $( \beta = 0 . 5 1 9 )$ , thus validating H5. In sum, search effort and domain knowledge had positive impacts on decision quality. Decision quality, in turn, had a positive impact on decision confidence. Perceived ease of use had a positive impact on decision confidence and perceived usefulness.

## 6. Discussion of contributions

The main theoretic contribution of the paper is the nomological network which propagates search effort and domain knowledge, mediated through decision quality and decision confidence, to impact perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. This network is a set of behavior decision theoretic antecedents to the technology acceptance model, and a key contribution of this study is that decision confidence is a direct antecedent to perceived usefulness. Additionally, search effort has an important positive impact on decision quality. The key implication is that users need to spend time with a PSE without it seeming like effort.

Adapting the framework from Lilien et al. [54], we found that input, process and outcome variables helped develop a useful PLS model. We devised a hybrid objective/subjective operationalization for decision quality using a combination of data envelopment analysis and the weighted additive method. This is a methodological contribution, as, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to combine the weighted additive method, which is well-established in the individual decision making literature, and data envelopment analysis, which has been deployed rarely at the individual level of analysis. Our findings demonstrate that the combination of methods was a useful technique for assessing decision quality. Since the two methods can be automated, they also represent useful techniques to be implemented by online sellers.

An implication of the PLS model is that online retailers should encourage online shoppers to spend more time and develop greater levels of domain knowledge. Educating the customer would enhance both decision quality and decision confidence, which were positively related. Educating the customer runs contrary to the ‘‘buy it now’’ merchandising tactic, but it may help accomplish the objective of having the customer purchase the product. Providing informational links, glossaries and buyers guides may delay purchase, but increase the effort investment and knowledge building that increase decision quality and decision confidence. We suggest that online sellers provide hyperlinks as follows:

1. Click here if you are knowledgeable about the domain (provide a PSE).

2. Click here if you want to learn about the domain (do not provide a PSE).

3. Click here if you are unsure whether you are knowledgeable (provide a quiz of domain knowledge). Then redirect the shopper to either 1) or 2), depending on the quiz score.

Another practical implication is that online sellers offering PSEs should enable customers to save their current set of items, so that they can return to it later, after learning more about the domain. Such a tactic is implemented by Amazon.com with ‘‘wish lists,’’ yet it is far from universal.

If the user has undecided preferences or minimal knowledge, a seller could either educate in an objective manner or shape preferences in a biased manner. For example, sellers may want to shape buyer preferences toward attribute combinations that generate greater profits. Future research could examine alternative communication modes as well. For instance, the user could learn in a conversational mode [56], which may be more effective in reducing the perception of seller bias.

The results show that decision confidence can be predicted from decision quality and perceived ease of use. Decision quality has an impact on perceived usefulness almost as strong in magnitude as the impact from perceived ease of use. Furthermore, the results show that decision quality, not merely the perception that the technology is useful in producing it, has a positive impact. Decision quality is rarely captured in TAM studies but in light of these results should be considered definable, measurable and significant.

## 7. Limitations

Many researchers have used laboratory experiments for studying decision support, including the effects of interactive decision aids [37], search effort and decision quality [17], situational awareness [52] and query interface design [70]. Although a laboratory experiment may be artificial and may foster unrealistic user behavior, we made the task as realistic as possible and checked for authenticity of behavior. A random assignment of subjects to PSEs likely equalized the capabilities and other characteristics of subjects. Since no subjects abandoned their shopping tasks or complained about lack of realism, a laboratory experiment was a reasonable choice.

The experimental subjects, college students, were assumed to be an adequate proxy for online shoppers. This was a reasonable assumption, as the students were motivated to choose optimally and the task was appropriate for them. For a broader sample of online shoppers and greater statistical power, a more heterogeneous sampling frame and larger sample could be gathered. A larger sample size would also enable the use of confirmatory structural equation modeling, e.g., with LISREL, to verify the model.

Our model was quantitative and structural, considering input, process and output variables, but not tracing the processes themselves. Some researchers recommend a verbal protocol for tracing a decision process, in which subjects vocalize their thought processes [26,59]. Despite the possible disruptiveness of a verbal protocol, this method can offer a running commentary useful for glimpsing decision makers cognitive processes as they happen [67,75]. Another relatively ‘‘clear box’’ approach would be to model the user as a Bayesian information processor. That approach would require the updating of probabilistic beliefs as users acquire information [33].

## 8. Future directions

This research has developed a model of online shopping for differentiated products using PSEs. Future research could continue to experiment with alternative PSEs, incorporating alternative PSE components, e.g., collaborative technologies, and information pertaining not only to the product, but to the seller as well. These tools could also be applied to online shopping for services to see how service shopping differs from product shopping. A full range of services, from commodity to differentiated, could be investigated.

Some research has found that it is important to have trust in the e-vendor, particularly through adherence to e-commerce and general business standards [28]. It would be useful to incorporate trust factors in our model. Trust ratings could be manipulated in a laboratory study by stating, for example, that ‘‘the Better Business Bureau has given this business a trust rating of six out of seven’’ to control for pre-existing trust attitudes of users toward real businesses. Alternatively, we could test the current model on existing businesses and add standardized trust surveys. Social presence of the e-vendor [29], the extent to which an evendor seems psychologically present, could also be an important additional factor. Other research has found that virtual sales agents can be perceived as more trustworthy than human sales agents [51], since virtual sales agents can be perceived as more helpful, informational and objective than a human sales agent. As the current research is extended to situations of postsales service, the issues of trust and social presence should be incorporated.

We designed this study to maximize internal validity, using a controlled study of PSE effectiveness on unbranded products in an unbranded web store in a laboratory setting. While this design was chosen to minimize the impact of extraneous factors, future research should consider how best to increase external validity. For example, one could extend our model by measuring, in a field study, intention to transact and whether a transaction occurs. Pavlou [58] developed a model of TAM, trust and perceived risk to explain 56% of the variance in intention to transact. Our behavior decision theoretic variables, decision quality and decision confidence, could be integrated with Pavlou’s model. The current study shows that decision confidence could play an important role in a model of intention to transact.

A longitudinal study of a cohort of online shoppers could show how search effort, domain knowledge, decision quality, decision confidence, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness change over time. Does effort increase monotonically, plateau after a certain point, or follow a parabolic curve? Do earlier PSE adopters have an impact on middle adopters and late adopters? Would later adopters be modeled similarly to earlier adopters or would they rather tap their knowledge through a recommender system built from early adopters? Research that answers these questions will help to improve PSEs, so that both online customers and merchants benefit.

## 9. Conclusion

We developed a PLS model, which comprises a set of behavioral decision theory antecedents to the technology acceptance model, to test our hypotheses regarding search effort, domain knowledge, decision quality, decision confidence, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. Qualitatively, the PLS model shows that the effects of search effort and domain knowledge are mediated through decision quality and decision confidence to impact perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. Quantitatively, the PLS model explains the variance in decision quality (17.3%), decision confidence (28.3%) and perceived usefulness (27.0%). Overall, this study shows that input, process and outcome variables are important for predicting the effectiveness of PSEs.

A PSE can be found in some shopping web sites that sell differentiated products. Web sites offering PSEs include the following: Active Buyers Guide, Epinions, PC Connections, Yahoo! Shopping and America Online Shopping. Interestingly, the following shopping sites have discontinued their PSEs: Lycos Shopping, MSN Shopping, Dealtime/Shopping.com and MySimon. Why have many Web sites discontinued PSEs? Do they lack the technology expertise of Active Buyers Guide and PC Connection? Do they lack the resources or large user populations of Yahoo! and America Online? Were their PSE designs too ‘‘black box’’ or too ‘‘clear box,’’ failing to achieve a ‘‘semi-opaque box’’? Our study did not answer these questions, but it tested hypotheses and produced findings that could help future sites in decisions about employing PSEs.

## Appendix A. Decision quality

In this appendix, we summarize techniques used for our two main measures of decision quality: the weighted additive method and data envelopment analysis.

## A.1. Weighted additive method

In consumer research, an additive utility function is often used [7,25,37,61].

$$
U (x) = U _ {1} \left(x _ {1}\right) + U _ {2} \left(x _ {2}\right) + \dots + U _ {m} \left(x _ {m}\right)
$$

where $x _ { i }$ represents a product attribute value and $w _ { i }$ represents an attribute weight. This is often simplified to a weighted additive linear model (WADD):

$$
U (x) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} w _ {i} * x _ {i}
$$

U is computed (but not necessarily shown) for each product in each set that subjects generate. In this study, sets of products were ranked by U for each subject, providing one measure of decision quality for each product for each subject.

## A.2. Data envelopment analysis (DEA)

DEA is an optimization method, based on linear programming, that can be used to identify dominated (suboptimal) or undominated (optimal) items in a set. DEA empirically derives and assigns optimal weights to all inputs and outputs. It can make simultaneous comparisons of multiple dependent performance measures and provide a scalar measure of best overall outcome, independent of user preferences and of the type and units of the input and output variables.

Traditionally, DEA has been used to simultaneously analyze multiple inputs and outputs of similar production facilities, such as factories, bank branches, or fast-food franchises, to compute their relative production efficiency. The concept and technique can apply more broadly, however. Dominance (also called efficiency) is a way of capturing optimality in any multi-dimensional attribute space. DEA enables the construction of an efficient frontier in multi-dimensional space, which represents the optimal combinations across the full range of the different inputs and outputs.

![](/api/attachments/RWFDG7UP/fulltext/images/005e489cdfd34c13484aeb3dfcb966dd6d1696abc5c478f7f65f850f8134dbfe.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Computer shopping (one input; two outputs).

In the context of this study, the DEA efficient frontier comprises the set of dominant products. This is more subtle than simply including one product that is dominant on each attribute (e.g., the cheapest price, biggest size, etc.). The DEA technique empirically derives the trade-offs among attributes and thus also includes dominant combinations of attributes. The DEA frontier is preference independent, as it is derived from the product space. Assuming a sufficient number of products are distributed across the frontier, there will be a product choice that is dominant for any given set of preferences. For an example of an efficient frontier illustrating two outputs (memory and disk space) and one input (cost), see Fig. 5.

Computers 1–6 are on the efficient frontier. They represent 100% efficient tradeoffs between memory and disk space, given one fixed input (cost). Computers 7– 10 are a non-zero distance from the efficient frontier. They represent combinations of memory and disk space that are less than 100% efficient.
