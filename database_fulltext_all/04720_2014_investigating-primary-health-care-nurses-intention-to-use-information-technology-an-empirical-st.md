# Investigating primary health care nurses' intention to use information technology: An empirical study in Taiwan

Shin-Yuan Hung <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Jacob Chia-An Tsai <sup>a,1</sup>, Chun-Chin Chuang <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, Chia-Yi 62117, Taiwan, ROC <sup>b</sup> Public Health Bureau, Taichung City, No. 811 Siangsin S. Rd., Nantun District, Taichung City 408, Taiwan, ROC

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 23 February 2011 Received in revised form 17 September 2013 Accepted 24 September 2013 Available online 5 October 2013

Keywords: Primary Health Care Information System Theory of reasoned action Nurses Information technology use

## a b s t r a c t

Information technology (IT) has become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of providing consistent care quality. The applications of the primary health information system (PHIS) in primary health care have rapidly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the care service delivery. Improved understanding of critical antecedents leading the PHIS usage can serve as a foundation for practice to develop implementation strategies and enhance system use for nurses. In accordance with the theory of reasoned action (TRA), this study aims to investigate the antecedents that in<sup>fl</sup>uence nurses to use the PHIS, and to incorporate a framework of technology acceptance by individual professionals to improve the IT attitude in the health care setting. A total of 768 valid questionnaires were completed by primary health care nurses. The results indicated that compatibility has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the perceived usefulness and perceived trust in the PHIS. If primary health care nurses perceive the PHIS to be trustworthy then they are more likely to consider it to be useful, In addition, when the PHIS is seen as both trustworthy and useful, then nurses are more likely to have a positive attitude toward using it. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have valuable implications for technology management practice in promoting PHIS usage by primary health care nurses.

## 1. Introduction

The primary health information system (PHIS) is a technological aid to primary health care services, and is expected to bring about a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant improvement in the quality of care practice and delivery. The implementation of information technology (IT) is being acknowledged to have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on health care quality and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [69,71]. Increasing the use of the PHIS by nurses is generally recognized as an important facilitator of primary health care improvement in Taiwan. But, these nurses are experiencing heavy workloads as the ratio of nurses to care recipients in urban areas is 1:14,273 people [25]. Because of this, taking on a new system appears burdensome to them in their already busy routine practice, hence promoting the PHIS to these nurses and avoiding negative feedback is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and costly [21,43,44,75].

It is unlikely that the full bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an integrated national care system can be fully realized without the usage of PHIS by nurses. Any single nurse resisting its use may impair care quality because the lack of updated information may result in misleading medical data in multiple sub-systems. Many health care organizations have experienced resistance to the implementation and the use of IT in the workplace from nurses and other health care professionals [13,38,39,45,51,67]. Generally, the use of the PHIS is discretionary and nurses are not compelled to use it, partly because they can still access the legacy systems. Moreover, health care professionals are entitled to make their own decisions regarding system usage [27]. In response to this concern, researchers have suggested that the decision-making process on IT usage should be addressed [6,27,58]. This could increase the possibility of adopting the new technology in their workplace [6,58]. Traditionally, discussions of IT acceptance by health care professionals have focused primarily on the role of physicians [13,19,52,74]. Whereas the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from these previous studies may not be suitable for extrapolation to nurses in primary health care [71], little attention has been addressed to IT acceptance by them [56,70], who are actually the main providers of primary health care [25,75].

Motivated by the need to further the understanding of nurses' intentions to use PHIS, this study structures the theory of reasoned action (TRA) as a basis for investigating these. Health care professionals usually differ from general users in terms of their innovative adoption process [71]; an extension of attitude within a framework for technology acceptance is used to comprehensively understand the critical antecedents of nurses' attitudes toward PHIS usage [26,41,48]. This study builds on Chau and Hu's [20] proposed framework where the individual context has a direct impact on IT acceptance; the technological context has a direct impact on the individual context and would be directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the implementation context. In the individual context, attitude and co-workers' viewpoints are proposed as two essential components. The perceived IT attributes in the technological context are described as perceived usefulness and perceived trust. In addition, introducing new technology into the workplace may radically change the established work processes. To address such concerns, system compatibility in the implementation context is described as the <sup>fi</sup>t between PHIS and work needs.

Understanding these factors could provide useful information to policy makers and government agencies on how to successfully introduce PHIS. Further, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of research regarding nurses' utilization of IT lacks adequate studies to explain attitude formation. The particular beliefs that contribute to attitude formation can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by observing the causal relationships in this study providing a reference base for IS vendors in planning and evaluating the PHIS design. We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss primary health care nursing and PHIS, the TRA theory, the framework for technological acceptance by an individual professional and the review of previous IT acceptance studies. Second, we propose the investigated model and hypotheses based on the theoretical support. Third, using the data collected from primary health care centers throughout Taiwan, we examine the causal links in the proposed model and provide several implications for associated practitioners. Lastly, limitations and future directions are brie<sup>fl</sup>y presented.

## 2. Theoretical background

2.1. Primary health care nursing and the primary health information system

Primary health care, with its focus on delivering basic care and disease prevention services for everyone, is proposed as a way to create a healthy environment [21,75]. Currently, Taiwan is facing the problem of an unequal distribution of health care resources with the majority of nurses and the location of larger medical institutes being in urban areas. Resources for patients living in the countryside are lagging behind [25]. Those who live in these rural areas (i.e. mountainous regions, remote islands, the countryside and small towns) rely mainly on the medical services provided by local primary health care centers [25]. These health care centers comprise the total epidemic prevention network, which means that primary health care nurses play an important role in disease prevention and the improvement of public health [25]. Through the reinforcement of primary health care services in each district, people living in the countryside receive adequate medical services and information from local primary health care nurses.

The advantage of building a primary health care system for people is that nurses are highly capable of organizing suitable care services to satisfy the basic needs of local patients [25]. The nurses have a deep understanding of patients' needs because of their long-term interaction with the local community [25,75]. They provide the basic level of health care service in Taiwan, and their actions deeply in<sup>fl</sup>uence the quality of medical services nationally. As suggested by the Department of Health (DOH) [25], primary health care nurses should put more emphasis on participating in the improvement and prevention of public health and also on developing a partnership with local community teams. The fast and changing health environment highlights several issues which primary health care nurses must face in Taiwan such as establishing a community health service network and promoting a standard for health service, and more importantly structuring a system for disease evaluation and monitoring [61].

Nurses have to spend additional effort to ensure that patient information is consistent and correct in the legacy systems, potentially causing an obstruction to comprehensive care service. The DOH intends to set up an integrated national primary health care system, PHIS, in each primary health care center to support the delivery of a better and faster response to local patients. Through the use of such a system, nurses have the potential to improve disease management and quality of care, and to provide more reliable care services. In early 2007, DOH was seeking an IS vendor to develop a PHIS which is capable of performing disease management, establishing a disease prevention system, and monitoring chronic disease care and other major health problems that may affect the public. The main requirements for a PHIS are addressed in the following: (a) to design the architecture for the primary health care that meets the speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements documented, (b) create an infrastructure that can be extended to allow the integration of the current sub-systems within the PHIS, (c) develop a module based system so that additional modules can be added to the system to meet the future needs. A beta of the full functionality is expected by the end of 2013.

This study is conducted in voluntary usage contexts in which primary health care nurses have a choice about their use of the legacy systems or PHIS in its implementation stage. The PHIS implementation process is <sup>fi</sup>rstly to update the sub-systems that are embedded in the legacy systems, and then have the key users interact with the newly updated functions and provide feedback for their development. However, it is all proving very time-consuming. Apparently, the best way to accommodate the change and reduce the risk of resistance is to keep the routine applications in the legacy systems for nurses to use alongside the updating process. Therefore, the legacy system and PHIS currently exist together. As any improper usage in an unfamiliar system may result in serious injury to patients, nurses are encouraged but not compelled to use PHIS in the implementation stage.

## 2.2. Theory of reasoned action

The TRA, developed by Fishbein and Ajezn [28], states that a person's intention to perform a given behavior is a function of his or her attitude toward that behavior and his or her subjective norm concerning that behavior. The attitude toward performing a certain behavior is assumed to be based on his or her beliefs about that behavior. In other words, attitude is the degree to which one has a positive versus a negative evaluation of a certain behavior [28]. The subjective norm is the person's perception that most people who are important to them think they should or should not perform the behavior [28]. The co-workers' viewpoint is like a kind of subjective norm that in<sup>fl</sup>uences a person to perform a certain behavior. Later, Ajzen [2] integrated perceived behavioral control with TRA to predict individual intention and behavior that are not completely under volitional control. In the theory of planned behavior (TPB), Ajzen [2] believed that persons with a higher perceived behavioral control are more likely to form a strong intention to perform a given behavior than those who perceive that they have lower control.

Based on the concept of the TRA, Davis [23] developed the technology acceptance model (TAM) to predict user acceptance of IT. The TAM posits that the main determinants of behavioral intention are perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use [23]. The TRA, the TPB, and the TAM have provided the theoretical basis for many studies on understanding intention toward IT usage and even for researchers to integrate various theories into their individual behavioral model [45,68,74]. In the TRA, attitude is the central construct in understanding and explaining behavioral intention [4,8]. Compared with the effect of the subjective norm, the causal relationship of IT acceptance with a user's attitude is much stronger than that with the subjective norm in normal situations [35,36,72]. Basically, the use of PHIS in primary health care services involves a series of evaluation processes within the nurses' mind to verify the value of usage. At the same time, the nurses may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social pressure, such as from colleagues, physicians or head nurses. The core concept throughout the TRA allows for the explanation and prediction of IT usage under volitional control, with the potential to increase the understanding of nurses' attitude toward PHIS usage.

2.3. A generic framework for technology acceptance by individual professionals

Nurses are professionals in an emergency environment. They can provide health care with skill and responsibility. Their knowledge, education, and training enable them to provide professional support to physicians and other cooperators to save patients' lives. Chau and Hu [20] proposed that individual professional acceptance of IT can be predicted by three associated contexts: the individual, the technological, and the implementation contexts. As shown in Fig. 1, this framework is a three-layered structure for examining individual professional acceptance of a particular technology, where the technological context serves as a middle ground between the individual context and the implementation context.

The individual context refers to the essential characteristics of individual users [20]. Chau and Hu [20] stated that the technological context concentrates on the important perceived technological attributes. In this context, the perceived technological attributes can be used as mediums for assessing the effects of the implementation context on the individual context. Chau and Hu [20] de<sup>fi</sup>ned the implementation context as “the speci<sup>fi</sup>c professional environment where the investigated technology acceptance takes place.” The implementation of a new technology in the workplace could potentially cause changes in the work process, and that is seen as a key challenge for the user and the organization. Therefore, the implementation context plays an important role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing PHIS usage since it has a direct impact on the process of delivery of care services.

2.4. Review of prior studies on technology acceptance by health care professionals

Few studies have attempted to integrate TRA, TPB, or TAM into the health care setting to understand the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT acceptance by health care professionals. Chau and Hu [19] decomposed the TPB and the TAM model to provide a basic framework for understanding physicians' acceptance of telemedicine. The results indicated that the causal link between attitude and behavioral intention was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in all three models. However, the causal link between the subjective norm and behavioral intention was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the TPB or the decomposed model. Yi et al. [74] integrated the TAM, the TPB, and the innovation diffusion theory (IDT) to examine the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence PDA acceptance by physicians. They found that perceived usefulness was the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant of physicians' intention to accept PDA. In contrast to Chau and Hu [19], they found that the subjective norm had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Unfortunately, they did not clearly examine this different <sup>fi</sup>nding but instead highlighted the importance of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in facilitating IT acceptance by health care professionals.

![](/api/attachments/AS35WKQ2/fulltext/images/ee6742c5bc88fa7d7f08fdc92000b3cd54aad35d62a2a62a3dfda9f78aa0eca9.jpg)  
Fig. 1. A framework for technology acceptance by individual professionals. Adapted from Chau and Hu [20].

Bhattacherjee and Hikmet [13] proposed a model to examine physicians' resistance to IT usage. The results indicated that resistance to change has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on IT usage. This resistance was caused by the physicians' worrying about losing control over their work through relying on the system. Chang et al. [18] used the uni<sup>fi</sup>ed theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) to investigate the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing physicians' acceptance of clinical decision support systems. The results showed that performance expectancy and effort expectancy have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on physicians' intention; however, the impact of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on intention was less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Pare et al. [52] conducted a survey to understand physicians' acceptance of clinical information systems (CIS). Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated that both a positive attitude toward CIS usage and greater perceived usefulness of the CIS were critical factors in fostering their acceptance of the system.

Alqurain et al. [6] found that the variance in nurses' attitudes toward the use of health care technology is associated with nationality, level of education, computer experience, and computer skill. Thus, providing adequate training for nurses is one way of increasing their positive attitude toward health care technology. Koivunen [39] found that computer and Internet skills played important roles in the successful implementation of the new IT in psychiatric nursing. The authors suggest that training and technical support should be provided to encourage nurses to use IT. Most studies based on the TRA or the TPB have found that IT usage is, to a greater extent, driven more by attitude than either the subjective norm or perceived behavioral control [35,36,72]. Similarly, Dillon et al. [26] found that nurses' attitudes toward electronic patient record systems (EPRS) are determined by overall attitude and age, and attitude is strongly related to the intention to adopt EPRS.

Several studies have been conducted in Taiwan, to understand the critical factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing IT acceptance by nurses. Yu and Yang [75] demonstrated that a positive attitude is the important factor encouraging nurses to participate in Web-based learning. In addition, Chen et al. [21] discovered that perceived usefulness is much more important than attitude in determining the acceptance of Web-based learning during the pre-implementation stage. Wu et al. [73] observed that compatibility, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected mobile health care system (MHS) acceptance by health care professionals. Compatibility has a direct effect on intention to use and perceived usefulness. The same <sup>fi</sup>nding has been rediscovered by investigating the acceptance of online courses in nursing schools [65]. Tung et al. found that perceived usefulness has mediated the effect between compatibility and user intention, and concluded that the more the nursing students perceive the online courses to be userfriendly, the more they will think them useful [65]. Further, Tung et al. [66] re<sup>fi</sup>ned their model [65] and incorporated trust into the model to probe the acceptance of electronic logistics information systems by nurses. The result supported the role of trust playing an important role in acceptance and showed that it has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on intention to use as well as perceived usefulness.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The proposed model is referred to as the PHIS usage model, as shown in Fig. 2. The dependent variable is the behavioral intention to use PHIS, which is theoretically posited as the proper construct to present IT usage [19,20,68]. In this study, we adopted a three-layered framework<sup>3</sup> [20] to further enhance the understanding of the antecedents of a nurse's attitude within a professional context.

![](/api/attachments/AS35WKQ2/fulltext/images/0bba1c79640c726efdeaeef2382e4138ea7c603a821a021791d3b05401466bea.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Research model.

The individual users are primary health care nurses. Nurses constitute a group of care providers who need to cooperate with other care providers, such as physicians and pharmacists. It is important to know that effective care requires nurses to work as a team where all the members hold the same values to avoid con<sup>fl</sup>ict [60]. Nurses take co-workers' opinions into account when making decisions and formulating attitudes toward innovation [60]. The nature of their working style may shape distinctive individual characteristics, which in turn produce potential effects on their IT usage. For primary health care nurses, attitude and co-workers' viewpoints are adopted as essential individual characteristics in their work environment corresponding to the elements in the individual context [20].

Nurses may view the technology attributes differently from the general users as they can be overburdened with a heavy and diverse professional workload [25]. A negative feedback, voiced frequently in the implementation stage is that the use of PHIS is timeconsuming and increases the amount of workload when the system output cannot be guaranteed to be accurate. A robust system with preventative actions to pre-failure conditions is necessary. The important technology attributes in a nurse's mind is a reliable system that does not disrupt workload productivity. Perceived usefulness and perceived trust are therefore posited as essential characteristics in the technological context, playing an important role in mediating the link between the individual context and the implementation context.

The implementation context of the PHIS is the setting where nurses provide basic care services to patients and interact with them. Implementing new technology may force nurses to adjust their work style, yet these adjustments could result in errors in care service delivery. On the other hand, because primary health care nurses generally follow procedures requested by physicians [25,60] and they increasingly become accustomed to a particular work style over time, nurses are more likely to use a system which is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally compatible with their work style. System compatibility is therefore posited as an essential characteristic in the implementation context.

The main purpose of this study is to understand the antecedents of attitude and behavioral intention involving PHIS usage. Through the integration of the TRA and the three-layered framework, six factors are proposed in the research model. The following section discusses the relationships among these factors and proposes related hypotheses.

## 3.1. The implementation context

Compatibility is the degree to which the PHIS is consistent with nurses' work practices or preferences. Determining whether it is consistent is an important issue because its function has been specifically updated and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to meet the current needs. When the user sees that a particular IT is compatible with their work practice or style, then they will start to realize the usefulness or relative advantage of using it [19,49]. An IT system that is consistent with its user's preferences is more likely to be perceived as an enabler of work performance [37]. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. The compatibility of PHIS with nurses' work styles has a positive effect on their perceived usefulness of it.

The implementation of the PHIS could lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes in the handling of primary health care services, altering many of the manual care procedures of the old system. Nurses need compatible technology [11] and a perception of greater compatibility would lead to greater trust in the system because the user would be more willing to rely on the functions provided by it. The results from a previous study indicate that system quality has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user trust [62]. Compatibility would create a belief that the PHIS is worth using and is consistent with the existing needs, resulting in increased trust in the system. In a review of prior studies, compatibility was shown to build trust in the e-commerce context as well as in the marketing one [17]. We believe that the degree to which the PHIS is perceived as consistent with their professional practice will affect nurses' trust concerning the use of the system. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. The compatibility of PHIS with nurses' work styles has a positive effect on their perceived trust in it.

## 3.2. The technological context

An information system has a greater likelihood of being evaluated as positive or favorable if it is perceived to be useful. Perceived usefulness refers to nurses' subjective evaluations of the extent to which using PHIS will increase their work performance. The anticipated work performance from using IT will have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the individual's attitude toward it [63]. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3. Nurses' perceived usefulness of PHIS has a positive effect on their attitudes toward it.

An information system has a greater likelihood of being evaluated as positive if it is perceived to be trustworthy. Perceived trust refers to the con<sup>fi</sup>dence a nurse has in the functions that the PHIS will perform. Pennington et al. [55] believe that perceived trust re<sup>fl</sup>ects a belief system that results in attitude change. A system will build trust beliefs in an individual's mind if it can perform the expected function and bring about the anticipated consequences, which in turn motivates the individual to have a positive evaluation of the use of the system.

A positive attitude is the likely outcome of the summation of trust beliefs regarding the system. Pavlou and Lin [54] asserted that trust is a kind of behavioral belief that directly in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer attitude in the online transaction context. McKnight and Chervany [47] described trust as related to positive feelings or attitudes. For example, trust creates a positive evaluation of online shopping when it provides a buyer with a positive expectation of a virtual transaction. Furthermore, trust decreases negative feelings toward online shopping, such as those involving risk considerations and privacy issues, leading a buyer to have a positive expectation of a satisfactory transaction. In the e-commerce <sup>fi</sup>eld, the attitudes regarding the seller are subject to the perceptions of trust in the seller, company, or brand image. Most previous studies have indicated that trust beliefs are related to attitude and that the relationship between attitude and trust should follow the belief–attitude– intention sequence [34,47,54,55]. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4. Nurses' trust in the PHIS has a positive effect on their attitudes toward it.

Trust has been recognized as a determinant of perceived usefulness, and there is theoretical support for incorporating perceived trust with perceived usefulness. In the e-commerce context, Gefen [31] indicated that trust would increase the perceived usefulness of a Web site because consumers believe that the products or services delivered by the vendor will satisfy their needs as well as the products' expected usefulness. Pavlou [53] believed that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trust on perceived usefulness is based on the credibility attributed by the customer to the sellers in providing their expected advantage. On the contrary, customers would not expect to gain any advantage if they do not perceive the sellers as trustworthy. If the PHIS can be trusted to perform as promised according to the nurses' beliefs, the likelihood of gaining the expected advantage from using the PHIS will exist in the nurses' minds. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5. Nurses' perceived trust in the PHIS has a positive effect on their perceived usefulness of it.

## 3.3. The individual context

The TRA asserts that behavioral intention is shaped by one's attitude and their subjective norm. Attitude refers to positive or negative feelings toward performing a particular behavior [3]. The more positive the individual's attitude toward PHIS usage is, the higher the possibility that he or she will perform the particular behavior. The subjective norm is the perception of the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence that most peers, colleagues, and friends who are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to the individual think he or she should or should not perform a certain behavior [3,63]. Chau and Hu [20] argued that the subjective norm has a less signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant effect on physicians' intention to use technology as they are likely to develop independent evaluations. Broadly speaking, most medical services are provided by a single or a few physicians, supported by a group of nurses. Nurses are accustomed to working with various cooperators; their training and education encourages them to respect others' opinions and suggestions. This leads them to put relatively more weight on evaluating others' opinions and suggestions regarding a particular behavior. Based on these arguments, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H6. Nurses' attitudes toward PHIS have a positive effect on their behavioral intention to use the system.

H7. Co-workers' viewpoints toward PHIS usage have a positive effect on nurses' intention to use the system.

Attitudes change in response to social in<sup>fl</sup>uences which in turn are affected by the opinions or suggestions expressed by colleagues or other meaningful peers [15,40]. For example, the source credibility of a message has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a critical determinant in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing one's attitude toward a certain behavior [14]. Health care professionals will believe that the use of the PHIS is a good idea if this message comes from a persuader who is important to him or her [76]. Moreover, they may develop a positive attitude if the behavior is recommended or advocated by colleagues who are important to them. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H8. Co-workers' viewpoints toward PHIS usage have a positive effect on nurses' attitudes toward PHIS.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Measures

For this study, a set of instruments was designed to measure the research constructs. All of them were adapted from previous studies and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to <sup>fi</sup>t the content of the primary health care system in Taiwan. Seven-point Likert's scales, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, were used for each item.

Compatibility, measured with three items and adapted from the instrument developed by Taylor and Todd [63], was de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Rogers [57] as “the degree to which the innovation <sup>fi</sup>ts with the potential adopter's existing values, previous experience and current needs.” Perceived usefulness, measured with four items and adapted from Taylor and Todd [63], was de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Davis [23] as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance.” Trust, measured with three items and adapted from the instrument created by Gefen [30], was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the con<sup>fi</sup>- dence a user has in his or her favorable expectations of how the particular system will perform, based, in many cases, on previous interactions.”

Attitude, measured with four items and adapted from Taylor and Todd [63], was de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Ajzen and Fishbein [3] as “personal positive or negative feelings about performing a given behavior.” The co-workers' viewpoints, measured with two items and adapted from Taylor and Todd [63], was de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Ajzen and Fishbein [3] as “the person's perception that most people who are important to him think he should or should not perform the behavior in question.” As the survey was conducted in the stage of PHIS implementation, the actual usage of PHIS in each primary health care center was not very widespread. Adopting intention to use to approximate the actual usage is generally supported by prior studies since it is a good predictor of actual usage [19,20,71,74]. Intention to use, measured with two items and adapted from the instrument developed by Venkatesh and Davis [68], was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “personal intention to use the particular system.”

A pretest is particularly important when instruments are applied to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. We conducted a pretest punctiliously in order to verify the measurement items used in this study. Three IT professionals and one health care information management professional were asked to review the measurement items. Based on their feedback, we modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the ambiguous wordings and addressed sequencing problems to prevent respondents' confusion. Further, we conducted a pilot test using the responses of 30 primary health care nurses to assess the questionnaire design. We collected their feedback on the presentation and organization of the questionnaire to ensure that respondents would be able to fully understand the meaning of each measurement item. The <sup>fi</sup>nal questionnaire used to measure the respective constructs is shown in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Sample and data collection

A contact person was selected in each health care center and the questionnaires were sent to them along with a covering letter. They delivered them to the targeted respondents then collected them later for analysis. Some valuable coupons and digital cameras were used as incentive goods to motivate the respondents' participation. Procedures were in place to ensure that data collection was performed in a consistent manner. The contact persons were encouraged to meticulously follow the step-by-step procedures on how to collect the data, and they were under no pressure to complete the survey quickly. Progress was tracked regularly and any questions or concerns were carefully addressed.

Primary health care nurses who had had direct experience of PHIS use supporting their daily work were the target respondents in this study. There were two reasons for this. Firstly, several research constructs such as perceived usefulness and perceived trust are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as perceived technological attributes that require only respondents who have had a certain experience of PHIS usage to answer. Secondly, only respondents with direct interaction with PHIS would be able to fully appreciate whether the PHIS <sup>fi</sup>tted the current needs or not.

There were a total of 2532 nurses who serviced 369 primary health care centers in Taiwan [24]. In order to increase the response rate, a reminder letter was sent to each primary health care center that failed to return the questionnaires after two weeks. A total of 818 questionnaires were returned. However, only 768 questionnaires were considered valid since 50 were not completely <sup>fi</sup>lled out. Hence, 30.3% of primary health care nurses are the target respondents in this study.

The demographic data on them is summarized in Table 1. Most were female with many years of experience in primary health care centers.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Measurement model

This study conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) to access the collected data in order to develop a measurement model and a structural model. Firstly, we conducted con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) using AMOS to test the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the measurement model. Secondly, the testing of the hypotheses was supplemented by an analysis using AMOS.

## 6. Discussion

This study adopted the TRA [3] and the three-layered framework [20] to investigate the antecedents of attitude and behavioral intention involving PHIS usage. This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study, to our knowledge, to explain the critical antecedents that affect the formation of IT attitude in the context of primary health care in Taiwan. The variance explained for each endogenous construct is higher than 40%, which implies that the critical factors in shaping IT usage by nurses have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study. The results indicate that the intent to use the PHIS involves a positive attitude and co-workers' viewpoints. A positive attitude is a combination of highly perceived usefulness, highly perceived trust, and positive co-workers' viewpoints. In addition, these nurses are more likely to consider the PHIS as useful if they perceive it to be trustworthy and less likely to create compatibility problems in the workplace.

Following the three-layered framework, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that system compatibility is the <sup>fi</sup>rst facilitator to shape a positive IT attitude. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with prior studies which show that IT is a useful instrument for improving work performance when it is fully compatible with the current work environment [65,66,73]. Moreover, the nurses would overcome their distrust of PHIS if they realized that the system was compatible with their daily practice. The applications in health care IT for the handling of medical data usually need to be customized, based on a professional's requirements and judgment [12]. When IT is tailored to the work environmental conditions of practice sites, it can be set up appropriately to give nurses more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in it [16]. They would be more willing to rely on a customized system, not fearing unexpected consequences.

The technological context is the bridge which connects the implementation context to the individual context. Attitude toward PHIS usage as determined by perceived usefulness and perceived trust is an essential characteristic in the technological context. The issues related to privacy, trust, data security and attitude have been highlighted by prior studies [42,48,66]. The empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings have indicated that perceived trust has a direct effect on attitude and also an indirect effect through perceived usefulness. PHIS can integrate the multiple subsystems and reduce mistakes evident in the manual input process; however, new technology could also introduce a new set of errors. Nurses would perceive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using the PHIS if the system performed according to their trust beliefs [66]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the nurses' perceptions of system bene<sup>fi</sup>ts would be recognized, especially if human medication errors could be reduced and the trustworthiness of the technology could be ensured. In this connection, nurses are more likely to have a positive attitude toward using PHIS if they believe that the system can bring about certain advantages without compromising on care quality. If using speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT in the work-place can increase job performance then it is a key determinant for health care professionals to construct a positive feeling toward technology [52,66,73]. Chau and Hu [20] explained that the nature of health care professionals' tool-oriented view of technology results in acceptance only when the desired performance can be achieved by using speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT in their practice. Results from the relationship between the implementation context and technological context showed that useful technology not only needs to satisfy the nurses' current practice but also needs to con<sup>fi</sup>rm their trust beliefs.

In the individual context, primary health care nurses' attitudes plays a relatively more important role than the co-workers' viewpoints [35,36,72]. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by Fishbein and Ajezn [28], a person's intention is mainly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by his or her attitude toward performing a given behavior. The co-workers' viewpoints have a significant impact on PHIS usage as well as on attitude. Although prior studies have indicated mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the relationship between the subjective norm and the intention to use [1,20,29,74,76], this study has demonstrated that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the co-workers' viewpoints on PHIS usage, which responds to a gap in previous studies, calls for testing social factors and health care IT acceptance [76]. Zheng et al. [76] argued that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence plays an important part as a facilitator especially when the cost of adoption is complex and the result unknown, and concluded that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact toward innovation adoption in the setting of health care. Generally, obtaining evaluations and information from colleagues to assess the cost and the consequence of innovation adoption is effective [76]. Compared with other health care professionals in the medical practice, nurses are more likely to require additional suggestions and information from co-workers when they are using IT [5] or making a decision [64]. Furthermore, the physician and nurse working relationship has been acknowledged as a critical factor that contributes to the quality of care in Taiwanese medical practice [60]. This evidence provides support for the role of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the innovation adoption process. Since the education program encourages primary health care nurses to cooperate with their colleagues as an effective team [25], we believe that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence should be considered to have an impact on primary health care nurses attitudes and behavioral intentions.

The confusion in explaining the effect of the subjective norm on behavioral intention has been discussed in IT literature over the past decade [59]. Schepers and Wetzels [59] found that the type of respondent, type of technology and different countries' cultures all have a moderating effect on the subjective norm and the intention to use. Although the results in Schepers and Wetzels's work indicated that the subjective norm had a larger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on behavioral intention in Western culture than in non-Western culture, we believe that the teamwork behavior in the Taiwanese health care setting is likely to come from a cooperative and collectivistic orientation [60] which increases the impact of peers' opinion on behavioral intention [59]. In our current work, the result also implies that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the co-workers' viewpoints on intention to use will be varying across different environmental factors such as location when using the PHIS. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the co-workers' viewpoints is stronger for nurses who work in rural areas than for those who work in urban centers. In Taiwan, nurses in urban health care centers have more experience and are channeled toward innovative adoption (i.e. computer training program), whereas nurses in rural centers have less experience and are not so directed toward it [21,74]. This could be the reason for the greater effect of supervisors/ peers persuasion in respect of nurses in rural areas.

## 7. Implications

## 7.1. Practical implications

PHIS implementation provides a way to improve disease management and the quality of care for each patient. However, resistance from nurses continues to pose a challenge to this process. This study suggests that the usage of PHIS by nurses can be a much more complicated process than expected. PHIS is a nation-wide system so the content and the interface is consistent across every primary health care center, making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by individual user's requirements once implemented. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effort should be put into the collection of nurses' requirements and suggestions delivered to the system developer. Although key users can participate in the PHIS development process, the interaction between the nurses and the system developer is not adequate, and as we observed, most of the participants are nurses only. Yet the applications in PHIS cover various needs in national health care management practices so we suggest that policy makers should also participate in the development process, drawing together national disease management and public health management. On the other side, the issue of system compatibility should be mitigated, by re-engineering the work process to accommodate PHIS more ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciently. However, any process of re-engineering requires additional time and cost, so the manager should balance the cost and the result of a disruptive change in the nursing process cautiously. The system developer should play an active role in the implementation process, conducting system integration testing to evaluate whether the updated version is compatible with the existing software and the work style of the nurses.

The trust toward PHIS can be improved if a common system implementation standard is established. For example, in the United States, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) is established for personal health information protection and provides guidelines for health care IT rules, standards and implementation. However, some of the HIPAA privacy rules may not be applicable in Taiwan. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between Taiwan and other advanced countries is that the medical records of patients in Taiwan belong to the hospital rather than the individual patient or insurance company. Because of this the medical providers should take on more responsibility for medical data privacy and security management [25]. Health care IT design in Taiwan always adopts the decisions of Health Level 7 (HL7) standards. Although the modifying of HL7 standards in Taiwan is faster than before, the rapid technological change still makes the progress of software vendors and health care stakeholders dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in regard to fully integrating HL7 standards into the system. This inconsistent governance makes maintaining a good system quality problematic and results in health care professionals starting to address the issues of data security and privacy in their practice. We would encourage policy makers to speed up the process of updating standards. Also the integrative process between the software vendors and standards since this study found that the issue of system trustworthiness has received signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention by nurses. Given that PHIS can reduce the cost of medical services, with the savings <sup>fl</sup>owing back to the insurance companies, medical providers and government agencies, we suggest that all those involved could co-develop the health care IT certi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This would re<sup>fl</sup>ect the quali<sup>fi</sup>cations of security and safety, which in turn could provide more indicators for health care professionals to determine whether the system can be operated safely or otherwise.

The education program is an effective interactive mechanism to structure the social network and then reinforce the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social factors on PHIS use. In this process, the manager is able to identify the leading users and permeate them through the social network, which in turn will improve resisters' negative evaluation of PHIS. We suggest that the education program should include two activities related to the technological attributes of PHIS. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is making nurses, especially the leading users; realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that result from the implementation of PHIS. It should address the issue that PHIS is capable of reducing work-loads and increasing work performance. Some studies showed that technology diffuses rapidly in the central social network if the user can perceive the advantages of it [69]. Using leading users' in<sup>fl</sup>uence to promote PHIS diffusion is an effective approach that could be adopted and this diffusion effect would be especially successful in the rural health care centers. At the same time, managers should be aware of whether the nurses' coworkers disapprove of their use of the PHIS. Minimizing any formal and informal disapproval from peers or colleagues is one way to increase PHIS usage.

The second activity is inviting software vendors to be involved indepth in the education sessions with the primary health care nurses. The PHIS design needs to take into account the security required for nurses from a variety of perspectives. Software vendors can demonstrate what mistake-proo<sup>fi</sup>ng mechanisms have been embedded into it. During the continuing education sessions, we believe that the nurses' feedback will comprise valuable information, and software vendors can focus their efforts on it. Further, the functionality of PHIS can also be assessed and veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by nurses at the same time, which may improve the positive PHIS attributes in the nurses' minds and thus promote acceptance of it in the future. In addition, we suggest that nursing school administrations should consider training future nurses in its use. Relaying its functions will greatly accelerate its acceptance.

## 7.2. Limitations and future research

Although this study provides insights for academics and practitioners regarding the usage of the PHIS, it nevertheless has several limitations. It focuses on extending the antecedents of nurses' attitudes and their impact on PHIS usage. However we believe that there are additional factors that have an impact on attitude or behavioral intention that were not included in this study. For example, we did not collect nurses' prior IT experience and the effect of training in the current work place. We did not measure the training period and specify a time frame for use, largely because most primary health care centers were conducting their training programs at different times. This limited our ability to further test whether nurses more readily accept PHIS after participating in a training program. Given that many studies have pointed out that IT competence/training has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on nurses' acceptance of IT and perceived IT values [7,21,39,73], we would encourage future studies to address these issues in order to extend our model. Furthermore, in mandatory conditions, a user's use intention is largely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the con<sup>fi</sup>dence they have in their ability to perform a certain behavior. As more and more health care IT implementation is under mandatory conditions (i.e. electronic health records and clinical information systems), we suggest that future study could probe whether perceived ability would be an important factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing nurses in becoming early or late adopters of technology.

The data collection conducted in Taiwan was of a cross-sectional nature and only primary health care nurses using PHIS were included. Thus generalizing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to primary health care in other countries would require veri<sup>fi</sup>cation in view of the differences in culture and policy. Moreover, we suggest that future studies investigate which antecedents are critical in PHIS usage by nurses in general or further, interviews could be conducted with the nurses who have resisted using PHIS in the implementation stage. This could provide more understanding about the challenges and problems they face. The dependent variable, intention to use, is measured by the self-reported intention to approximate actual usage in the PHIS implementation stage, while several prior studies discovered that nurses' perceptions of technological attributes and behavioral intention tended to change over time [7,44]. The current results provide a baseline for future research to study the change of technological attributes at the post implementation of PHIS and thus enhance the understanding of IT attitude formation over time. If such future studies are conducted, researchers could extend our model to examine subsequent actual use of PHIS. Through the comparison of the differences between behavioral intention (self-reported questionnaire) and the actual use, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings could improve the possible measurement bias in this study.

As this study showed, system compatibility is a key facilitator given that most IT innovation in health care organization will usually change the manual procedure. Yet, it is unclear which critical functionality of PHIS can reduce the problems of nurses' concerns and which design can best <sup>fi</sup>t their needs. Future research would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from taking a task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) [33] perspective to understand user preferences and task needs in the design of health care IT for nurses. Although social in<sup>fl</sup>uence has shown inconsistent results, the co-workers' viewpoints play an important role having a direct and indirect effect on intention to use. However, we measured the co-workers' viewpoints generally instead of including different types of social interaction mechanisms [76]. Such understanding requires the expansion of the normative component into different mechanisms to test this effect in the future, and thus advance our knowledge of the co-workers' viewpoints in IT usage by health care professionals.

Last, although preventive procedures had been built up in advance to reduce the possible bias that can arise from using of varying contact individuals in the data collection, this study suggests that future research must be even more careful. The communication between each contact individual and their respondents may not have been totally equal in terms of the explanation and description of why our survey was being done. The nature of words used, facial expressions and subjective opinions may have indirectly affected whether or not the respondent completed the questionnaire. Inevitably the contact individual may have introduced personal bias into the questions thus affecting the understanding of respondents, causing them to fail to answer questions objectively. To avoid such problems arising it is critical to explain carefully the objectives of the survey to the contact individuals and set up communication procedures to de<sup>fi</sup>ne what is required of the respondent, prior to the survey. Contact individuals must retain an objective perspective concerning the data collection. Researchers should monitor and control the whole process to check if any inconsistencies are arising among the different contact individuals. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c instructions should be carefully developed to avoid the situation where the respondent does not understand a question properly [50]. A covering letter with clear instructions is necessary to ensure that the respondents fully understand the questions being asked and are responding in the appropriate context.

## 8. Conclusions

This study applies the TRA model with the framework for technology acceptance by individual professionals to study PHIS usage in the primary health care setting. Based on the data collected from the nurses who had direct interaction with PHIS in the implementation stage, we found support for our proposed model that compatibility has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on shaping a positive evaluation of the functionally useful and trustworthy. The responsibility of improving system compatibility should not be limited to the engineers involved in the system development but should be also extended to management who could indicate whether there are any speci<sup>fi</sup>c resources available for adjusting the existing working process to be more in line with the new system. Perceived usefulness is not only in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the degree of system compatibility but also by the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of system trustworthiness. As we discussed, in order to enhance perceived usefulness, management should build up a strategy which can create system compatibility and then carry out a detailed test of the system. This would ensure that the PHIS is compatible with work requirements and the output can be trusted by professionals.

Further, perceived usefulness and perceived trust both have a direct effect on changing nurses' attitudes toward using PHIS that in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences behavioral intention. Within the health care center there needs to be a systematic evaluation and a certi<sup>fi</sup>cation service in practice. Systematic job evaluation and IT certi<sup>fi</sup>cation services are the feasible ways for management to inform nurses that using PHIS will bene<sup>fi</sup>t their job performance without the worry that its use may carry potential risks to patients. The management should con<sup>fi</sup>gure a systematic approach to monitor nurses' work performance and ensure that secure standards are in place and functioning effectively. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the co-workers' viewpoints on behavioral intention is supported in this study but the relationship will be moderated by environmental factors. It shows that a nurse's positive image can be enhanced as they follow his or her colleagues' recommendations to develop the initiative to use PHIS. Additional efforts in management include creating an open atmosphere that allows nurses to feel comfortable sharing their viewpoints about using PHIS with co-workers.

Traditionally, nurses have been highlighted as a group of care providers who are unwilling to accept IT when it is implemented in the workplace [6,22]. The present study contributes to the understanding of PHISs in Taiwan and identi<sup>fi</sup>es critical roles in the formation of IT attitudes. This work advances prior knowledge that focused on understanding IT usage by nurses. In the foreseeable future, the increasing trend among primary health care centers to computerize their work place will lead to the mandatory use of PHIS. As such, our research model may not perform as well in such contexts as it does when usage is voluntary. Given that mandatory usage is outside an individual's volitional control, a theoretical concern will threaten the validity of our proposed model. The integration of TPB and our proposed model could be an alternative solution to this potential issue as it has the theoretical base to explain usage intention in low volitional control. The decision-making process model built in this study can serve as a research foundation that helps the practice for enhancing IT usage by health care professionals.
