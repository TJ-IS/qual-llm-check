# Jack of All, Master of Some: Information Network and Innovation in Crowdsourcing Communities

Elina H. Hwang,<sup>a</sup> Param Vir Singh,<sup>b</sup> Linda Argote<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. This study investigates how the information that individuals accumulate through helping others in a customer support crowdsourcing community influences their ability to generate novel, popular, and feasible ideas in an innovation crowdsourcing community. A customer support crowdsourcing community is one in which customers help each other develop solutions to their current problems with a company’s products. An innovation crowdsourcing community is one in which customers propose new product ideas directly to a company. Because a customer support community provides information regarding customers’ current needs and provides opportunities to help individuals activate relevant means information, we expect that an individuals’ experience of helping in a customer support community enhances the individuals’ new product ideation performance. By utilizing a natural language processing technique, we construct each individual’s information network based on his or her helping activities in a customer support community. Building on analogical reasoning theory, we hypothesize that the patterns of individuals’ information networks, in terms of breadth and depth, influence their various new product ideation outcomes in an innovation crowdsourcing community. Our analysis reveals that generalists who have offered help on broad topic domains in the customer support community are more likely to create nove ideas than are nongeneralists. Further, we find that generalists who have accumulated deep knowledge in at least one topic domain (deep generalists) outperform nongeneralists in their ability to generate popular and feasible ideas, whereas generalists who have accumulated only shallow knowledge across diverse domain areas (shallow generalists) do not. The results suggest that the ability of generalists to outperform nongeneralists in creating popular and feasible ideas is contingent on whether they have also accumulated deep knowledge.

## 1. Introduction

Because of advances in information technology, many companies invite their customers to suggest new product ideas through a practice called “innovation crowdsourcing.” Through an innovation crowdsourcing community,<sup>1</sup> customers can propose new product or service ideas directly to a company. Customers possess market needs information, thus have the potential to generate high-quality new product ideas (Lakhani et al. 2013). For instance, Poetz and Schreier (2012) found that ideas generated by users scored significantly higher in terms of novelty and customer benefits than ideas generated by professionals. Further, consumers prefer to buy products that are developed by other consumers because customers feel empowered by being involved vicariously in the design process (Dahl et al. 2015). Given customers’ potential to add value to new product development, a large number of companies have initiated innovation crowdsourcing.

Along with attention from industries, innovation crowdsourcing is drawing increasing attention from academia. Several studies have investigated the characteristics of individuals who are more likely to create successful ideas (e.g., Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006, Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010): An individual’s gender and expertise marginality explain his or her success in an innovation crowdsourcing community. For example, the likelihood of idea acceptance is positively related to increasing distance between the solver’s field of technical expertise and the focal field of the problem. Females, who are considered to be in the outer circle of the scientific establishment, are more likely to create successful solutions than males. Further, Bayus (2013) found that individuals’ prior winning experience lowers the likelihood of future winnings. Another stream of research on innovation crowdsourcing focuses on how firm policies, such as incentive systems, influence the number of high-quality idea submissions (e.g., Huang et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2014; Ales et al. 2016, 2017; Korpeoglu and Cho 2017). Higher rewards for winning ideas (Liu et al. 2014) induce higher-quality submissions and an increased amount and speed of firms’ feedback on ideas induce a significantly higher number of idea submissions (Huang et al. 2014).

Even though the existing literature offers valuable insights about the antecedents of idea success, previous studies have taken an atomistic view of idea generators and have not addressed how idea generators’ social interactions in crowdsourcing communities influence their future idea success. However, social interaction is a critical feature of crowdsourcing communities. Users of crowdsourcing communities interact with each other through mechanisms such as commenting, voting, or helping each other. These interactions provide opportunities for individuals to learn from each other (Singh et al. 2011, Ransbotham et al. 2012, Hwang et al. 2015). Because participants often discover and develop ideas through their interactions with others in communities, we argue that characteristics of their social interactions influence the quality of ideas they generate.

This study aims to advance our understanding of how social interactions influence individuals’ ideation outcomes. Specifically, we investigate how the information that individuals accumulate in the process of helping others in an organization’s customer support community affects the quality of their new product ideation outcomes in the organization’s innovation crowdsourcing community. A customer support crowdsourcing community is one in which customers help each other develop solutions to their current problems with a company’s products; an innovation crowdsourcing community is one in which customers propose new product or service ideas directly to a company. A number of companies such as Dell, BestBuy, Unilever, General Electric, and PlayStation simultaneously cohost an innovation crowdsourcing community and a customer support crowdsourcing community within the same platform. Other companies such as Starbucks, Salesforce.com, and Philips host only an innovation crowdsourcing community.

The innovation literature suggests that two types of information are required before a high-quality new product idea is generated (Baker et al. 1967, Baker and Freeland 1972). One type of information is needs information, which refers to knowledge of a need, problem, or opportunity. The other required type of information is means information, which refers to the knowledge for satisfying the need, solving the problem, or capitalizing on the opportunity. We propose that the trace of an individual’s helping activities in a customer support community represents the pattern of needs and means information accumulated through helping others. That is, in the process of helping others, helpers learn about the current needs and problems of other customers. In addition, through contemplating and formulating solutions to other customers’ issues, helpers can activate relevant means information from their own memory. Such needs and means information builds to form an information network for each individual. The information network is a subset of individuals’ entire stock of knowledge, and this subset is the one that is most likely to be activated when individuals use analogica reasoning for idea generation: Previous research has found that people have difficulty activating relevant information from their memory unless the information was recently accessed in a similar context (Higgins 1996) We analyze how the structure of individuals’ information networks, in terms of their breadth and depth, af fects various aspects of their new product ideation outcomes. Breadth refers to the scope of information an individual has, and depth refers to the level of understanding an individual has in a domain area.

We evaluate the impact of individuals’ information network structure on three different dimensions of their new product ideation outcomes: (1) whether an idea is novel, (2) whether an idea is popular, and (3) whether an idea is feasible. Our first dimension, novelty, captures an individual’s ability to develop a nonredundant idea that has not been previously submitted to a focal innovation crowdsourcing community. Our second dimension, popularity, is reflected in how many votes an idea generates. Our third dimension, feasibility, is the most conservative measure of idea quality and captures whether an idea is implemented. In order for an idea to be implemented by the company, an idea should be novel, useful, and feasible. These dimensions closely reflect the characteristics of innovative ideas that are emphasized in the innovation literature (Schumpeter 1934, Amabile et al. 1996, Franke et al. 2013, Fu et al. 2013)

We propose that individuals who have helped others on a broad range of topic areas (generalists) are more likely to create novel ideas than those who have not (nongeneralists). Here, we categorize individuals based on the pattern of information each accumulates through helping others in the customer support community. Novel ideas build upon existing bits of knowledge through the process of recombining them in new ways. After controlling for the quantity of information, generalists have more diverse needs and means information than nongeneralists, which increases their probability of creating a new recombination. Although broad information can be a sufficient condition to generate novel ideas, we propose that it is not sufficient to generate popular or feasible ideas because ideas that incorporate diverse knowledge components can have unanticipated flaws. We theorize that only those generalists who accumulate deep knowledge in at least one domain area (deep generalists) are able to outperform nongeneralists in their ability to create popular or feasible ideas, because deep knowledge enhances generalists’ ability to recombine diverse information effectively. Specifically, we expect that only deep generalists are able to create ideas that garner a significantly higher number of customer votes than the ideas of nongeneralists. We also expect that deep generalists are more likely than nongeneralists to create ideas that are feasible. In sum, we expect that the ability of generalists to outperform nongeneralists in creating popular and feasible ideas is contingent on whether they also possess deep knowledge in at least one of their knowledgeable topic domains. We develop theory and empirically test our hypotheses by evaluating 8,110 new product ideation “projects” conducted by 2,643 individuals in an innovation crowdsourcing community hosted by a telecommunication company.

Our study advances knowledge in several areas. First, this study advances understanding of innovation crowdsourcing by (1) investigating how the information that individuals accumulate in a customer support crowdsourcing community influences their new product ideation outcomes in an innovation crowdsourcing community and by (2) employing multiple dimensions of new product ideation performance as outcome measures. Second, our work advances understanding of innovation by investigating the effects of both the breadth and depth of information on innovation outcomes. Our study makes a unique contribution by analyzing how the effect of broad knowledge on innovation outcomes is contingent on the presence of deep knowledge. Lastly, this study also contributes to the social network literature by demonstrating how a natural language processing technique (Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)) can be used to extract and analyze the actual information that flows through individuals’ helping networks. Practically, our findings provide information about how those firms that are already cohosting two crowdsourcing communities can enhance the quality of submitted ideas by mobilizing deep generalists to create more ideas. Further, the results of this study have the potential to inform firms about the advantages of hosting customer support and innovation crowdsourcing communities simultaneously.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

2.1. Broad Information as a Source of Novel Ideas Rather than breaking out of the old to produce the new, researchers have proposed that novel ideas build upon existing knowledge through the process of recombining knowledge in new ways (e.g., Schumpeter 1934, Gilfillan 1935, Usher 1954, Nelson and Winter 1982, Kulkarni and Simon 1988, Hayes 1989, Weisberg 1999, Fleming 2001). An important cognitive process of recombining existing knowledge components is analogical reasoning (Gentner 1983; Gick and Holyoak 1983; Dunbar 1995, 1997). Analogical reasoning is the process through which knowledge acquired in one domain is transferred or applied to another domain. Popular examples of analogical reasoning are provided by Thomas Edison’s laboratory creation of novel products by blending existing knowledge (Millard 1990): Edison and his colleagues adapted and recombined their existing knowledge in electromagnetic power from the telegraph industry to create new products in the lighting, telephone, phonography, railway, and mining industries.

Analogical reasoning involves activating, adapting, and recombining existing knowledge to create a new idea. Confronted with creative tasks, individuals first search their existing knowledge stored in memory (Newell 1990). Previous research suggests that individuals have difficulty retrieving relevant knowledge. In stead, out of the “space of possibilities in memory,” knowledge is more likely to be retrieved if it was recently activated (Higgins 1996). Once existing knowledge is retrieved, it needs to be adjusted to a current problem in order to be recombined and transferred. This process requires identifying common relational structures across knowledge domains. Individuals with deep knowledge are better than novices in this transfer process. Relative to novices, individuals with deep knowledge are better at abstracting the underlying structures of problems and ignoring their superficial attributes (Simon 1981, Gentner 1983, Collins and Burstein 1989). This ability allows them to surpass novices in identifying common relational structures across disparate knowledge domains. Thus, deep knowledge enables individuals to apply knowledge from one problem to another with a similar underlying structure. Novices often get distracted by surface attributes and fail to identify the underlying structure of problems.

The analogical reasoning literature suggests that activating diverse knowledge domains contributes to the novelty of ideas. This is because a greater number of distinct recombinations can be made from a greater number of disparate raw knowledge components (e.g., Fleming 2001). An experiment documented that participants who are given multiple analogies are more likely to generate original ideas than participants who are given a single analogy (Dahl and Moreau 2002). Researchers on structural holes in social networks (Burt 1992, 2004) also find that individuals occupying positions with access to diverse information are more likely to create innovative ideas than individuals who do not occupy structural hole positions. From the structural hole theory perspective, Edison’s laboratory was able to routinely create innovative products because laboratory members were able to borrow, adapt, and recombine solutions across industries by occupying a network position that bridged unconnected industries.

In addition to offering a better opportunity for novel recombination, exposure to diverse information can also enable individuals to overcome cognitive fixation, the tendency to focus on prior knowledge, which can hinder individuals from thinking outside the box (e.g., Maier 1931, Duncker 1945). Cognitive fixation is often blamed as the main cognitive obstacle to innovation. McCaffrey (2012) provides an example of cognitive fixation in the tragic Titanic accident. Fixated on the fact that icebergs usually sink ships, passengers on the Titanic overlooked the possibility that the iceberg could have been their lifeboat. Given the size of the iceberg (between 50–100 feet high and 200–400 feet long) and the likelihood that it would not sink, passengers could have climbed on it to stay out of the water for the four hours before help arrived. According to cognitive psychology literature, one solution to reduce cognitive fixation is to increase exposure to diverse information. Exposure to diverse information helps individuals to redefine problems and to create novel solutions (Dugosh and Roland 2000, Rietzschel et al. 2007).

Recognizing the importance of diverse information, practitioners in new product development field actively seek ways to incorporate multiple analogies in the new product development process. A product design firm, IDEO,<sup>2</sup> purposely established work routines that facilitate its employees’ access to a broad range of existing knowledge (Hargadon and Sutton 1997). For example, IDEO designers are encouraged to stockpile old products and parts in their offices or common spaces. This archive of past designs enables employees to locate and reuse existing knowledge from different domains. At IDEO’s brainstorming sessions, designers who are not associated with a focal project are invited to discussions in order to increase the chances of applying solutions from one field to another (Hargadon and Sutton 1997). Steve Jobs, former CEO of Apple Inc., also stressed the importance of diverse knowledge on innovation: “Creativity is just connecting things. . . . Unfortunately, a lot of people in our industry haven’t had very diverse experience” (Wolf 1996).

Taken together, this discussion suggests that individuals who have offered help on a broad range of knowledge domains (generalists) in a customer support community are more likely to generate novel ideas than those who have offered help in a narrow range of knowledge domains (nongeneralists). Through contemplating solutions to diverse customers’ issues, generalists not only obtain diverse needs information but also activate diverse means information from memory. Because both needs and means information are necessary input to new product ideation processes (Baker et al. 1967, Baker and Freeland 1972), accumulation of such diverse needs and means information is expected to increase the probability of generalists creating novel ideas in an innovation crowdsourcing community. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Generalists are more likely to create novel ideas than nongeneralists.

## 2.2. Contingent Effect of Deep Knowledge on Relationships Between Broad Information and the Popularity and Feasibility of Ideas

Little attention has been paid to the conditions required for individuals to utilize their broad information to create popular or feasible ideas. In order to be innovative, a novel idea should be useful to many customers (popular) and technically and financially feasible to produce (Schumpeter 1934). We propose that generalists’ ability to create popular or feasible ideas is superior to that of nongeneralists only if generalists have deep knowledge in at least one knowledge domain.

We theorize that deep knowledge enables generalists to make more successful analogies for two reasons. First, deep knowledge enhances generalists’ analogical reasoning ability by enabling them to map relevant features across disparate knowledge domains. Through in-depth understanding of a knowledge domain, individuals develop abstract representations of their knowledge, which enables them to pay attention to relevant, structural features across different domains (Newell and Simon 1972, Novick 1988, Glaser 1989). Hence, generalists with deep knowledge are more likely to see how one problem is analogous to another and thus are more able to import solutions across domains than generalists with shallow knowledge. In contrast, individuals lacking deep knowledge tend to pay more attention to irrelevant, superficial features, which distracts them from attending to more meaningful connections across diverse problems. As a result, deep knowledge has been found to influence an individual’s ability to use analogy effectively (Collins and Burstein 1989, Vosniadou 1989, Casakin 2004).

Second, deep knowledge enables individuals to identify constraints of solutions generated through analogical reasoning. An idea that incorporates diverse knowledge components can have unanticipated flaws because it usually involves unfamiliar and untested combinations of existing knowledge (Fleming 2001). Hence, in order to generate feasible ideas, a generalist should be able to filter out flawed ideas. Generalists with deep knowledge are better able to identify flaws of new combinations than those without deep knowledge because generalists with deep knowledge have elaborate understanding of existing knowledge components, which enables them to identify the constraints of new combinations (Simon 1981, Eckert et al. 1999). In support of this argument, a laboratory study that investigated the use of analogy in an architectural design setting (Casakin 2004) found that individuals with deep knowledge generated a smaller number of ideas than those without deep knowledge because individuals with deep knowledge filtered out flawed solutions in advance. Even though individuals with deep knowledge generated a smaller number of ideas, the overall quality of their solutions was superior to that of individuals without deep knowledge because many of their solutions were not feasible. In addition, in a chess game setting, Chase and Simon (1973) found that novice players were more likely than experienced players to conduct an exhaustive search in order to find an appropriate solution, whereas experienced players were able to limit their search to solutions that would lead to promising outcomes.

By using a radiation problem and a military problem, Holyoak and Koh (1987) illustrate how deep knowledge enables individuals to use analogies successfully. The radiation problem is how to use X-rays to destroy a stomach tumor; the military problem is how to capture a centrally located fortress by having small units of soldiers attack simultaneously along multiple roads. Although the two problems exhibit fairly distinct surface-level attributes (i.e., cancer fortress, X-rays soldiers), an individual with deep knowledge in radiology and cancer (i.e., a radiologist) would be able to focus on the underlying structural similarities of the two problems and to borrow a solution from the military problem to develop a feasible radiation solution (i.e., focus multiple low-intensity X-rays on the tumor from different directions).

Another example of successful analogical reasoning comes from an open innovation setting. In 1989, the Exxon Valdez oil spill occurred in Alaska, spilling close to 11 million gallons of oil and contaminating 28,000 km<sup>2</sup> of oceans. The Ocean Spill Recovery Institute (OSRI), an industry and government-funded think tank, was faced with the critical problem of recovering oil that had frozen to a viscous mass in oil recovery barges. OSRI posted the challenge on Innocentive.com, an open innovation platform, where a winning solution came from a nanotechnology professional. Despite having no background in the oil industry, the individual proposed deploying an existing tool common in the concrete industry that used vibration to keep cement in liquid form during mass cement pours. The individual reasoned that attaching the tool to a long pole and inserting it into the oil recovery barges would keep the oil from freezing and enable it to be pumped from the barge (Lakhani 2009). On the basis of his deep knowledge in nanotechnology and the cement industry, the individual was able to connect OSRI’s problem to the analogous problem found in the cement industry and proposed the cement industry’s solution to solve OSRI’s problem, a solution for which OSRI paid \$20,000.

Researchers debate whether, in order for an individual to benefit from deep knowledge, the knowledge should be in the focal problem’s domain or not (e.g., Gentner 1983, Dunbar 1995, Gentner et al. 1997, Dahl and Moreau 2002, Christensen and Schunn 2007, Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010).

Several researchers have emphasized the importance of deep knowledge in a problem domain in enhancing analogical reasoning ability because it offers an idea generator domain-specific experience and knowledge that he or she can use to find feasible solutions in the problem domain (e.g., Simon 1981, Casakin 2004) The radiology-military analogy illustrates this case: The medical doctor utilizes his deep knowledge in radiology to map relevant features from a distant knowledge domain (military strategy) to find a solution in radiology. His deep knowledge in radiology helps him map underlying structures across the two domains and apply a solution generated in the military domain to radiology. Other researchers propose that individuals with deep knowledge outside of a problem domain have better chances of generating an innovative solution to a problem than individuals with deep knowledge in the domain. This view suggests that the problem space is not fixed and can be redefined by problem solvers. And this very redefinition of a problem can lead to more creative solutions (Newell and Simon 1972, Marengo et al. 2000, Dosi et al. 2003, Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010) because individuals are not constrained by prior assumptions about the focal problem field. The oil spill solution illustrates this case. Although it might be more intuitive to think that a professional in the oil industry should be better able than nonoil industry professionals to create a solution to the oil spill problem, the solution came from a professional in nanotechnology who did not have any background in the oil industry.

Individuals with deep knowledge within the foca problem domain are able to see the deep structure of the actual problem to be solved, which is a major advantage in developing popular/feasible solutions, but these individuals can also suffer from cognitive fixation when thinking about solutions within their deep knowledge domain, which is a disadvantage. By contrast, individuals with deep knowledge outside the domain can see the deep structure of external problems and develop more innovative solutions that are not constrained by prior assumptions. Individuals whose deep knowledge is outside the problem domain, however, lack depth in knowledge of the focal problem domain, which can be a disadvantage. Because of these theoretical tensions, which kind of deep knowledge will be more valuable and therefore, where popular/ feasible solutions to problems are most likely to arise can vary across contexts. This study was not designed to rigorously address the question of which circumstances make deep knowledge within the focal problem domain more or less valuable than deep knowledge outside the focal problem domain. Nevertheless, in Section 4, we explore which sort of deep knowledge is more valuable in the context of this study.

In sum, the enhancing effects of deep knowledge on an individual’s analogical reasoning ability can occur, regardless of whether deep knowledge resides in the focal problem’s domain or not. What is key is that the individual possess deep knowledge in some domain. Individuals who possess deep knowledge in a domain are better at abstracting the underlying dimensions of problems and applying knowledge from one problem to another than individuals who lack deep knowledge. As noted earlier, exposure to broad information helps individuals redirect their attention away from their prior knowledge and overcome cognitive fixation. Thus, we argue that individuals need both deep knowledge and broad information in order to make successful analogies. In line with the above arguments, we expect that only those generalists who have deep knowledge are capable of creating ideas that are popular or feasible. Deep knowledge enables generalists to utilize their knowledge more effectively by enabling them to make more meaningful connections across diverse information and to identify the constraints of potential solutions. Thus, we hypothesize that the ability of generalists to create ideas that are popular and feasible is contingent on whether they have deep knowledge. More specifically, we predict the following:

Hypothesis 2a (H2a). Generalists’ ability to create more popular ideas than nongeneralists is contingent upon the presence of deep knowledge.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b). Generalists’ ability to create more feasible ideas than nongeneralists is contingent upon the presence of deep knowledge.

## 3. Empirical Method

## 3.1. Research Context:

## Crowdsourcing Communities

Our empirical context is crowdsourcing communities hosted by a telecommunication company. The company sells mobile phones, SIM (Subscriber Identification Module) cards, data plans, and bundled telecommunication services. Unlike conventional mobile telephone operators, the company crowdsources many of its operations such as customer support, new product development, marketing, and sales from its own customers. In return for their contributions, customers are rewarded with “payback points,” which can be cashed out, credited against their monthly bills, or donated to charitable institutions.<sup>3</sup> With free registration, anyone can join and participate in these communities, but one must be a customer of the company with an active SIM card in order to be compensated.

The two major crowdsourcing communities hosted by the company are customer support and innovation communities. In the customer support crowdsourcing community, customers help each other to solve problems that they are currently facing with the firm’s products or services. In essence, the customer support crowdsourcing community almost replaces a call center for the company. Aside from confidential billing questions, which are handled by approximately 30 dedicated customer support employees, the company lets its customers handle all other customer issues. Any registered members can post their issues related to the company’s products or services to the customer support community and any members of the community can provide solutions to those issues. The majority of the posted issues are technical problems, such as problems regarding exporting/importing contacts, dealing with various error messages, swapping SIM cards, or using SIM cards abroad. According to the company, the average response time to questions is three minutes, day or night, with 95% of questions being answered within an hour.

The other major crowdsourcing community of the company is an innovation crowdsourcing community. The company’s innovation community allows its members to propose any new product or service idea at any time. Unlike open innovation tournament settings, where solutions to a specific challenge are crowdsourced during a limited time frame,<sup>4</sup> this innovation community does not set a specific problem to be solved. Rather, the company aims to obtain diverse ideas on any topic. The community also allows its members to evaluate others ideas through a voting system. Customers can cast one vote for each idea they want to be implemented Customer votes are called Kudos in the community.

Ideas submitted to this innovation community move along the following path. Once an idea is submitted, the company screens all submitted ideas to filter out redundant ideas. If a very similar or the same idea has been offered earlier, the idea is marked as redirected and linked back to the original idea. If an idea is new to the innovation community, its status remains as proposed until it receives at least 20 customer votes. If a proposed idea receives 20 or more votes, it is eligible to be reviewed by the company’s management team and is labeled as under consideration. If selected for implementation by the company, the idea’s status is changed to either coming soon or implemented. Until ideas are officially launched, they are marked as coming soon. Once formally launched, ideas are marked as implemented. If not selected, the idea is marked as not for us.

Each month, the company recognizes implemented ideas and individuals who created those implemented ideas by publicly announcing them in its official blog The company also financially rewards those individuals by providing payback points. Further, for all reviewed ideas, the company provides written feedback. Table 1 describes selected ideas submitted to the innovation community. As can be seen from the table, although having more Kudos (customer votes) is associated with a greater likelihood of implementation, having a high number of Kudos does not guarantee idea implementation: Not all ideas that obtained high numbers of votes are implemented. The company explained that a major reason for not implementing those popular ideas is that they were not seen as technically or financially viable.

## 3.2. Data

Helping activity data from the customer support community are used to capture the needs and means information that each idea generator accumulated over time. Our data span approximately three years (35 months) starting from the company’s inception in November 2009. During our empirical window, a total of 177,560 customer support issues were posted to the customer support community and 1,692,391 solutions were offered to those issues: on average, nine solutions were offered to each customer issue. Only a very small percentage of the posted customer issues (less than 0.0003%) were not offered help. Figure 1 shows the total posting activities in the customer support community during our empirical period. It shows that the number of postings increased significantly for the first 30 months after the launch and stabilized around 12,000 issues (118,000 solutions) per month.

Data from the innovation community were used to evaluate the impact of individuals’ information network structures on their new product ideation outcomes using three distinct measures: (1) whether the idea is novel,<sup>5</sup> (2) how many customer votes an idea received, and (3) whether the idea is implemented. Whereas the customer support community was launched at the company’s inception, the innovation community was launched two months after the company’s inception. The number of new product idea submissions steadily increased over time and starting from the 26th month, and the number of idea contributions stabilized around 300 ideas per month. During our empirical period, a total of 8,396 ideas were submitted. For our empirical analysis, we dropped ideas that were still under review at the end of our empirical window. This exclusion leads us to the final data set of 8,110 ideas that were generated by 2,643 unique individuals. Among the 8,110 ideas, 426 ideas ( 5%) were implemented by the company. The innovation crowdsourcing setting offers a unique opportunity to study innovation for two reasons. First, researchers can observe all innovation attempts, including both successful and failed ones in the innovation crowdsourcing setting. This unique data opportunity allows researchers to observe a complete picture of innovation activities and examine previously unavailable characteristics of them. ## 3.3. Constructing Information Network

An information network is a two-mode network with size n × m, where n is the number of individuals in our data set (2,643) and m is the number of topic domains (115). These information networks evolve over time as individuals offer more help in the customer support crowdsourcing community.

We used individuals’ helping activity data in the customer support community to construct an information network for each individual because a tie formed between a helper and a help seeker at the community represents the accumulation of needs and means information by the helper, which is valuable for new product ideation (Baker et al. 1967, Baker and Freeland 1972). By helping others to find solutions, helpers learn about current problems or needs of other customers. Also, in the process of formulating solutions, helpers activate relevant means information from their memory. Because the innovation literature suggests that both needs and means information are important antecedents of innovation performance (Baker et al. 1967, Baker and Freeland 1972), we constructed individuals’ information networks based on their helping activities and analyzed how the structure of their networks, in terms of their breadth and depth, influences their new produce ideation outcomes.

In order to construct individuals’ information networks, we need to know who provided answers on which topic area. For example, if a customer, Angela, provided an answer regarding tethering in the customer support crowdsourcing community, an edge will be formed between Angela (an individual node) and Tethering (a topic node) in her information network. In our research context, message topics are not preclassified. Given the large amount of data (approximately 2 million messages) in an unstructured text format, it is a challenging task to classify topics using human coders. So, we employed a natural language processing technique, Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), to address this challenge. LDA is a probabilistic topic modeling technique, a suite of algorithms that aim to discover and annotate large archives of documents with thematic information (Blei et al. 2003). The algorithms do not require any prior annotations or labeling of the document. Instead, the topics emerge from the analysis of the original texts. Specifically, LDA assumes that each document is a mixture of topics, which is generated from a Dirichlet distribution, and extracts hidden topics by backtracking a set of co-occurring words in each document.<sup>6</sup> In our setting, a document refers to all messages at a thread level. This includes a question and its corresponding answers in the customer support crowdsourcing community and an idea message in the innovation crowdsourcing community.

Our content analysis using LDA involved four steps. First, we extracted the entire corpus of question and answer messages from the customer support crowdsourcing community as well as the idea messages from the innovation crowdsourcing community during the 35 months of our empirical window. Then, we merged messages at a thread level. That ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ in the case of the customer support community, we merged a question message and its corresponding solution messages in one document. In the case of the messages in the innovation community, each idea constitutes one thread, consequently one document. Second, we preprocessed the messages by removing stop words $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , “a,” “the”) because they contain little topical content. Also, we stemmed words into their roots $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , converted “teth ering” to its root word “tether”). Third, we ran LDA<sup>7</sup> to discover the topics of each document. We determined the number of topics based on the Bayesian model selection approach (Griffiths and Steyvers 2004), where the optimal number of topics is the one that leads to the highest posterior probability. The final output of the LDA analysis was 115 topics with a set of keywords that belong to each topic. For each topic, we read through the top 10 keywords generated by LDA to confirm that the keywords represent a meaningful topic. Lastly, using the 115 topics, we labeled all question and answer messages in the customer support community and constructed information networks for each individual’s idea submission. Figure 3 illustrates selected topics and their keywords that we identified by using LDA.

Individual i’s information network for idea k submission is captured in a matrix $I N _ { i k } = \{ a _ { i j } \} ,$ , where the matrix $I N _ { i k }$ is a two-mode network in which rows represent individuals and columns represent information domain areas. Each cell value $a _ { i j }$ represents the cumulative<sup>8</sup> number of answers individual i has offered to a domain area j at the time of idea k submission. With 2,643 individuals and 115 domain areas, $I N _ { i k }$ is a 2.643×115 matrix. Our data set contains 8.110 ideas Consequently, we created 8,110 information networks for each question. The resulting information networks are used as base matrices to calculate each individual’s level of information breadth and depth for each idea submission. 3.5.1. Dependent Variables. We examined how individuals’ information network structures formed by their helping activities in the customer support crowdsourcing community affected three characteristics of their new product ideation outcomes. That is, for idea k that is submitted by individual i, we examined (1) whether the idea is novel, (2) whether the idea is popular, and (3) whether the idea is feasible.

<sup>3.5.1.1. Novel Idea.</sup> Our first hypothesis proposed that generalists are more likely to create novel ideas than nongeneralists are. $N o v e l _ { i k }$ is the outcome measure for our first hypothesis. This measure evaluates an individual’s ability to come up with an idea that has not been previously proposed to the focal innovation crowdsourcing community. As the number of participants increases, innovation crowdsourcing communities face challenges in generating new, nonredundant ideas. For instance, in our empirical setting, the proportion of redundant ideas increased from $1 7 \%$ in the first quarter to approximately 60% in the 10th quarter, when more individuals participated in the community. Individuals’ ability to contribute novel ideas to an innovation crowdsourcing community is critical in sustaining the benefits of the crowdsourcing platform. Previous studies have found that firms can benefit from even small increases in the novelty of their new product concepts because customers are willing to pay more for novel products (Dahl and Moreau 2002).

In order to determine if an idea is new to the focal innovation crowdsourcing community, we used the idea status information the innovation crowdsourcing community provides. In the innovation crowdsourcing community, the company initially screens all ideas for nonredundancy. If an idea is identified as a redundant idea (either identical or very similar to previously submitted ideas), the idea is linked back to its original idea. After being linked back to its original idea, the redundant idea is marked as redirected. According to the company, their initial screening of ideas for redundancy is comprehensive, so the status “redirected” reliably captures redundant ideas. ${ \mathrm { S o } } ,$ we used this idea status information to construct $N o v e l _ { i k }$ . The variable equals 1 if individual $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ idea k is new to the focal innovation crowdsourcing community and 0 if redirected.

<sup>3.5.1.2. Popular Idea.</sup> Popular , the second outcome measure of this study, represents an individual’s ability to create an idea that appeals to other customers. In the innovation crowdsourcing community, individuals can cast one vote for each idea if they want the idea to be implemented. Other customers’ opinions on a new product idea is a reasonable proxy of potential usefulness of an idea because it indicates whether other customers want the idea to be implemented. Accordingly, we used the number of votes received by each idea to measure the popularity of the idea. $P o p u l a r _ { i k }$ is the number of customer votes that individual i’s idea k received. An idea that was submitted earlier would have more time to obtain more customer votes. $T i m e _ { k } ,$ a monthly index that indicates when idea k is submitted, controls for this.

3.5.1.3. Feasible Idea. $F e a s i b l e _ { i k }$ is the most conservative measure of ideation outcomes. In order to be selected for implementation, an idea should be (1) new, (2) useful, and (3) feasible to produce. A company will not accept an already existing idea or an idea in which only a few customers are interested. A company takes into account the economic and technical feasibility of an idea when deciding which ideas to implement. Hence, $F e a s i b l e _ { i k }$ evaluates an individual’s ability to generate successful ideas that are accepted by the company. $F e a s i b l e _ { i k }$ equals 1 if individual $i ^ { \prime } { \mathrm { s } }$ idea k is accepted by the company for implementation. Otherwise, it equals 0.

3.5.2. Independent Variables. Our independent variables are indicator variables that distinguish individuals into groups based on the structure of information $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ breadth and depth) accumulated through their helping activities in the customer support community. Figure 5 depicts how we categorized individuals into four groups: (1) individuals who have broad information and deep knowledge in one of the topic domains (High Breadth with Depth: Deep Generalists), (2) individuals who have broad information but do not have deep knowledge in any of the domains (High Breadth without Depth: Shallow Generalists), (3) Specialists (Low Breadth with Depth) and (4) Low-Information Group (Low Breadth without Depth). Informationbreadth , which captures the scope of individual i’s needs and means information that is represented by i’s information network at the time of idea k submission, is measured by counting the number of distinct topic domains on which individual i has provided at least five answers. As a robustness check, we calculated information breadth based on different thresholds other than five answers. With thresholds of three answers and up, the directions and significance of our results remain consistent. On average, individuals are knowledgeable on 11 topic domains, with a standard deviation of 29.47. On the basis of the information breadth, we segregated individuals into generalists and nongeneralists. We considered an individual to be a generalist if an individual’s information breadth was greater than or equal to one standard deviation above the mean level of information breadth.<sup>11</sup> If not, we considered an individual to be a nongeneralist.

After identifying generalists and nongeneralists, we further segregated individuals based on whether they had deep knowledge in any of their topic domains. We used the total number of answers offered to each topic domain to measure an individual’s degree of knowledge depth in a given topic domain. We considered that an individual accumulated deep knowledge in a topic domain if the individual belonged to the top 10% solution providers<sup>12</sup> in the topic domain. The threshold for deep knowledge varies across domains, but on average an individual had to offer at least 63 solutions in one topic domain in order to be considered to have deep knowledge on the topic domain.

3.5.3. Control Variables. Individuals’ new product ideation outcomes can also be influenced by other factors. In order to tease out their effects from the effects of individuals’ information structures on new product ideation outcomes, we incorporated several control variables. For easier comparisons among estimates, we normalized all of our control variables so that their values fell between 0 and 1.

First, research on learning-by-doing or learning curves has documented considerable evidence that organizations’ or individuals’ productivity increases as they gain more experiences (Argote 2012). For example, in service organizations such as hospitals, surgical teams decreased the time required to complete surgical procedures as they gained more experience performing surgeries (Reagans et al. 2005). Singh et al. (2011) also documented an individual learning-by-doing effect in an open source software project setting. In our empirical setting, individuals can learn to produce new, popular, or feasible ideas as they gain more experience generating ideas. To control for this learning-by-doing effect, we included the Ideation experience variable, which is operationalized by summing up the cumulative number of past idea submissions by individual i at the time of idea k submission.

Second, Helping experience controls for the total quantity of information that one’s helping network represents. This variable is incorporated in order to control for the effect of information quantity on individuals’ information network structure (in terms of breadth and depth) affects new product ideation outcomes after accounting for the effect of their information quantity. Helping experience is measured by the cumulative number of all answers previously offered by individual i at the time of idea k submission.

Third, we incorporated Solution Exposure to control for any effect from the amount of solutions individual i has obtained through the customer support community. Solution Exposure is calculated by summing all answers obtained by individual i up to time t. Fourth, our data set spans over 35 months. In order to control for unobservable differences in outcome variables caused by time changes (e.g., the competition level), our model includes the variable Time , which is a month index at the time of idea k submission. Lastly, we included an individual-specific effect into our estimation model in order to control for unobservable individual-specific heterogeneity.<sup>13</sup>

We took several steps to check the robustness of our results. First, we investigated alternative explanations for our findings. In order to tease out the effect of individuals’ information network structures from other potential drivers, we incorporated the following control variables: Ideation experience , Helping experience , Solution Exposure , and Time . Ideation experience is included to control for a potential learning-by-doing effect. In all models, the parameter estimates of Ideation experience are positive and statistically significant, which suggests that learning by doing occurs in an innovation crowdsourcing community. That is, with more experience submitting ideas, individuals are more likely to generate ideas that are new to the community, that receive a greater number of customer votes, and that are accepted by the firm. These results are consistent with the learning (Argote 2012) and creativity literature (Weisberg 1999, Simonton 2004), in that past cumulative experience is positively related to productivity and creativity. Helping experience controls for the amount of helping activity. The positive and statistically significant coefficient of Helping experience indicates that, in general, helping others in a customer support com munity enables individuals to create better ideas. Coefficients of Solution Exposure are insignificant in all models, which indicates that an individual’s amount of asking activity does not affect his or her new product ideation outcomes. Further, the estimates of Time across the models are negative and statistically significant, which indicates that it gets harder over time to generate new ideas, to receive customer votes, and to get ideas accepted by the company. Lastly, we ran our model after incorporating Prior $s u c c e s s _ { i k }$ to control for individual $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ prior successful ideation efforts in the innovation community. This variable is operationalized by summing up the total number of implemented ideas generated by individual i before the submission of idea k. Our main results stay consistent after incorporating this control variable. In addition to the above mentioned control variables, we also included random effects for each individual to control for unidentified heterogeneity across individuals.

Second, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to check for the robustness of our results across different threshold levels of measuring individuals’ information breadth and depth levels. For our main analysis, we assumed that an individual was knowledgeable in a domain area if he or she had provided at least five answers on the domain area up to time t. As a robustness check, we calculated information breadth based on various thresholds. From thresholds greater or equal to three answers, the directions and significance levels of our results remain consistent with those reported here. Further, we checked the sensitivity of our results across different cutoff points for generalists and nongeneralists. For our main analysis, the cutoff point for generalist was one standard deviation above the mean level $( \mu + \sigma )$ , which was 40 topic domains. As we decreased the cutoff point to the mean (µ) level, which was 11 topic domains, the effect magnitude of $G e n e r a l i s t _ { i k } ,$ Deep generalist , and Shallow generalist stayed fairly consistent, and the statistical significance and direction of the effects remained the same. We also took a similar approach to a sensitivity analysis for information depth. For our main analysis, we considered that an individual possessed deep knowledge in a domain area if the individual belonged to the top 10% solution providers for the area. Thresholds between 5%–15% produced results consistent with those reported here. We report the details of the above sensitivity analyses in Figures A and B in the online appendix.

Third, instead of using a continuous measure of information breadth, our main analysis reduced individuals’ information breadth to a categorical variable because we believe that the categorized independent variable conveys the main idea of this study more crisply. Although we checked the robustness of our main results by changing the thresholds for categorization, we also conducted another robustness analysis by keeping information breadth as a continuous variable. In this analysis, the key independent variables are $B r e a d t h _ { i k }$ and $D e p t h _ { i k } ,$ , where $B r e a d t h _ { i k }$ is the number of individual $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ information topic domains at the time of idea k submission and $D e p t h _ { i k }$ is a dummy variable that indicates whether individual i has deep knowledge in at least one topic domain at the time of idea k submission. Results show that $B r e a d t h _ { i k }$ has positive and statistically significant effects for all three outcome measures: $\beta { = } 1 . 7 3$ $( p ^ { - } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ for $N o v e l _ { i k } , \beta = 1 . 7 9 ( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ for $P o p u l a r _ { i k } ,$ and $\beta = 2 . 1 2 \ ( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ for $F e a s i b l e _ { i k }$ . These findings regarding breadth are consistent with results previously discussed and presented in Table 4. $D e p t h _ { i k }$ has a positive and significant effect on Popular<sub>ik</sub> $( \beta =$ $0 . 9 7 , \ \bar { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and $\bar { F e a s i b l e _ { i k } } ~ ( \beta = 2 . 5 4 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ but no effect on Novel . Lastly, the interaction effect between Breadth and $D e p t h _ { i k }$ variables was not significant for $N o v e l _ { i k }$ but was significant for Popular<sub>ik</sub> $( \bar { \beta } = 1 . 5 0 )$ $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and Feasible $( \beta = 6 . 8 3 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . These results are consistent with the results of our main analysis because our main results show that ideas created by generalists, who have broad information, are more likely to be novel, more likely to obtain a greater number of customer votes, and more likely to be accepted by the company. Further, the fact that the interaction effect is statistically significant for $P o p u l a r _ { i k }$ and $F e a s i b l e _ { i k }$ is also consistent with our main results, where we find that deep—but not shallow—generalists are more likely to garner votes and have their ideas accepted.

Fourth, individuals’ information networks are constructed based on their cumulative helping activities in the customer support crowdsourcing community. This network construction is based on the implicit assumption that information/knowledge does not depreciate over time. However, knowledge can depreciate over time (Argote 2012). In order to check if our results are robust after considering this potential knowledge depreciation effect, we constructed individuals’ information networks under different assumptions and conducted sensitivity analysis. Specifically, for this sensitivity analysis, we assumed that individuals hold knowledge only for six months rather than holding it indefinitely. On the basis of this new assumption, we reconstructed 8,110 information networks for each idea k and reran the set of regression analyses. We found that the magnitude of the effects became slightly bigger under the assumption of knowledge depreciation but that the differences were not statistically significant from analyses based on the assumption of no depreciation. The significance and the directions of the effects are consistent with the main analysis reported here. Results of this robustness analysi are reported in Figure D in the online appendix.

Fifth, there could be an unobserved time-variant factor—individuals’ direct experience of problems— that could affect individuals’ participation level in the customer support community as well as their idea quality in the innovation community. That is, the direct experience of problems could be a plausible common cause. Provided the presence of a common cause, the estimates of our two control variables that capture individuals’ participation level in the support community $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ Help experience and Solution exposure ) would be biased. In order to check whether the common cause could account for our statistical results for Help experience and

Solution exposure<sub>ik</sub>, we conducted the following robustness check. We incorporated a variable, Directly experienced problem , that measures whether individual i has experienced a problem in the topic area of idea k before the submission of idea k. This variable captures whether individual i’s idea k deals with the issues for which i has directly experienced problems and has sought help in the support community. We constructed the variable as a dummy variable. Our results remain consistent after including this new variable: The direction and significance of parameter estimates of Help experience and Solution exposure stay consistent. Also, we find that only a small portion of ideas (@ 3%) are generated by individuals who actually experienced problems that the proposed idea intends to fix. Hence, we conclude that the common cause does not seem to be a viable alternative explanation for our results. Results of this robustness analysis are reported in Figure E in the online appendix.

Sixth, because a few individuals contributed a disproportionately greater number of ideas in the innovation community, we checked whether our results are mainly driven by the behaviors of those active individuals. We conducted a supplementary analysis where we grouped observations based on individuals join date: We identify early, middle, and late entrants to the innovation crowdsourcing community who joined the community on the 3rd, 15th, and 27th month, respectively. We subgroup our data in this way because individuals who participated in the innovation community early on show up with higher frequency than others in our data set and thus get higher statistical weights in our analysis. If our main results were unduly influenced by the greater weights given to the early entrants, the parameter estimates of the three groups who joined the community at different times would differ significantly. We found that the results are qualitatively consistent across the three subgroups: The direction and significance of effects are consistent across the three subgroups. Results of this robustness analysis are reported in Figure F in the online appendix.

Seventh, we explored whether a novel idea is more likely to be an original idea to existing target market products by using expert ratings. For this validation, we recruited two experts in the telecommunication industry. We randomly sampled 100 ideas from two groups: (1) 50 ideas that were redirected (redundant ideas) and (2) 50 ideas that were not redirected (nonredundant ideas). Then, we asked the experts to assess the originality of the ideas compared with existing target market products. The originality is measured by five-point rating scales (where 1 = low originality and 5 = high originality). Interrater reliability was satisfactory (Krippendorff’s alpha equals 0.81). We found that redirected (redundant) ideas scored significantly lower (mean equals 2.12) than nonredirected (nonredundant) ideas (mean equals 3.47) in originality $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$

Lastly, we checked for potential multicollinearity problems, given that some of the model variables are moderately correlated. If substantial collinearity problems are present, parameter estimates can be unstable to very small changes in the data (Greene 2011). To examine the influence of multicollinearity, we computed variance inflation factors (VIFs) for each of the independent variables. A VIF quantifies the severity of multicollinearity by measuring how much of the variance of coefficients is increased because of correlation among the explanatory variables (Marquaridt 1970). A VIF of 10 and above is suggested to indicate a multicollinearity problem (O’Brien 2007). The test revealed no diagnostic problem in our case: Mean VIFs of all models (see Table 4) were below 2.

We performed a number of supplementary analyses. First, we further segregated nongeneralists into two groups: (1) nongeneralists with deep knowledge (specialists) and (2) nongeneralists without deep knowledge (low-information group). Then, we used the low-information group as our control group and examined how deep generalists, shallow generalists, and specialists perform compared with low-information group individuals. Our results suggest that specialists did not outperform the low-information group in any of the three outcome measures: Specialists did not differ from the low-information group in their ability to generate new ideas, popular ideas, and implemented ideas. As we elaborated in the Theory section, diverse knowledge is critical in generating a novel idea. Both specialists and the low-information group lack diverse knowledge and thus are not able to generate novel ideas. Additionally, it is difficult for an idea to receive many customer votes without being novel, and it is impossible that the redundant idea be implemented by the company in our innovation crowdsourcing setting. We believe this is why our analysis did not reveal statistical differences between specialists and the low-information group in their ability to generate novel, popular, and feasible ideas.

Second, our main analysis examined how two different types of generalists (i.e., deep generalists and shallow generalists) perform compared with nongeneralists. We further analyzed our data to compare performance outcomes between deep generalists and shallow generalists by keeping shallow generalists as a control group. Our regression analysis revealed that, compared with those ideas generated by shallow generalists, ideas generated by deep generalists were more likely to be new $( \beta = 0 . 8 \bar { 5 } , p < \bar { 0 } . \bar { 0 0 } 1 )$ , more likely to obtain a greater number of customer votes $( \beta = 0 . 6 \dot { 4 } ,$ $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and more likely to be accepted by the company $( \beta = 0 . 9 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ).

Third, we further explored if there were performance variations among deep generalists. Specifically, we investigated whether ideas generated within one’s deep knowledge area or outside of one’s deep knowledge area were better. As we discussed in the previous Theory section for Hypotheses 2a and 2b, the enhancing effect of deep knowledge in analogical reasoning has been found both in cases where the deep knowledge is in the same domain as the problem and in cases where it is in a different domain than the problem. In this supplementary analysis, we investigated which type of deep knowledge is more valuable in creating novel, popular, and feasible ideas in our empirical setting. We found that ideas that are generated within their idea generator’s deep knowledge area tend to be more redundant and less novel $( \beta = - 1 . 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ than ideas generated outside the generator’s deep knowledge. This finding is consistent with the cognitive fixation theory, in that deep knowledge in the problem domain hinders individuals from thinking outside the box. Further, we did not find evidence of any differences between ideas that are generated within versus outside of deep knowledge, in terms of the number of customer votes and the likelihood of acceptance.

We speculate that three factors could have contributed to the lack of differences for popularity and feasibility outcomes as a function of whether the idea generator’s deep knowledge was or was not in the area of the focal problem. First, in the context of our study, nonnovel (i.e., redundant) ideas are “redirected” ideas, and are no longer candidates to be judged “feasible.” Also, redirected ideas are likely to have shorter windows to receive customer votes than nonredirected ideas, and thus have less opportunity to become “popular.” Thus, the negative effect of deep knowledge in the problem domain that we observe for the outcome of novelty could flow through to the outcomes of feasibility and popularity. The net effect could be to offset any advantages of deep knowledge within the problem domain on these outcomes of popularity and feasibility. Second, the knowledge domains (topics) in our empirical context are very fine-grained. The average “distance” between knowledge domains here is small compared with, for example, the distance between military strategy and cancer treatments or between cement mixing and oil spill recovery discussed in the theory section. As a result, any effects that might arise from the distance between domains in this context could simply be too small to be material. Third, prior research on the effect of analogy distance on innovation outcomes has tended to study challenging technical problem solving rather than the generation of new products or services as we did in the current study. The trade-offs pertaining to the locus of deep knowledge might be less salient in contexts of ordinary problem solving than in contexts of challenging technical problem solving.

Lastly, we examined if deep generalists with a greater number of expertise areas outperformed those with a lower number of expertise areas. Specifically, we ran regressions to see if having a greater number of deep knowledge domains increases the likelihood of gener ating a new idea, of receiving a greater number of customer votes, and of generating an accepted idea. For all regressions with three different outcome variables, the parameter estimates for the number of deep knowledge variables was statistically insignificant, indicating that the number of deep knowledge domain areas was not a significant predictor of new product ideation outcomes. Further investigation reveals that our data set may not have enough variation to detect the effect of the number of deep knowledge domains on idea quality because the majority ( 80%) of deep generalists have less than three deep knowledge domains.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

Traditionally, new product development has been concentrated mostly within organizations’ internal R&D departments. Advances in information technology have facilitated the flow of information and collaboration across geographic and organizational boundaries (Levina 2005, Cummings et al. 2009, Hwang et al. 2015). Nowadays, organizations are becoming more creative in incorporating social technologies to aid their performance. For example, many companies have built technology-enabled collaboration networks with their customers to obtain innovative new product ideas. These recent efforts of crowdsourcing new product development from customers have great potential to add value to firms because customers can direct important market needs information into the new product development process.

This study proposed and empirically examined how individuals’ helping activities in a customer support community affect their new product ideation outcomes in an innovation crowdsourcing community. We proposed that individuals who have helped others on diverse topic domains (generalists) have a higher probability of creating novel ideas, compared with nongeneralists, because generalists accumulate more diverse needs and means information, which increases the probability of successful new recombinations. Yet, although diverse information can engender novel ideas, we further proposed that diverse information is not sufficient to generate ideas that are popular and that are later implemented by a company. Specifically, we proposed that deep knowledge in at least one domain is necessary to generate ideas that are popular and accepted by a company, because deep knowledge helps individuals to make more meaningful recombinations, by enabling them to identify analogies across diverse pieces of information. Deep knowledge also enables individuals to generate feasible ideas by allowing them to identify constraints of potential solutions.

Using three years of data involving 8,110 ideas that were created by 2,643 individuals, we empirically tested our hypotheses. Consistent with our predictions, we found that generalists have a higher probability of creating novel ideas than nongeneralists. Further, we found that only those ideas that are created by generalists with deep knowledge (deep generalists) obtain a greater number of customer votes and are more likely to be implemented by a company than ideas created by nongeneralists. Generalists who accumulated only shallow knowledge across diverse domains (shallow generalists) do not perform significantly better than nongeneralists, in terms of the popularity or acceptance of their ideas.

Our study advances knowledge in several areas. First, it contributes to the literature about innovation crowdsourcing. Specifically, our study offers complementary insights to previous studies that examined individual characteristics that lead to higher idea quality in an innovation crowdsourcing community (e.g., Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010, Bayus 2013). Previous studies found that ideas created by individuals who have proposed multiple ideas (Bayus 2013) and who occupy socially and technically marginal positions (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010) are more likely to be implemented by firms than ideas proposed by individuals lacking these characteristics. Our study offers complementary insights to previous work by showing how the social interactions of participants in a customer support crowdsourcing community impact their success in an innovation crowdsourcing community. Our results suggest that individuals benefit by spanning customer support crowdsourcing and innovation crowdsourcing communities.

In addition, our study advances the innovation crowdsourcing literature by examining multiple characteristics of submitted ideas as outcome variables. Whereas previous studies (e.g., Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010, Bayus 2013) investigated one outcome (i.e., idea acceptance), our study investigates multiple idea characteristics: novelty, popularity, and feasibility. Employing multiple outcome measures provides a richer view of the effects of our focal variable, individuals’ information network structures. For instance, our results show that individuals’ information network structures have distinct effects across different outcomes: The positive effect of broad information on idea novelty is not contingent on the presence of deep knowledge, whereas the effect of broad information on idea popularity and feasibility is contingent on the presence of deep knowledge.

Our work also contributes to the literature on innovation. As the key process of innovation is recombining existing knowledge (Schumpeter 1934, Hargadon and Sutton 1997), scholars have explored the implications of existing knowledge structures on innovation outcomes.

Studies have shown that broad information (multiple domains) leads to novel combinations that increase the variance of product performance because the quality of unfamiliar combinations is uncertain (Fleming 2001, Taylor and Greve 2006). On the other hand deep knowledge has been found to contribute to increases in mean performance. For instance, in the context of 3M, Boh et al. (2014) found that employees with a greater depth of expertise in their core areas were more likely to generate technically influential patents than their counterparts who lacked deep expertise. Although past work offers valuable insights about how knowledge depth and breadth contribute to an innovation outcome, researchers have not investigated how the effect of one dimension is contingent upon the presence of the other. Our study fills this gap by investigating how deep knowledge and broad information interact to influence various new product ideation outcomes. The findings of our study provide insight into the desirable knowledge configurations (Fichman 2004) an individual should possess in order to be innovative.

The present study further contributes to the innovation literature by introducing a new context to study innovation: The innovation crowdsourcing setting, which offers a unique opportunity to study innovation, for two reasons. First, unlike patenting activities of inventors, where unsuccessful patenting activities are unobservable, researchers can observe all innovation attempts, including both successful and failed ones in the innovation crowdsourcing setting. This unique data opportunity allows researchers to avoid sample selection problems and to observe a complete picture of innovation activities. Second, the innovation crowdsourcing setting also enables researchers to investigate innovation outcomes at the individual level, which has been difficult to investigate in many contexts.

This study also contributes to the social network literature. Social network scholars emphasize the value of diverse information in generating good ideas (Burt 2004) The main theoretical argument is that a certain network position, a structural hole, is more favorable for obtaining diverse information than other positions. Consequently, an actor occupying the network position is more likely to generate good ideas. With a few exceptions (e.g., Aral and Van Alstyne 2011, Wu 2013), most network studies have not observed the actua information that passes through one’s network. For example, an actor occupying a network position that connects different groups is presumed to obtain more novel information than an actor not occupying such a bridging position (e.g., Burt 1992, 2004; Ahuja 2000; Cummings 2004), and a weak tie is presumed to transmit more novel information than a strong tie (e.g., Granovetter 1983, Montgomery 1992). The actual information that passes through the network, however, is treated as a black box, mainly because of data limitations or data-processing challenges. In this study, we contribute to the social network literature by demonstrating how a natural language processing technique (LDA) can be used to extract and analyze the actual information that flows through individuals helping networks.

This study also has several practical implications. First, the results of this study can inform firms’ decisions about whether to simultaneously host customer support and innovation crowdsourcing communities Companies such as Dell, BestBuy, Unilever, General Electric, and PlayStation simultaneously host customer support and innovation crowdsourcing communities. Yet other companies such as Starbucks, Salesforce.com, and Philips host only innovation crowdsourcing communities. Our findings suggest that hosting both communities has benefits because a customer support community offers idea generators an opportunity to accumulate current needs information as well as to activate means information from their memory. This needs-and-means information enables idea generators to create high-quality new product/service ideas. Second, our findings suggest that those firms that are already hosting two crowdsourcing communities could trace individuals’ activities more closely. Companies hosting an innovation crowdsourcing community usually monitor community-level statistics, such as the number of ideas submitted and the number of participants, but typically do not collect detailed statistics about their participants’ activities. Our results suggest that, by collecting data about individuals’ microlevel activities in a customer support community, companies would be able to identify those individuals who have a higher probability of creating better ideas. By mobi. lizing those individuals who help others in broad areas but with at least one focused knowledge domain companies might be able to increase the quality of ideas they obtain from innovation crowdsourcing communities. Third, our findings suggest that idea generators need to develop significant levels of knowledge breadth and depth in order to come up with popular and/or implemented ideas. In our data, only 14% of the idea generators are generalists and a mere 6% are deep generalists. By monitoring individuals’ helping activities in a customer support community and by guiding them to engage in certain helping activities to boost their knowledge profile, companies could increase the number of individuals who are likely to create implementable ideas.

This study has a few limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, in the current study, we captured the information network of individuals based on their helping activities in a customer support community. We believe that this is a reasonable approach to capture an individual’s information network for our purpose because an individual’s helping activity indicates that the individual accumulates both solution and needs information, which are a prerequisite for innovative outcomes (Baker et al. 1967, Baker and Freeland 1972). It would be interesting to investigate how customer needs information obtained through observing posted messages influences individuals’ new product ideation outcomes. For this study, community users’ viewing log data were not available. With viewing log data, researchers would be able to examine whether passive gathering of customer needs information also improves one’s new product innovation outcomes.

Second, our findings suggest that hosting a customer support community and an innovation crowdsourcing community together might have complementarity effects. It would be very interesting to examine if there indeed exists complementarity of hosting these two crowdsourcing communities by looking at the performance of innovation crowdsourcing communities of multiple companies, some of which have a customer support community and others of which do not.

Third, our study was not designed to determine the relative benefit of deep knowledge within or outside the problem domain on innovation outcomes. As we discussed in the theory section, the substantive question of the location of deep knowledge on individuals innovation outcomes has attracted attention in the innovation literature. Rationales exist for why either deep knowledge within or outside the problem domain would be more beneficial in generating innovative ideas. One view is that domain experts should be able to outperform nondomain experts because domain experts have prior experience and knowledge in the domain that they can use to find novel, popular and feasible solutions in the problem domain. An alternative view, however, suggests that the problem space is not fixed and can be redefined by problem solvers. And this very redefinition of a problem can lead to more creative solutions. According to this view, deep knowledge outside the problem domain would be more valuable than deep knowledge inside the problem domain. The two competing views have not yet been fully reconciled by scholars as previous empirical studies have found mixed results. We speculate that differences in the role of knowledge inside or outside the focal problem domain might be driven by contextual differences. That is, certain characteristics of a context might increase the quality of solutions based on withindomain deep knowledge compared with that based on outside-domain deep knowledge and vice versa. One potential contextual variable could be whether a problem requires a “breakthrough” solution or not. We speculate that, in the case of breakthrough solutions, deep knowledge from domains outside the focal problem would be more valuable than deep knowledge within the same domain as the focal problem. For instance, the problem types listed on InnoCentive tend to be those that have already stumped in-domain experts, and so require cross-domain experts to overcome cognitive fixation. By contrast, for “ordinary” problem solving or more incremental innovation, within-domain knowledge might be more valuable than outside-domain knowledge. It would be a fruitful avenue for future research to test this hypothesis as well as to investigate other contextual variables that explain when deep knowledge within the domain of the focal problem and when deep knowledge outside the domain of the focal problem is most valuable.

To summarize, this study investigated how the information that individuals accumulate in a customer support crowdsourcing community influences their ability to generate novel, popular, and feasible ideas in an innovation crowdsourcing community. Overall, we found that helping activities in a customer support community enable individuals to create better ideas (in multiple dimensions) in an innovation community. Specifically, we found that generalists, who have provided solutions on diverse topic domains, are likely to create more novel ideas than nongeneralists. Further, we found that only those generalists who possess deep knowledge in at least one domain area outperform nongeneralists in terms of the popularity and feasibility of their ideas. Generalists who have only shallow knowledge across diverse domains do not differ from nongeneralists in their ability to create ideas that are popular and later implemented by a company. Thus, the ability of generalists to outperform nongeneralists in creating popular and feasible ideas is contingent on whether they also possess deep knowledge.
