Recent years have witnessed a change in firms’ innovation patterns, from closed to open, in which information technology (IT) plays an important role. This paper aims to examine the notion of IT-enabled absorptive capacity in the IS literature by theorizing and testing the role of IT in two externally oriented organizational learning processes, which are either interactive with partners in the knowledge alliance or non-interactive with other firms in the knowledge network. In particular, this work formulates a conceptual model explaining how a firm’s IT investment moderates its organizational learning processes in knowledge alliances and networks, which sheds light on the role of IT as an enabler of absorptive capacity critical for obtaining various innovation outcomes. Using a panel data set from the U.S. pharmaceutical industry, the results show supportive evidence for the moderating role of IT in strengthening the organizational learning processes from knowledge alliance experience to coinvented knowledge, and from knowledge network centrality to assimilated knowledge, which in turn improve firm competitiveness.

Keywords: IT-enabled absorptive capacity; organizational learning; knowledge alliances; knowledge networks; absorptive capacity theory; innovation

## 1. INTRODUCTION

With the advances in information technology, access to external knowledge across firm boundaries is now a key source of innovation (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Kleis et al. 2012, Tambe et al. 2012). Innovation activities have thus moved from closed to open forms, with various stakeholders directly or indirectly exchanging knowledge in shared physical or social contexts (Chesbrough et al. 2008, Dodgson and Rothwell 1994). The growing literature on innovation has raised the interest of firms in tapping into external resources, and researchers have also begun to examine innovation dynamics from interorganizational and network-based perspectives (Frank and Ribeiro 2014, Gassmann et al. 2010, Lane and Lubatkin 1998, Powell et al. 1996, Vanhaverbeke 2008). Such studies focus on outside-in knowledge flows, and show that firms have to develop their absorptive capacity to learn from external sources of knowledge (Hoang and Rothaermel 2005, Leonard-Barton 1992, Tsai 2001).

In Cohen and Levinthal’s (1990) original concept, absorptive capacity is defined as a firm’s ability to assimilate and utilize external knowledge for commercial ends, and thus a new way to view organizational learning and innovation. The subsequent absorptive capacity literature has drawn on a process-oriented perspective, and conceptualized absorptive capacity as a set of different organizational learning processes (Lane and Lubatkin 1998, Lane et al. 2006, Lewin et al. 2010, Zahra and George 2002). Consistent with the prior literature, this study defines absorptive capacity as the externally oriented organizational learning processes of a firm that are carried out to obtain and create knowledge across its boundaries (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Teece et al. 1997, Winter 2003). In these externally oriented organizational learning processes, visible knowledge alliances with partners and invisible knowledge networks among firms have been recognized as important external knowledge sources that can aid innovation (e.g., Hoang and Rothaermel 2005, Tsai 2001).

The recent IS literature increasingly suggests that one of the most critical enablers of absorptive capacity is information technology (IT) (Joshi et al. 2010, Roberts et al. 2012), while IT is also seen as a catalyst of organizational learning (Tippins and Sohi 2003) and innovation (Chi et al. 2010, Bardhan et al. 2013, Dong 2010, Dong et al. 2013, Kleis et al. 2012, Tambe et al. 2012, Xue et al. 2012). Despite the fact that the notion of IT-enabled absorptive capacity has been widely used in IS research, the understanding of how IT enables absorptive capacity with regard to various different externally oriented organizational learning processes is still limited. In other words, little is known about what happens inside the black box of IT-enabled absorptive capacity.

The current study aims to fill this gap in the literature by explaining how IT strengthens the externally oriented organizational learning processes in knowledge alliances and networks, and thus how it can enhance a firm’s absorptive capacity. The research question addressed in this paper is how IT facilitates organizational learning processes in knowledge alliances and networks, as these two contexts have been highlighted in the literature. These two contexts represent organizational learning processes that are interactive with visible alliance partners (e.g., Lane and Lubatkin 1998) or interactive with other firms in an invisible network of knowledge flows (e.g., Tsai 2001, Allen et al. 2007). Since no prior study in the literature attempts to simultaneously consider the organizational learning processes that occur in knowledge alliances and networks, this work thus aims to provide a more holistic picture by taking into account both of these contexts simultaneously.

We formulate a conceptual model that connects knowledge alliance experience and knowledge network centrality to the corresponding organizational learning outcomes as co-invented knowledge and assimilated knowledge, respectively. IT investment is theorized as a key moderator enhancing these organizational learning processes. Our model differs from prior studies by reconceptualizing alliancebased, interactive and network-based, non-interactive externally oriented organizational learning processes as a firm’s absorptive capacity. Furthermore, the model highlights IT’s role as an enabler of absorptive capacity through facilitating these processes. By doing so, this work is able to offer novel understanding to the IS literature as to what IT-enabled absorptive capacity is and how IT enables absorptive capacity.

#

Compared to the extent IS literature, this paper contributes an insightful theory of IT-enabled absorptive capacity by theorizing the role of IT in alliance- and network-based organizational learning processes. These processes lead to co-invented knowledge based on prior successful experiences of knowledge exchange in alliances, or the knowledge that is assimilated from the intangible networks that exist among firms in an industry. Given that recent studies in the pharmaceutical industry reflect the importance of alliances and open innovation due to the distributed nature of knowledge (Chesbrough and Crowther 2006, Chiaroni et al. 2008, Cooke 2005, Powell et al. 2005), we focus on empirically testing our theory based on the sample of firms from the U.S. pharmaceutical industry.

The empirical results based on a panel data set from the U.S. pharmaceutical industry reveal that IT plays a significant moderating role in strengthening the organizational learning processes that occur in knowledge alliances and networks. The findings further show that these organizational learning processes enabled by IT can improve firm competitiveness by generating learning outcomes such as coinvented and assimilated knowledge. Therefore, simply getting access to external resources in a knowledge alliance or network does not automatically lead to desirable innovation outcomes, unless a firm is able to leverage its strategic resources, such as IT investments, to facilitate organizational learning. IT-enabled absorptive capacity can thus lead to more effective organizational learning processes, and so is critical for innovation and the creation of competitive advantage.

The rest of paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature and summarizes the theoretical background. The hypotheses and conceptual model are presented in Section 3. Data and measures are introduced in Section 4, while Section 5 then reports the empirical results. The main findings are discussed in Section 6, and the conclusion and its implication are presented in Section 7.

## 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1. Open Innovation and Absorptive Capacity Theory

Since Schumpeter (1934) first proposed that innovation is a path to economic growth, the conventional definition of innovation has been widely investigated in the hope of establishing a sequential model based on the characteristics of closed innovation activities within an organization. However, with innovation activities dynamically evolving, the innovation process has advanced from a sequential, closed form to an open process. In this open process, various specialized participants absorb, emit and exchange knowledge inputs in shared physical or social contexts (Dodgson and Rothwell 1994, Tidd et al. 2001). As researchers have begun to understand innovation activities as a series of knowledge exchanges and spillovers (Inkpen 1996, Kogut and Zander 1992, Tsai and Ghoshal 1998), a more dynamic conception of innovation is required to capture how knowledge can be acquired from external sources, such as knowledge alliances and networks (Amesse and Cohendet 2001, Hoang and Rothaermel 2005, Myers and Rosenbloom 1996, Tsai 2001).

A number of studies have been conducted regarding the growing relevance of external sources of innovation rather than a reliance on internal R&D, resulting in the notion of “open innovation” (Chesbrough 2003, 2006). Open innovation describes the situation in which many innovative firms have shifted to an open model for their activities, using a wide range of external knowledge sources to help them achieve their goals. It highlights the use of purposive inflows and outflows of knowledge to accelerate innovation. This paradigm thus proposes that firms in an open innovation process should aim to acquire and use external knowledge.

Open innovation can shed light on absorptive capacity theory, which emphasizes externally oriented organizational learning (Vanhaverbeke et al. 2008). Research on organizational learning has existed at least since Cyert and March (1963) and Cangelosi and Dill (1965), and the literature has grown dramatically in recent years. Organizational learning occurs while the organization exploits internal

experience or assimilates external knowledge. Since open innovation is essentially externally oriented learning processes that occur by reusing and recombining existing elements of knowledge (March 1991, Nelson and Winter 1982), absorptive capacity theory is a powerful lens through which to understand how a focal firm can generate innovation and build competitiveness by working with external partners or assimilating knowledge spillovers. Because firms do not automatically appropriate the value of open innovation, their absorptive capacity becomes critical in enabling them to benefit from external sources of knowledge (Schilling 2012). Therefore, the degree to which a firm can obtain desirable outcomes from its open innovation activities depends on how it carries out externally oriented organizational learning processes, and thus on its absorptive capacity. For instance, Powell et al. (1996) claim that the development of absorptive capacity by pharmaceutical firms, with regard to how they manage organizational learning in knowledge alliances, is an important source of innovation in this industry.

## 2.2. Absorptive Capacity as Externally Oriented Organizational Learning

In Cohen and Levinthal’s (1990) original work, absorptive capacity is defined as a firm’s ability to recognize the value of external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. They thus propose absorptive capacity theory as a new way to understand the learning and innovation carried out by organizations. Subsequent scholars have drawn on a process-oriented perspective and conceptualized absorptive capacity as different externally oriented organizational learning processes (Lane and Lubatkin 1998, Lane et al. 2006, Lewin et al. 2010, Zahra and George 2002). This study pays attention to two particular externally oriented organizational learning processes, rooted in visible knowledge alliances and the invisible knowledge networks that exist among firms.

Past studies proposed that externally oriented organizational learning is an assimilating and transforming process (Crossan et al. 1999), which is based on the degree of “interaction” among firms within a specific industry (Vera and Crossan 2004, Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011). These works suggest that externally oriented organizational learning occurs in a context composed of the organization itself and the

environment in which it is embedded. As a result, two types of externally oriented organizational learning processes are commonly seen, those that entail direct interaction with others in a formal knowledge alliance, or without direct interaction with others, in invisible knowledge networks (Chesbrough et al. 2008, Laursen and Salter 2006, Allen et al. 2007).

As noted above, alliance-based and network-based organizational learning processes are particularly important for the open innovation in pharmaceutical industry (Hughes and Wareham 2010). Knowledge alliances are widely used in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry, where the scale benefits of such relationships can help firms to launch blockbuster drugs, make technology bets, accelerate clinical trials, and increase the desirability licensing partners. Such companies can cover the entire value chain, from discovery to product testing, by engaging in knowledge alliances, due to the critical nature of innovation in this industry (Gassmann and Reepmeyer 2005, Jones 2000, Powell et al. 1996, Tapon and Thong 1999). On the other hand, the rise of biotechnology and its interdisciplinary requirements necessitate knowledge assimilation from different external sources, further impeding any single firm’s closed innovation activities (Chiaroni et al. 2008, Jones 2000). This has led to a new approach of organizational learning that occurs without direct interactions with other firms, and instead relies on an invisible knowledge network that exists among pharmaceutical firms (Chesbrough and Crowther 2006, Fetterhoff and Voelkel 2006, Kleyn et al. 2007).

## 2.3. IT-Enabled Absorptive Capacity

There is growing interest in the role of IT in enabling absorptive capacity or organizational learning. Alavi and Leidner (2001) show that IT can enhance firms’ assimilation of external knowledge by creating organizational memory in the form of electronic repositories. Tippins and Sohi (2003) reveal that firms can accumulate valuable external knowledge that is stored and preserved for future use by IT, and thus aid organizational learning. Subramani (2004) finds that supply chain management (SCM) systems can be used to transfer domain-specific knowledge between a focal firm and its partners. Malhotra et al. (2005)

explain why standard IT interfaces are useful to configure absorptive capacity and thus assimilate marketing-facing knowledge from supply chain partners. Similarly, Malhotra et al. (2007) suggest that standard IT interfaces facilitate mutual knowledge creation with supply chain partners. Saraf et al. (2007) distribution channel partners. Song et al. (2007) similarly suggest that communication technologies help to foster knowledge sharing. Joshi et al. (2010) develop the concept of IT-enabled absorptive capacity, and find that it is associated with innovation outcomes, treating it as an exogenous black box in their model. Chi et al. (2010) further suggest that IT-enabled absorptive capacity leads to the development of innovation in pharmaceutical alliances. Roberts et al. (2012) systematically review the literature on IT and absorptive capacity, and suggest that it is important to deepen our understanding of the mechanisms through which the former enables the latter. Frank et al. (in press) also provide an overview regarding IT as one of the critical factors influencing the performance of knowledge transfer across different organizations.

These earlier studies explicitly or implicitly reveal that IT helps support the acquisition and assimilation of external knowledge, in the form of IT-enabled absorptive capacity. Moving one step beyond this, a new IS perspective for understanding IT-enabled absorptive capacity that is addressed in this study will be how IT can be used to enable absorptive capacity in relation to organizational learning in specific contexts, such as knowledge alliances and networks.

## 3. HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

## 3.1. Organizational Learning in Knowledge Alliances and Networks

As researchers have begun to understand innovation activities as a series of inter-organizational interactions between a focal firm and external knowledge providers (Inpken 1996, Tsai and Ghoshal 1998), it has become apparent that a more open and interactive conception of collaborative alliances is required (Amesse and Cohendet 2001). These studies pointed to the growing relevance of knowledge

#

alliances rather than a reliance on internal R&D, meaning that organizational learning can be regarded as resulting from distributed inter-organizational knowledge alliances or partnerships, rather than from a single firm (Coombs et al. 2003, Powell et al. 1996). The notion of knowledge alliances was originally raised in a discussion on the history of trans-organizational innovation at the firm level (Millar et al. 1997), and since then knowledge alliances, one approach to open innovation, have been identified as a useful means by which a firm’s strategic flexibility can be increased in order to access external knowledge (Fritsch and Lukas 2001, Pisano 1990, Porter and Stern 2001).

The cumulative successful experiences from prior knowledge alliances can enhance a firm’s future collaborations with external knowledge providers (Anand and Khanna 2000, Dyer and Singh 1998, Silverman and Baum 2002). In addition, several studies have noted that a firm’s general alliance experience can positively affect its subsequent alliance performance (Hoang and Rothaermel 2005, Kale et al. 2002, Zollo et al. 2002). We thus argue that a firm’s successful experience of a knowledge alliance will be useful with regard to knowledge coinvention with its subsequent partners. This is because working to codify knowledge and create systems to efficiently coordinate and disseminate information with knowledge alliance partners are possible mechanisms for the development of a firm’s ability to successfully manage collaborative knowledge coinvention efforts, which can then be leveraged in subsequent alliances (Hoang and Rothaermel 2005). For example, in the product development that occurs in the pharmaceutical industry, Rothaermel and Deeds (2004) find that a beneficial biotech-product development path will depend on the effects of experiences from prior exploration alliances in “research” on subsequent exploitation alliances focusing on “development”. It leads to the following hypothesis.

H1: The knowledge alliance experience of a firm is positively associated with its subsequent coinvented knowledge.

A network perspective emphasizes the importance of knowledge spillover, which is how knowledge can be learned from external sources without direct interactions in a knowledge network (Muller and Zenker

#

2001, Tsai 2001). Researchers have suggested that an innovative firm can receive intensive knowledge flows and learn more from external sources by getting access to other firms’ knowledge. Such linkages of knowledge flows among firms form an invisible knowledge network in an industry, enabling a firm to particular, in a field of rapid technological development such as pharmaceuticals, the locus of innovation is found within the knowledge networks that sustain a fluid and evolving community of industry professionals (Powell et al. 1996). Drawing on a network perspective, the position of a firm in the knowledge network of its industry is particularly important. Several decades of research have shown that the degree to which a firm is able to access external knowledge is characterized by its network position (Freeman 1979, Van de Ven 1986, Ibarra 1993), and that a firm can enjoy better learning outcomes and produce more innovation outputs if it occupies a central network position that offers access to external knowledge developed by other firms in the industry (Cross and Cummings 2004, Tsai and Ghoshal 1998).

By occupying a central network position, a firm is more likely to access useful knowledge from other participants in knowledge network. As a result, the proportion of external knowledge elements in the resultant recombined knowledge created by the focal firm is significantly increased by its greater centrality in the knowledge network, due to more opportunities for assimilating external knowledge from other firms (Powell et al. 1996, Tsai 2001). It leads to the following hypothesis.

H2: The knowledge network centrality of a firm is positively associated with its subsequent assimilated knowledge.

## 3.2. The Moderating Role of Information Technology

While discussing open innovation, a number of studies show that one of the most important drivers of this process is IT (e.g., Dong 2010, Dong et al. 2013, Frank et al., in press, Kleis et al. 2012, Tambe et al. 2012, Xue et al. 2012). Conventionally, the division of labor, task specialization, and coordination costs shapes the composition of an innovation team, while the innovation outputs of a team will be determined by the number of collaborators and partners, their skill levels, and the division of specialized labor (Becker and Murphy 1992). While prior studies only generally propose the existence of IT-enabled absorptive capacity (Joshi et al. 2010, Roberts et al. 2012), the current work suggests that investment in IT enables a firm’s absorptive capacity through facilitating externally oriented organizational learning processes across various contexts.

As the scale of collaboration increases, knowledge alliances require greater investment in IT to lower coordination costs and enhance organizational learning, thus making positive results more likely. For example, IT can facilitate access to codified knowledge by lowering the costs of accessing shared knowledge in a collaborative alliance (Cui et al. 2012, For ). IT can also facilitate the development of more efficient processes for managing allian ces, such as when a partnerspecific alliance sets up an online mechanism for accessing knowledge and enabling the mutual creation of knowledge (Ding et al. 2010, Malhotra et al. 2007). In addition, IT enhances organizational memory, and thus the ability to store, retrieve, and share prior successf riences of knowledge alliances (Tippins and Sohi 2003), which can then be passed on to subsequent collaborations. Therefore, when knowledge alliance participation is enabled by IT, organizational learning can be strengthened by more the effective ways of collaboration this allows, and the learning that can occur based on previous successful experiences. This should then lead to better, outcomes with regard to knowledge coinvention. In a specific industry context, it has been revealed that IT-based knowledge sharing in pharmaceutical alliances, where the implementation of digital platforms enables the sharing of knowledge between the virtual communities of drug discovery project teams, can results in a more productive innovation process (Narayanan et al. 2004, Zahay et al. 2004). We thus propose that greater IT investment is likely to strengthen the link between knowledge alliance experience and subsequent coinvented knowledge, suggesting a positive moderating role of IT in the organizational learning processes that occur in knowledge alliances. Based on this, the following hypothesis is presented.

H3: IT investment strengthens the positive relationship between a firm’s knowledge alliance experience and subsequent co-invented knowledge.

Although a growing number of IS studies have examined how IT can enhance absorptive capacity and innovation (Joshi et al. 2010, Malhotra et al. 2005, Roberts et al. 2012), few works explore the impact of a company’s position in the network of knowledge flows among firms, and how IT investment affects the link between knowledge network position and organizational learning outcomes. This study proposes a positive moderating role for IT in enabling a firm to assimilate external knowledge in its knowledge network. For example, IT can encourage the acquisition and assimilation of external knowledge by supporting information exchanges and sharing among firms (Barua et al. 2004, Rai et al. 2006, Saraf et al. 2007). The wide exchange of information has been found to enable organizational learning and innovation in supply chains (Dong 2010, Subramani 2004). In general, IT supports the acquisition, dissemination, and interpretation of external knowledge, as well as enhancing organizational memory, and thus the ability to store and retrieve the assimilated knowledge (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Tippins and Sohi 2003). Therefore, based on the knowledge search routines enabled by IT, organizational learning will be strengthened by broadening the search for knowledge elements from others to be used in knowledge recombination projects. It is then expected that the outcomes of knowledge assimilation in a knowledge network will be enhanced. In a specific industry context, some empirical works show that IT infrastructure (network and communication technologies, in particular) allows distributed pharmaceutical companies to get access to each other’s knowledge, and thus improve the overall efficiency of the innovation process (Forman and van Zeebroeck 2012, Kleis et al. 2012, Thomke 2006). Therefore, we further propose that greater IT investment is likely to strengthen the link between knowledge network centrality and subsequent assimilated knowledge, suggesting a positive moderating role of IT in the organizational learning processes that occur in a knowledge network. Based on this, the following hypothesis is presented.

H4: IT investment strengthens the positive relationship between a firm’s knowledge network centrality and subsequent assimilated knowledge.

## 3.3. Learning Outcomes and Competitive Advantage

IT is causing a revolution in knowledge alliances and networks with regard to organizational learning from external parties, which in turn can lead to the development of sustainable competitive advantage (Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Tambe et al. 2012). In more detail, the externally oriented organizational learning processes that take place in a knowledge alliance or knowledge network can enhance the integration of diversified knowledge flows by facilitating interorganizational collaboration or knowledge exchange. Such open innovation processes can upgrade a firm’s competitiveness by enabling superior organizational learning outcomes, as coinvented and assimilated knowledge (Chesbrough 2006, West 2008).

Coinvented and assimilated knowledge can enhance a firm’s competitive advantage (Lee 2000, Nelson and Winter 1982, Peffers and Santos 1996, Pianta 2004). Research has shown that collaborative R&D can lead to better learning outcomes in patent coinvention due to the use of diversified sources of knowledge and problem-solving mechanisms (Breschi and Lissoni 2005). Similarly, the assimilation of external knowledge can result in high quality knowledge recombination due to the variety of knowledge sources being applied (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Powell et al. 1996, Zahra and George 2002). Such high quality innovation outcomes can lead to greater competitive advantage, manifested by larger market share (Nelson and Winter 1982, Schumpeter 1934). For example, Hughes and Wareham (2010) focus on the open innovation patterns of pharmaceutical firms, and find that both building collaborative partnerships and extending existing knowledge networks can increase the value captured by firms, thus enhancing competitiveness. This leads to the final hypotheses, as follows.

H5a: The coinvented knowledge of a firm is positively associated with its competitiveness.

H5b: The assimilated knowledge of a firm is positively associated with its competitiveness.

## 4. METHODOLOGY

## 4.1. Data

This study uses four independent sources to construct a panel data set to test the hypotheses. The empirical analysis focuses on the U.S. pharmaceutical industry (SIC code: 2834) in 2003 to 2005. The U.S. pharmaceutical industry is noted for its activity with regard to alliance formation, organizational learning, and innovation (e.g., Chi et al. 2010, Hoang and Rothaermel 2005, Powell et al. 1996, Rothaermel and Deeds 2004, Schilling 2009), and thus provides an ideal research context for this study. Moreover, IT plays an important role in facilitating organizational learning and knowledge creation in this industry (Narayanan et al. 2004, Kleis et al. 2012, Thomke 2006, Zahay et al. 2004). In addition, focusing on a single industry can also control for numerous confounding factors related to industry characteristics.

To construct the sample, we first obtained the data for all U.S. public firms in the pharmaceutical industry with a SIC code 2834 from Standard and Poor’s Compustat database. Financial data for R&D investment, financial leverage, firm size, and market share were obtained. Second, the Compustat data was matched with an alliance data set based on company name. This alliance data set was collected by coding all new drug approval files from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) since 2003. Because this study is interested in successful knowledge alliance experiences, knowledge alliances that led to the approval of new drugs were examined. Third, the data was matched to the National Bureau of Economic Research’s (NBER) Patent Citations database containing all U.S. patent citations from 1976 to 2006 by firm GVKEY (Hall et al. 2001), in order to construct the knowledge network of pharmaceutical industry and knowledge-related measures. Finally, IT data was obtained from the Harte Hanks’ Computer Intelligence (CI) database in 2003 to 2005, and merged this with the other data by company name. These procedures resulted in a final sample of 24 distinct drug companies for the years 2003 to 2005, with a total of 68 firm-year observations. This sample size is comparable to that used in past research on firm alliances and networks (Tsai 2001: n = 60)<sup>2</sup>.

## 4.2. Measures

Knowledge alliance experience: Knowledge alliance experience was measured by two alternative measures — alliance count and alliance intensity — in order to capture both the absolute and relative experience of a firm’s successful participation in such alliances. For each year, the alliance count was measured by the number of successful knowledge alliances that a focal firm participated in, which were those that successfully developed approved drugs (Hoang and Rothaermel 2005). Alliance intensity was measured by the number of successful knowledge alliances divided by the total number of new drugs approved by the FDA in the focal year.

#

Knowledge network centrality: The knowledge networks of firms were constructed based on the patent citation pairs made by all public firms in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry in a specific year. Patent citation pairs can indicate knowledge flows among firms (Katila and Ahuja 2002, Wang et al., in press), which were aggregated to the firm level and weighted by the number of citations made by the focal firm. Network centrality has long been suggested to reflect external knowledge access in a network (Borgatti 2005, Freeman 1979). Since different centrality measures have been used in prior studies, this work incorporated four widely used, alternative measures to capture the centrality of a focal firm in the knowledge network.

Specifically, degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality were calculated for each firm in a specific year (Opsahl et al. 2010). Stata 12.1 was used for the network analysis. The command “netsis” was used to calculate centrality measures (Miura 2012), and the command “netplot” to visualize the network (Corten 2011)<sup>3</sup>. Figures 2 to Figure 4 show the resulting knowledge network of U.S. pharmaceutical industry from 2003 to 2005. It is worth noting that, although the sample consists of 68 firm-year observations, we included all public firms in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry in the network analysis, in order to calculate reliable and valid centrality measures.

Degree centrality was calculated by the number of ties that a focal firm i had in a specific year t, shown by the following formula:

$$
D e g r e e _ {i t} = \frac {N o . o f t i e s _ {i t}}{N o . o f f i r m s _ {t}}\tag{1}
$$

Closeness centrality was calculated by the inverse of the average shortest distance between a focal firm i and other firms in a specific year t, shown by the following formula:

$$
C l o s e n e s s _ {i t} = \frac {N o . o f f i r m s _ {t} - 1}{\sum N o . o f t i e s a s s h o r t e s t d i s t i a n c e _ {i t}}\tag{2}
$$

Betweenness centrality was calculated by the number of times that a focal firm i was on the shortest path between a pair of other firms (m and n) in a specific year t, as follows:

$$
B e t w e e n n e s s _ {i t} = \sum \frac {N o . o f s h o r t e s t p a t h s o f m a n d n v i a i _ {i t}}{N o . o f s h o r t e s t p a t h s o f m a n d n _ {t}} / \frac {1}{2} (N o. o f f i r m s _ {t} - 1) (N o. o f f i r m s _ {t} - 2)\tag{3}
$$

Eigenvector centrality was calculated by the following steps. Let $A = a _ { i j }$ be the adjacency matrix of the knowledge network (value = 1 if there is a tie, value = 0 otherwise) in a specific year $t , x _ { i }$ denote the score of the ith node in a row, and $x _ { j }$ denote the score of the jth node in a column. We thus have:

$$
x _ {i t} = \frac {1}{\gamma} \sum a _ {i j t} x _ {j t}\tag{4}
$$

In vector notation, Equation (4) can be rewritten as:

$$
X = \frac {1}{\gamma} A X o r A X = \gamma X\tag{5}
$$

Finally, eigenvector centrality is the greatest eigenvector solution γ with all positive entries, as follows:

$$
E i g e n v e c t o r _ {i t} = \max (\gamma)\tag{6}
$$

Intuitively, degree centrality captures a focal firm’s number of knowledge sources as compared to the size of the knowledge network. It indicates the degree to which a firm can get access to a variety of knowledge sources in the network. Closeness centrality represents the average distance between a focal firm and other firms in the rest of the knowledge network. Therefore, this measure reflects how close a firm is to other firms in the network. Betweenness centrality reflects the number of times a focal firm is on the shortest path of knowledge flows between other firms in the knowledge network. It captures the importance of a firm in the network in terms of bridging knowledge flows among others. Eigenvector centrality is proportional to the sum of centralities of a focal firm’s neighbor firms in the knowledge network. This measure thus indicates the importance of a firm in the network in terms of other firms it exchanges knowledge with.

Coinvented knowledge: Patent outputs have been widely used as the measure of knowledge outputs (Chi et al. 2010, Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012). Two alternative measures were created for coinvented knowledge, which are the number of coinventors (i.e., other assignees sharing the ownership of a patent with the focal firm) and the number of coinventions (i.e., patents assigned to two or more assignees, including the focal firm) in a specific year. Natural logarithms were used to reduce the skewness of these two measures.

Assimilated knowledge: Patent citations represent the degree to which a novel knowledge invention has recombined existing knowledge elements (Fleming 2001, Katila and Ahuja 2002). Cross-citations reflect the degree to which a firm assimilates and utilizes external knowledge elements from other firms in its own knowledge recombination processes (Rosenkopf and Nerkar 2001, Wang et al., in press). We therefore used two alternative cross-citation measures to capture the level and degree of assimilated knowledge (Wang et al., in press). Specifically, the number of cross-citations a focal firm made and the ratio of cross-citations over the total citations a firm made in a specific year were calculated. The natural logarithm was used to reduce the skewness in the number of cross-citations.

IT investment: This study followed a recent operationalization of IT investment which combines different prevailing approaches (Xue et al. 2012). Specifically, the Hedonic method was used to derive the price indices for a list of IT hardware components, including IBM mainframe, non-IBM mainframe, minicomputer, PC, LAN, mainframe tape drive, mainframe disk drive, and dumb terminal (Chwelos et al. 2010). Multiplying the counts of a firm’s IT hardware components by these corresponding prices generated the level of IT hardware investment. Using the Hedonic method improves on the traditional estimation of IT hardware investment, which relies exclusively on the mainframe price index to account for all types of IT hardware (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996).

Second, IT hardware investment was deflated by the PC price index and the price index for “Computers and Peripheral Equipment” from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), in order to account for the changes in quality of IT hardware. Third, the average compensation of IT personnel at the industry level was obtained from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), and multiplied the industry-specific compensation by the number of IT personnel in each firm, generating the estimation of IT labor investment. Fourth, IT labor investment was deflated by the price index for the “Total Compensation Cost” from BLS.

Finally, a well-established procedure was used to calculate total IT investment as the sum of IT hardware investment plus three times the IT labor investment (e.g., Dewan and Min 1997, Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Xue et al. 2012). Total IT investment was normalized as the ratio of total IT investment over the total assets of a firm derived from the Compustat database. While the IT data does not include software investment, it has been suggested that this is proportional to IT labor investment and can be proxied by two times the figure for this (Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996). Therefore, the measure of IT investment consists of a firm’s investment in IT hardware, software and labor.

Firm competitiveness: Since this study is particularly interested in firm competitiveness, as manifested by the market share of firm resulted from firm adaptation in organizational learning processes organizational learning (Nelson and Winter 1982), market share was used to measure the competitiveness of a firm in the marketplace. Specifically, this work followed Bharadwaj et al. (1999) to compute the weighted market share based on the sales of all U.S. pharmaceutical firms in the Compustat database with SIC code 2834. Control variables: Several important firm characteristics were controlled in the analysis. First, R&D investment was controlled for, as measured by R&D spending over total sales (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Second, financial leverage was also controlled for, as measured long-term debt over total assets, which reflects a firm’s capital structure and slack resources (Bromiley 1991)<sup>4</sup>. Third, firm size was controlled and proxied by the natural logarithm of total sales. Finally, year dummies were included to control for time fixed effects. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was used to analyze the data. ## 6. DISCUSSION

This study reconceptualizes absorptive capacity as alliance-based, interactive and network-based, noninteractive organizational learning processes. The role of IT is theorized as facilitating these externally oriented organizational learning processes rooted in the visible knowledge alliances and invisible knowledge networks among firms. While prior alliance experience has been recognized as a source of organizational learning (Huang and Rothaermel 2005), this study suggests that firms also search for external knowledge in invisible knowledge networks that only consist of knowledge flows. These two

#

organizational learning processes are both important for the creation of new knowledge, which in turn jointly contribute to firm competitiveness.

By using a panel data set from U.S. pharmaceutical firms in recent years, the results showed that IT plays an important moderating role in facilitating alliance-based and network-based organizational learning processes, consistent with our explanation of how IT enables a firm’s absorptive capacity. The IT resources invested by a firm enable its absorptive capacity by strengthening the organizational learning processes in relation to alliance experience and network position, thus leading to the coinvention and assimilation of knowledge. Specifically, a firm’s prior experience of engaging in successful knowledge alliances was found to be associated with its subsequent coinvented knowledge with partners, and the centrality of a firm in the industry knowledge network was found to be related to its subsequent assimilation performance. More importantly, IT investment played a significant moderating role in these relationships by strengthening the links from experience to coinvented knowledge and from centrality to assimilated knowledge. In turn, coinvented and assimilated knowledge was found to positively affect firm competitiveness. Furthermore, the results showed that IT investment does not moderate the relationship between closeness centrality and assimilated knowledge. This finding may indicate the boundary condition of the proposed theory. Recall that closeness centrality reflects the average distance between a focal firm and other firms in the knowledge network. Therefore, for those firms that already have access to others’ knowledge, IT does not provide much assistance in organizational learning processes.

Our study contributes to recent IS research on IT-enabled absorptive capacity (Joshi et al. 2010, Malhotra et al. 2005, Roberts et al. 2012) by revealing the mechanisms through which IT is used to facilitate the organizational learning processes constituting absorptive capacity. This study examined the mechanisms in two different contexts under which a focal firm explicitly learns from external partners in visible knowledge alliances, or implicitly learns from other firms in invisible knowledge networks. However, knowledge alliance and networks do not automatically lead to coinvented or assimilated knowledge at the embedded firms. This is because firms vary in their investments in IT, which facilitates or hinders their organizational learning processes in knowledge alliances and networks.

## 7. CONCLUSION

The paper contributes to a better understanding of how IT enables absorptive capacity by moderating externally oriented organizational learning processes involving interactive learning from knowledge alliance partners and non-interactive learning in industry knowledge networks. A panel data set from the U.S. pharmaceutical industry is used to validate this study’s model of IT-enabled absorptive capacity. The empirical findings reveal that IT investment can strengthen the links between knowledge alliance experience and coinvented knowledge and between knowledge network centrality and assimilated knowledge, which in turn improve firm competitiveness. This study finds the mechanisms through which IT enables absorptive capacity, and opens up the black box of IT-enabled absorptive capacity. Not only does this work reconceptualize absorptive capacity as alliance-based and networkbased organizational learning processes, it also provides solid evidence on IT’s role as a key catalyst

Our study has several limitations, as follows. First, departing from prior empirical efforts of directly operationalizing IT-enabled absorptive capacity (e.g., Joshi et al. 2010), this study tries to uncover the mechanisms through which IT is used to enable absorptive capacity. Therefore, it is necessary to separate the measures of IT and absorptive capacity consisting of different organizational learning processes. Thus study examines two particular organizational learning processes that constitute absorptive capacity, those in knowledge alliances and networks. Future research may adopt alternative typologies to conceptualize other specific externally oriented organizational learning processes to extend our understanding of IT’s role in enabling absorptive capacity.

Second, the sample was derived from firms in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry, which is characterized by the frequency of innovation activities that are carried out in knowledge alliances and networks (Chi et al. 2010, Hoang and Rothaermel 2005, Powell et al. 1996). While we construct a panel data set from multiple sources, the sample size is relatively small due to the availability of firm-level IT data. While

#

this sample size is comparable to prior studies on firm alliances and networks (e.g., Tsai 2001: n = 60), and the results based on a small sample should be seen as having good internal validity (as the smaller statistical power leads to more conservative significance tests), external validity is a concern when generalizing the current findings. Future studies may collect larger samples from the U.S. pharmaceutical industry or other industries to examine the present findings, and thus obtain complementary evidence on IT-enabled absorptive capacity.

The empirical findings of this work reveal the importance of IT in facilitating organizational learning processes in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry, which has some important managerial implications. Conventionally, IT is used for the sharing and reuse of knowledge throughout an enterprise (Lee and Choi 2003). Corporate IP-based networks, telecommunications, and delivery systems all facilitate the transfer of knowledge across firm boundaries. These technologies also offer the infrastructure to facilitate virtual, interpersonal interactions among R&D teams that ensure the transfer of explicit and tacit knowledge (Lee and Choi 2003, Rice 1994).

Externally oriented organizational learning processes rely on IT to create digital connections and online access or databases for the discovery and sharing of the knowledge that can be incorporated into new pharmaceutical products (Zahay et al. 2004). The role of IT in the pharmaceutical industry is not only to support communication systems, but also to provide a digital platform for the sharing of knowledge between a virtual community of drug discovery projects, thus resulting a more productive innovation processes, such as “blockbuster” innovation (Gilbert et al. 2003, Narayanan et al. 2004). Some work practices in pharmaceutical firms, such as those related to open source biotechnology (Birney 2002, Lambert 2007) and open source biotech software (Stajich et al. 2002, Stajich and Lapp 2006), show the importance of IT for enabling organizational learning and absorptive capacity. Interestingly, we found that IT investment does not strengthen the benefits of closeness centrality with regard to the assimilation of knowledge. This that IT will not strongly influence organizational learning if a pharmaceutical firm already has access to external knowledge sources in a knowledge network.
