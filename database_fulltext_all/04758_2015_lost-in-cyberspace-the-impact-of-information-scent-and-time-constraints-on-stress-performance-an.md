# Lost in Cyberspace: The Impact of Information Scent and Time Constraints on Stress, Performance, and Attitudes Online

ABSTRACT: As competition online increases, website owners investigate ways in which they can attract and retain more users. One avenue is to reduce frustration and stress for the users. Furthermore, many website users are rushed when browsing for specific information on a website. To save time and prevent frustration, website owners should try to maximize information scent, that is, visual, audio, and semantic cues that are meant to lead or guide the user to his or her informational goal. This paper presents and tests a model to predict how information scent can reduce the amount of stress that consumers experience when seeking information under time constraints. The study also demonstrates the relationships between information scent, time constraints, stress, performance, and attitudes toward the website. Results demonstrate that high information scent is an important design goal for a website, and latent semantic analysis can be a useful tool for measuring scent. In addition, rather than an attribute of an overall site, the concept of scent is demonstrated to be dependent on both the website and the task(s) being performed by the user. This finding demonstrates that to maximize users’ satisfaction and ability to accomplish their goals, website designers need to determine what tasks users need to accomplish, and to make sure that the links on each page point clearly to the appropriate destination to meet those goals. The latent semantic analysis tool can provide an indication of strength and clarity of the links. Clear links gain even more importance when considering the time constraints of users. Measurable stress explains some of the variance in performance and attitudes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: human–computer interaction, information scent, Internet, latent semantic analysis, online anxiety, online stress.

Online retail sales continue to increase steadily as a proportion of total retail sales. Although fluctuations have taken place each quarter, online stores have experienced greater gains and lower declines than physical stores in every quarter since the first statistics were gathered in 1999. While the growth continues, the overall amount of retail sold online remains less than 7 percent of all retail sales in the United States [18]. Despite steady growth, competition online is fierce because merchants from all over the globe often compete in the same marketspace, rather than being limited to a given geographic market. Competition has even increased at the local level with a combination of GPS technologies and mobile e-commerce. All of these pressures as well as others [2] are likely to push online merchants to take steps to improve the online user’s shopping experience to draw both new and repeat customers away from competitors.

There are many opportunities to improve the online user’s shopping experience, and thereby increase the buyer’s intentions to return to the website in the future. Many studies have supported the assertion that a website’s design can greatly influence a user’s satisfaction [10]. Some studies have focused on product costs [9], and others have examined nonfinancial costs such as a user’s time and effort [25, 48, 65, 66].

Unfortunately, we have not yet arrived at a science of website design. Many sites need usability improvements in a wide variety of areas [23]. Sites are sometimes organized poorly, which causes users to become disoriented and ultimately lost. In those cases, users must backtrack and sometimes even resort to “brute force” browsing to locate desired information. Delay has also been investigated in a growing research tradition [16, 17, 25, 29, 30, 47, 65, 66, 67]. One of the most important difficulties, receiving a large amount of attention in the literature, involves the “information scent” of the links that lead a user to deeper levels of a site [25, 57, 59, 61, 72].

Information scent is defined as the ability of cues on a given page to enable the user to discover an efficient path to the desired outcome [25, 57, 59, 61]. Users infer information scent from available navigational aids such as links, titles, captions, or icons that guide the users toward desired information on a target page within the website. When there is high information scent, a user is easily able to match the available cues on the website to his or her internal mental model of the information retrieval problem, thus making it easier to evaluate which path is likely to lead to the desired information within the website. Poor information scent plagues many sites, and researchers have examined consequences such as negative impacts on attitudes toward the site, intentions to return, and ability to find desired products [24, 25]. Previous research [54] has included task difficulty as one of fifteen success factors that influence information systems (IS) success, and poor information scent is likely to present major difficulties in using systems.

Another difficulty that limits the ability of users to find desired information is that sometimes buyers desire or need to limit the amount of time they can devote to finding the information to enable them to make a purchase. A variety of day-to-day pressures exist, such as work deadlines, limited breaks, family situations, a desire for socialization, or the anticipated expiration of desired information (e.g., limited time sales, airline deals, or changing content on a news site). These demands have increased in recent decades to such an extent that mental health in the United States is in jeopardy [73].

Even if individuals are not rushed, the extra time required to wander, sometimes aimlessly, around a site that has many levels and categories provides measurable stress [28]. Stress has been studied in IS recently [15], and physiological measures have been used to study emotional [4, 27] and other reactions in IS [70]. We are all satisficers, and cannot devote large amounts of time to many tasks; as a result, we instead devote limited amounts of time and mental effort to most tasks, with the expectation that it is sufficient. However, rushed searches may result in suboptimal outcomes, when compared to those that can be performed when more time is available. Time constraints can thus affect attitudes and future intentions to use a website by making even more salient any flaws in its usability. Constraints can also increase users’ stress when using a site because they wish to minimize the time they are spending, so as to respond to the pressures mentioned above.

These two issues in website design lead us to the following research questions:

RQ1: Do more stringent time constraints raise users’ levels of stress and lower performance when attempting to find particular information on a website?

RQ2: Can information scent reduce users’ stress and raise performance when attempting to find particular information on a website?

RQ3: Do higher levels of stress and lower levels of performance harm users’ attitudes toward the website?

To address these issues, we investigate the effects of time constraints on stress and website attitudes. Further, we explain the role of information scent in reducing the negative effects of time constraints because it improves the ability of website users to accomplish their information retrieval tasks. By increasing the usability of the website, especially under time constraints, stress will be reduced, and website attitudes will be improved, which could eventually increase intentions to continue to use the website.

We further explore how the ability to locate desired information impacts individuals’ attitudes toward the website while controlling for the perceived quality of the website. This study employs a model to explain how navigational aids can improve user performance on, and attitudes about, actual websites. We further demonstrate how information scent can be objectively measured through latent semantic analysis. Finally, we propose and test a theoretical model of the interrelationships between time constraint, task performance, stress, and websiterelated attitudes.

## Theoretical Background and Model Development

Our research model is based on three theories: optimal foraging theory (as applied to knowledge work via theories of information foraging and information scent) [25, 57, 61], the heuristic systematic processing model [11], and theories of attribution [31, 36, 37]. Each of these individual theories, and the extensions to our model, are described in this section.

Prior to explicating our research model, we first constrain and explain the boundaries of this study to differentiate it from other related studies. First, although this study uses information foraging theory (IFT), the study is not concerned with explaining, understanding, or predicting the optimal searching behavior for information on a website [20, 29, 62]. Rather, it is focused on the effects that searching for information, particularly information that is hard to locate, have on information seekers in terms of success and frustration (i.e., the physiological stress response that an individual feels when not achieving desired outcomes). Given this focus, we are not concerned with how the individual searches for information, but with the levels of cues and how the presence or absence of these cues has a cognitive and physiological impact on the information seeker.

Second, unlike previous work that has studied the effect of website delay on information retrieval and the detrimental effects it triggers in information seekers [20, 24, 25, 29, 30], this study adopts a related but unique focus on the effects of time constraints on information seekers. While those previous studies manipulated download delay on artificial websites to determine impacts on users’ perceptions of the website and their ability to locate information, this study examines a distinct aspect of time: we manipulate how much time is available to the seeker without introducing artificial delay between displayed pages [24, 25, 30]. This type of approach increases our understanding of the information retrieval process that is bounded within a time frame that may more realistically represent searches performed by actual users. This study makes the assumption that information seekers have scarce time to allocate to any particular information retrieval activity [3], and are thus limited in their ability to locate information based on how much time they make available to perform such a task.

We present the research model in Figure 1. The model specifies information scent and time constraints as independent variables, and stress, performance, and attitudes as dependent variables. The model proposes that users with time constraints will experience increased levels of effort-related stress, and will perform more poorly on information-seeking tasks. On the other hand, tasks with high information scent will reduce the level of effort-related stress and will also be associated with higher levels of performance.

Based on attribution theory [31, 36, 37], users who are unable to easily retrieve information will attribute abnormally low retrieval rates to the website (rather than to their own skill) and as a result will have more negative attitudes toward the site. It is likewise predicted that increased levels of stress will spill over and increase the likelihood of negative attitudes toward the website. The model therefore includes attitudes as a consequence of these antecedents.

We first explain information foraging theory as the underlying theory to explain how individuals look for information on websites, as previously explained and applied by Pirolli and others [40, 57, 60, 61]. We then turn to the heuristic systematic processing model [11] to explain how the information foraging process is affected by time constraints. Time constraints serve to limit the ability of the individual to engage in systematic processing, and would explain why and how individuals would maximize their efforts, with less time, to continue searching for desired information on websites. We then provide theoretical support for the hypotheses in our model.

## Information Foraging Theory

Information foraging theory hypothesizes that users will attempt to maximize their gains from valuable information per unit of effort (cost) expended during a search for the information. Individuals attempt to optimize their information-seeking behaviors by maximizing the rate of gaining valuable information while minimizing the cost of finding, extracting, and understanding the information gathered [57, 61]. In terms of IFT, the received benefit is the information the user is attempting to locate. The value of information is determined within the context of a particular situation. For example, a person who is looking for airfares on a travel site might ascribe little value to dozens of the links on the home page, such as those leading to booking cruises or rental cars, because they are not germane to his or her task [57]. On a future visit, however, one of those links might become central to a new goal.

Most intrasite links provide access to a lower-level page in a hierarchy, many of which are grouped in some way because limited screen and cognitive capacity prohibit the ability to have every link available on the home page. The groupings are sometimes quite intuitive, such as those leading to “SLR Cameras” versus those leading to “Video Cameras.” Unfortunately, however, the groupings are sometimes arbitrary to information seekers. Someone looking for a Sharp-brand flat panel television in 2012 at Sharpusa.com needed to click on “Entertainment” from the top level to find the current models from that time. During the same year, if the shopper decided to check out the price of a particular model at Sears, clicking on “Entertainment” at Sears.com led to a page filled with gaming systems, games, books, movies, and music. The proper link at Sears.com was “Electronics and Computers,” not “Entertainment” as it was at the SharpUSA site.

IFT posits that information is found in patches, which are sets of pages grouped by the links on websites. The forager must constantly decide whether to continue looking for information in the current patch or to move to a new information patch with potentially more desirable information [57, 59]. This process is quite rational [12], and is guided by information scent of navigational cues. Most websites organize the content according to one or more schemes that make sense for tasks they imagine foragers will seek to perform. For instance, in March 2014, the Samsung.com/us site had four top-level buttons: “Discover,” “Shop,” “Apps,” and “Owners.” Someone looking to find Samsung’s newest wearable device announced February 24, 2014, without remembering its name would have to click on “About Us,” then on “News and Public Disclosure,” then scan down the list to the very bottom to find that it is the “Samsung Gear Fit.” It is possible, perhaps even likely, that the task might seem too difficult and subsequently the forager will try another patch (website).

It is important for the designer to take into account the tasks that users will try to accomplish on the site. It is nontrivial to determine all of these tasks. For instance, in 2012, the only way to find newly released TVs from Sharp was to find press releases two levels down the website. If someone wanted to know if Sharp had any new LED-backlit displays under \$5,000 in a 52-inch size, the only way to find the answer was to click on each 52-inch model in the product listings and read its description, then look through the new model listings in the press releases section described above. After finding what seemed to be the only one, the list price of \$10,999 would lead the shopper to conclude that the answer to the question is “no.” Such a multidimensional task is quite common in a brick-and-mortar store, and a salesperson can answer it instantly. However, it can be much more difficult to perform on a website unless the designer knows specifically to address that task.

More recently, Sharp has redesigned its site to include TVs right on its home page, and now the three links include “TV & Audio,” “Appliances,” and “Business Products.” Someone looking for the newest television model only has to click on the large photo or on the first of the three links to see models announced on their news releases site three days earlier. However, Sharp 3D glasses, easily available on many merchant sites, are not listed on Sharp’s site as of March 2014. No links lead to those products. The search box does not even reveal those accessories, even after trying actual product numbers found elsewhere. A customer who wishes to compare models with the intent of purchasing an extra pair would conclude that Sharp has no 3D glasses.

## Information Scent

Information scent refers to a seeker’s evaluation of proximal, imperfect, informational cues (e.g., web links and other navigational aids) to assess the relative likelihood of finding valuable and potentially-desired information located on other pages within the website [57, 62]. The user will judge the value by assessing the “scent” provided by the link text or icon, to the extent that it promises to lead to the desired content [61]. Stronger correspondence of the meaning gleaned from the link and the desired content will result in higher information scent for finding the potential item [38].

Information scent was developed within IFT by observing how animals search for food. Animals will continue to graze as long as the smell of the food remains sufficiently strong to indicate that they should continue along a particular trajectory. However, when the smell of food becomes weak, animals will retreat from the current path and attempt to find a stronger scent in a new direction. Pirolli and Card [57] applied this concept to information search on the web and explained that searchers will continue to look for the desired information within the path as long as the “smell” that is perceived from the available navigational cues is strong enough to continue.

With the previous example from Sharpusa.com, a link such as “Entertainment” is an imperfect indicator of finding information about a particular type of television, while a link that says “LED-Backlit Televisions” or even “Products” would have a much stronger scent to guide the user through the task, as the site now does in 2014. Such a link might not have been provided anywhere in the site by the previous designer, who failed to imagine that a user interested in the newest products would need or want to access such information. And even though the designer two years later has allowed clear links for finding just-announced televisions, a scent for 3D glasses products could not be found.

To navigate websites, it is imperative that users be able to comprehend text, images and other forms of communication that prepare them for choosing which particular links to navigate [7]. Users act on navigational cues that they perceive as being most relevant to the goal of their information retrieval task [7]. Information scent plays a vital role in providing information to foragers about which links will lead to the desired option that resides on distal web pages [61].

While information scent is an attribute of a site, it is not defined by “one” overall scent. Scent is defined in the context of a particular task. In the 2014 version of the Samsung site, the link “News and Public Disclosures” under “Investor Relations” and in turn, under “About Us” might be most promising to the forager who is looking for news related to the company. But that link is not so strong when one is trying to find information about the new wearable Samsung Gear Fit, without knowing its precise name. Because the ability to perform the task can affect the user’s perception of the overall quality of the site, the task cannot be ignored in assessing information scent. While people tend to like to characterize an entire site as “bad” or “good,” it is an oversimplification to ignore the task at hand. Also, as evidenced by the illustrations above, sites can change dramatically over time and make tasks easy that were once difficult or vice versa.

Browsing is an important context that is important for developers to consider, as it is required for effective search [51]. Employing a search engine on a site would theoretically allow users to jump directly to, or at least much more closely to, the target page. Unfortunately, those searches are highly dependent not only on the terms used but also on the skills of users and characteristics of the engine itself. It is also important to establish that the result of a search still creates the need to perform browsing of another kind: browsing among the search results. Furthermore, upon making the best choice after “browsing” a set of search results, the seeker will often face another browsing task to drill down through the resultant target site. For instance, entering “3D glasses” on the SharpUSA site provides many bewildering hits but no direct path to 3D glasses. Eventually a user discovers it is necessary to navigate directly to a particular TV model to find the glasses. Surprisingly, even entering a current, actual model number results in no hits at all. Thus, information foraging and search technologies are complementary, and searching technology cannot avoid the strong negative effects of poor information scent for finding information on a site.

On the Internet, foragers need hyperlink cues in the form of text, pictures, and animations to lead them to where relevant information is located. Higher information scent provides an increased likelihood for the presence of relevant, accurate information and can thereby save time. Likewise, low scent for a given task would indicate a lack of cues to guide the information seeker to the desired information and would thus decrease the likelihood of quickly or accurately locating desired information. Low information scent could cause the user to discontinue use of the website [24, 25], and to proceed to a new vendor to restart the foraging, in what is presumably a more promising location to obtain the information (a site or page with higher information scent for that task).

Information scent reduces the cost of information retrieval by indicating whether the forager should continue browsing in a given area of the website or continue to a new area, or even move to a different website. Thus an information seeker can more efficiently locate information rather than committing substantial cognitive resources to determine whether or not to continue searching in a given location that may or may not contain the desired information. By providing an indication of the likelihood of retrieval success, information seekers can devote more cognitive resources to the information itself rather than to the retrieval tasks.

By increasing the amount of cognitive resources that can be devoted to information retrieval, and also by providing more salient, accurate cues regarding the location of desired information, strong information scent should increase the successful retrieval of desired information. Based on these reasons, anchored in information foraging theory [57, 61], we predict:

Hypothesis 1: Information scent will have a positive impact on successful information retrieval task performance.

## Time Constraints in Information Retrieval

As IFT is focused on information search in the web environment, we turn to the model proposed by Chaiken [11], the heuristic-systematic dual processing model, to further explain human information processing with limited resources. People process information either systematically or heuristically, given that they are motivated and have the ability to process information. Ideally, if time is not constrained, information will be processed in a systematic fashion, assuming that basic motivation for processing has been achieved [6]. However, if a constraint, such as limited time, is placed on users’ ability to process information, they will adopt heuristics, or information-processing shortcuts [19].

Time-constrained seekers resort to heuristic information processing so that an acceptable decision can be reached in the allotted time. Due to feelings of time pressure, a seeker might not review all information in a systematic fashion, and therefore might be unable to reach a more optimal solution that would be discovered through systematic processing. For example, a TV shopper might omit all of last year’s models, all that cost more than \$2,495, and all that are not rated highly by external reviewers. While this approach prunes the list to a more manageable size, it will not be optimal, failing, for example, to uncover a top-rated find that is just \$4 above the specified price cutoff, at \$2,499.

Both processing information more quickly and filtering the information to reduce its volume [69] rely on the use of heuristics to increase the pace of the task. Those heuristics often allow the user to gather only enough information to arrive at a locally optimized solution based on the information that is gathered through his/her retrieval activity [61]. Local optimization indicates that information seekers working under time constraints satisfice overall [57]. The forager attempts to acquire all the potentially available information until the cost exceeds the value of the informationseeking task, at which point the seeker will reach a bounded, optimal solution based on the limited information that is available [58].

Similarly, according to information processing theory [68], users with insufficient time available to complete retrieval tasks will have more difficulty completing them due to decreased available cognitive resources. The time constraint may not allow users to recheck work or identify errors in their original retrieval tasks. Thus, tasks that are completed are more likely to contain errors because of the incomplete, inaccurate, or unrelated information gathered by the user. Therefore:

Hypothesis 2: Time constraints will be associated with inferior information retrieval task performance.

## Stress

Stress occurs when an individual experiences a reduction of mental capacity due to conflicting goals, desires, or limitations in cognitive resources [22]. Information retrieval is a task that has been shown to have a high potential for causing stress [35], especially when the user is confronted with insufficient time or cognitive resources necessary to complete tasks at the desired level of performance [45].

Previous research in psychology has identified two opposite effects of stress on individuals [22], forming an “inverted-U” relationship between stress and performance. At low levels, stress has a positive relationship with performance, given that people need some basic incentive to move into action from a resting position. However, when stress passes a given threshold, performance begins to decrease. At higher levels, this effort-related stress inhibits the ability of an individual to process information [69] and causes a sense of worry or anxiety, which interferes with cognitive functioning [22].

In addition, with reduced capacity to process information, users will suffer a reduced ability to correctly filter information and locate desired information, and therefore will be less likely to complete their tasks successfully. The effort-related stress experienced by users reduces their ability to properly focus their cognitive resources, resulting in poorer performance. By impairing cognitive resources, users are unable to either systematically review information or even to apply thoughtful heuristics that could enhance performance [19, 22]. Further reducing cognitive capacity, stress can also increase the possibility of errors, lengthen the time required to complete tasks, or increase the likelihood of distractions, all of which have been shown to reduce performance in information retrieval tasks [19, 22].

The negative effects of stress on performance have long been studied in other contexts [19, 22, 35], and within the neuroIS research stream [27]. We thus extend these findings to an online setting for information retrieval and propose:

## Hypothesis 3: Stress will be associated with lower performance scores.

Information scent is predicted to reduce stress due to the ability of information scent to provide guidance to users, thereby reducing stress as a by-product of information retrieval. As stress limits available cognitive resources that could be used to review or filter information, information scent could ameliorate this problem by conserving a user’s cognitive capacity. Objects that are described as having high information scent for the given task would have an increased likelihood of supplying information that would enable the user to complete the desired task, and thus reduce the probability of an error or inability to complete the task. By initially identifying and choosing links with higher information scent, there is increased likelihood that more information relevant to the retrieval task will be found, and thereby used to complete the task.

Information scent can also alleviate stress encountered in information retrieval tasks by improving user performance. The inability to achieve an information seeking goal will result in stress [19, 22, 35]. However, as previously described, providing higher-scent cues to lead the user to the desired goal will enable more efficient searching and reduce the likelihood that the user will experience stress from failing to achieve the goal. Achieving the information seeking goal reduces stress from the information retrieval task itself and the stress of filtering and understanding the information gathered. Previous work has found that providing users with frameworks or guides to enable the successful completion of a task often reduces the level of stress [22]. Scent is such a heuristic that serves as a guide to process where desired information is located. Therefore:

## Hypothesis 4: Information scent should negatively impact stress.

Previous studies have found that people experience effort-related stress when subjected to a time constraint, especially when the constraint is perceived as insufficient to complete tasks such as those chosen for this study [35]. Stress is a result of competing cognitive functions experienced by the user (i.e., the need to seek desired information vs. the time constraint placed on the retrieval by an external entity). By forcing users to work within an insufficient time frame, users should experience increased amounts of stress [34, 71]. The conflicting drives to complete the task correctly but within an insufficient allotment of time causes an individual to experience increased amounts of performance-reducing stress, reducing the amount of cognitive resources available [46].

Previous research has long found that time constraints are typically equated with higher levels of stress, especially in tasks such as the ones presented in this study [19, 22, 35]. We thus extend this established relationship to the online information retrieval context and propose:

Hypothesis 5: Time constraints will be positively related to stress.

## Predictors of Website Attitudes

Having proposed how information scent and time constraints affect stress and performance, we now describe how stress and performance can serve as antecedents for attitudes formed by users.

Website attitudes is defined as the users’ feelings toward a website [52]. Website attitudes have been a common variable of interest in a number of e-commerce related studies, partly due to the strong predictive power of attitudes toward website-related intentions [10, 14, 25, 63]. Previous work has largely explored how attitudes are formed from different design decisions and measured their subsequent effects on intentions or behaviors. We expand upon the literature stream by explaining how performance and stress serve as novel antecedents for website attitudes.

Attitudes are affective evaluations, and as such, are highly susceptible to modification due to the current emotional state of the individual [5, 8, 56]. Stress, due to internal mental conflict, is often associated with emotions of frustration and annoyance. Individuals who experience heightened levels of stress often experience conflicting cues from the website, which leads to a negative evaluation regarding its appearance, performance, and design. These conflicts can cause users to feel frustrated and upset, thus leading to a negative affect toward the website. The unpleasant experience can also be attributed to the site being of poor quality, leading to negative affect. Because of this negative affect and the halo effect [49], largely due to negative attributions [36, 37], this emotion will carry over to and influence attitudes regarding the website as a whole [32]. We therefore hypothesize:

## Hypothesis 6: High stress will result in more negative website-specific attitudes

As the Internet has become a common tool in today’s society, many individuals have become very confident in their ability to routinely locate information on a website [43]. However, if a user experiences an unexpectedly easy or difficult time in locating information on a website, he or she might attempt to find a cause for this unexpected outcome [37]. Because extensive previous experience provides a baseline expectation, the reason for succeeding or failing to meet expectations is likely to be attributed to an external source [36, 37]. These external attributions will lead users to credit or blame the website for high or low performance, respectively. Performance is defined as the extent to which navigation is successful/easy or unsuccessful/difficult.

In addition, attitudes are composed of emotional and belief components. The ability or inability of the user to find desired information on the website will impact his or her assessments of the website’s usefulness and its ease of use, both important antecedents of website attitudes [1, 53]. Given that performance will impact the beliefs and attributions of the website, we propose:

Hypothesis 7: Performance will be positively related to website-specific attitudes.

## Methodology

Our study used a 2 (high vs. low information scent) × 2 (high vs. low time constraints) within- and between-subjects factorial design. Subjects were tested in treatment sessions twelve subjects at a time. All were exposed to the same time constraints (the between-subjects treatment). Each subject in a session was exposed to both high and low information scent conditions, comprising the within-subjects treatment. Subjects were exposed to two different contexts within the study, an online mattress store and an online sporting goods store.

During the experiment, subjects completed ten information retrieval tasks. Subjects began with the first website and completed five tasks on this site. After the indicated time (for time-constrained manipulations), or once they completed their tasks (for non–time-constrained manipulations), subjects completed the attituderelated questionnaire for that site. They then proceeded to the second website and completed a new set of five experimental tasks, following the same procedures as those for the first site.

What was different about the second site was that the tasks provided to them were those that would lead users to encounter the opposing scent categorization for the site/task pairing. In other words, subjects encountering high information scent for the first site and set of five tasks would encounter low information scent for the next site/task pairing. Conversely, those encountering low information scent for the first site/task pair would encounter high information scent for the second site/task pair. These were counterbalanced so that exactly half of the subjects encountered high scent on the first site, and low scent on the second site. The other half of the subjects encountered low scent on the first website, and high scent on the second website.

In addition, the order of websites was counterbalanced so that an equal number of subjects viewed each website. A fourth of the subjects viewed only the two mattressrelated websites, and another fourth viewed only the two sporting goods websites. The remaining half of the subjects were assigned one mattress and one sporting goods website to review, with counterbalanced order of presentation for each of the two fourths making up that half.

## Subjects

Subjects were recruited from introductory psychology and information systems courses for course credit and the possibility of a cash prize for top performers. Although 131 subjects were recruited for this study, 12 subjects were removed due to incomplete responses, which resulted in a final sample size of 119. The subject sample consisted of 80 males (67.2 percent) and 39 females (32.8 percent) with an average age of 20.4 years (standard deviation [SD] of 2.0 years). The subjects were predominantly Caucasian (84.0 percent) Americans (92.4 percent), with an average of 2.4 years of college education (SD of 2.3 years).

## Procedures

The researchers explained that the experiment consisted of browsing within two websites, without the aid of a search engine, to answer specific questions that were provided in a packet that contained a text version of all verbal instructions, a page that contained five information-related foraging tasks for an assigned website, and a questionnaire assessing attitudes toward the website. A second section contained an additional five tasks for a second website with the same questionnaire on the following pages. These tasks are listed in Table 1.

All subjects began the experimental tasks at the same time in each group, and a projected stopwatch was used to synchronize starting and stopping times for timeconstrained manipulations. One of the experimenters quite conspicuously sat in the back of the lab with a clear view of all screens to monitor and dissuade participants from employing search tools. This move was successful in completely eliminating that behavior. The use of search tools by some participants and not by others would introduce unwanted variance into our results.

## Information Scent Manipulation

As evaluation of information scent, commonly considered as an attribute of a site, depends upon the task being performed in a given context, the sites’ information scents for the experimental tasks were initially evaluated by the authors. We created a pool of twenty-four tasks for each grouping of websites, with twelve of the twentyfour tasks intended to lead the user to find low scent on the first site and high scent on the second site, and the other twelve intended to provide an encounter with high scent on the first site and low scent on the second site. That is, six tasks were designed to fit each of the following four groups, which paired: (1) tasks for which the first site had low scent; (2) tasks for which the first site had high scent; (3) tasks for which the second site had low scent; and (4) tasks for which the second site had high scent.

Ten out of the twelve tasks that provided the best contrast between high- and lowscent site–task pairings were retained for use in the experiment, which resulted in five high-scent tasks on website A and five low-scent tasks on website B. Furthermore, the five low-scent tasks on website A were designed to be the five high-scent tasks on website B and vice versa. The tasks that were included in the packet provided to the subjects are listed in Table 1. The four discarded tasks were not used in the study.

The validity of high and low information scent scores were judged by a panel of five experts. These experts were provided training about information scent and information foraging theory. Interrater reliability for the chosen tasks was measured at 90 percent. These results confirmed the intended high and low information scent evaluations in the created pool of tasks for each of the four websites. See Table 1 for the final selection of tasks per website, and their associated site information scent ratings. We report the statistical support for high and low information scent manipulations in the “Measures” portion of this section and in Tables 2 and 3.

## Time-Constraint Manipulation

The handout and verbal instructions indicated that the lab session had either a time limit of 6.5 minutes to work on these tasks or no constraint. The time constraint was based on a pilot study of the experiment with twelve subjects. The average time to complete tasks was 11 minutes and 45 seconds. In order to manipulate effectively the time constraints for the majority of participants, we halved the average time to complete the tasks and rounded to the nearest half minute, which resulted in a 6.5- minute time constraint. This effectively created a time limit that was deliberately insufficient to complete the given tasks.

To ascertain the effectiveness of the time constraint, we compared the average number of correct responses of the time-constrained subjects (2.14 correct responses out of a set of five tasks) to those who completed the tasks without time constraints (2.82 correct responses out of the set of five tasks, a 31.8 percent increase). A t-test of these means resulted in a significant difference between the groups (t = 14.72, p < .000), indicating that performance was significantly and negatively impacted by the time constraints.

## Experimental Websites

Four commercial websites, two regional bed and mattress stores and two regional sporting goods stores, were chosen for this study. Examination of the websites ensured that they were of roughly equivalent breadth and depth. The stores were located far from the area of the university to limit the number of subjects who might have visited and purchased from the sites. The large size of the sites made it unlikely for participants to easily or accidentally locate desired information, requiring subjects to use links as navigational cues to browse for, and eventually locate, products, services, or information.

As previously explained, subjects viewed only two of these four websites. Half of the subjects viewed only one context, sporting goods or mattress, and the other half viewed one website from each domain. The order in which they viewed the sites was completely counterbalanced, and the probability of each website being viewed and in the given order was held constant for all subjects. This enabled us to ascertain whether the order and site type (mattress versus sporting goods) had an impact on the subjects and their perceived attitudes. Our analysis revealed no such effects.

## Measures

Riedl et al. [63] provide cautions about rating scales; therefore, we attempted to provide a variety of measures, including objective (galvanic skin response [GSR]), calculated (information scent) subjective (rating scales). The mixture of measures reduces compounding of rating scale difficulties in our model [63].

## Information Scent

Information scent was measured using latent semantic analysis as performed by IUNIS (an information scent measurement tool developed by Chi et al. [12]) to provide a quantitative assessment of the information scent of the tasks with each experimental website. The analysis was performed on the task text and all available text on the home page for the given website. The tool provides a measure of scent by evaluating the similarity of the words used for the task goal and the words used as website links. The results of latent semantic analysis were very similar to the raters judgments, and support the high- and low-information scent task assignments used in the study. These tasks are listed in Table 1.

The results of this analysis for the tasks are shown in Table 2, and Table 3 shows the paired t-test sample of mean differences within the website (high vs. low scent) and between the website and its sister site within the same store domain (high scent of the site vs. low scent for the sister site). The last test shows that, with the same tasks, switching to the other website (designed with opposite scent with respect to the task) makes a significant difference in information scent measured. These measurements were used for testing the empirical model.

## Website-specific Attitudes

Previously developed and validated instruments were used to gather the attitudes toward the website [24, 25]. Questions, shown in Table A1, were modified slightly to reflect the context of the experiment, tasks performed, and the websites used.

## User Performance

User performance was an objective score obtained by counting the number of experimental tasks completed successfully out of the ten assigned. We controlled for differences between websites by standardizing all performance scores for the website used for each set of five tasks, across constraint conditions. This standardization controlled for differences in the nature of the websites. For example, for five tasks that a subject performed on website 1, the raw score was reduced by the mean performance for the website, and then divided by the standard deviation to obtain a standardized performance Z-score.

## Stress

All subjects wore nonintrusive Body Media™ biometric readers<sup>2</sup> (much like cuffs used to measure blood pressure, except that they were not tightened enough to constrict blood flow). Four different physiological measures were used as formative indicators of stress, all of which have been used in previous medical research on stress as proxy indicators [34, 71].

The first measure, GSR, is the level of measured conductivity facilitated by moisture on the surface of the skin. Due to the variance of time that subjects were exposed to the websites, a baseline GSR was obtained by creating an average of the GSR that was measured while the experimenters obtained informed consent from the subjects and explained the study. This baseline was then subtracted from the GSR score that was obtained by averaging the scores obtained during the fourth, fifth, and sixth minutes after the experiment tasks began for each task set. This differential was then standardized for all subjects.

Three other measures were heat flux (HF), near body temperature (NBT), and skin temperature (ST). For all three, procedures similar to that used for GSR were used to calculate the baseline and differential, standardized scores. HF represents the temperature of the heat emitted by the body. The same procedures were used to calculate a baseline and a changed score to result in a differential score for HF, which was then standardized for all subjects. NBT measured the outer edge of the heat flux sensor. Finally, ST indicated the current temperature of the surface of the skin itself.

## Time Constraint

As described earlier, each constraint level was modeled based on the number of minutes that subjects were given to complete the tasks. Subjects with the time constraint were given 6.5 minutes to complete the task. Those in the no-constraint group were told that they could take as long as they needed.

## Analysis and Results

We performed our analysis using the partial least squares (PLS) method. PLS is especially suited for early theory development (such as presented in this paper) as opposed to situations where prior theory is highly developed and further testing and extension is the primary objective. In those cases, techniques such as maximum likelihood or generalized least squares are often preferred [13, 26]. We used the package SmartPLS (2.0 beta version) [64] for our analysis.

## Formative Constructs

Validating formative indicators is more challenging than validating reflective indicators, because the established procedures to determine the validity of reflective measures do not apply to formative measures [55], and the procedures for validating formative measures are less known and established [21]. Researchers have generally used theoretical reasoning to support the validity of formative constructs [21], although there are approaches that can be used beyond theoretical reasoning alone [44, 55]. Although no technique is widely established for validating formative measures, we followed a promising modified multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) approach [41, 42, 44].

For each formative item, we created new values that were the product of the original item values by their respective PLS weights (representing each item’s weighted score). We then created a composite score for the formative stress construct in this study by summing all the weighted scores for a construct. We then produced correlations of this calculated value with its respective indicators (Table A2).

To test convergent validity, we checked whether all the items within the construct highly correlated with each other and whether the items within the construct correlated with the construct value. This was true in all cases, implying convergent validity. Although we would ideally want inter-item correlations to be higher within a given construct, this cannot be strictly enforced because there are exceptions depending on the theoretical nature of the formative measure [21, 41]. Thus, we believe the most meaningful discriminant validity check with formative measures is to look at the degree to which items within a construct correlate with a given construct. We report adequate correlations between indicators and the formed stress construct.<sup>3</sup> However, given that all indicators have good correlations, they are all being maintained.

Finally, we used another approach to assess formative validity, as suggested by Petter, Straub, and Rai [55], which involves testing for multicollinearity among the indicators. This is particularly important with formative indicators because multicollinearity poses a much greater problem than is the case with reflective indicators. ## Discussion

Our research model is largely supported, as we have summarized in Table 6. The remainder of this section will summarize the results of the study.Our model indicates that information scent, time constraints, and stress are significant predictors of performance. Information scent was a strong predictor of performance $( \beta = 0 . 5 9 6 )$ supporting H1. Both time constraints and stress were negative predictors of performance with time constraints reporting a more negative effect on performance $( \beta = - 0 . 4 7 7 )$ than stress $( \beta = - 0 . 0 8 4 )$ , which supports both H2 and H4. Just over half $( R ^ { 2 } = { . 5 1 8 } )$ of the variation in performance is explained by information scent, time constraint, and stress, in decreasing order of influence. The significance of the information scent antecedent on performance provides strong evidence that poor scent will impair the ability of users to accomplish their tasks.

Our results also show that only information scent was a significant predictor of stress, but time constraints were not. Specifically, all tasks that were performed with high information scent were able to moderately reduce the level of felt stress $( \beta = - 0 . 1 3 2 )$ , supporting H4. However, we found no support for the relationship between time constraint and stress, which thus fails to support H5. We speculate that time constraint was not a significant antecedent of stress because, in the laboratory, users might assume they have been given the time constraint deliberately and that the outcome of losing the chance to win a prize might not have been so serious. In the field, time constraint would likely become a more significant stressor for people caught in a rush to accomplish more tasks than their available time.

Our results also indicate that overall website attitudes are positively predicted by performance (H7), but not by stress (H6). Subjects with higher levels of performance had much more positive attitudes toward the website $( \beta = 0 . 6 1 4 )$ . However, despite predicting a moderate amount of stress $( R ^ { 2 } = . 2 2 9 )$ , higher levels of stress did not cause individuals to have more negative attitudes toward the website. It is possible that attributions of stress were internal rather than external, which indicates that subjects might have blamed themselves or other targets rather than the sites for their reactions to the stress-inducing manipulations. For instance, they could have blamed the experimenters or the tasks themselves.

## Implications

Three main goals of this paper were investigated: (1) to explain how time constraints impact stress and performance; (2) to demonstrate how information scent impacts stress and performance; and (3) to show how stress and performance serve as antecedents of website attitudes. To accomplish these goals, we proposed a model that was largely supported by the results of an experimental study. In the remainder of this section, we discuss the implications of this study for researchers and practitioners.

For researchers, we provided evidence that multiple theories are sometimes needed to bring together even a relatively modest set of constructs. Many models are focused mainly on cognitive or affective constructs, rather than integrating them. Therefore, we showed that multiple theoretical frameworks can fit together and models built from such roots can indeed provide a relatively large amount of explained variance.

This study also employed new methodological approaches, integrating both objective and subjective measures. Using objective measures such as GSR, latent semantic analysis, and task performance helps to avoid common method bias. Researchers can use those measures with relatively affordable equipment such as the Body Media™ devices and free software such as the latent semantic analysis tool offered by the University of Colorado (http://lsa.colorado.edu/). These tools require some training but do not require degrees in neurobiology or linguistics. In contrast, objective measures can indeed be obtained through fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging) equipment, but both equipment costs and required experimenter expertise are extremely high.

We also proposed and demonstrated that the performance of users in completing their tasks is an important element in determining their attitudes toward websites. Based on attribution theory, we demonstrated that when subjects have more difficulty locating desired information on a website as compared against other sites, they attribute this to the website. A user’s belief that a site’s design is poor compared to other sites results in more negative website-specific attitudes. Unlike most other studies on website design and e-commerce, this study shows that performance, usually considered to be an attribute of an individual, serves as a novel yet potent antecedent of attitudes toward a website. The use of attribution theory can provide alternative viewpoints and research questions that might have been overlooked in previous studies derived from other theoretical bases.

Information scent, from information foraging theory, was shown to be an important element in understanding user performance and stress, which ultimately strongly influence attitudes toward the site. This theory can be extended to nearly any domain where users must choose among elements displayed on a screen, including web links to pull-down menu selections to search results, and perhaps even to choices of “apps” to use on a mobile phone.

Information foraging provides an important, strong lens through which website design can be evaluated. Previous studies have focused on individuals’ perceptions of website quality, design features, layout, and so on, and their effects on intermediating variables such as ease of use, information fit to task, trust, and quality. This study brings information scent to the forefront and highlights the variety of goals and desires driving individuals who search for information on a website. Rather than focusing on how design elements and perceptions of these elements impact each user, IFT shows and proposes that users are diverse, and their unique search tasks show that users’ needs are distinct and separate. First-time users on a website are likely to have different needs from loyal users, and both would differ from those browsing for pleasure or even for work purposes.

Each individual might even have different tasks that they are trying to complete, and the scent for each task could vary greatly on the same web page. Previous studies on human–computer interaction, specifically on the design of websites, have not usually taken this person–task perspective into account when assessing and providing guidelines relative to the optimal design of websites. This study provides a lens to do so, and the tools wherein future research can explore the person–task view of the website and how that can alter the design of the website and impact desirable outcomes.

Our study is also unique in its approach to testing the effects of information scent, time constraints, and performance on website-specific attitudes through the use of real websites. The results support the idea that information scent is not a contrived artifact that is present only in an experimental setting (as in [25]), but can demonstrate strong effects with subjects using real websites.

Furthermore, our manipulations of information scent on four different websites were able to cause first-time users to a website to perceive it more positively or negatively, despite no actual changes to the website. Thus, we highlight the importance of task and resultant information scent when assessing attitudes of site users. The information needs of the information seeker must be taken into account, as those needs will alter the perceptions of the user in assessing how well the website is able to fit their needs.

Finally, our findings show that effort-related stress is most strongly related to information scent and not from time constraints, but that time constraints are a strong predictor of performance. Given that there are many antecedents of stress, it is notable that we explained a meaningful amount of variance in stress in our results $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 2 9 )$ . Furthermore, the use of a biometric device to measure stress is a novel and important contribution to stress, information seeking, and processing research. The use of such a tool meets the call for research that begins to unlock the “black box” of the user [70]. Specifically, by relying on biometric readings of the body, our measure of stress is based on results that cannot be altered by the mental filters an individual may employ when completing a questionnaire about stress.

For practitioners, we provide evidence that website owners should focus on information scent of their sites, taking into account typical tasks users would attempt. We have demonstrated that information scent can be quantitatively analyzed through a free latent semantic analysis (LSA) tool or through the use of expert assessment, which correlates very strongly with the objective LSA tool (> 90 percent).

Designers should first create a comprehensive list of tasks that users are likely to perform on the website. Tasks can be identified by compiling the goals or objectives that many website users would need to complete (e.g., “How can I contact customer service?”). To ascertain that users will encounter high scent, the designer would then test that the text links on the desired web pages correlate well with the identified tasks using latent semantic analysis, by performing a one-to-many comparison. Continuing the example, a link “Contact us” or “Customer Service” would have a very high scent score. However, “Help” would not score as high, and “About us” might be even lower. Thus, practitioners should compare the text used in the links on the webpage against the identified user tasks. Ideally, scores that exceed 0.40 using latent semantic analysis between a task and the web page would be identified as having high information scent.

Also, building on other studies that have focused on information scent, our analysis highlights the fact that, while information scent is a commonly used attribute of a site, proclaiming that an entire site has “good scent” or “poor scent” is superficial. Information scent must be assessed with respect to a particular goal, just as an animal doing a search in a particular yard might find varying scents all around. If searching for food, the scent might be strong. If searching for another animal, it might be weak.

Likewise, our analysis firmly links the manipulations with the tasks users are trying to perform. Therefore, a site itself should only be condemned or praised with respect to a particular task or set of tasks. Our study shows that one site can clearly dominate another when considering one set of carefully chosen tasks, while the reverse can be the case when using an alternative set of tasks. Designing links from a comprehensive list of important tasks will ensure that high-level links are not neglected.

## Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. The first limitation is the use of college students as subjects. While we cannot claim that all other adults would provide the same support for our hypotheses, there is little reason to believe that students would behave and react differently than others in the context of our study. In a metaanalysis of the technology acceptance model, King and He [39] found that students provide results that are similar to those from other sampling frames. Furthermore, we expect that the student population is quite suitable for the testing of Internet users, as they tend to be fairly proficient in this domain.

Our goal in this study was to explore whether we could detect stress and then measure its impacts on performance and attitudes. The results indicated that we did provide a situation in which there was a significant amount of stress that had measurable impacts. Future studies are needed to discern whether researchers would characterize this stress as “high” or “medium,” thus identifying where on the inverted U it lies.

Another limitation is that the sites might not have been as interesting to participants as sites that they normally browse. To address this potential problem, we (1) attempted to make the questions interesting and challenging, and (2) provided a monetary incentive for participant speed and accuracy.

Furthermore, we did not include individual traits that would be provided in an expanded model. Examples of individual traits would be stress-proneness or impatience of each individual in a study. A future study should measure such traits to account for more of the variance in statistical models.

We also did not collect process tracing or tracking of users’ browsing patterns, largely due to potential interference with subject performance and stress. Had we collected these data, the stress outcomes might have been artificially stimulated from introspection required by tracing. We also did not track browsing patterns because we were most interested in focusing on the browsing task itself. Logging the clicks of the subjects is a promising avenue for future research, as stress levels might correlate closely to the loss or gain of information scent. The challenge will be significant, as those future researchers might find difficulties caused by latency between a stimulus and the skin response, which is moisture detected by a GSR device from secretions on the skin. The moisture must travel from subcutaneous layers out to the surface of the skin. A neuroscience-based study that detects electrical signals (e.g., electroencephalogram [EEG]) might be most useful given its lower latency [27].

In addition, experimental tasks in a lab might not represent tasks that the participants normally undertake. We minimized this potential problem by moving outside of a controlled and fabricated lab-only site toward four actual sites. The sporting goods sites and browsing tasks included a diverse selection of sporting goods, increasing the number of participants who would be interested in fulfilling the tasks. Sports of one kind or another are of interest to a large number of people. The mattress sites were chosen because of the widespread use of mattresses in modern society for several hours each night, and therefore virtually everyone has at least passing interest or experience with this topic.

Likewise, the experimental setting might not have mimicked a real setting exactly, whether online or in a physical store. In a physical store, other stressors can also be an issue, when one considers personal budgetary difficulties and having to choose a product of acceptable quality. Fortunately, the portability of the biometric devices provides an opportunity to conduct a future study in that setting; a future study could be conducted that allows users to wear the band when they actually shop. Controls will be a serious concern in such a study, so the constraints of the laboratory described here should be relaxed carefully, gradually, and strategically.

For instance, one constraint was to prohibit and monitor violations in the use of both internal and external search engines, which diminished realism to some extent. However, as described above, users have several difficulties with search engines [50], and even when searches are successful, users must follow the scent of the most promising search result. Additionally, the quality of the internal search engines differed significantly among our sites. Therefore, in future studies, if searching is permitted, step-by-step user actions would need to be tracked and analyzed.

In addition, although the study involved only online retail stores, there is opportunity to extend it to business contexts such as intranets or business-to-business applications. Such a study would need to be taken to the field, rather than conducted in a university laboratory with students.

Also, although the use of galvanic skin response is a commonly used measure of stress [33], our study found still more variance unexplained by that measure. Two likely explanations are that there are many factors that exert stress on individuals simultaneously, and/or participants “gave up” trying to answer questions when they encountered the low-scent task–site pairs, no longer trying hard to win the potential prizes that could be earned by high performance. Because they might more easily give up in our lab study, our results are likely to be conservative; those who would “give up” would likely experience lower stress than those racing the clock up to the last second. However, the significant and meaningful prediction of stress by poor information scent is an interesting indication that people are indeed physically affected by difficult navigation in a website. We are not aware of any previous study in management information systems having detected significant and meaningful objective measures of stress.

Finally, the choice of using real websites for this study is inherently limiting. Besides prohibiting the use of search engines as described above, we selected websites that were roughly equivalent in depth and breadth. Beyond our attempts to increase experimental control, we also standardized performance across websites and controlled for any possible variations that occurred across these websites. We believe that the increased external validity from using real sites outweighs any disadvantages from lower experimental control.

## Conclusion

This study was meant to provide several implications for both researchers and practitioners. For researchers, we demonstrated the usefulness of validating an experimenter’s hope or assertion that a site has high or low scent by using a latent semantic analysis tool, and used the resultant measure successfully in our model.

We also provided a model that linked effort-related stress, a possible measure of user frustration, to antecedent site characteristics and consequent performance and attitudes. While an $R ^ { 2 }$ of .229 shows that the model does not explain most of the variance in stress, we found reasonably strong evidence that time constraint plays a role in performance and, ultimately, website attitudes. Future research should examine the time interval over which GSR is measured by using tasks of a much longer duration, and further explore the interactive relationship between stress and information scent. Other measures besides GSR should also be investigated, although the formality and extensive procedures of fMRI might be incompatible with effective manipulation of time constraints.

For practitioners, the linkage of task to information scent provides strong advice for site designers to produce a large set of important tasks that a wide variety of users are likely to attempt, and design and test their site based on the task set. The larger the set that is identified, the higher the scent that actual users will encounter. However, a designer might quickly find it difficult to be comprehensive in listing those tasks.

Finally, the expanding prevalence of time constraints emphasizes the need to provide quick access to important site contents. This finding could help site designers become more effective, and entice more consumers to purchase from online outlets again and again. Reducing the ranks of disenfranchised users can begin with developing a comprehensive set of target tasks for a website and improving the information scent on the website to support those tasks.
