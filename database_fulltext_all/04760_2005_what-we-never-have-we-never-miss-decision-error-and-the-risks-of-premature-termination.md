# What we never have, we never miss? Decision error and the risks of premature termination

## Abstract

The history of IT systems construction contains many examples of large-scale failures that were compounded by undue persistence – a phenomenon known as escalation of commitment. Yet organizations also need to guard against erring in the opposite direction, that is, terminating (or never starting) a project that, on economic grounds, ought to be completed. This paper highlights the notion of premature termination, suggests why such errors are possible and discusses the implications for practice.

Journal of Information Technology (2005) 20, 170–176. doi:10.1057/palgrave.jit.2000046

Keywords: escalation of commitment; de-escalation of commitment; decision dilemmas; risk

## Introduction: ‘The system was blinking red’

vents of 11 September 2001 stunned the world. Yet the official report into the disaster suggests that it was not entirely unexpected. One of the most chilling passages of the report entitled, ‘The System Was Blinking Red’ is peppered with reports of suspicious incidents such as foreigners known to possess jihadist connections paying in cash for flight training on Pan Am’s Boeing 747 flight simulators who clearly had no intention of becoming commercial pilots and of persons identified as major league killers passing money around. The report depicts images of intelligence officials (ignorant of the flight training reports) searching CIA databases for information about the movements of those ‘major league killers’ and discovering that they held US visas; then re-examining old cables trying to ‘figure out what those cables meant’, (The 9/11 Commission Report, 2004: 267) and waiting for information from other departments in an effort to unearth information about possible terrorist connections knowing that ‘something bad was definitely up’ (The 9/11 Commission Report, 2004: 268).

Their efforts were unavailing because of the void between foreign and domestic intelligence agencies:

The foreign intelligence agencies were watching overseas, alert to foreign threats to US interests there. The domestic agencies were waiting for evidence of y a threat from sleeper cells within the United States. No one was looking for a foreign threat to domestic targets. The threat that was coming was not from sleeper cells. It was foreign-but from foreigners who had infiltrated into the United States. (The 9/11 Commission Report, 2004: 263) Given time the intelligence agencies might have assembled enough pieces of the jigsaw to fill that void but by late August 2001, ‘Time was short, and running out’. (The 9/11 Commission Report, 2004: 277)

What occurred is uncannily akin to the failure of sensemaking that preceded the Three Mile Island disaster where the warning lights were blinking but no one understood their meaning because of the interaction of multiple failures not in direct operational sequence. Moreover, to paraphrase Perrow (1984: 31) if anyone had a hunch about what was happening they failed to pause long enough to examine the implications of their intuition.

US visa requirements have since been tightened. The US government wants to go much further, however and incorporate fingerprints and a digital image of the traveller’s face in all passports. The proposed system, named US VISIT, aims to deter terrorism by making passports harder to fake. Moreover, since passport information can be scanned straight into security data bases, US VISIT will enable intelligence agencies to instantly identify terrorist suspects and track their movements. Yet US VISIT has met with a barrage of criticism. Critics say that it is too expensive, (estimates of costs vary between \$3–\$10 billion), too complex (it would be the largest IT project ever undertaken in the US), would harm tourism and trade and might not be effective anyway. In consequence, the US government has been forced to postpone work on the new system and speculation exists that it may never be built (Land of the Free, 2003; Garrahan, 2004).

Critics of US VISIT may have a point. Such solutions may cost more lives than they save by diverting resources from other projects. Large-scale IT projects, moreover, are indeed risky and there have been many cases of ill-judged ventures that have escalated out of control (e.g., Griffiths and Willcocks, 1994; Drummond and Hodgson, 2003). Yet it is equally arguable that by blocking the venture critics may be endangering lives. While we know something about what causes escalation (e.g., Drummond, 1996a, b; Monteaglre and Keil, 2000; Heng et al., 2003; Natovich, 2003; Sabherwal et al., 2003), comparatively little attention has been paid to the opposite risk namely that important projects like US VISIT may be stifled – a phenomenon known as premature termination.

This paper highlights the notion of premature termination and suggests why such errors are possible. The paper begins by defining premature termination and what constitutes a ‘good’ decision. There follows an explanation of why organizations may make such errors couched in the language of escalation theory. In a nutshell, it is argued that errors of escalation and premature termination may reflect similar driving forces. A tentative attempt is then made to specify the particular pre-conditions likely to give rise to errors of premature termination. The various theories are illustrated with case examples. Emphatically, these are only intended to facilitate comprehension and not to ‘prove’ a point. (For a discussion of the difference between illustrating and testing theory see Merton, 1968). The paper ends with suggestions for practice.

## Premature termination

Premature termination may be defined as failing to adopt or progress a project that on economic grounds ought to be pursued. Economics tells us that in order to make an optimal decision we should weigh marginal benefits, that is, current and future benefits, against marginal costs and select the option that offers benefits that most outweigh costs (e.g., Samuelson and Nordhaus, 1985; Frank, 1991). This advice is problematic because costs and benefits can only be estimated and are sensitive to the discount rate selected.

In any case, all projects involving uncertainty run the risk that expectations might not be met. So adoption and progress decisions are likely to be beset with conflict with actors disagreeing about how best to allocate scarce resources (e.g., Pfeffer, 1982; Staw, 1996; Drummond, 2001).

## Psychological influences

Economics also tells us that non-economic variables should not influence such decisions. In practice all decisions are subject to emotional influences and cognitive biases that can undermine utility maximization. Crudely speaking conflict about whether to adopt or progress a particular project is likely to feature project champions proclaiming the benefits of the project and exit champions publicly or privately decrying it as a waste of money or something that will never work. Self-justification theory predicts that people tend to develop attitudes to suit situations that they have committed themselves to in order to protect themselves from ego-damage. For instance, Schoorman’s (1988) field study of performance appraisals found that supervisors who were over-ruled in recruitment decisions subsequently tended to rate employees below what their performance data actually merited. Likewise, Bazerman et al. (1982) observed that similar biasing occurred when predicting employees’ future performance.

Ego-defensive behaviors can manifest themselves in a variety of ways in IT projects. For example, project TAURUS a d500 million IT venture that collapsed in 1993 was beset from the start by problems of trying to reengineer ‘off the peg’ software named VISTA to perform a novel task. Yet some of the problems were allegedly more apparent than real, reflecting key engineers’ opposition to the decision to utilize VISTA to drive TAURUS instead of accepting their recommendation that the software be designed from scratch (Private Communication, 1995).

Since few complex projects go completely smoothly, feedback during the construction phase is likely to comprise a mixture of positive and negative information. Individuals with egos to defend tend to process information selectively, that is, they typically pay more attention to information that appears to support their pre-conceived views while paying disproportionately less attention to contradictory data. Conceivably exit champions are likely to be keenly aware of negative feedback. Peter Rawlins (former Chief Executive of the London Stock Exchange) clearly resented TAURUS calling it ‘an albatross’ and ‘a brick around his neck’ (Drummond, 1996a). While no one has seriously suggested that Rawlins’ eventual decision to cancel the project was wrong, Rawlins may nevertheless have unconsciously taken an under-sanguine view of the value of the project and/or the likelihood of successful completion. Significantly, his account of events leading up to the decision to cancel TAURUS contains a hint of egodefensiveness:

I had had enough two years on of people telling me, ‘Don’t worry Peter it will be with us soon.’ All of my antennae telling me that my original instincts that this thing stank were right. (Drummond, 1996a: 146)

I kept banging on because it didn’t ring true to me. Everybody else was prepared to just tuck it under the carpet and keep on asking the easy question, ‘How much longer?’. Almost superficial due diligence. (Drummond, 1996a: 147)

Even disinterested actors may be error prone when it comes to processing information. Evidence suggests that actors who were not responsible for decisions to commission projects tend to be prospectively rational when feedback is negative, focusing upon how to increase outcomes by intensifying information searches and other cognitive activity (Staw and Ross, 1978). Logically, prospective focusing should produce more economically rational decisions than retrospective focusing where actors are more concerned with information that justifies past actions. Since, however, prospectively rational actors tend to be highly conscious of endogenous causes of failure namely foreseeable setbacks likely to reoccur, they may well over-estimate the risks of continuing with a project.

Premature termination may also be related to perceived efficacy. Whereas, individuals with high efficacy scores tend to be prone to errors of escalation (presumably because their innate self-confidence persuades them that they cannot fail) (e.g., Whyte et al., 1997) project managers with low efficacy scores may be unwilling to support difficult projects and/or give up too easily when feedback is negative. To be more precise, learned helplessness is the converse of perceived efficacy – where the individual perceives no link between efforts and outcomes and is therefore less likely to conduct information searches and engage in other forms of cognitive activity that might enable them to recover from adversity (Staw and Ross, 1978).

## Audience effects

Adoption and progress decisions usually involve an audience. The presence of an audience can prompt actors to take economically sub-optimal decisions in order to maintain or enhance credibility. For instance, Brockner et al. (1981: 78) observed that people who set limits in public were more likely to terminate an investment decision regardless of economic data when limits were reached, than subjects who set limits privately. In de-briefings, respondents said that their decisions were indeed influenced by social pressure, ‘I thought it would look good to quit’. Conceivably, exit champions who publicly decry a project may experience similar pressure. Moreover, symbolism is important in leadership. Leaders who wish to be perceived as successful must be careful to distance themselves from apparently failing projects (Pfeffer, 1977). Moreover, shutting down projects can enable a leader to show their prowess in a display of firm and decisive action. ‘If we stop it we stop it,’ said Rawlins of TAURUS, ‘I didn’t want anyone panicking, I wanted it all trussed, tied up, and done’ (Drummond, 1996a: 150).

## Organizational factors

In 1990, when the UK considered introducing computerized cattle tracking to help control the spread of BSE there was no evidence that BSE was transferable to humans. Moreover, even if BSE could jump the species barrier medical scientists were advising that people would only contract the disease by consuming huge amounts of infected material. Expense was another factor. Even the most basic information requirements for disease control are fairly complex as they involve knowledge of dam and any progeny, where and when an animal was born, where and when it has been moved to and the identity of any animals it may have been in contact with. Speed of tracing can also be important depending upon the nature of the disease meaning that farmers have to be supplied with hardware and software, trained, encouraged and ultimately compelled to input data accurately and promptly (BSE Inquiry, 2000). It was such a formidable task that the idea was dropped.

After 3 years in December 1993 after the number of confirmed cases of BSE had escalated dramatically an official in the Ministry of Agriculture Fisheries and Food noted:

Veterinary officers have had to carry out extensive tracing of livestock movements for disease control purposes y These tracing exercises were unfortunately severely hampered by inaccurate stock movements y. To give an indication of the size of the problem, during a recent exercise in one particular county 43% of breeding records and 26% of animal movements records were found to be incomplete or non-existent. (BSE Inquiry, 2000: Vol. 12: 18 italics in original)

It was a small survey but it depicts a system in utter confusion leaving no alternative to mass cull and slaughter when it emerged that humans could contract a variant of BSE by consuming as little as 1 g (two peppercorns) of infected material.

In retrospect, if computerized cattle tracking had been introduced in the early phases of the BSE crisis, the UK beef industry would subsequently have been in a much stronger position to meet European requirements that exports must have BSE free provenance. In 1990 the ramifications of BSE lay far out into the future and decision-makers were dealing with seemingly remote contingencies. The episode could be regarded as a failure of scientific imagination rather than a decision error. Yet, when Foot and Mouth disease erupted in the UK 10 years later in 2000, there was still no proper computerized system of animal tracking (Foot and Mouth Disease, 2001). Why were the lessons of BSE not acted upon?

Resource allocation decisions in complex organizations may be ultimately determined by politics. Politics reflects the use of power in the pursuit of interest. Interest typically seeks to influence decision processes in order to advance change or to block it. The resolution of political contests is reflected in ‘who gets what’ in the organization (Lasswell, 1936; Miller, 1962), which means that decisions do not always observe economic logic though they may be cloaked by the appearance of rationality (Pettigrew, 1973; Pfeffer, 1982; Drummond, 2001). For instance, certain city councils in the UK insisted upon departments using manifestly slow and cumbersome direct labor organization control systems long after superior dedicated software became available. Ostensibly, the aim was to ensure consistency and make full use of existing resources. Coincidentally, the old system reposed control with the center.

The pursuit of interest can be naked. Sauer (1993 see also Meyer and Rowan, 1978) suggests that the success or failure of IT systems ultimately turns not upon technical merit but whether or not they command support from powerful actors. When the manual system for settling securities trades almost collapsed in 1987, the Stock Exchange proposed an IT-based system named TAURUS 1. TAURUS 1 was designed to serve institutional investors who accounted for over 70 percent of the value of trades on the London stock market. It could have been built in 6 months using tried and tested technology. Yet the Stock Exchange was forced to abandon the project in favor of the disastrous hybrid design because the retail broking industry objected to being excluded. The New York Stock

Exchange’s preference for open outcry trading is likewise self-serving as brokers profit from the informational cues contained in public trading. The losers are the large investors in the stock market like pension funds currently obliged to trade via the exchange (Under threat, 2004).

Politics frequently reflect the so-called ‘non-decisions’, that is, ‘what is not’ – the ‘taken for granted’ assumptions about what is appropriate when considering IT solutions. The West Coast Main Line is the busiest intercity route in the UK yet until recently and sections of track were controlled by semaphore signals – IT dating from the Victorian era (Meek, 2004). The City finally achieved a system of electronic settlement for securities in 1995 in the shape of CREST. CREST has been successful in that requirements have been met and the system has gained user acceptance. The ‘what is not’ is that UK securities’ trading remains a relatively slow and expensive business despite huge advances in IT and computing power. For instance, until very recently dividends to shareholders were still paid manually – a reflection of retail brokers’ reluctance to support innovations that could de-intermediate them. Metal strips on UK passports contain hardly any basic information far less biometric data. Yet until such data is introduced, border control agencies have no quick and reliable way of verifying that the person carrying the passport is who they claim to be (Garrahan, 2004). Significantly, the UK government’s response has been one of marked silence.

## Potential preconditions for premature termination

So far it has been suggested that premature termination may reflect psychological, social and organizational pressures similar to those that have been linked to errors of escalation (e.g., Staw, 1981; Staw and Ross, 1987; Drummond, 1995, 1996a, b). When are organizations more likely to err in the direction of premature termination? Although the existence of conflict implies that decisions ultimately turn upon the balance of power between competing factions, the risk of premature termination may be heightened under certain conditions. This literature is examined next.

## Prospect theory and premature termination

Although the global Rothschild banking dynasty was founded upon the early adoption of carrier pigeons as a primitive form of Internet, their pre-eminent position in the world financial system would soon have crumbled if they had continued to rely upon this form of IT beyond the invention of the telegraph. It is likewise inconceivable that Freshfields would have emerged as one of the leading law firms in the City of London if the ‘new fangled’ telephone had remained confined to the basement – to be used only for making appointments and never, on any account, for discussing client’s business.

Paradoxically, it may be the most successful organizations that are most reluctant to adopt innovatory IT solutions. This is because when an organization is successful decisions are likely to be formulated as a choice between gains (Whyte, 1986). Prospect theory predicts that decision-makers tend to become risk averse when choosing between gains preferring an outcome that is certain to one that is merely probable even though the latter offers a much higher monetary expectation – the ‘proverbial bird in hand’ in other words. (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979; see also Whyte, 1986, 1989; Moon et al., 2003). The Stock Exchange’s batch processing TALISMAN system survived long after ‘real time’ processing became available because users trusted it. A member of the Stock Exchange technical staff said, ‘Talisman wasn’t a particularly good system y. It had the merit of working. That was all.’ (Drummond, 1996a: 50).

## Budgets and premature termination

Budgets are a plausible force for premature termination. A financial budget may be defined as, ‘a quantitative expression for a set time period of a proposed future plan of action by management,’ (Tan and Yates, 2002: 301citing Horngren, Foster and Datar). Mental budgeting theory suggests that individuals tend to compartmentalize resource allocations and track expenditure accordingly (Heath, 1995; Heath and Soll, 1996; Johnstone, 2002). Budgets (financial or mental) can curb the so-called ‘runaway’ projects by setting limits on expenditure and forcing decision-makers to justify additional resource allocations. Yet, budgets can be counter-productive because exit champions may cite budget depletion to justify terminating a project. This is erroneous because budget depletion constitutes a non-informative loss, that is, it tells the decision-maker nothing about the state of the project and whether it is worth completing (Heath, 1995; Tan and Yates, 2002).

For instance, having successfully streamlined trading via project TALISMAN in 1979, the London Stock Exchange planned to computerize its antiquated paper-driven settlement systems (exchange of cash and shares) when it suddenly cancelled the project and dismissed the highly experienced technical team because TALISMAN, ‘had had it all too good for too long,’ (Drummond, 1996a: 50). Economic rationality demands that in allocating resources decision-makers weigh the probability of future outcomes and the value of future outcomes. Project TALISMAN may have dominated the agenda for 4 years, absorbing the lion’s share of time, money and attention but these were noninformative losses. The decision was taken without properly examining the economic case for continuance.

## Alternatives and premature termination

Economics teaches that we should terminate a project if a financially superior alternative investment opportunity becomes available – even though the project may be successful in its own terms (Northcraft and Wolfe, 1984). In this view, jettisoning a typewriter in favor of a word processor is the correct (economically rational) course of action even though the typewriter may be brand new.

Not all decisions are as clear cut, however. Although evidence suggests that the availability of alternatives can limit escalation (McCain, 1986), there is no guarantee that an alternative project will turn out to be more valuable and/ or easier to complete than an existing one. Conceivably, perceived alternatives can produce premature termination if, for example an existing project is faltering. That is, decision-makers confronted by negative feedback may be tempted to evaluate an alternative option more favorably than they would if feedback were positive in order to justify switching. The dynamic might also operate in reverse. That is, the emergence of a seemingly alluring alternative may prompt decision-makers to denigrate an existing project in order to justify switching.

## Diffusion of blame and premature termination

It was observed that the risk of premature termination is increased where a setback can be blamed upon a third party. Blame refers to a social account used to assign cause to a negative event, for example, if a project manager claims that a project has gone over budget and fallen behind schedule because external suppliers have failed to deliver. Interestingly Leatherwood and Conlon found that respondents who were personally responsible for resource allocation decisions but who could blame setbacks on a third party tended to opt for termination regardless of economic criteria. It is unclear why this should be so, but, contrary to escalation theory (see especially Bowen, 1987; Funder, 1987) it suggests that negative feedback does not invariably prompt re-investment. It is a speculative point but the scapegoats may not always be human – how often do we hear of blame-attributed information systems that are allegedly ‘rubbish’?

## Negative framing and premature termination

Framing refers to mathematically equivalent situations expressed positively or negatively. For instance, a bottle that is half full is identical to one that is half empty. Although logically framing should not affect decisionmaking, in practice a project that is framed as having a 10 percent chance of failure is likely to receive much more critical scrutiny and questioning than a project that is framed as having a 90 percent chance of success (e.g., Dunegan, 1993). While negative framing should result in better (that is more economically rational) decisions, it is associated with lower levels of confidence (Kuvass and Selart, 2004) and is thus a plausible precursor of premature termination.

Although it is unclear what causes a decision to be negatively framed in the first place, it seems reasonable to suggest that projects that are perceived as failing, that is, late and over-budget are more likely to be negatively framed than projects that appear to be succeeding. Prima facie evidence of failure may enable exit champions to get their definition of reality (bottle half empty) accepted. The risks may be heightened when negative feedback includes vivid images. More specifically, Schwenk (1986) has observed that when it comes to evaluating projects decision-makers tend to pay more attention to vivid images than statistical and factual data that may flatly contradict sponsors’ extravagant claims. Conceivably, such so-called vividness effects could operate in reverse. That is, if decision-makers are confronted with a dire image or event associated with a project, they may terminate it without pausing to conduct an objective appraisal of the situation. When a patient allegedly died waiting for an ambulance, the London Ambulance Service immediately abandoned their new computerized system for dispatching ambulances (Report of the Inquiry into the London Ambulance Service,

1993). Yet, no firm link between the patient’s death and the admittedly faltering system was ever proven.

## Implications for practice

The forgoing pointers may turn out to be quite a good fit, as the transition from escalation to de-escalation in failing projects is marked by reduced tolerance for failure, more publicly stated limits, more awareness of problems, more outcome-orientated evaluations and more separation of responsibility for approving and evaluating projects. Before discussing the implications of this paper for practice, however, it is important to address the limitations of the escalation paradigm as a conceptual lens for viewing decision-making.

A reviewer of an earlier draft of this paper asked how we can know a project has failed given that many are not properly evaluated in the first place. In the escalation, literature failure is defined as occurring when it becomes obvious that expectations cannot be met. The definition is problematic because in order to procure support for a project, in the first place sponsors may need to exaggerate the benefits and downplay the costs. Consequently, when projects are reviewed they may not have met all the extravagant claims that were made for them and yet still be worth pursuing on grounds of utility.

There is another dimension to the issue of failure. The escalation literature is rooted in the functionalist tradition of knowledge that privileges economic rationality and thus views departures from normative economic prescriptions as inherently problematic (for a discussion see Morgan, 1990). Baskerville and Land (2004) argue that the IT literature has been more concerned with technically efficient systems than with considering their impact upon the organization, yet an IT system can be technically and economically successful, and yet fail because it threatens to undermine the social fabric of organization. The author’s fascinating account of a newly installed military command system shows how economic rationality (relieving senior personnel of the burden of attending situational report meetings) conflicted with existing organizational arrangements based upon trust with the result that ‘the system’ fell into disuse and was eventually quietly discarded. Unlike Talisman this was potentially ‘a great system’ but no one wanted it. The case does not fit the escalation paradigm because the system met all the technical expectations and yet it was a failure nonetheless. The point is to see something one way is not to see it another. What we see depends upon the conceptual lens deployed. Escalation theory throws the spotlight on certain things while obscuring other important phenomena.

Mindful of those caveats how can this paper facilitate better decision-making? Bowen (1987) has argued that it is unfair to accuse decision-makers of violating the norms of rationality in unstructured situations when the correct (economically rational) course of action is not always plainly evident – BSE, for example. Prospect theory offers some albeit limited guidance for making adoption decisions where future preferences are unclear. The theory predicts that when problems are framed as a choice between losses decision-makers tend to become risk seeking preferring to avoid an immediate and definite loss but at the risk of subsequently incurring a much greater loss (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). The decision-makers clearly realized that introducing computerized cattle tracking implied a definite loss at time A. They seem to have been less concerned about the loss that might accrue by time B. Opportunity is the converse of risk and the window of opportunity in complex organizations is typically fleeting. Decision-makers not only need to consider the risks of adopting a project they should also consider the possible consequence of avoiding the risk.

Decision-makers should also be aware that existing prescriptions for mitigating escalation are potentially double edged (Ross and Staw, 1991; Simonson and Staw, 1992; see also McNamara et al., 2002). Limit setting is one of Simonson and Staw‘s most important recommendations. ‘Zero based’ budgeting – a technique where at every point in the funding cycle each project is evaluated as a new project may curb escalation but if budgeting theory holds true, there is a heightened risk off erring in the opposite direction – particularly if limits are made public and/or existing projects are evaluated alongside seemingly alluring alternatives.

Simonson and Staw also recommend changing the project manager to reduce escalation. This recommendation assumes that turnover reduces responsibility and that a newcomer is more likely to engage in decision vigilance. While this strategy may be effective for non-performing bank loans, (see Staw et al., 1997) and other short cycle projects, managerial turnover can de-stabilize ‘long haul’ projects such that the cure may be worse than the disease (McNamara et al., 2002). Faltering projects can sometimes be salvaged by dividing them up into smaller entities. An ‘exit champion’ who publicly commits themselves to termination, however, may hurl the baby out with the bath water.

Decision-makers might also be well advised to treat reality and rationality not as a concrete entity but as a ‘useful fiction’ from which to base decisions (Morgan, 1990). More specifically, whereas the mainstream functionalist tradition of business education assumes environments are ‘out there’ waiting to be discovered, an interpretivist perspective suggest that we create them to a large extent by paying selective attention to information and then act as if our creations are forcing us to which ultimately they are (Weick, 1979)! This implies that we have some control over our environments and therefore we can change them. Indeed, decision-makers can guard against the various framing effects mentioned in this paper by reformulating problems. They can guard against the manipulation of vividness effects. They can treat alternatives with caution and ignore non-informative losses. Getting project champions and exit champions to subtract their egos from the equation and to ignore the pressures of an audience is likely to prove more difficult. Moreover, even the most absurd decision can be made to appear rational. An interpretivist perspective would suggest that instead of seeing rationality as a guide, decision-makers should view it as an achievement, a social construction, forged from multiple nonrational forces and political factions competing to get their views accepted (Brown, 1978; Abolafia and Kilduff, 1988). In this view, the rationale for adoption and progress decisions can be seen as myth, clothed in the trappings of rationality. Myths reflect the dominant viewpoint about what should be done – partly true and therefore partly untrue (Hedberg and Jonsonn, 1977). It is the element that is untrue that should give decision-makers pause.

## Concluding comments

Economists might argue that the notion of premature termination is superfluous as market forces invariably have the last word. In this view, economic competition and demands for ‘best value’ require organizations to make optimal use of IT or perish. The point is that market forces are not always immediately evident. This observation applies with particular force to complex organizations where human fallibility, politics, creative accounting and a myriad of other influences can combine to create a complex and poorly understood reality. By the time a crisis exposes a mistake it may be too late as business has already migrated, diseases like BSE are out of control and the terrorists have struck.

The history of ‘long haul’ IT projects has not been a uniformly happy one. Organizations are right to question whether those projects are worth the risk. However, they should also consider whether there might not be more to be lost by not taking the risk. Images of mega projects spiraling out of control make good headlines in the media. Yet the losses imparted by escalation may be slight compared with the opportunity costs of premature termination. It is sometimes said that what we never have, we never miss. Try telling that to the victims of those decisions.

## About the author

Helga Drummond is Professor of Decision Sciences at the University of Liverpool Management School. Her research interests include escalation (and de-escalation) of commitment, developing metaphors to conceptualize action in organizations and power and politics. Her research sites have ranged from high-profile organizational debacles including project TAURUS and the collapse of Barings’ bank to a field study of a dilapidated hairdressers’ shop.
