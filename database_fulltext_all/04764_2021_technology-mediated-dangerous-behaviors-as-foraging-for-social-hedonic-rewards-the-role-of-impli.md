# TECHNOLOGY-MEDIATED DANGEROUS BEHAVIORS AS FORAGING FOR SOCIAL-HEDONIC REWARDS: THE ROLE OF IMPLIED INEQUALITY <sup>1</sup>

Ofir Turel School of Computing and Information System, Faculty of Engineering and Information Technology, University of Melbourne, Parkville, VIC, AUSTRALIA {oturel@unimelb.edu.au}

Technology-mediated dangerous behaviors (TMDBs), such as posting selfies while driving or posting private information, are prevalent and potentially require interventions. Knowledge about the drivers of these, and specifically the role of information in stimulating such behaviors, is limited. To address this gap, this paper turns to foraging and risk-sensitivity theories. These theories suggest that animals engage in more dangerous behaviors when their perceived need for calories is high. Similarly, humans increase financial risk-taking when they perceive dissatisfaction with what they have. Importantly, inequality information can increase such perceptions and change people’s risk-taking propensity. Adapting these ideas, the paper postulates that TMDBs resemble food-seeking in animals in that they are goal oriented, can be dangerous, and yield unknown (probabilistic) rewards. Therefore, TMDBs are explained using foraging and risk-sensitivity theory angles. Focusing on social media users (Studies 1-4; four experiments; total n = 2,504), I argue that (1) it is reasonable to view users as foraging the “fields of social media” for social-hedonic rewards, (2) it is possible to alter their risk appetite and TMDBs through inequality information and upward comparison mechanisms, (3) this process can be mediated not only through cognitions, but also emotions, and (4) perceived scarcity of rewards and social comparison orientation affect this process. With Study 5, the paper extends the core aspects of this theoretical perspective to the U.S. state level and argues that objective financial inequality can explain differences between states in terms of TMDBs such as texting while driving and relative interest in TMDBs such as prank videos. The findings largely support these assertions. They illuminate the role of information, notably inequality, in driving TMDBs, extend prior research focused on basic needs (e.g., physiological needs in the case of food intake decisions) to an evaluation of higher-order human needs (e.g., needs for belongingness, esteem, and self-actualization) catered to by nonphysiological, social-hedonic rewards, and point to important mechanisms that translate inequality into TMDBs.

Keywords: Foraging theory, risk-sensitivity theory, social media, risky behaviors, decision-making, technology-mediated dangerous behavior

## Introduction

Dangerous behaviors are actions with probabilistic variation in adverse, typically psycho-physiological, outcomes (i.e., undesirable harms, see Stephens and Krebs 1986). Information technologies (IT) such as social media sites and mobile phones, afford, and some may say unintentionally drive, various dangerous behaviors, dubbed technology-mediated dangerous behaviors (TMDBs). TMDBs are IT-mediated acts with possible adverse outcomes. The adverse outcomes might never emerge but, in some cases, they can be immediate; in other cases, they can accrue over time. Examples include taking selfies in risky situations, such as on the edge of a cliff, posting inappropriate self-images (e.g., nude, drunk) on social media, texting while driving, posting private information, and using social media during class time. All of these examples are TMDBs in that they may lead to potential harms. For instance, in 2017, there were 404 deadly crashes involving mobile phone use while driving in the US, resulting in 434 fatalities (National Center for Statistics and Analysis 2019). In addition, social media use during class time has been shown to adversely affect students’ grade point average (Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2016), taking risky selfies can lead to injuries and death (Dokur et al. 2018), and “sexting” may be a precursor to suicide (Ahern and Mechling 2013). Given such potential harms, it is vital to study the predictors and mitigators of TMDBs.

There have been several attempts, albeit somewhat isolated, to theorize on specific TMDBs. Such studies have assumed that TMDBs are goal-oriented actions aimed at maximizing rewards (i.e., sought-after end goals of the action, Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2016). That is, people engage in TMDBs as a means to achieve something desirable, such as the immediate gratification of “likes” or social status gains (Hayashi et al. 2016). Given the goal-orientation of TMDBs, they have often been treated as rational acts. For example, the theories of reasoned action and planned behavior (Ajzen 1985) have been used to explain sexting (Walrave et al. 2014), posting drinking/drunk pictures (Steers et al. 2019), and using social media while driving (Gauld et al. 2017). Another lens of analysis assumes that such behaviors are the outcome of a struggle between difficult-to-control drives to perform rewarding TMDBs and the ability to override these drives. Such studies primarily use dual-system theory (Hofmann et al. 2009) to, for example, explain social media use in class (Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2016), swearing on social media (Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2018) and using social media while driving (Du et al. 2019).

While valuable and informative, such perspectives overlook the role of the information the technology provides to its users in driving TMDBs. Such information, especially concerning variability in possible rewards, has been shown to be a critical input for risky decision-making in both animals (Caraco et al. 1980) and humans (Mishra et al. 2015). Therefore, without discounting the importance and validity of the abovementioned theoretical perspectives, I suggest that an information-focused angle may be useful for understanding how the explicit or implicit information that technologies provide about variability in possible rewards might drive TMDBs. Examining this association is the broad objective of this study. Addressing this objective can not only extend theory on TMDBs that has thus far focused on user perceptions and self-regulation abilities, but also provide notable practical insights.

To address this objective, I first posit that users who enact TMDBs follow typical motivation theory processes (McClelland 1987) in that they expect rewards (e.g., “likes,” reduced anxiety) that cater to their higher-order human functional needs, such as needs for socialization, recognition, and self-enhancement (Katz 1960; Maslow 1943). This is consistent with the goal-orientation assumption of prior TMDB studies (Bazargan-Hejazi et al. 2016) and assumes at least some rationality—conscious or subconscious—when engaging in TMDBs (Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2016). I next note that dangerous behaviors in both animals (McNamara and Houston 1992) and humans (Payne et al. 2017) often include another risk, beyond the danger itself, namely the risk of not obtaining desired rewards. This risk reflects the probabilistic potential of behaviors to produce the needed quantity of rewards (Stephens and Krebs 1986); Behaviors may produce it but may also fail to do it or even produce losses (Weber et al. 2004). For example, a social media user may or may not accumulate the desired number of “likes” (the end-goal) for a selfie, akin to an animal that may or may not collect sufficient calories in its foraging effort.

Prior research in animals (Caraco 1982) and humans (Payne et al. 2017) has shown that information about this risk (variability in rewards) increases appetite to seek rewards (e.g., food in animals, and money in humans) and to engage in dangerous behaviors. Such variability in rewards is implied by inequality information. Inequality represents a state in which some people (or animals) have much more of a resource or reward and some much less of it. Inequality is thus high when there is a considerable variation in the distribution of rewards (Mishra et al. 2015). In this paper, I focus on inequality information and borrow from animal foraging theories (Stephens and Krebs 1986) and, more narrowly, from risk sensitivity theory (RST, Caraco 1981; Caraco et al. 1990) to explain TMDBs. I seek to examine (1) whether TMDBs can be explained from a foraging theory perspective, according to which IT users engage in TMDBs to “forage” for social-hedonic rewards (i.e., nonphysiological sought-after gains in social attainment, reduced anxiety and/or internal joy, e.g., increased social attention, sense of belongingness, alleviation of stress, and immediate gratification); (2) whether inequality in the division of such rewards can alter cognitions (e.g., perceived need, perceived scarcity) and emotions (e.g., envy), and through these changes, alter people’s TMDBs; and (3) how relevant information attributes (e.g., available comparison data) and personal attributes (e.g., social comparison orientation) can influence these processes.

I address these research questions in five studies that leverage foraging theory and RST for developing hypotheses regarding TMDBs. My theory posits that humans have social-hedonic reward intake needs, akin to the caloric energy needs of animals, which they can satisfy by foraging in the “fields of social media” (or other leisure technologies) and enacting TMDBs that will help them address their needs.

It further posits that when exposed to inequality in the distribution of rewards, users sense a need to obtain more rewards to feel satisfied (Study 1), develop stronger envy in others (Study 2), perceive stronger reward scarcity (Study 4), and ultimately present higher endorsement of TMDBs (Studies 1 and 2) and enactment of TMDBs (Studies 3, 4, and 5). In addition, given the centrality of social comparison processes in this theory (Brosnan and de Waal 2003), I examine how the comparison reference point (downward vs. upward comparison) influences this process (Study 3), and how social comparison orientation (an individual difference) regulates this process (Study 4).

Each of the four experiments (Studies 1-4) focuses on different “online foraging” behaviors, TMDBs, and translation mechanisms, as a means to corroborate and expand findings from individual studies and increase diversity and confidence in the findings. These studies focus on social media site users because of the high prevalence rates of social media use and TMDBs on such sites (Brevers and Turel 2019). I supplement the experiments with the analysis of archival objective U.S. state-level data on financial inequality (Gini index), the number of tickets issued per capita for texting while driving, and relative interest in prank videos compared to programming language videos (Study 5). Overall, the findings (1) explain TMDBs from an innovative perspective, (2) extend foraging theory and RST from animal food intake and human economic decision-making domains to TMDBs, (3) extend the range of rewards that foraging behaviors target from calories and money to social-hedonic rewards that cater to higher-order human needs (e.g., “likes”), (4) extend the mediating mechanisms from cognitions (perceived need) to emotions (envy), (5) show that reference points for social comparison and social comparison orientation afford and regulate the association between inequality and TMDBs, (6) demonstrate theory validity at the individual and national levels, and (7) reveal that it is feasible to alter IT users’ TMDBs through inequality information.

## Theoretical Background

Foraging theory is a collection of models that explain forager behaviors (e.g., scanning a patch for food). It assumes that animals have expectations about foraging spots (e.g., probability of finding many pollens in a specific field) and that their foraging behavior is informed by circumstances, such as energy budget deficit (Mangel and Clark 1986). The theory suggests that animals are goal-oriented strategic decisionmakers that try to optimize their net energy gains (caloric intake minus the energy expended for obtaining the food) from foraging activities, and while considering the dangers involved in the activity (Kacelnik and Houston 1984; Pyke 1980; Stephens and Charnov 1982; Ydenberg et al. 1994). It specifically posits that (1) animals focus on a particular problem (typically either which animals/plants to target and consume or how long to stay in a patch), (2) there is a common currency by which they decide between options (e.g., typically net energy gain), and (3) there are constraints on the animal’s ability to generate the desired gain (e.g., internal constraints, such as limited vision, or external constraints, such as predators, see Stephens and Krebs 1986).

Risk sensitivity theory (RST) is a notable extension of foraging theory because it accounts for the risk in foraging, defined as variation in the nondeterministic outcome of food-seeking.<sup>2</sup> This extension is needed because the net energy gain in foraging activities is stochastic, and some patches offer a higher riskreward ratio compared to others (Caraco et al. 1980; McNamara and Houston 1992). Based on findings from multiple species (e.g., shrews: Barnard and Brown 1985, dark-eyed juncos: Caraco 1981, white-crowned sparrows: Caraco 1982, and minnows: Cerri and Fraser 1983), RST suggests that animals with negative energy budgets (i.e., those that are not given enough food for meeting their daily needed calories) shift their reward-utility curve from concave-down (risk averseness) to concave-up (risk proneness), such that instead of seeing declining marginal returns for each additional reward unit, they start seeing increased marginal returns for each additional reward unit and consequently engage in more dangerous and riskier behaviors (Stephens and Krebs 1986). That is, animals risk proneness is not fixed; it is instead a function of their perceived need for calories (Mishra and Fiddick 2012). When animals experience increased need, it is more likely than not that animals will choose high-risk high-reward patches and be willing to accept more danger in their foraging behavior (Barnard and Brown 1985; Caraco et al. 1980).

Food intake is essential for the survival of any species. However, at least for humans, food satisfies just one basic need in Maslow’s hierarchy of needs (Maslow 1943). Humans also seek to satisfy various complex higher-order needs, beyond physiological ones, such as needs for belongingness, esteem, and self-actualization (Maslow 1943). Importantly, foraging theory and RST can also be applied to humans’ higher-order needs. Specifically, humans can be conceived as foraging for financial rewards (Payne et al. 2017). Their risk appetite changes as a function of their perceived financial needs, such that people are willing to take higher risks when they perceive that they need more of the target reward (Mishra and Fiddick 2012; Mishra and Lalumière 2010). Such extensions of foraging theory and RST to humans have previously focused on physiological safety and esteem needs (based on Maslow’s [1943] hierarchy of needs), all of which are served by obtaining more financial resources. I extend this perspective here by arguing and showing that people can also “forage” for nonphysiological social-hedonic rewards that cater to a broader set of common functional needs (e.g., belongingness, esteem, and self-actualization needs).

An important extension of RST suggests that inequality is a powerful tool for altering the perceived need for target rewards. Inequality does this by creating a much higher, often unattainable outcome, which serves as a target for upward comparison (Brown et al. 2007). Upward comparison is a process through which people compare their status to a desirable reference point and subconsciously set goals to get closer to it (Gibbons and Buunk 1999; Wang et al. 2017). Even though inequality information also creates another group of people, much below the median, that can serve as a target for downward comparison, there is an asymmetry in such comparisons: people tend to focus on and emphasize upward comparisons (Boyce et al. 2010; Ferrer-i-Carbonell 2005). As such, upward social comparisons carry more weight in economic decisions and inequality information acts as a de facto representation of strong disadvantage (Payne et al. 2017). These upward comparisons create a sense of unfair inferiority compared to others, which propels people to try harder to catch up with the upper echelons (Mishra et al. 2015) through, for example, engaging in risky behaviors (Payne et al. 2017). Indeed, when information about relative disadvantage is not disclosed, people with inferior rewards do not engage in risky behaviors, compared to cases where they receive the same reward but their relative disadvantage is revealed (Mishra et al. 2015). Inequality is also an important driver of risky behaviors in animals. For example, bumblebees that have experienced other bumblebees stealing their nectar (hence creating inequality), often choose to explore riskier patches (Cartar 1991).

Such findings allude to possible similarities between animals and humans in that the risk appetite of both can be a function of their perceived needs, which can be informed by inequality. Moreover, such findings allude to the possibility of the applicability of RST to a broad range of rewards and needs; it applies not only to net calorie gains and basic physiological needs, as in the case of animals, but also to net financial rewards in humans. As such, it is plausible to consider applications of RST to higher-order needs (e.g., need for achievement) and to examine whether humans forage for technology-mediated rewards. Here, I extend the theory to the case of social-hedonic rewards, because they serve the critical basic needs of humans beyond physiological needs such as food (Katz 1960; Maslow 1943). Overall, RST provides a unique explanation for dangerous behaviors in animals and humans, because “foraging models have predicted effects and phenomena whose occurrence was not predicted by other theories” (Stephens and Krebs 1986, p. ix). ## Hypotheses

Foraging theory and RST describe many mechanisms through which inequality information can change risk appetite and dangerous behaviors (Stephens and Krebs 1986). As such, I rely on prior research on financial decision-making (Mishra and Fiddick 2012; Mishra et al. 2015; Mishra and Lalumière 2010; Payne et al. 2017), mediating mechanisms of risktaking in animals and humans (Miller et al. 2011; Packer et al. 1990; Real and Caraco 1986), and factors that can affect social comparison processes (Gibbons and Buunk 1999; Wang et al. 2017) to theorize on and elucidate mechanisms that can influence TMDBs.

Prior research has shown that economic inequality in terms of financial rewards increases people’s propensity toward risk-taking (Payne et al. 2017). This follows examples from the animal domain, where animals engaged in riskier and more dangerous foraging when inequality was observable (Cartar 1991). It is explained by the RST extension of foraging theory, according to which the risk propensity of living creatures increases as they sense they are being deprived from desired goods (e.g., food in the case of animals, and money in the case of humans). Inequality is a powerful mechanism that triggers this sense of deprivation in humans and motivates them to take risks to try to catch up with the upper echelons of the distribution of rewards (Payne et al. 2017). Assuming that IT users change their risk preferences in a similar way to animals seeking food and humans in financial decision situations, I expect that inequality signals can increase online risk-taking in IT users, through the risk proneness-alteration mechanism of RST. Simply put, IT users exposed to inequality will try harder, compared to others, to catch up with people who have more of a desired reward (i.e., upper echelons, as implied by the inequality information), even when the behavior they are about to engage in is dangerous. Given the goal orientation of such acts, I expect that inequality will affect both the endorsement and enactment of TMDBs because the endorsement of goal-oriented actions often translates, albeit imperfectly, into action (Ajzen 1985). It has been further shown that this process is partially mediated through the sense of inferiority that inequality signals instill in people, and specifically through an increased subjective sense of deprivation and an elevated sense of the need for rewards to be happy (Payne et al. 2017). This happens because such perceptions create internal motivation to seek rewards (Mishra and Fiddick 2012; Mishra and Lalumière 2010) and are exacerbated when people have a reference point for upward comparisons (Wang et al. 2017), such as that provided by high-inequality information (Payne et al. 2017). Extensions to humans have shown that humans become more risk-prone in their search for financial rewards when their perceived need for minimum gains to be satisfied increases (Payne et al. 2017). That is, the perceived need for the target reward, in both animals and humans, drives risk-taking behaviors. Humans, though, have needs beyond food and financial needs, and these needs can motivate their actions (Maslow 1943). I posit that it is reasonable to expect that natural desires to cater to higher-order needs (e.g., the need for socialization) can also motivate risk-taking behavior.<sup>3</sup> This assertion assumes that people are goal oriented and therefore try to cater to their higher-order needs through technologymediated foraging for rewards. This is in line with motivation theories (Maslow 1943), in that gaps between perceived needs and what a person actually has, motivate action (Katz 1960).

Such need perceptions in both animals and humans depend on their perceived deprivation from target resources. In animals, hunger drives a stronger sense of the need for food (Lawrence and Illius 1989). In humans, financial needs are a function of upward social comparisons to others and consequently a function of subjective relative deprivation (i.e., the feeling of resentment when others have more, see Payne et al. 2017, p. 2). Such relative deprivation manifests in dissatisfaction with what one has (e.g., dissatisfaction with financial rewards compared to others, see Payne et al. 2017). Thus, subjective relative deprivation is a key driver of perceived need. This is in line with perception development theories (Grossberg 2012), according to which beliefs (here, about relative deprivation) inform higher-order summative perceptions (here, about perceived need). Importantly, people’s dissatisfaction with their own rewards can be informed by inequality because inequality information provides often unattainable targets for upward comparisons and inflates people’s sense of inferiority compared to others (Payne et al. 2017).

Taken together, the above assertions imply that the inequality effects on TMDBs are expected to be at least partially mediated through a subjective sense of deprivation and the consequent elevated sense of a need for rewards to be happy (Payne et al. 2017). ## H1c: The positive effect of inequality on endorsing TMDBs (H1a) is partially mediated via subjective relative-deprivation from the reward and perceived need for the reward.

While prior research has focused on perceptions that translate inequality into risky behaviors (Payne et al. 2017), emotions, and especially envy can also drive risky and dangerous behaviors (Cappelen et al. 2013; Kwon et al. 2017; Lahno and Serra-Garcia 2015). Envy captures the feeling that “arises when a person lacks another's superior quality, achievement, or possession and either desires it or wishes that the other lacked it” (Parrott and Smith 1993, p. 906). As such, inequality can serve as a key source of envy (Alvarez‐Cuadrado and Long 2012; Podder 1996; Smith et al. 2016), in part through social comparisons with the upper echelons of the comparison group (Suls and Wills 1991). Such upward social comparisons are prevalent in technology-mediated settings (Appel et al. 2016) because technology affords easy comparisons to others and the pool of people that one can compare oneself to is immense (Turel 2019). This idea extends to social media as well. People may envy others in their social network because they have, for example, more “likes” or “friends” (Appel et al. 2016; Blease 2015; Taylor and Strutton 2016). Given that inequality creates a much higher reference point and upward comparisons are weighted more heavily than downward comparisons (Boyce et al. 2010; Ferrer-i-Carbonell 2005), inequality is expected to produce envy.

Envy, in turn, increases behavioral tendencies aimed at reducing this unpleasant emotion through improving one’s status compared to others or harming others (Cohen‐Charash 2009). As such, envy often drives increased risk-taking (Cappelen et al. 2013; Kwon et al. 2017; Lahno and Serra-Garcia 2015), which may manifest on social media through behaviors such as reactive self-enhancement (Krasnova et al. 2015). This occurs because envy informs the development of goal-directed behaviors (Bagozzi et al. 2000), and people may opt to target high-risk high-reward options to alleviate unpleasant states of feeling envy (Hvide 2002; Kwon et al. 2017; So et al. 2015). Integrating these ideas, I hypothesize:

## H2: The positive effect of inequality on endorsing TMDBs (H1a) is partially mediated via envy.

H1 and H2 assume that people can infer targets for upward comparisons from inequality information and that they more often engage in upward than downward comparisons. Prior research supports such assertions, showing that people prefer and utilize upward comparisons more than downward comparisons in financial decisions (Boyce et al. 2010; Ferreri-Carbonell 2005). In addition, prior research suggests that if people were to engage in downward comparisons, inequality would not produce changes in their risk appetite (Payne et al. 2017). Indeed, only upward comparisons create a sense of unfair inferiority, which drives risky behaviors (Haisley et al. 2008; Mishra et al. 2015). Such comparisons represent in people’s (and monkeys’, see Brosnan and de Waal 2003) minds a strong disadvantage. I, therefore, hypothesize:

H3a-b: (a) IT users prefer to view information that may serve as a target for upward social comparison, compared to information that may serve as a target for downward social comparison, and (b) only when engaging in upward social comparisons, will IT users increase their enactment of TMDBs.

Another common concept in the foraging theory and social comparison literatures is perceived scarcity (also known as a perceived rarity), which reflects perceptions of a limit on the supply or number of suppliers of a desired resource (Brock 1968). Inequality information can signal scarcity through social comparisons (Buunk and Mussweiler 2001). The lower bound of inequality information can signal that it can be very difficult to obtain the desired rewards, and at the same time, the upper bound can elevate the desired level of rewards (Lynn 1992). Thus, I hypothesize:

H4a: Inequality increases reward scarcity perceptions.

Importantly, scarcity perceptions can drive risky behaviors in animals (Beketov and Liess 2007; Goodell 2003; Merkle et al. 2013) and humans (Miller et al. 2011). For example, when food is scarce, animals take risks and forage in human habitats (Merkle et al. 2013). In humans, product scarcity can drive spontaneous purchases that disregard real value (Cialdini and Cialdini 1993). When people perceive that they do not have enough of what they seek, they develop a “scarcity mindset” that changes their goal-directed decision-making and drives riskier decisions (Huijsmans et al. 2019). Based on commodity theory (Brock 1968), scarcity can create a sense of urgency and stronger action proclivities in humans because people place a higher value on scarce products (Lynn 1991). When facing scarcity, people act less deliberately, even when it is dangerous, because scarcity information reduces their ability and motivation to process relevant information, including danger (Cialdini and Cialdini 1993). However, through arousal scarcity can also increase motivation and the effort people are willing to invest to process relevant information (Inman et al. 1997). This effect is consistent with reactance theory (Brehm 1966): scarcity restricts people’s freedom, and such restrictions can increase the motivation to elaborate and attend to relevant signals (Clee and Wicklund 1980).

In investigating how the tension between the two contradicting scarcity effects can be resolved, previous research has shown that when facing scarcity, integrating relevant information into decisions depends on people’s motivation to elaborate (Suri et al. 2007). Borrowing from the literature on upward comparisons (Wang et al. 2017), I suggest that social comparison orientation is an individual difference that can alter people’s motivation to elaborate and take relevant information such as danger into account. Social comparison orientation captures people’s tendency to be attentive to and behave based on others’ actions (Gibbons and Buunk 1999). It has been shown that social media use drives upward comparisons only when people are high in social comparison orientation (Wang et al. 2017). In a similar vein, I posit that people who care about social comparisons (i.e., those high in social comparison orientation) are more likely to elaborate on the value of online social-hedonic rewards (e.g., “likes”) and on how rewards can help them improve their social status, regardless of scarcity. As such, their decision will be less influenced by scarcity and be instead motivated by their need for “likes.” In contrast, when people are low in social comparison orientation, they are more likely to be influenced by scarcity information and likely to put less emphasis on the social mobility value of TMDBs. In that sense, social comparison orientation serves to regulate the integration of scarcity into decisions. Hence, I hypothesize:

H4b: The positive effect of perceived scarcity on TMDBs is moderated by social comparison orientation, such that it is pronounced in low levels of social comparison orientation.

Integrating H4a and H4b produces the following hypothesis:

H4c: The positive effect of inequality on TMDBs is partially mediated via elevated reward scarcity perceptions, the effect of which is negatively moderated by social comparison orientation.

H1-4 collectively represent an individual (micro)level theory of TMDBs. Supporting theories at multiple levels of analysis (micro and macro) is desirable because it can add robustness to theories and increase their validity, especially since the world is a complex multilevel system (Klein et al. 1999). Hence, I seek to extend the key idea of the individual-level theory that inequality can drive TMDBs, from the individual to the national level of theorizing and analysis. To do so, I note that many individual-level phenomena can be aggregated into group-, organizational-, or national-level phenomena (Kozlowski and Klein 2000). This happens through bottomup emergent processes that describe how individual-level properties can be aggregated to form collective phenomena (Klein and Kozlowski 2000). Composition is such a process; it assumes that higher-order phenomena are simple aggregations of lower-level phenomena, and that higher-level constructs should therefore play a similar role in models but at a higher level of theorizing and analysis (Kozlowski and Klein 2000). Using the logic of composition processes, it is reasonable to assume that inequality and TMDBs can both manifest at the national level. Taking this perspective, national-level inequality is an emergent concept that aggregates the inequalities that residents feel, and nationallevel TMDBs are the composition of TMDBs enacted by individuals. As such, if the composition assumption is true, one can expect the idea that inequality drives TMDBs to extend from the individual level to the national level of theorizing and analysis (Kozlowski and Klein 2000).

In partial support of this perspective, it has been shown that individual-level inequality effects extend to the national level (Payne et al. 2017). For example, national-level financial inequality is associated with higher rates of unsafe sex (Holtgrave and Crosby 2003), and web searches for risky loans (Payne et al. 2017). As such, using the same foraging and risk-sensitivity theory ideas, I expect that financial inequality at the U.S. state level, as a manifestation of the inequalities that residents of the state feel, increases the rate of and interest in TMDBs in the state. The logic is that the collective sense of financial inequality can elevate the motivation of residents of a state to engage in TMDBs that can help them alleviate the painful sense of inferiority imposed by the inequality they sense. This is consistent with the “status anxiety hypothesis,” (Layte and Whelan 2014) according to which, in high inequality conditions, people compare themselves upwards and develop anxiety about their lowerthan-desired status (Sommet et al. 2018). From this perspective, TMDBs can help people alleviate their status anxiety. This alleviation is rewarding because in highinequality states there are a number of extremely wealthy people representing a small proportion of the population; thus, most people (including moderately wealthy people) would still have unattainable targets for upward comparison. Thus, in high-inequality places, people of almost all levels of socioeconomic status would be inclined to engage in TMDBs as a means of alleviating status anxiety. As such, if H3b is true and can manifest at the state-level of analysis, I expect that:

H5: State-level financial inequality is positively associated with state-level interest in and enactment of TMDBs that help people alleviate status anxiety.

The proposed hypotheses imply the following research model (Figure 1), which is examined in five studies. Research design and design choices are described in the next section.

## Research Design

A multistudy approach was employed for two reasons. First, it was infeasible to test all hypotheses in one study. Second, I aimed to achieve four objectives: corroboration, expansion, compensation, and diversity (Venkatesh et al. 2016). First, all studies corroborate the direct effect of inequality on endorsing or enacting TMDBs. Second, Studies 1-4 use different mechanisms (moderators and mediators) to explain this effect. Each expands the direct effect insights. Third, the different approaches employed, including experiments and national data analysis, compensate for the limitations of each approach and ultimately lead to stronger confidence in the findings. Lastly, the focus on different foraging targets and TMDBs in the different studies creates diversity that increases the generalizability and validity of the findings.

In order to create this diversity, a focus group of ten Facebook users who were graduate students was asked to read the study by Payne et al. (2017) and to suggest social media (1) foraging targets, (2) TMDBs that resemble the ones used in Payne et al. (2017), and (3) reasonable inequality values. Consensus was reached regarding “likes,” birthday wishes, and, generally, immediate gratification and pain (e.g., boredom, inferiority) relief as foraging targets. Posting images in risky situations (i.e., situations that pose physical [e.g., while driving] or psychological-developmental [e.g., while in class] risk to the person), posting risky images (i.e., images that may be abused such that they could psychologically hurt the person if shared or used in professional situations—e.g., posting racy/sexy pictures of oneself and rebellious pictures involving drinking beer), posting personal information (e.g., birthdate), posting controversial (high-risk high-reward) images (i.e., images that not everyone may like that hence reflect a low chance of producing a high number of “likes,” and a high chance of producing a small number of “likes,” posting prank videos (these can promote dangerous behaviors), and mentions of alcohol/substances or posting such images (a specific instance of posting risky images) were identified as typical TMDBs. Adaptations of these foraging targets and TMDBs were used in the different studies in no particular order. It was suggested that using variations of the inequality numbers used in Payne et al. (2017) times 100 could reasonably represent the distribution of “likes” and birthday wishes. These numbers were used with random noise (e.g., \$1 in the financial inequality study was translated into 100 “likes”± noise in this study). The efficacy of these distributions to produce desired results was pilot tested (See Appendices). Table 2 summarizes information about the employed studies and the hypotheses addressed by each one.

## Studies 1-5

## Study 1: Foraging for “Likes” with a Profile Picture

Study 1’s objectives were to examine the effect of inequality on the endorsement of TMDBs (H1a), and to test whether this process is partially mediated through increased dissatisfaction with what one has on social media and the consequent sense of elevated perceived need (H1c). To this end, it used inequality in terms of “likes” on Facebook to instigate TMDBs that reflect foraging for “likes.” It focused on three TMDBs in the context of a new profile picture: the likelihood of posting a new profile picture in risky situations, the likelihood of posting risky profile pictures, and preference for posting low-risk, low-reward profile pictures.

## Procedures and Measures: Study 1

Procedures were consistent with Payne et al. (2017). They required the completion of an online survey, through which I provided and manipulated fictitious information regarding the distribution of “likes” when people post a new profile picture. In a between-subjects design, half of the participants were randomly assigned to the high-inequality condition and the rest to the low-inequality condition. The presented distributions varied in inequality in the number of “likes,” but had the same mean (See Figure 2).

Participants completed the first part of the survey, which collected demographics, control variables, and descriptive information. Next, they were presented with the manipulation. After reviewing it, they were asked questions that (1) checked the understanding of the presented information and manipulation validity, and (2) captured, in a random order, the endorsement of three TMDBs. Procedures and instruments were pilot tested with 100 Facebook users; the tests, procedures, and instruments are described in Appendix A.

## Results: Study 1

All analyses were performed in SPSS 26. All scales were reliable, with αs > 0.765. Items were, therefore, averaged to produce variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to confirm that the low and high inequality groups did not differ in key attributes. There was only a small random a priori difference in attitude toward cool and rebellious selfies (See the “Pre-intervention” section in Table 3). Next, ANOVA was used to examine whether the manipulation worked. Results (see the “Manipulation checks” section in Table 3) indicated that the high inequality group perceived the bottom third of the distribution to have significantly fewer “likes” than the top third, and the distribution to be less equal, compared to the low inequality group. Lastly, ANOVA results (See the “Postintervention” section in Table 3) indicated that the high inequality manipulation drove significantly higher dissatisfaction with participants’ own number of “likes,” needed number of “likes” to be happy, and the endorsement of the three TMDBs. Figure 3 depicts differences between manipulation groups. After completion, it was confirmed that participants were still unable to guess the purpose of the survey (i.e., they were blinded to it).

Next, the hypothesized mediation mechanism was tested with the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Hayes 2017), using 2000 resamples (Model 6, with two mediators). I estimated three models controlling for age, gender, and attitude toward rebellious selfies (an a priori difference)—one for each TMDB. See results in Table 4.

The models predicting the likelihood of posting selfies in risky situations and posting risky selfies support H1a by revealing a direct effect of inequality on the endorsement of at least two TMDBs, even after controlling for the mediation mechanism. The direct effect was significant for preference for low-risk low-reward selfies but became nonsignificant after accounting for the mediating mechanism. H1c is supported by all three models, as indicated by the confidence intervals that exclude zero. In all instances, there was an indirect effect of inequality on endorsement of TMDBs, mediated through dissatisfaction with their own number of “likes” and the consequent elevated perceived need for “likes” to be happy. Overall, the findings largely support H1a (in two out of three instances), and fully support H1c (in all three instances). They show that (1) perceived inequality in “likes” on a social media site increases endorsement of TMDBs that reflect foraging for “likes,” and (2) this process is either partially or fully (in the case of preference for low-risk low-reward selfies) mediated through dissatisfaction with one’s own number of “likes” and consequent elevated perceived need for “likes.”

## Study 2: Foraging for Birthday Attention

Study 2 aimed at extending Study 1’s findings to a different rewarding social media feature, namely birthday wishes, and examined the possibility that inequality translates into TMDBs not only through cognitions, as in Study 1, but also through emotions (H2). To this end, it used inequality in terms of the number of birthday wishes people receive on Facebook to instigate envy and TMDBs that reflect “chasing” birthday attention.

## Participants: Study 2

The same recruitment procedures as in Study 1 were employed here, focusing on 13 different sections of the same course. Out of the 518 invited participants, 464 completed the study (89.6% response rate; age range 19-48, $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { A g e } } = 2 3 . 3 0 , \mathbf { S } \mathbf { D } _ { \mathrm { A g e } } = 4 . 1 3 ; } \end{array}$ 52.8% women). Reported number of Facebook friends varied from 1-10 to over 1,000 with a modal range of 201-500 friends (33.4% of respondents). Participants reported Facebook use of between less than one hour/day to at least six hours/day, with a modal value of about two hours/day (33.4% of respondents).

## Procedures and Measures: Study 2

The study employed the same between-subjects design and procedures as in Study 1, with inequality manipulations using birthday wishes information (See Figure 4). It largely used the same scales as in Study 1, adapted to focus on birthday wishes. Instead of focusing on post-intervention dissatisfaction and the need for “likes,” it captured post-manipulation feelings of envy. See items, sources, and reliabilities in Appendix B.

## Results: Study 2

All analyses were performed in SPSS 26. All scales were valid and reliable, with αs > 0.723. Analyses of variance were used to check that the low- and high-inequality groups did not differ in key attributes. There were no a priori differences in such attributes (See “Pre-intervention” section, Table 5). Next, ANOVA was used to examine manipulation understanding. Results (See “Manipulation checks” section, Table 5) indicated that the high-inequality group identified the bottom third of the distribution as having significantly fewer happy birthday wishes than the top third and identified the distribution as less equal, compared to the low-inequality group. Lastly, ANOVA was used to examine the manipulation effects on envy and endorsement of the target TMDBs. Results (See “Postintervention” section, Table 5) indicated that high inequality drove significantly higher envy and TMDB endorsements, compared to low-inequality information. Between-group differences are depicted in Figure 5. After completion, participants were unable to guess the purpose of the survey. Next, the hypothesized mediation was tested with the PROCESS macro (Model 4, one mediator). I estimated three models, controlling for age and gender, one for each TMDB. Results are given in Table 6. The models support a direct effect of inequality on the endorsement of all three TMDBs (H1a). They also support the indirect-effect hypothesis, via envy (H2) for the likelihood of posting selfies in risky situations and the likelihood of posting risky selfies, but not for the preference for low-risk low-reward selfies. As such, the findings provide further support, beyond Study 1, for H1a and partially support H2 (for two out of three TMDBs). They reaffirm the foragingtheory ideas that were validated in Study 1 and extend them to a different behavior, as well as an emotion-based mediation mechanism.

## Study 3: Foraging for “Likes” with Birthdate Posts

Study 3 aimed at (1) moving from endorsement of behaviors to actual behaviors, (2) elucidating the comparison mechanisms that users follow and their effects, and (3) extending the findings of Studies 1 and 2 to three different TMDBs (posting one’s birthdate, posting a picture of beer, and posting text referencing alcohol). Based on the focus group, posting private information like one’s birthdate can be perceived as dangerous. Similarly, posts in professional settings that include licit substances such as alcohol can be conceived as dangerous because they may damage one’s professional image.

## Participants: Study 3

Out of a new pool of 1,151 invited participants (Facebook users who were university students), 972 completed the study (84.4% response rate; age range 18-55, $\mathrm { M _ { A g e } = 2 3 . 9 2 , S D _ { A g e } = 5 . 0 0 ; }$ 49.0% women). They had, on average, 437.5 friends (SD = 388.1) on Facebook and used it for an average of 1.91 hours/day (SD = 1.29).

The first part presented them with a fictitious distribution of “likes” in prior years for students who opted to participate in a fun voluntary activity of posting their birthdate (considered private, and hence its sharing represents some danger), with a picture and a comment as a means to obtain “likes” from other participants. This study also included a standard paradigm for capturing upward or downward social comparisons (Payne et al. 2017; Wheeler 1966). People were presented with the middle part of the distribution of “likes” obtained last semester and were asked to choose which part of the distribution (here, top or bottom third of the distribution) they wanted to see. This choice represented a social-comparison reference point for the between-subjects condition. Some decided to view the bottom part of the distribution, which afforded downward comparison, and others chose to view the top part of the distribution, which afforded upward comparison. I also randomly assigned people, like in Studies 1 and 2, to view one of two types (low or high inequality) of fictitious distributions of “likes.” Thus, this study employed a 2 (inequality condition: low vs. high) x 2 (reference point condition: downward vs. upward comparison) between-subjects design (see presented information in Appendix C).

After making the comparison point choice and viewing the manipulated information, in the second part, participants were taken to a social-media-like page and reported their birthdate (day and month only).<sup>5</sup> They were given an opportunity to post it (by clicking a “post” button) or to decline to post (by returning to the survey). It was made clear to participants that they would receive extra credit points regardless of their choice to post or not. Those who made the birthdate post were asked to select one of two pictures to go with their post: a cupcake with candles vs. a beer with candles and post it, and to free-type a comment and post it. After posts were made, the system notified participants that viewing others’ posts and the “like” function would be available soon. Participants were debriefed at the end of the study and told that this functionality was fictitious. This part enabled capturing three actual TMDBs: posting the birthdate [0 = no, 1 = yes], picture choice [0 = cupcake, 1 = beer], and mentions of alcohol in the freetyped text (coded manually by two raters, which were fully consistent [0 = none, 1 = yes]).

The third part captured demographic, descriptive, and control variables, including age, gender, contacts on Facebook, hours of use/day, perceived importance of birthday, perceived risk of birthdate posting, a multidimensional posting motives scale, passive Facebook use, and impulse control. It was administered after the behavioral part to prevent biases. See Appendix C for items, sources, and reliabilities.

## Analysis and Results: Study 3

Analyses were performed in SPSS 26. All multi-item scales were reliable with αs > 0.727, and were averaged for analysis. ANOVA was used to confirm that neither of the randomly assigned low- and high-inequality groups (each with n = 486) differ in key attributes. There were no a priori differences (See “Preexisting attributes” section Table 7). Chi-square tests (See “Post-intervention” section, Table 7) indicated that when upward and downward comparison conditions are collapsed, there are no significant differences in TMDBs between the low- and high-inequality conditions. This suggests that the inequality effects observed in Studies 1 and 2 might result from specific rather than from all comparison processes. Supporting H3a, participants chose significantly more upward comparisons (57.6%) than downward comparisons (42.4%) (95% CI for the difference = [10.77%; 19.54%], p = 0.000), and the proportion of upward comparisons was significantly (p = 0.001) above chance (50%).

Next, I used chi-square tests to examine differences in the behaviors across the four quadrants created by the two conditions (two levels of inequality, and two types of comparison reference). Birthdate posting was the highest (p = 0.000) in the high inequality-upward comparison condition (143/260 = 55% compared to 255/712 = 35.8% in all other conditions). Posting the beer picture was only marginally significantly (p = 0.085) higher in the high inequality-upward comparison condition (72/143 = 50.3%), compared to all other conditions (104/255 = 40.8%). Similarly, mentioning alcohol in the post (example comment: “let’s get drunk”) only marginally significantly (p = 0.099) differed between the high inequalityupward comparison condition (9/143 = 6.3%) and all other conditions (5/255 = 2%). All differences are in the expected detection and are presented in Figure 6. They support the idea that inequality drives TMDBs in form of posting private information primarily when people utilize upward rather than downward comparison processes (H3b). They provide marginal support to the same idea in the case of posting beer pictures or mentioning alcohol.

Next, in order to further examine H3b, I employed logistic regression and controlled for potential covariates. Results (Table 8)<sup>6</sup> show a significant positive interaction term for birthdate posting, indicating that comparison interacts with inequality condition to increase TMDBs, even after accounting for a wide range of controls. The coefficient (β = 0.61) implies that people exposed to inequality and information that affords upward comparisons are 84% (e<sup>0.61</sup> = 1.84) more likely than others to chase after “likes” through posting their birthdate.

## Study 4: Foraging for “Likes” with Prank Video Posts

Study 4 aimed at (1) elucidating a scarcity/social comparison orientation mechanism capable of translating inequality information into action (H4a-c), (2) extending the finding of prior studies to a different TMDB (posting prank videos), and (3) moving from expectations (as in Studies 1 and 2) to actual behaviors.

## Participants: Study 4

Out of a new pool of 870 invited participants (Facebook users who were university students<sup>7</sup>), 718 completed the study (82.5% response rate; age range 18-50, $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { A g e } } = 2 3 . 8 7$ $\mathrm { S D _ { A g e } } = 4 . 1 9$ ; 48.9% women). They had, on average, 380.8

Facebook friends (SD = 311.0) and used the platform for an average of 1.94 hours/day (SD = 1.20).

## Procedures and Measures: Study 4

The same recruitment procedures, as in the previous studies, were employed here. Participants completed three parts of an online survey. The first part presented a fictitious distribution of “likes” when people posted prank videos in the previous semester (See Appendix D). It created two conditions with n = 359 in each (between-subjects design, low vs. high inequality). The second part included a social-media-like page on which participants were asked to report a link to a prank they like. They were next given an opportunity to post the prank (by clicking a “post” button) or to decline to participate (by returning to the survey). It was made clear to participants that they would receive extra credit points regardless of their choice to post. The third part captured the focal constructs of perceived scarcity and social comparison orientation, as well as descriptive and control variables. See items, sources, and reliabilities in Appendix D.

## Analysis and Results: Study 4

All analyses were performed in SPSS 26. All multi-item scales were reliable with αs > 0.838 and were averaged for analysis. ANOVA was used to confirm that the randomly assigned lowand high-inequality groups did not differ. This was supported by the results given in Table 9, which also suggest that the manipulations created the expected differences in perceived scarcity and the proportion of prank posting, both of which were significantly higher in the high-inequality condition.

Next, the moderated-mediation model (with four covariates: age, gender, Facebook contacts, and Facebook use), as implied by H4a-c, was tested with the PROCESS macro (Model 14) (Hayes 2017), using 2000 resamples. Results are given in Table 10. They support H4a by showing that inequality increased perceived scarcity $( \beta = 0 . 3 6 6 , p = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . They provide further support for H1b by showing that inequality increased posting pranks. Because women were coded as “1,” the findings imply that they were less likely compared to men to post pranks. Importantly, the negative and significant interaction term supports H4b. The conditional indirect effects of inequality on posting pranks, as mediated by perceived scarcity, were significant at low (-1SD) and medium (mean) levels of social comparison orientation, but not at high levels (+1SD) of socialcomparison orientation. This supports H4c.

## Study 5: Foraging for Rewards by Texting while Driving

Study 5 aimed at (1) testing the role of inequality in driving TMDBs in real-life settings, (2) extending the theory from the individual level to the U.S. state level, and (3) extending the findings of the prior four studies from inequality in online rewards to objective financial inequality.

To do so and test H5, state-level financial inequality was operationalized using the Gini index, which is the most widely used measure of societal inequality; values of 0 indicate full equality, and values of 1 indicate full inequality (one person has all the income and the rest none) (Gastwirth 1972). A higher Gini index implies that there are more people who have large perceived income deficits versus what is found in a place with a lower Gini index. The 2016 Gini index values for each U.S. state (including the District of Columbia [DC]) were extracted from the U.S. Census Bureau site (M = 0.47, SD = 0.02)<sup>8</sup>. To operationalize TMDBs at the state level, I focused on one available measure, namely the 2016 number of issued tickets/citations for texting and driving per 100,000 licensed drivers for each state (M = 1398.5; SD = 3280.8).<sup>9</sup> To the extent that we can assume that enforcement efforts were reasonably similar among U.S. states, the number of citations per capita for violating driving laws by texting while driving captures a state-level form of TMDB that is an aggregation of individuals’ texting-while-driving. It reflects a TMDB that can help people alleviate the fear of missing out or balance social and work pressures; it can also provide immediate gratification, as well as work and social rewards. Three states had no ban on texting while driving during the study period and no data were available for an additional five states. Hence, the operational dataset included 42 states plus D.C. (n = 43). The correlation between the two variables was significant (r = 0.453, p = 0.002). Removing one outlier that had a moderate Gini index but a high rate of tickets (Delaware) strengthened the correlation (r = 0.630, p = 0.000). Figure 7 plots the association using log-log scaling (select state names are provided). Financial inequality explained about 23% of the variance in tickets per 100,000 licensed drivers. These results were robust and held after accounting for possible confounds as controls. The 2016 Gini coefficient predicted 2016 tickets per capita $( \beta = 6 4 , 7 4 5 . 4 , p = 0 . 0 2 9 )$ after accounting for the state-level percentage of smartphone ownership, the percentage of people with a bachelor’s degree, median age, and interest in speeding tickets<sup>10</sup> (all nonsignificant). This model explained 33.4% of the variance in tickets per capita for texting while driving.

Overall, the findings support H5 and indicate that state-level financial inequality is positively associated with a manifestation of state-level texting while driving (tickets per 100,000 licensed drivers), even after accounting for controls that reflect police enforcement efforts, age, education, and technology use facets of states.

In order to increase the robustness of the findings and extend them to other TMDBs, I tested the association between the 2018 Gini index of states and the relative proportion of YouTube searches for “pranks” vs. “Python” or “C++” (programing languages) in 2018 using Google Trends. This tool enables comparison of the relative prevalence of searches in each state and hence makes it possible to compare interest in a TMDB (prank) and legitimate educational videos, such as those on Python or C++. The average proportion of searches for pranks on YouTube was 85% vs 15% for Python (SD = 0.050). This shows that pranks were much more popular than Python videos across the U.S. The average proportion of searches for pranks on YouTube was 44% vs 56% for C++ (SD = 0.065). This shows that pranks were almost as equally popular as C++ videos across the U.S. Importantly, the proportion of YouTube searches for pranks relative to searches for Python (r = 0.400, p = 0.004) or C++ (r = 0.549, p = 0.000) was significantly positively associated with the Gini index. This shows that as financial inequality in states grow, the relative interest in pranks vs. programming languages increases. These findings provide another layer of support for H5 and the proposed state-level theory. They show that the ideas developed regarding texting while driving can apply to other TMDBs.

## Discussion

This paper extends foraging and risk sensitivity theories to the case of TMDBs, examines the ability of information manipulations that are rooted in these theories to alter users’ engagement in common TMDBs, and theorizes and tests key mechanisms that translate presented information into TMDBs. These objectives were motivated from a practical standpoint by the growing prevalence of TMDBs and the consequent need to better understand their drivers as a first step for developing interventions. The theoretical motivation included the need to understand the largely overlooked role of information in driving TMDBs. Focusing on this role also highlights the need to extend foraging and risk sensitivity theories from mostly animal domains and basic physiological needs to the domain of online human behaviors and to higher-order needs. This extension can allow innovative theorizing in other information systems (IS) research domains by accounting for possible effects of the information we generate, present, and share regarding users’ goal-oriented decisions aimed at serving their higherorder functional needs.

In a series of five studies that focus on diverse TMDBs, socialhedonic rewards, IT artifacts, and higher-order functional needs of users, this paper demonstrates that foraging and risk sensitivity theories can explain TMDBs. It also points to key mechanisms that facilitate this link. The findings are summarized in Table 11. Taken together, the findings demonstrate that (1) it is reasonable to characterize users of leisure technologies as foraging for social-hedonic rewards, (2) information about inequality influences users’ endorsement of and engagement in TMDBs, (3) it does so via various emotion and cognition processing mechanisms that can also be affected by individual differences, and (4) the effects of inequality on TMDBs can manifest at the nationallevel of analysis. Ultimately, because the risk proneness of leisure technology users is not fixed and can be affected by inequality information, the findings imply that it is possible to leverage the ideas presented here for creating desirable changes in user behaviors.

## Research Implications

First, TMDBs have been largely viewed as goal-oriented acts. They have thus far been explained from rational plannedbehavior (e.g., Steers et al. 2019) or dual-system (e.g., Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2016) perspectives. These perspectives have largely overlooked the ability of explicitly provided or inferred information about what one has, compared to what others have, to bias user decisions, even though such information-based social-comparison mechanisms have been frequently employed by leisure technologies for instigating use (Chozick 2020). At the same time, the role of social comparisons has been studied in the context of well-being but not as a potential mechanism that can drive TMDBs (Appel et al. 2016; de Vries et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2017). By borrowing ideas from the animal domain (Caraco et al. 1980; Mangel and Clark 1986) as applied to humans’ economic decision-making (Mishra et al. 2015; Mishra and Lalumière 2010), I extend such social comparison ideas to the case of TMDBs. I specifically borrow from foraging theory and RST to show in five studies that explicit or implied inequality in social-hedonic or financial rewards is an important overlooked input for social comparison processes that can extend the understanding of TMDBs.

The results highlight the importance of inequality for theory development. Inequality is an important topic in reference disciplines (Payne et al. 2017) but is rarely discussed in IS research, despite the fact that IS users are exposed to various inequalities, such as inequality in the pay and working conditions of men and women IT professionals (Sumner and Niederman 2004) and inequality in terms of “likes” and “followers” (Khamis et al. 2017), and despite the fact that inequality may be an important biasing input factor for risky decisions (Mishra et al. 2015; Mishra and Lalumière 2010). The series of studies in this paper demonstrates that inequality can drive TMDBs, directly and indirectly, through perceptions and emotions. I hence call for future studies to incorporate inequality into research models, better account for the role of information in instigating online behaviors, and further explore mechanisms that underlie the effects of information about others and inequality on user behaviors.

Second, the findings of Studies 1-5 consistently show that it is reasonable to view users of many leisure technologies as foraging for social-hedonic rewards. That is, I extend foraging (Stephens and Krebs 1986) and risk-sensitivity (Caraco et al. 1990; Caraco et al. 1980) theories to apply not only to animals seeking food or humans making financial decisions (Payne et al. 2017) but also to technology users who have higher-order needs and try to address them by seeking the needed intake of nonphysiological rewards, in part through TMDBs. These are important extensions because they set the basis for IS researchers to leverage the large body of work on animal foraging behaviors (Stephens and Krebs 1986) for modeling IS use behaviors that involve a broad range of needs, rewards, and risks. While I explain select TMDBs here through foraging theory, similar ideas may be applied in future research, to other TMDBs in nonleisure settings (e.g., IS security policy violations as foraging for work-related rewards).

Third, the findings of Studies 1-5 also consistently demonstrate the validity of the extension of the risk-shifting idea, borrowed from the animal domain (Cartar 1991) to IS users. In essence, these studies show that the risk proneness of IS users is not fixed and that it can be attenuated by their perceived need for rewards, the perceived scarcity of rewards, social comparison orientation, and envy. They also show that some of these factors, such as perceived need, scarcity, and envy, are influenced by inequality information. That is, when users perceive that others have more of the ITmediated rewards than they do, they increase their proneness to engage in TMDBs. This extension is important because it can enrich the myriad of IS theories that involve risk assessments, such as theories related to the escalation of commitment in IS projects (Liang et al. 2021), IS security behaviors (Farivar et al. 2018), coping with system risks (Tu et al. 2015), and online consumer behavior (Turel et al. 2010). The use of RST can allow such models to account for the role of information in driving risky and dangerous technologymediated behaviors.

Fourth, the results of Studies 1-4 extend the understanding of the mechanisms of risk shifting processes in humans, including in IS users. Prior research has shown that inequality can create risk shifting in financial decisions, in part, through changes in dissatisfaction with what one has and the perceived need for rewards to be happy (Payne et al. 2017). I not only extend these results to a different context and to nonphysiological rewards (e.g., “likes,” birthday wishes) but also show that emotions, specifically envy, can mediate the effect of inequality on TMDBs (Study 2). These results extend the role of envy in IS research, from a threat to life satisfaction (Krasnova et al. 2015) to a mechanism that shifts people’s risk disposition. This paves the way for future embedment of envy, and possibly other emotions, in models of TMDBs.

The results of Studies 3 and 4 shed further light on translation mechanisms. Study 3 shows that upward comparisons are the key facilitator of inequality information effects; these effects do not accrue when upward comparisons are not afforded. This extends the ideas and findings from Payne et al. (2017) to TMDBs and paves the way for a deeper integration of the research streams on online social comparisons (Appel et al. 2016; de Vries et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2017), foraging, and risk sensitivity theories (Stephens and Krebs 1986). Study 4 points to a moderated-mediation mechanism that includes perceived scarcity and social comparison orientation. It shows that inequality increases scarcity perception, and through this, can influence TMDBs. Nevertheless, scarcity is inputted into a user’s mental calculus only when social comparison orientation is low. When it is high, socialhedonic considerations trump other information. This perspective integrates mediating mechanisms of risk-taking in animals and humans (Miller et al. 2011; Packer et al. 1990; Real and Caraco 1986) with research on individual differences in social comparison processes (Gibbons and Buunk 1999; Wang et al. 2017). This is an important extension of foraging theory and RST because it can explain differences in responding to inequality information. It also highlights the need for IS research to integrate scarcity into models of user decision- making. While scarcity has been shown to influence consumer behaviors (Suri et al. 2007), its effects on IS user behaviors, beyond what was demonstrated in this study, represent an important gap that should be addressed in future research.

Lastly, the findings of Study 5 not only demonstrate critical societal implications of my theory, but also point to three important theoretical implications. First, they show that the inequality effects, as prescribed by foraging theory and RST, can be aggregated to society, presumably using bottom-up emergent processes. That is, when high societal inequality can be inferred, individuals will engage in more TMDBs and will collectively produce higher levels of TMDBs in society. Second, they show that the inequality that instigates TMDBs does not have to pertain to the target rewards. Specifically, financial inequality is not necessarily addressed directly by texting while driving or seeking prank videos. Instead, such actions presumably allow people to deal with unpleasant anxiety, the sense of inferiority, and fears of missing out, and through this, achieve immediate gratification. This provides indirect support for the “status anxiety hypothesis,” (Layte and Whelan 2014) according to which people may engage in TMDBs as a means of feeling better and alleviating their sense of inferiority, which may stem from implied financial (or other) inequality (Sommet et al. 2018). Third, as opposed to the experiments in Studies 1-4, in which I provided explicit inequality information to users, inequality in Study 5 was inferred. While people can check the inequality scores of their states, I assume that most people subconsciously infer inequality from daily life within the states they live in (e.g., seeing homeless people near luxury houses while driving to work). This implicit inequality information sufficed for producing a significant increase in TMDBs. Together, these insights suggest a potentially broader role for the concept of “inequality” in IS theories and research. Inequality can form along many dimensions, whether implied or explicit, and can manifest in TMDBs at various levels of analysis.

## Practical Implications

The findings show that it is possible to alter the TMDBproneness of users through implied or provided inequality information. First, while doing so may be unethical and even dangerous in certain circumstances (e.g., enhancing proclivity to take risky selfies), it can also be used ethically and for good purposes. For example, hiding inequality information about “likes” or promoting a greater sense of equality (e.g., by presenting fictitious information about what peers have) may promote safer online behaviors and reduce the prevalence of common TMDBs. While maybe not intentional, this is supported by new moves by Facebook and Instagram that started hiding the number of “likes” for other people such that people can see only the “likes” for their posts. This, of course, reduces variability in “likes” and prevents upward comparisons. Similarly, if organizations want to prevent risky, attention-grabbing posts on organizational social media, they could reduce the motivation for them by removing response (reads, likes, etc.) counts. Second, the mere knowledge that inequality effects exist could be used to train users to be aware of their biases and to stop and think before they engage in TMDBs. For example, organizations that are interested in reducing their legal exposure could train employees to avoid the pitfall of chasing after “likes” and attention and hide counts for such activities on organizational social media.

## Limitations and Future Research

Even though the experiments were carefully designed and pilot tested, accounted for many controls, and focused on diverse TMDBs, foraging targets and designs, Studies 1-4 suffer from typical external-validity limitations of experiments. While I overcome external validity issues with Study 5, it should be acknowledged that Studies 1-4 focused on one group of participants and provided fictitious inequality information in an artificial and predigested format (a graph). Future research should seek to overcome such limitations by replicating these studies in additional natural settings and by expanding the range of controls. In addition, I assumed that TMDBs are goal oriented and relied on the conscious and/or subconscious mental calculus of information. Because separating the conscious and subconscious parts, or the rational and irrational parts of the behavior is beyond the scope of this study, I call on future research to examine such issues. Moreover, my theory focused on the role of information in driving TMDBs, and on the mechanisms prescribed by foraging theory that translate inequality into TMDBs. Future studies could integrate this perspective with existing models of human behavior, such as the theory of reasoned action and dual-system theory. Lastly, the findings of Study 5 do not afford a deeper examination of the association between levels of income and texting and driving or other TMDBs. Because 99% of state populations tend to be far below the top 1% in terms of income, an interesting question to examine in future research would be whether earners of lower vs. higher incomes differ in the sense of inferiority they feel, compared to the top 1%, and whether TMDBs are weighted more heavily toward lower-income people.

## Conclusion

This paper extends foraging and risk-shifting ideas from animals to technology users, and from the intake of caloric and financial rewards to the intake of social-hedonic nonphysiological rewards. It demonstrates the generalizability of foraging and risk-sensitivity theories across different online behaviors. Given the ever-growing landscape of TMDBs, further research in this domain is critical. Given the theorydriven insights regarding the ability to manipulate TMDBs, I call for research and practice that considers and applies these ideas to intervene and reduce TMDBs among technology users.

## Measures: Study 1

The pre-manipulation part of the survey included descriptive variables regarding participant demographics (age, gender), and Facebook use in terms of hours per day, number of friend/contacts, and Facebook use extent (four items adapted from Venkatesh et al. (2008)). It also ensured familiarity with Facebook using direct questions; no exclusions were made based on this criterion. Next, it captured attitudes toward three TMDBs, as identified by a focus group: posting racy/sexy selfies, posting selfies of risky situations (e.g., on the edge of the roof), and posting rebellious-risky selfies (e.g., drinking beer); each employed three items adapted from typical attitude scales (Bagozzi et al. 2004; Barki and Hartwick 1994). Following Payne et al. (2017) and borrowing items from this study, it also directly asked about the minimum number of “likes” for being happy with such posts (need for “likes”), realistically expected number of likes if one had made such posts on their Facebook page (expected “likes”), and approximate number of “likes” one received last time they posted one of these selfies (actual “likes”). These measures were used to determine that there were limited or no a priori differences between the high- and low-inequality groups.

All post-manipulation scales but one were borrowed from Payne et al. (2017) and adapted to the domain of Facebook. I first performed manipulation checks with two questions, asking participants to reflect on the provided distribution and interpret it. I also captured the need for “likes,” and the dissatisfaction one has with his/her number of “likes,” as direct outcomes of the manipulations. Next, I captured the three target TMDBs: Likelihood of posting selfies in risky situations was captured with four items from Turel and Qahri-Saremi (2016), likelihood of posting risky selfies with three items, each reflecting a different risky selfie, adapted from Payne et al. (2017), and preference for low-risk, low-reward selfies, adapted from Payne et al. (2017). The order of post-intervention scales was randomized to minimize order effects; they were all presented next to the relevant manipulation information (see main text). The survey used two trap questions to ensure attention. Three records that failed the trap questions were excluded. At the end of the survey, I included an open-ended question to test whether participants can guess what this study is about (blindness check). Example responses include “I do not know,” “I am not sure,” “what people share on social media,” “peopl likelihood of liking posts,” and “using social media as an interactive platform.”

These procedures and instruments were pilot tested with 100 Facebook users (58% women, $\mathrm { M _ { a g e } } { = } 2 . 3 7 , \mathrm { S D _ { a g e } } { = } 3 . 7 4 )$ , out of which 50 were randomly assigned to each inequality group. Results showed that all scales were reliable with αs > 0.752. They further showed that the manipulations were understandable, with the high inequality group reporting a higher gap $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ between the bottom and top quantiles of Facebook users in terms of “likes” (M = 1.38, reflecting perceiving a lot fewer “likes” in the bottom quantile compared to the top one) compared to the low inequality group (M = 3.06, reflecting perceiving only somewhat or slightly fewer “likes” in the bottom quantile compared to the top one). Similarly, perceived equality was higher $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ in the low inequality group (M = 3.37) compared to the high inequality group (M=2.16). Lastly, the findings showed that the manipulations produced the expected effects. The group that was exposed to high inequality reported a higher need for “likes” to be satisfied with a new profile picture (M = 5.32 vs. M = 4.30, p<0.037), a higher likelihood of posting risky selfies (M=2.87 vs. M=2.14, p<0.004), a higher likelihood of posting selfies in risky situations (M = 2.67 vs. M = 2.07, p < 0.004), and a lower preference for safer (low-risk low-reward) selfie choices (M = 3.57 vs. M = 4.25, p < 0.006). ## Measures

Descriptive measures (age, gender, Facebook contacts, Facebook hours/day) were similar to those used in Studies 1 and 2; the key difference was that Facebook contacts and hours/day were freely reported as numeric values, as opposed to using a Likert-type scale, like in Studies 1 and 2. Perceived birthday importance captures the significance people attribute to their birthday. It was captured with three items based on Payne et al. (2017). The original scale included one question, and I extended it here by using synonyms and similar phrases. The three-item scale was pilot tested with n = 38 students and deemed to be appropriate (α = 0.908).

I also added several control variables based on the premise that these can influence online and social media behaviors. First, I included a measure of the perceived risk in posting one’s birthdate (defined as the potential for loss when posting one’s birthdate), because risk is often used in mental calculus processes (Featherman and Pavlou 2003). At least in the U.S., one’s birthdate is considered sensitive information that can be used for discrimination, prejudice, and identity theft; and its online sharing with an unknown audience is hence expected to invoke some risk concerns. I captured risk with three items with the highest loadings from the overall risk scale in Featherman and Pavlou (2003). Indeed, an average of 2.17 (SD=1.07) on a 1-5 scale indicated that people perceived slight to moderate risk in posting their birthdate (without year).

Second, because people vary in their posting motives (the reasons for posting), and these can mediate the influence of personality on users states and behaviors (Chen and Roberts forthcoming), I captured users’ posting motives with a twelve –item<sup>11</sup> four-dimensional drinking motives scales (Cooper 1994) as adapted in Chen and Roberts (forthcoming) to the context of social media. These motives include conformity (“an external, negative reinforcement motive that drives an individual’s SNS use in a way that avoids social disapproval or rejection”), enhancement (“an internal, positive reinforcement motive that drives an individual’s SNS use to increase positive affect”), social motive (“an external, positive reinforcement motive that drives an individual’s SNS use to increase interpersonal affiliation”), and coping motive (“ an internal, negative reinforcement motive that drives an individual’s SNS use in order to decrease negative affect”) (Chen and Roberts forthcoming).

Third, I captured impulse control because it can affect dangerous behaviors, independently of the focal constructs (Chen and Roberts forthcoming; Turel and Qahri-Saremi 2018). To capture it, I used the short three-item measures from Weinberger’s Adjustment Inventory (Weinberger et al. 1987) as adapted in Chen and Roberts (forthcoming). In addition, I captured the level of passive SNS Use with three items from Wang et al. (2017). I did so because passive use influences user states (Verduyn et al. 2015), and it can be associated with upward social comparison processes (Wang et al. 2017). Lastly, I measured fashion consciousness as an unrelated marker variable (Lindell and Whitney 2001) with two items from Gould and Stern (1989) as adapted in Chen and Roberts (forthcoming). I expected that if no common method bias exists in the data, this variable will have low correlations with the model’s variables. It was indeed not correlated with any of the focal constructs $( p > 0 . 3 8 6 )$ . It was also not correlated with most control variables, except for with gender (r = 0.130, p = 0.000) and contacts (r = 0.144, p = 0.000). These are reasonable correlations that indicated that in this sample, women and people with more Facebook contacts were also more fashion conscious. This correlational pattern alleviated concerns regarding common method variance and increased confidence in the self-reports, as respondents seem to have paid attention to their responses.

Like in the other studies, at the end of the survey, I included an open-ended question to test whether participants can guess the intentions of this study. Example responses include “I’m not sure,” “birthday celebration,” and “I think this survey is about birthday celebration habits.” Items and reliabilities for multi-item scales are given in Table C1.

## Measures

Descriptive measures (age, gender, Facebook contacts, Facebook hours/day) were captured with the same instruments used in Study 3. In this study, I focused on the mediated-moderation mechanism of perceived scarcity and social comparison orientation. Hence, I added scales to capture these concepts beyond the binary independent variable (condition: 0 = low inequality, 1 = high inequality) and the binary dependent variable (0=did not post prank, 1=post pranks). Perceived scarcity was captured with a four-item scale based on the perceived scarcity items in Gupta (2013), Suri et al. (2007), and the use of synonyms. Social comparison orientation was captured with the three items with the highest loadings from Gibbons and Buunk (1999) as used in Wang et al. (2017). These scales were pilot tested with n = 32 students and deemed to be appropriate (α<sub>perceived</sub> <sub>scarcity</sub>=0.817 and α<sub>social</sub> <sub>comparison</sub> <sub>orientation</sub>=0.828). Like in the other studies, at the end of the survey, I included an openended question to test whether participants can guess the intentions of this study. Example responses include “I’m not sure,” “pranks,” and “likes.” Items and reliabilities for multi-item scales are given in Table D1.
