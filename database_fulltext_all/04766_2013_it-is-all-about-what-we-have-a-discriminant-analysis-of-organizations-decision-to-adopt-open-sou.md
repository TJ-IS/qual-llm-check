# It is all about what we have: A discriminant analysis of organizations decision to adopt open source software

## a b s t r a c t

This research reasons that human capital, that is, knowledge, skills, experience, abilities, and capacities possessed by employees, plays a vital role in the adoption of open source software (OSS) by organizations. Based on the survey responses of 104 OSS-adopting organizations and 111 non-adopting organizations in China, a discriminant analysis of organizations' OSS adoption behaviors was conducted. The current <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the argument that OSS-adopting organizations can be clearly distinguished from their non-adopting counterparts in terms of their availability of internal OSS human capital, accessibility to external OSS human capital, organizational size, IT department size, and criticality of IT operation. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Open source software (OSS) has become an essential substitute to proprietary software for organizations when they make software adoption decisions. OSS can bene<sup>fi</sup>t organizations, relative to proprietary software, in many ways, including enhanced autonomy in modifying and customizing software with the source code, lower or even zero software acquisition cost, lower risk of being dependent on a single proprietary software vendor, and potentially higher software reliability supported by a much wider pool of software developers globally [27,33]. The development of OSS products and community has offered the prospect of effective alternative business solutions and new business opportunities for many organizations [21].

In foreseeing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and market potential brought about by OSS [6], many national governments have publicly announced various initiatives to support the growth of OSS [26]. For instance, several major European cities have shown their technological innovativeness by adopting and implementing OSS [8]. Large corporations, such as Hewlett-Packard, IBM, and Sun Microsystems, have also released OSS for free downloads (e.g., Java Platform, Enterprise Edition or Java EE) and offered value-added services based on such software [27].<sup>3</sup> The adoption of OSS in higher education area has also been popular in the

US [40]. The increasing prominence of OSS in the software market has led to the growing belief that OSS will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly challenge the market dominance of proprietary software.

Notwithstanding the market potentials and advantages of OSS, market observers caution that more work is necessary, such as promoting OSS usage in organizations, before OSS can overtake proprietary software as the dominant software choice [23]. To the extent that organizational purchase constitutes the largest share of software demand, and the proposed bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and success of OSS can only be realized after adoption [17,34], understanding the propensity of organizations toward OSS adoption and the discriminating factors between OSSadopting organizations and non-adopting organizations is imperative. This paper argues for the need to understand the unique properties of OSS and then identify the concerns that might create barriers for organizations to adopt OSS [9,30]. Despite the importance of this inquiry, insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient discussions have been initiated, and most current studies focus on the innovation, governance, and competition of OSS projects [18,42]. Therefore, more effort needs to be exerted to enrich the present understanding of OSS adoption, especially by organizations [1].

Based on the literature review, a major hurdle to OSS adoption is the perceived uncertainty in service and support that could lead to an increase in the organization's cost to switch from extant software to OSS and using OSS on a continuous basis [46]. Thus, intellectual capital in the form of organizational OSS human capital might be a critical resource to overcome this barrier and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the organization's decision to adopt OSS as a type of innovative initiative [37]. Drawing on human capital theory [4] and innovation adoption literature [39], this paper conducts a discriminant analysis of organizations' OSS adoption based on a survey in China. The results support the proposition that OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations can be clearly distinguished in terms of availability of internal OSS human capital, accessibility to external OSS human capital, organizational size, IT department size, and IT criticality.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and hypotheses

The perceived uncertainty in OSS service and support, a major adoption barrier, is attributed to the nature of OSS development, which is often based on the informal networks of voluntary software developers. Hence, OSS service and support are not as guaranteed as those of proprietary software [10]. Such uncertainties are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the form of various types of cost (e.g., OSS support costs) [46], which consequently lead many organizations to judge OSS to be inappropriate for adoption. For example, one of the major reasons that 500 managers of Australia's top <sup>fi</sup>rms rejected OSS is the perceived lack of reliable technical support [15].

This situation further lends credence to the use of human capital perspective as the theoretical lens to examine organizations' OSS adoption. Scholars debate whether OSS-adopting organizations should possess the necessary human capital, such as knowledge, skills, abilities, and capacities possessed by people [4], either internally (e.g., its IT staff) or externally (e.g., consultants), given that it can reduce the perceived uncertainty and risk associated with OSS service and support. The availability of internal and external knowledge could further affect the usage of OSS in a predominant way [41].

## 2.1. Human capital: intangible capital on top of intellectual capital pyramid

According to the resource-based view of an organization [28], resources, which are rare, valuable, inimitable, and non-substitutable, are more likely to produce sustainable competitive advantages for the organization. Differences in innovation adoption behaviors across organizations can be attributed to the variance in their resources and capabilities [37]. In this information era, compared with physical and <sup>fi</sup>nancial capital, intellectual capital is more likely to produce a competitive advantage in innovation adoption because it is often rare and socially complex, thereby making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate and transfer [28]. Intellectual capital includes not only the legally enforceable intellectual property rights but also all the tangible and intangible aspects of intellectual assets that an organization develops and accumulated over time [45]. Among all types of intellectual capital, human capital, which refers to the knowledge, skills, experience, abilities, and capacities possessed by people [4,29], is at the apex of the intellectual capital pyramid [7].

An organization's human capital is accumulated through employee education, on-the-job training, and work experience. Although human capital theory was originally developed to examine the economic value of education [4], more recently, its application has been extended to organizational staff selection, training, compensation, human resource management, and innovation adoption practices in general [5,29,38].

At the organizational level, human capital is considered as a valuable and rare resource, which enables the owner organization to adopt innovations that its competitors cannot [43], thereby providing the basis for accruing competitive advantage [2]. An organization is unlikely to adopt an innovation successfully unless much of the required specialized expertise exists within the organization or such knowledge can be acquired easily or economically from the market [14]. For most OSS products where formal technical support may no longer be guaranteed, the internal availability or external accessibility of human capital will be the more important considerations than for products where service and support are provided by commercial enterprises (e.g., Windows and SAP).

As with other capital investments, the management of human capital can also be broken down into “make-or-buy” decisions [24].

On the one hand, organizations may internalize employment and build their human capital stock by initiating training and staffdevelopment [5,19]. On the other hand, organizations may externalize employment by contracting or outsourcing certain functions to service agencies [32]. Therefore, in this study, OSS human capital is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the knowledge, skills, abilities, capacities, and experience with OSS possessed by people either internal or external to an organization. Conceptually, an organization's OSS human capital can be divided into two parts: (1) availability of internal OSS human capital, which refers to availability of the organization's staff members with the relevant skills and experience in OSS, and (2) accessibility to external OSS human capital, which refers to the extent an organization has access to external consultants, programmers on OSS forums, or an IT educational resource to support OSS adoption and use.

## 2.2. Human capital and organization's decision to adopt OSS

Based on the concept of technology-sensing capability, one of the two components of technological opportunism<sup>4</sup> [36], OSS-adopting organizations should enjoy a higher availability of internal OSS human capital because such capability enables them to become more active in technological environmental scanning for OSS development, more sensitive to technological changes in OSS, and more receptive to the adoption of OSS. Leveraging on their internal OSS human capital, these organizations are likely to perceive less uncertainty and risk in the service and support of OSS when they adopt and deploy OSS innovations within their organizations. Based on the concept of technology-responding capability, the other component of technological opportunism [36], OSS-adopting organizations with higher availability of internal OSS human capital are also likely to be more capable to respond to technological trends in OSS development. Organizations that are sensitive to changes in their environment are likely to create enough momentum to change and innovate [12], whereas organizations with a great response capability tend to consider an innovation as an opportunity rather than a threat as they perceive greater control over the outcome [13]. In a survey conducted by the Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) magazine,<sup>5</sup> of the ten essential questions that were raised for CIOs to ask themselves when deciding whether OSS is appropriate for their organizations' IT strategy, the top two are as follows: “Is there adequate in-house expertise to manage open source deployment, modification and maintenance?” and “How significant may limited support be for its implementation and maintenance?” These two questions suggest that the availability of internal OSS human capital is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant discriminant factor between OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations. We therefore propose:

H1. OSS-adopting organizations are likely to have higher availability of internal OSS human capital compared with their non-adopting counterparts.

Access to suppliers of IT-related services is one of the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient ways for an organization to accomplish a host of decision-making, adoption, and implementation tasks associated with new technologies [39]. Organizations with access to top-notch, low-cost suppliers of IT-related training and consulting have more choices and <sup>fl</sup>exibility to carry out innovation–adoption strategies [39]. Most of the “suppliers” in the OSS product context are different from traditional software producers such as Microsoft. On the contrary, they are third-party IT consultants or developer groups on the OSS forum that are externally accessible to organizations.

Although external OSS human capital exists abundantly in the form of developers in OSS forums, consultants, freelance programmers (e.g., university students), and some support from commercialized OSS products, organizations may have different levels of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in accessing them. For example, although nearly all OSS projects have online mailing lists, an organization that is not sensitive to the technology environment may experience dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in locating the most relevant external human capital to assist and support its OSS deployment when needed. An organization's ability to <sup>fi</sup>nd such external resources and its skill to manage them remotely will make a difference in its accessibility to external OSS human capital.

OSS-adopting organizations with higher accessibility to external OSS human capital can conveniently use this expertise to learn more about OSS innovations to reduce their levels of perceived uncertainty and risk associated with OSS adoption and deployment [16]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, external OSS human capital can be a reliable source of information on the risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using OSS products, and an alternative source of expertise for OSS-adopting organizations. Thus, it will also act as a discriminant factor between OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations. Hence, we propose:

H2. OSS-adopting organizations are likely to have higher accessibility to external OSS human capital compared with their non-adopting counterparts.

## 2.3. Other organizational variables and OSS adoption

Although the major focus of this research is on human capital, it recognizes that an organization encompasses not only the “soft-side” (i.e., human capital) but also the “hard-side” (i.e., organizational demographic characteristics) of corporate resources. Hence, in completing the study, three organizational demographic variables are explicitly considered: organizational size, IT department size, and IT criticality.

Prior studies indicate that organizational innovation adoption behavior is positively associated with organizational and IT department sizes [31] because larger organizations or IT departments will have more diverse resources and more complex facilities that presumably foster innovation adoption. In addition, they have greater resources to withstand potential loss from unsuccessful innovation adoption. However, in the context of OSS adoption, this relationship may be reversed since the most important driver for OSS adoption is reduced cost, including both direct savings (no or low acquisition fee) and indirect savings (no upgrade fees, lower hardware requirements) [10]. Compared with large organizations and their IT departments, smaller organizations and their IT departments with less <sup>fi</sup>nancial leverage for innovation adoption will be more concerned with cost-cutting in software acquisition, and they are more likely to adopt OSS [22]. In the extreme case of small start-ups, proprietary software may simply be too expensive to obtain, whereas OSS will be an ideal substitute. Thus, we propose:

H3. OSS-adopting organizations are likely to have smaller organizational size compared with their non-adopting counterparts.

H4. OSS-adopting organizations are likely to have smaller IT department size compared with their non-adopting counterparts.

Organizational innovation adoption behavior is also positively associated with the criticality of IT operations, which represents the degree of technological impact on the business operations of organizations [25]. Higher IT criticality will bring higher requirements on innovation adoption and deserve more investment. However, in the context of OSS adoption, a deep-rooted perception, whether right or wrong, remains in the minds of CIOs or IS managers that OSS is not for mission-critical applications. Experimenting with open source, with its attendant risks in terms of potential infringement, security, and maintenance, might be regarded as anathema because of its unique development style in which service and support are not guaranteed. Hence, IT criticality should be a discriminant factor between OSS-adopting organizations and non-adopting organizations. Thus, we propose the following:

H5. IT criticality is likely to be lower in OSS-adopting organizations compared with their non-adopting counterparts.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Development of measures

Data were collected from an online survey administered to CIOs and IS managers in China. Respondents were <sup>fi</sup>rst asked to indicate whether their organizations had adopted OSS. Evidence has showed that OSS adoption in large companies is advancing from applications to platforms [35], thus, to avoid confounding effects arising from product differences, the current survey focused only on the adoption of platform-based OSS [10].

Where available, questionnaire items were drawn from previous research; otherwise, new items were created (see Table 1). Special care was taken to ensure that items adapted from prior studies were updated with current terminologies or revised to adhere more closely to the general principles of item construction, such as avoiding double-barreled questions. The two human capital constructs used are mainly based on the extant conceptual de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the constructs found in [3,14]. In the current work, IT criticality followed the construct description by Miller and Doyle [25], whereas organizational and IT department sizes were both objective measures with only one item per construct. Respondents reported the industry in which their organizations operated. All latent constructs were measured with multiple items on 7-point Likert scales, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

The content validity of all items was carefully assessed. First, all items were scrutinized by two colleagues with expertise in measurement theory and questionnaire design to identify and rectify potential problems in framing and wording. Second, all items were tested by conducting two sorting sessions, one unlabeled and one labeled, with three judges in each session. Some minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made based on the sorting results. Four IT professionals were invited to comment on the layout of the questions for the online survey and highlight questions that were confusing or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to answer. Subsequent revisions of the instrument were again veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by two IT professionals before the survey was administered.

## 3.2. Sample and data collection procedure

The survey was endorsed by $\mathsf { C S D N } , ^ { 6 }$ one of the largest IT associations in China, which agreed to distribute the survey to their VIP members, who were mainly CIOs and IS/IT managers in various organizations in multiple industrial sectors. The endorsement was necessary because the survey primarily targeted the CIOs or IS/IT managers of each organization who are cognizant of the IT operations in their organizations and should be able to provide valid and accurate information. The online survey was administered to 500 participants, randomly sampled from the VIP members of CSDN. Special care was taken to determine the sample frame. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, organizations represented by the sampled respondents must not have any products related to open source as this study is concerned with OSS adopters and non-adopters but not OSS proponents (e.g., RedHat). ## 4. Data analysis

The multivariate technique used in this research is Discriminant Analysis. The design of this study, with two subgroups creating a dummy dependent variable composed of OSS-adopting and nonadopting organizations, ideally suited this type of analysis. The independent or discriminant variables measured the characteristics on which the two subgroups were expected to differ, including availability of internal human capital, accessibility to external human capital, organizational size, IT department size, and IT criticality. Given that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the organizations were from the computer/electronics industry, the study <sup>fi</sup>rst compared the means of internal HC, external HC, organization size, IT size, and IT criticality between organizations in the computer/electronics industry (49 non-adopting organizations; 49 adopting organizations) and those in other industries (62 nonadopting organizations; 55 adopting organizations). The results revealed non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the non-adopting organizations in the computer/electronic industry and in other industries; likewise, no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were found for the adopting organizations in the computer/electronic industry and other industries. The subsequent analyses were performed independent of the industry of the participating organizations.

The strength of the discriminant function can be depicted by four important statistics. First, the eigenvalue (0.793) is a measure of the total variance in the discriminant variables. Second, the canonical correlation (0.665) is similar to Pearson's correlation r, whereby the value re<sup>fl</sup>ects the closeness of the relationship between the discriminant function and the dependent variable. Third, the squared canonical correlation (0.442) can be interpreted as the proportion of the variance in the discriminant function explained by the two subgroups, and serves as a good indicator of the fairly strong explanatory ability of the discriminant function. Table 5 supplements Table 4 by depicting the strength of the discriminant function in classifying respondents as non-adopting and adopting organizations. 79.5% of the grouped cases are correctly classi-<sup>fi</sup>ed by the discriminant function, further supporting the power of the discriminant function and the discriminant variables used in this study.

## 5. Discussions and implications

This study constitutes one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst discriminant analyses of the characteristics of OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations. The results indicate that human capital constructs, such as availability of internal OSS human capital and accessibility to external OSS human capital, can be clearly distinguished conceptually and empirically in terms of their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on OSS organizational adoption decision. Overall, a strong empirical support is present for the hypotheses on human capital constructs [4] as discriminant variables for OSS organizational adoption decision. Furthermore, OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations can also be clearly distinguished by organizational size, IT department size, and IT criticality.

For the human capital constructs, the results show that OSSadopting organizations have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher availability of internal OSS human capital and accessibility to external OSS human capital compared with non-adopting organizations. IS leaders perceive OSS human capital, whether internally available or externally accessible, as an important prerequisite for organizations to adopt OSS.

For the other three discriminant variables, OSS-adopting organizations have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly smaller organizational size, smaller IT department size, and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower IT criticality compared with non-adopting organizations. These characteristics are contrary to the conventional wisdom that larger organizations or larger IT departments or organizations with higher IT criticality are more prone to innovation adoption. As discussed previously, OSS is unique in the sense that no acquisition fee is required, which may provide a “leapfrogging” opportunity for small organizations or small IT departments with scarce spare resources for the acquisition of proprietary software to move forward rapidly through the adoption of cutting-edge technology such as OSS. This condition may help explain why OSS-adopting organizations have smaller organizational size and IT department size compared with non-adopting organizations. However, the uncertainty in service and support resulting from the unique OSS development process may explain why OSS is considered inappropriate by IS leaders for mission-critical applications within their organizations. Thus, OSS-adopting organizations have lower IT criticality compared with their non-adopting counterparts.

## 5.1. Limitations

Before discussing the implications of this study, the study's limitations must be laid out <sup>fi</sup>rst. The primary limitation relates to the issue that only one respondent is surveyed for each organization, and that person might not be the best representative of his/her organization's actual opinions. However, to the extent that the questionnaire was administered to the organizations' IS leaders, who were likely to be the most cognizant of their environment, we believe that the single respondent problem should not be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fl</sup>aw.

Both independent and dependent measures were gathered through self-reports at a single point of time, which gives rise to the possibility of common method bias in this study. This effect was minimized in two ways. First was through implementing the online web-based survey questionnaire in such a way as to prevent respondents from back tracking to change their answers. Second was through presenting the pages of the survey items in a random manner to discourage respondents from <sup>fi</sup>guring out the relationship between the independent and dependent variables. A telephone-based follow-up survey on 25 randomly selected OSS-adopting organizations and 25 non-adopting organizations was also conducted. A comparison of responses collected from the main and follow-up surveys revealed a high degree of consistency between the two. Hence, this potential limitation may not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the results.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to literature on both innovation adoption and human capital theory. In terms of the former, one of the common phenomena in innovation adoption studies is that researchers tend to develop general and all-round models that include all possible factors to explain a speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation adoption problem without considering the unique properties of the innovation. For example, in the OSS context, a case study conducted by Dedrick and West [10] empirically examined the organizational adoption of platform-based OSS using the general organizational innovation adoption framework, namely, technology organization environment (TOE), which categorizes all possible adoption factors into these three dimensions. Although the TOE framework has been widely used by IS researchers, its underlying assumption that a universalistic theory of innovation adoption can be developed to predict the adoptions of all types of innovation has been criticized [11]. The search for a universal innovation adoption theory may be inappropriate given that fundamental differences exist across innovations, and dissimilar innovations create different barriers for organizations in their adoptions. To address the shortcomings of the existing researches, this study has approached the issue of organizational adoption of OSS from a unique theoretical angle based on the distinctive characteristics of OSS and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c barrier these create for adopting organizations. With this speci<sup>fi</sup>c research approach, this work contributes to innovation adoption theory by contending that studying an innovation adoption phenomenon requires the following: (1) to understand the innovation's unique properties, (2) to identify the concerns that the organizations have on its adoption, especially the prohibiting factors or inhibitors, and (3) to focus on <sup>fi</sup>nding solutions to the prohibiting factors for innovation adoption. This perspective is essential in the current innovation adoption <sup>fi</sup>eld and will encourage researchers to pursue more innovation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c research.

From the perspective of human capital theory, this study extends its applicability to technological innovation adoption, and this extension will also add to the extant literature on the relationship between intellectual capital and innovation adoption. Likewise, the present work serves as a call for more attention to be directed at understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of intellectual capital in an increasingly technologically complex business environment. Furthermore, in operationalizing the human capital construct from both internal and external perspectives and demonstrating that these two dimensions are conceptually and empirically distinct in terms of their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on OSS organizational adoption decision, the current study also enhances the human capital theory and highlights for future research the necessity of considering this separation to capture the inner mechanism of human capital functions. Third, the human capital construct is presented and tested in the OSS adoption context, which is rarely examined in adoption literature in an integrative manner. Further demonstrated in this paper is that human capital, derived from economics, can be applied as a discriminant factor for organizational OSS adoption behavior.

## 5.3. Practical implications

The present <sup>fi</sup>ndings also have implications for organizations that promote OSS and are eager to learn the requirements for increasing OSS adoption and diffusion. Particularly, human capital has been found to be a crucial factor in discriminating OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations. This factor provides a basis for more targeted OSS marketing and promotion. The availability of internal OSS human capital as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant discriminant variable between OSS-adopting and non-adopting organizations may remind OSS proponents that more effort is necessary to assist organizations in building their internal OSS human capital, which will then help <sup>fi</sup>rms overcome the adoption barrier. Possible approaches include providing regular communication, trainings, seminars, conferences, and workshops on OSS for potential adopting organizations. Particularly, marketers can also actively promote the use of OSS in educational institutions (e.g., schools and universities). With proper encouragement, support, and training, students should be able to learn how to develop and use OSS effectively. This strategy generates the “alumni effect” that will increase the organization's internal OSS human capital via future recruitment [20]. From the perspective of accessibility to external OSS human capital, OSS proponents should exert the effort to provide service and support that are readily accessible to potential adopters to reduce the perceived uncertainty and risk in OSS deployment. The recent emergence of new OSS business models, such as Professional OSS or Commercialized OSS companies JBoss, Redhat, and MySQL [44], can be explained by the present results as an effort to provide more reliable external OSS human capital. This research provides further direction for OSS proponents to target small organizations or small IT departments without suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources for software acquisition. For example, introduction and promotion of new OSS products can be carried out in a forum or association of small and medium-sized enterprises to increase OSS awareness among such organizations. As for the implication obtained from the result of IT criticality, instead of targeting only organizations in which IT operation is not critical, OSS proponents and the entire OSS community should organize to change the perception that OSS is not for mission-critical applications. Improving the reliability of OSS products and highlighting the successful cases of OSS applications in government, military, and <sup>fi</sup>nance organizations may help serve this purpose.

For potential adopters, recognizing the importance of building internal human capital and having access to external human capital are imperative. More effort should be devoted toward increasing both forms of human capital. For instance, potential adopters contemplating to adopt OSS should heavily encourage and support their internal staff to learn OSS by creating a supportive atmosphere. They may also turn to external sources for advice and assistance to supplement internal human capital (e.g., through workshops and short-term training). The present <sup>fi</sup>ndings will help raise the awareness of organization decision-makers on the importance of human capital and assist them in devising a process to speed up the response to technological changes in the business environment. This position is increasingly important given the rising availability of OSS as an alternative technological solution to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll organizational needs. In conclusion, the theoretical and empirical results of this study are expected to motivate more research on human capital as an important driver for organizational intention to adopt OSS.
