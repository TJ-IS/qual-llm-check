# The strategic value of IT insourcing: An IT-enabled business process perspective

Wen Guang Qu <sup>a,\*</sup>, Wonseok Oh <sup>b</sup>, Alain Pinsonneault <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Global Entrepreneurship Research Center, School of Management, Zhejiang University, 388 YuHangTang Road, Hangzhou, Zhejiang 310058, China <sup>b</sup> Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, 1001 Sherbrooke West, Montreal, QC, Canada H3A 1G5

## a r t i c l e i n f o

## a b s t r a c t

Although research on the business impact of IT outsourcing abounds, little is known about the relative strategic value of IT outsourcing and IT insourcing. Drawing upon the knowledge-based view of the firm, this study postulates that, compared with IT outsourcing, IT insourcing is more effective for developing IT-enabled business processes (IEBP), which subsequently lead to superior firm performance, Our analysis of the data from Information-Week and Compustat shows that IT insourcing is positively associated with IEBP, while the relationship between IT outsourcing and IEBP is not statistically significant. We also find that IEBP have a significant influence on firm performance. Finally, the effect of IT sourcing mechanisms on IEBP and the effect of IEBP on firm performance are both moderated by the type of innovation related to IEBP. The results suggest that in order to improve their performance firms should consider IT an integral part of their strategic core and should be proactively involved in the internal development of IT resources. We conclude with managerial implications and directions for future research.

## 1. Introduction

In recent vears, IT outsourcing¹ has become firmly established as a mainstream mechanism for obtaining IT resources and capabilities (Dibbern et al., 2004; Lacity et al., 2009). Since its prominent early adoption by Kodak, approximately 20 years ago, IT outsourcing has grown remarkably, while at the same time morphing into various forms (Chen and Bharadwaj, 2009; Cullen et al., 2005). For example, a report by the Gartner Group (Harris et al., 2009) indicated that worldwide spending on IT outsourcing would rise rapidly, from \$268 billion in 2009 to \$325 billion by 2013. Moreover, the emergence of new types of arrangements, such as offshore outsourcing, Software-as-a-Service (SaaS), and cloud computing, has accelerated the proliferation of the market-based IT outsourcing (Dibbern et al., 2008; Susarla et al., 2009), both within firms (e.g., software development and knowledge management) and across firm boundaries (e.g., supply chain management)

Notwithstanding the much-publicized popularity of IT outsourcing, many firms continue to rely on IT insourcing, which is defined as the organizational arrangement that a firm relies on internal IT department to obtain IT services (Dibbern et al., 2004). Moreover, a considerable number of firms have decided to terminate their outsourcing contracts and bring their outsourced IT functions back in-house (Veltri et al., 2008; Whitten and Leidner, 2006). For instance, JP Morgan Chase announced the early termination of its seven-year \$5 billion contract with IBM to consolidate the company’s data center and improve distributed computing capabilities (Forelle, 2004). Similarly, Sainsbury’s, the second largest supermarket chain in the UK, ended its 10-year, £3 billion outsourcing contract with Accenture three years early because the contract had failed to produce the anticipated increase in productivity (Rohde, 2004). Various reasons (e.g., poor service quality and high cost) have been offered for early contract terminations, and companies with early terminations often elect to switch to IT insourcing and develop their own IT capabilities rather than seek another outsourcing arrangement with a different service provider (see Table 1 for a list of major IT back-sourcing cases).

Over the past two decades, IS researchers have addressed a variety of IT sourcing issues, including the antecedents of outsourcing (Ang and Straub, 1998; Loh and Venkatraman, 1992; Tiwana and Bush, 2007), risks inherent in outsourcing (Gefen et al., 2008; Hahn et al., 2009; Oh et al., 2006), outsourcing relationships and processes (Goo et al., 2009; Heiskanen et al., 2008; Kern and Willcocks, 2002) and contract issues (Chen and Bharadwaj, 2009; Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan, 2008). One stream of research has investigated how IT outsourcing affects firm performance (Grover et al., 1996; Koh et al., 2004; Lee et al., 2004; Saunders et al., 1997; Seddon et al., 2007; Thouin et al., 2009). The main observation from this field is that firms can benefit from IT outsourcing when they follow best practices, such as outsourcing only commoditized IT functions, and developing partnerships with IT vendors. However, these studies typically evaluate IT outsourcing benefits by comparing manager satisfaction before and after the adoption of IT outsourcing. A direct comparison between IT outsourcing and insourcing in terms of performance impact has received limited attention in this line of research. It is thus yet unclear whether or not firms that rely on outsourcing will achieve better performance than firms that rely on IT insourcing.

Another stream of research has compared the effectiveness of IT outsourcing with that of IT insourcing in terms of cost savings. For example, based on a sample of 14 firms, Lacity and Hirschheim (1993) investigated the economic outcomes of IT sourcing decisions. In contrast to the prevalent view among practitioners that IT outsourcing outperforms IT insourcing (Kass and Caldwell, 1990), this case study revealed that IT insourcing can be as effective as IT outsourcing in light of cost savings. In the follow-up study, Hirschheim and Lacity (2000) validated the results of their earlier study by including additional insourcing cases in the analysis. Based on a study of 61 outsourcing and insourcing decisions, Lacity and Willcocks (1998) found that both IT outsourcing and IT insourcing can be cost-effective, but that selective IT outsourcing (a mix of outsourcing and insourcing) is the most effective mechanism. Although appropriate and important, a comparison based solely on cost savings might reflect only the operational dimension of IT-related performance, while providing limited insights into the value-creating aspect of IT performance

To summarize, prior studies have either failed to compare IT outsourcing with insourcing directly or made the comparison based solely on cost savings. However, we believe that a value creation perspective is essential for the comparison between the two alternatives because IT can be a source of value-creation and competitive advantage (Bharadwaj, 2000;

Our study extends the extant research by comparing the value-creating potential of the two IT sourcing mechanisms through the lens of IT-enabled business processes (hereafter, IEBP) (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Ross et al., 1996). IEBP refer to key business processes enabled or innovated by IT applications, such as IT-enabled customer service processes (Ray et al., 2005), knowledge management (Tanriverdi, 2005), and supply chain processes (Rai et al., 2006). Recent research has suggested that IEBP are critical to understanding the business value of IT investments (Barua et al., 2004; Melville et al., 2004; Rivard et al., 2006). The basic tenet of this view posits that the main role of IT is to create or enhance firms’ business processes, which are the central aspects of firm performance. Due to the strategic importance of IEBP to firms, we assess the extent to which each IT sourcing mechanism contributes to the development of IEBP, which, in turn, contributes to firm performance.

A motivation here is the recognition that firms could potentially reduce their IT costs through outsourcing arrangements. However, it is unclear how such arrangements affect the effectiveness of their business processes, especially IEBP. For example, if a firm’s main priority for its IT strategy is not focused on the reduction of costs, but rather on the effectiveness of IEBP, which sourcing mechanism should be chosen? Which sourcing mechanism offers more strategic value? For what aspect of IEBP is IT insourcing or IT outsourcing particularly effective? To what extent does IEBP affect firm performance? This paper attempts to shed light on these issues, with emphasis on the strategic value of IT insourcing

Our study has implications for both research and practice. It extends the outsourcing literature that has previously focused on cost savings alone by investigating the ‘value’ creation potential of alternative IT sourcing mechanisms (thereby recognizing that IT sourcing mechanisms can be a source of value creation as well as a means of cost reduction). From a practice perspective, the study has the potential to yield helpful guidance to firms in choosing appropriate IT sourcing mechanisms in such a way that maximizes the returns.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we draw on the knowledge-based view of the firm (Conner, 1991; Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992) and develop a theoretical framework in which IT sourcing mechanisms influence firms’ IEBP, which in turn have a significant influence on firm performance. The research model is then tested using data from InformationWeek magazine and the Compustat database. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion of the study’s implications and recommendations for future research.

## 2. Theories and hypotheses

Transaction cost economics (TCE) (Coase, 1937; Williamson, 1985) is the dominant conceptual framework for understanding why firms exist. The theory posits that firms (or hierarchies) exist because they can mitigate the costs/risks that arise from opportunistic behaviors (Williamson, 1985). The knowledge-based view of the firm (Conner, 1991; Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992) extends TCE by claiming that firms exist not only to reduce transaction costs, but also to capitalize on their inherent advantages over the market-based mechanism with respect to knowledge creation/sharing and innovation. For instance, Kogut and Zander (1992) argued that firms structurally outcompete markets in terms of sharing and deployment of the knowledge of individuals and groups within an firm because the firm contains and utilizes higherorder organizing principles that the market cannot supply, such as shared coding schemes, common value, and a shared language. Conner (1991) suggested that firms have advantages over markets because under a common firm-level culture and routines, the knowledge transplantation between activities and the creation-redeployment of specific assets will be easier.

In particular, coordination mechanisms and shared knowledge within a firm have been suggested to contribute to knowledge creation and innovation (Gulati and Singh, 1998; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). For instance, Grant (1996) claimed that coordination mechanisms (e.g., rules and directions, sequencing, routines, and group problem solving and decision making) and common knowledge (e.g., shared language, commonality of specific knowledge, and shared meaning) that exist within firms make the firm a better governance mechanism than the market for integrating specific knowledge and creating organizational innovations.

The development of IEBP is one kind of innovation that requires the integration of IT knowledge and business knowledge (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Boynton et al., 1994). According to the knowledge-based view of the firm, smooth coordination and shared knowledge between IT and business units within firms should be helpful for the integration of IT-business knowledge and the development of IEBP. IS research has also provided some support for this view. For instance, on coordination, Lind and Zmud (1991) suggested that rich interaction between technical and managerial units are necessary elements with regard to nurturing IEBP. Reich and Benbasat (2000) asserted that communication and interaction between IT and business managers increase the level of alignment between IT and business obiectives, On shared knowledge. Bassellier et al. (2003) found that shared knowledge between IT professionals and business professionals can improve the IT-business partnership and increase business managers’ motivation to champion IT projects and develop IEBP.

As the knowledge-based view of the firm suggests the coordination and shared knowledge between IT and business units may be different depending on the choice of the arrangement, either IT outsourcing or IT insourcing. For instance, when IT insourcing is used, coordination is between internal IT and business units. However, with IT outsourcing, coordination is between outside IT vendors and business clients. The former coordination scenario is usually smoother than the latter because coordination mechanisms within firms such as routine meetings and co-location are likely to encourage frequent and progressive formal and informal interaction and communication between IT and business units. Consequently, smooth coordi nation between internal IT and business units provides firms with fruitful grounds upon which IEBP may be cultivated.

Shared knowledge between IT and business units may also be more intensive under IT insourcing than under IT outsourcing. Share knowledge is typically developed over a long period of time and is subject to path dependence (Ray et al., 2005) Therefore, when firms rely on their internal IT units to acquire IT services, the shared knowledge and personal relationships between IT and business units will continue to grow as a result of the collaborative, long-term cumulative relationship. In contrast, when firms rely only on outsourcing to obtain their IT resources, the path-dependent, shared knowledge between IT and business units is not likely to be fully developed and utilized. This is because IT staff affiliated with external vendors may not fully understand the specific business processes that the client firm has created and inherited over time, and business staff at client firms may not fully recognize the capacity and capabilities of IT supplied by an external vendor. As pointed out by Willcocks et al. (2004), because of a lack of knowledge about their clients’ long-term strategic goals, most IT vendors find it difficult to deliver on their promises of IT innovation and added value. Taken together, since IT insourcing offers advantages over IT outsourcing with respect to utilizing path-dependent, shared knowledge and enhancing coordination, which are necessary for the integration of IT-business knowledge and the development of IEBP, the former should be more effective at cultivating IEBP and thus leads to better IEBP, ceteris paribus.<sup>2</sup> On this basis we propose that:

## Hypothesis 1a: IT insourcing will lead to better IEBP than IT outsourcing.

Although we suggest that IT insourcing is generally more effective than IT outsourcing for cultivating IEBP due to its advantages in coordination and shared knowledge, the importance of such advantages may vary depending on the types of IEBP. Firms typically use a wide array of IT applications that differ substantially in terms of their strategic orientation and objectives. Prior research has distinguished between different types of process innovations enabled by IT in terms of their objectives and the magnitude of their impact on business performance (Grover et al., 1997; Lyytinen and Rose, 2003; Swanson, 1994). For example, according to Swanson’s (1994) framework, Type I innovation is defined as process innovation restricted to the functional IS core. Type I innovations include the use of new technologies in software development (e.g., the use of object-oriented technology and Internet computing).<sup>3</sup> Type II innovation applies IT to the administrative tasks of a firm, such as the deployment of IT applications related to accounting and human resource management. Type III innovation refers to the deployment of IT applications that are more strategic and constitute the core of the business, such as supply chain management (SCM) systems and RosettaNet-based inter-organizational systems. In short, Type III is particularly germane to key business processes and strategic activities, such as operations, logistics, and marketing, whereas Type II is concerned with supporting activities, such as infrastructure and human resource management. Finally, Type I typically deals with the processes related to system and application development and, therefore, matters mostly to a firm’s IT infra structure. Although variations exist, this typology of IT-based innovations has been widely used in IS research and serves as foundations for classifying various types of IT systems and applications (e.g., Chatterjee et al., 2002; Grover et al., 1997; Zhu and Kraemer, 2005).

The impacts of IT sourcing mechanisms on IEBP may be contingent on the types of innovation underlying IEBP. More specifically, we predict that the advantages of IT insourcing over IT outsourcing in terms of coordination and shared knowledge will be more substantial for Type III innovations than for Type I innovations.<sup>4</sup> According to prior research (Lyytinen and Rose, 2003; Swanson, 1994), Type III innovations are related to firms’ core business such as production and supply chain, while Type I innovations are restricted to the functional IT unit and IT infrastructure. Consequently, the implementation of Type III innova tions tends to require greater coordination and better shared knowledge between IT and business units than does the implementation of Type I innovations. For instance, the implementation of customer relationship management (CRM) systems may require a high level of coordination and knowledge sharing between IT and marketing units (Boynton et al., 1994; Lind and Zmud, 1991), while the adoption of Internet computing or object-oriented technologies in software development is likely to demand only limited involvement of business units.

Because the implementation of Type IL innovations requires smooth coordination and a high level of shared knowledge between IT and business units, and IT insourcing provides greater capacity and capability for such coordination and knowledge sharing than IT outsourcing, it is expected that IT insourcing is more suitable and effective for implementing Type III innovations and developing IEBP than IT outsourcing. For the implementation of Type I innovations, however, the advantages of IT insourcing over outsourcing in coordination and shared knowledge may not be as substantial because business units typically do not constitute an integral part of Type I innovations. Accordingly, we propose that:

Hypothesis 1b: The advantages of IT insourcing over IT outsourcing are more substantial for the development of IEBP derived from Type III innovations than for IEBP derived from Type I innovations.

Previous studies (Melville et al., 2004; Rai et al., 2006) suggested that effective IEBP provide firms with sustainable competitive advantage. Piccoli and Ives (2005) identified four types of barriers (i.e., IT resources, complementary resources, IT projects, and preemptive barriers) that prevent rivals from imitating firms’ IEBP. They suggested that competitors cannot easily replicate the competitive advantage created by IEBP because a firm often has an idiosyncratic set of IT assets and a unique repertoire of organizational resources that are complementary to those assets. Many studies support the view that IEBP enhances firm performance through increased profitability or market value. For example, Barua et al. (2004) found that the effectiveness of firms’ day-to-day business activities, including electronic transactions and information exchanges with customers (e.g., sales, customer service, and new-customer acquisition) and suppliers (e.g., procurement), is positively associated with financial performance. Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien (2005) revealed that IT support for core activities that are market-access-related, integrity-related, and functionality-related, improve operational and market-based performance Similarly, Rai et al. (2006) found that IT-enabled integration of supply chain processes, including physical flow, information flow, and financial flow integration, is significantly associated with firm performance as measured by operational excellence customer relationships, and revenue growth. Accordingly, we propose that:

Hypothesis 2a: IEBP are positively associated with firm performance.

Finally, IEBP enabled by different types of IT might influence firm performance differently. Since business-oriented Type III innovations are likely to affect many critical aspects of a firm’s operations, their impact should be broader and more sig nificant than that of Type I innovations, which are confined to the IT unit and IT infrastructure (Lyytinen and Rose, 2003; Swanson, 1994). Moreover, effective Type III innovations typically entail intensive involvement from business units – a fact that makes this type of innovations highly complicated and difficult for competitors to imitate (Piccoli and Ives, 2005). As a result, not many firms have the full capability and capacity to deploy effective Type III innovations. According to the resource-based view, valuable, rare and imitable resources will be the source of value-creation and strategic advantages (Barney, 1991; Wernerfelt, 1984). In this light, Type III innovations may have substantial contribution to firm performance. In contrast, Type I innovations are usually based on technological innovations that do not require much involvement of business units. Many firms may have the ability to conduct such innovations if they have, or are able to recruit, technology-capable staff. Consequently, according to the resource-based view, Type I innovations may not contribute to firm performance as much as Type III innovations are capable of. In this respect, IEBP derived from Type III innovations may improve firm performance in a more intensive and sustainable manner than do IEBP derived from Type I innovations. Accordingly, we propose that:

Hypothesis 2b: IEBP derived from Type III innovations will have a stronger effect on firm performance than IEBP derived from Type I innovations.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Data

The primary source of IT-related data used in this study is from InformationWeek, a magazine that has since the early 1990s published a series of special reports on firms’ IT performance. Our study used the data from the 1997 to 2000 InformationWeek 500 reports.<sup>5</sup> In these reports, the magazine ranked the 500 most innovative organizations on the basis of their innovative use of IT in business<sup>6</sup> and provided specific IT-related information, including the size of IT budgets, the number of IT employees, and the proportion of the IT budget spent on outsourcing. The data from InformationWeek have been widely used in IS research, particularly in studies investigating the business impact of IT investments or resources (e,g.. Bharadwai 2000; Bharadwaj et al., 1999; Santhanam and Hartono, 2003).

Our analysis initially included 169 of the 500 firms in the 1997 InformationWeek 500 report; more than 300 firms were excluded either because they were not publicly listed or their specific IT data (IT budget or IT outsourcing ratio) were not available through the report. Our final sample contained 143 firms because 26 of the 169 firms were removed due to their incomplete financial information available from Compustat (e.g., due to delisting and mergers). We tested whether there is significant difference between firms included in our sample and those excluded (i.e., removal bias), based on the revenue and income data reported in the 1997 InformationWeek report. The t-tests showed that the difference is not significant (p = 0.137 and p = 0.468 respectively). The firms included in our sample had revenues of \$10.4 billion and 53,000 employees on average. The annual IT budgets of these firms ranged from 3.8 million to \$4.4 billion, with a mean of \$273.6 million. Our sample covers 33 industries according to the two-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code.

## 3.2. Variable description

The key variables used in this study include IT outsourcing and IT insourcing investments, IEBP, and firm performance (see Table 2). The extent of IT outsourcing (or IT insourcing) is measured based on the money spent on IT services supplied by external third-party vendors (or on in-house IT investments). The values of IT outsourcing and IT insourcing were computed by using the data from the 1997 InformationWeek 500 report.<sup>7</sup> The report provided data on IT investment budgets and outsourcing ratios for firms, from which the specific amounts that the firms allocated to IT insourcing and outsourcing were computed. The amount of these investments was then normalized using the firm's revenue to control for the potential effect of firm size, as done in prior research (Bharadwaj et al., 1999).<sup>8</sup>

Prior studies have suggested that IT-enabled organizational processes, such as knowledge management (Tanriverdi, 2005), electronic business (Barua et al., 2004), enterprise integration (Barki and Pinsonneault, 2005), and software development (Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen, 2004) are of strategic importance to firms. For each year in 1998–2000, Information-Week surveyed senior IT executives regarding the extent to which their firms use IT to support these four activities: (1) knowledge management (e.g., using data mining tools), (2) electronic business (e.g., selling products online), (3) enterprise integration (e,g., standardization on ERP), and (4) software development (e.g., using obiect-oriented technology) (i.e., the four components of IEBP; see Table 3 for the detailed survey questions). Subsequently, firms were evaluated based on the innovative use of IT in support of their business operations for the aforementioned four specific areas and were assigned gold, silver, or bronze medals in each of these areas. These ‘‘medals” were allotted on a curve for each area; a third of the firms received gold, a third received silver, and the remaining third received bronze medals. After that, an overall rank (i.e., the InformationWeek 500 ranking of innovative IT users, from 1st to 500th) was assigned to each firm based on its innovative use of IT and the medals it received for the aforementioned four areas (Weston, 1998).

The scores of overall IEBP were measured based on the InformationWeek 500 overall ranking (i.e., ordinal number from 1st to 500th) in the 1998–2000 reports.<sup>9</sup> Since the ranking of firms may not accurately reflect actual differences that exist in their IEBP, a procedure was undertaken to normalize the ordinal data for statistical analysis (Cohen et al., 2003, p. 247). Based on the normal distribution of IEBP level among firms and the population size,<sup>10</sup> the ranking data were converted into z-values corresponding to the level of firms’ IEBP. These z-values were then used in the final analysis, similar to many studies (e.g., Jones et al., 2004; Salmivalli et al., 2000) that have utilized this z-value transformation of ranked data in statistical analyses.

ROA and Tobin’s q<sup>11</sup> were used as proxies for firm performance, reflecting firm profitability and market value, respectively. Both ROA and Tobin’s q have frequently been used as performance measures in IT business value research (e.g., Bharadwaj et al., 1999: Hitt and Bryniolfsson, 1996). ## 4. Discussion and implications

## 4.1. Main findings

At least three aspects of our findings need to be discussed in great detail. First, the results indicate that although no significant relationship is found between IEBP and IT outsourcing, IT insourcing is significantly associated with IEBP. These results support our conceptual arguments, which posit that due to the enhanced coordination and shared knowledge between IT and business units facilitated by IT insourcing, IT insourcing should outperform IT outsourcing in regard to cultivating IEBP. This finding is also consistent with the essential idea of the knowledge-based view of the firm. That is, coordination mechanisms and shared knowledge within firms are particularly important for knowledge creation and developing organizational innovations (Conner, 1991; Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992).

Our results suggest that different types of IT investments (insourcing and outsourcing) should be distinguished in the study of IT business value. By relying on internal IT departments to provide IT services, firms can cultivate shared knowledge and smooth coordination between IT and business units over an extended period of time (Cha et al., 2009; Ray et al., 2005). Such shared knowledge and enhanced coordination in turn will facilitate the integration of IT knowledge and business knowledge and lead to the development of IEBP (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Boynton et al., 1994). As a result, there is a positive association between IT insourcing and IEBP

Second, we find that IEBP are positively associated with firms’ profitability (ROA) and market value (Tobin’s q). While prior research has shown that investments in IT positively affect firm performance, many practitioners wonder why and how IT investments enhance firm performance (Bharadwaj et al., 1999; Melville et al., 2004). Recent studies posit that IT can improve a firm’s performance by renovating or improving its business processes, such as customer services, new product development and supply chain operations, which in turn lead to a better firm performance (Rai et al., 2006; Ray et al., 2005). In this respect, IT-enabled enhanced or innovative business processes are critical elements for understanding the business value of IT. The results of our study support this idea. We find that IT investments, and IT insourcing in particular, facilitate the development of IEBP, which improve firm performance such as ROA and Tobin’s q.

Finally, our results show the merit of the typology of IT-related innovations, which was developed by Swanson (1994) and has been widely used in IS research (e.g., Lyytinen and Rose, 2003; Zhu and Kraemer, 2005), in classifying IEBP. We distinguished IEBP derived from Type I innovations (for software development) from IEBP derived from Type III innovations (for core business), and find that the advantages of IT insourcing over IT outsourcing are more important for Type III innovationrelated IEBP. In addition, Type III innovation-related IEBP have stronger impacts on firm performance than do Type I innovation-related IEBP. This result suggests that this typology of innovations provides a fine basis for classifying different types of IEBP.

## 4.2. Implications and limitations

Given these results, this study provides useful implications for research and practice. In terms of research, we have investigated the business impacts of IT sourcing mechanisms through the lens of IEBP. Prior studies have compared the impacts of IT outsourcing and IT insourcing from a cost-savings point of view. However, little research to date has compared IT outsourcing to IT insourcing in terms of their roles in value creation, such as facilitating the development of IEBP. Understanding the strategic value-creation aspect of alternative IT sourcing mechanisms is increasingly important to the development and implementation of effective IT strategies. Our study contributes to the literature by providing grounds to associate alternative IT sourcing mechanisms with IEBP and firm performance.

Our study also has several implications for managers. The results indicate that managers should consider IT insourcing if their goal is to pursue IT-enabled strategic opportunities. According to a recent survey, investment in IT outsourcing has increased substantially over the past several years (Harris et al., 2009). This suggests that companies have increasingly relied on IT outsourcing to obtain IT resources and capabilities. One explanation for this increased propensity to use IT outsourcing is that many firms do not make sufficient effort to identify and capture the strategic value of IT, but instead view IT-related resources as non-core activities, or even as cost burdens. This is in line with research findings that have demonstrated that achieving cost saving is the main motivation behind IT outsourcing (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Loh and Venkatraman, 1992). Our study suggests that creating value and, more importantly, gaining strategic advantages from their IT investments require firms to proactively adopt IT insourcing and consider it as part of their core activities. IT should not be viewed as a peripheral tool that helps firms focus on other core competences; instead it should be considered a core competency in and of itself.

In fact, as Table 1 shows, many firms have brought their outsourced IT back in-house as they have begun to recognize the strategic importance of in-house IT development. For instance, Austin Adams, J.P. Morgan’s CIO, stated in a recent survey: ‘‘We believe that managing our own technology is best for the long-term growth and success of our company” (Ramsaran 2004, p. 80). Similarly, Jerry Gross, Washington Mutual’s CIO, said in an interview:

‘‘It occurred to me that in some cases it would be better to re-insource back into the Washington Mutual IT group because the functions involved such close interaction with customers” (Overby, 2003).

Vicky Kelly, Bendigo Bank’s CIO, was even more straightforward:

‘‘In banking now, the lines between the business of banking and IT are becoming blurred. We need the IT knowledge within our organization so we can use it to help the business and we want immediate access so we can change direction at a moment’s notice. Outsourcing doesn’t provide that” (Stewart et al., 2002, p. 19).

However, far more companies still rely heavily on external IT vendors and give insufficient strategic priority to developing IT resources internally, even though many of their IT outsourcing initiatives have not lived up to expectations. We believe that as the growth of IT outsourcing continues to explode, the ‘‘IT doesn’t matter” (Carr, 2003) controversy will remain and become more widespread than it is now.

Finally, managers who consider IT insourcing should understand that building or rebuilding IT operations in-house is difficult and costly and unlikely to be accomplished in a short period of time (Whitten and Wakefield, 2006). This may be one of the reasons that many companies seek to hand over control of their IT resources to external vendors. A well-defined IT vision and strategy that delineate what should be developed internally needs to be clearly articulated before adopting or switching to IT insourcing.

This study has several limitations that could be addressed in future research. The companies in our samples are large, public firms who were considered innovative IT users and therefore may have a high level of internal IT competence. Research has indicated that firms competent in IT can gain more benefits from insourcing than from outsourcing (Nevo et al., 2007). Also, as suggested in Footnote 2 earlier, the development of IEBP requires firms to have certain level of IT competence and firms lacking such competence may have no choice but seek help from external IT vendors (i.e., IT outsourcing). For these reasons, our results may not be generalizable to firms with a low level of IT competence.

In addition, our study is based on IT investment data, which does not provide detailed information on how and where the investments were made. Also, it is difficult to assess how effectively the firms in the sample have implemented and used the IT systems. The majority of IT business value studies (e.g., Bharadwaj et al., 1999; Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1998) that have used investment data also suffer from this limitation. Future studies can compensate for this deficiency by investigating which IT functions are typically outsourced or insourced, and how well the systems were used when different sourcing mechanisms were adopted.

Moreover, our analysis could be replicated with more recent data, because the scope of IT outsourcing has rapidly expanded in recent years to include new forms of outsourcing arrangements (e.g., offshoring and utility-based outsourcing). However, regardless of the different types, all of these new arrangements are still market-based sourcing alternatives and, therefore, our findings will remain reasonably accurate even when new data are used. The relative advantage of IT insourcing over outsourcing, yet, may vary across different forms of IT outsourcing. For instance, the integration of IT-business knowledge may be very difficult in the case of offshoring due to the cultural differences, remote distance and so on. Therefore, future research may replicate our findings in light of recent trends.

Finally, the method used in this study cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality, meaning that our results may be partially due to IT-capable firms investing heavily in IT insourcing. In order to mitigate this problem, one-year lag was set between IT investment and IEBP in this study. In addition, our study focuses on the value-creating aspect of alternative IT sourcing mechanisms in order to extend the current cost-saving perspective in the literature. However, both value and costs are critical to practice. Future research may further our understanding by investigating the compound effects of both costsaving and value-creating aspects. Alternatively, future research may identify contextual conditions under which firms would better focus on cost saving or value-creating. For instance, in industries where IT leads to radical changes in business models and causes lost of process innovations (Dehning et al., 2003). firms may focus on the value-creating aspect of IT. In contrast, in industries where IT only plays a peripheral role, firms may only need to consider its costs. Finally, it should be noted that IT insourcing will not automatically lead to effective IEBP. Future research may design and test procedural guidelines on how firms can accumulate IT-related resources internally and develop IEBP.

## 5. Conclusion

IT sourcing has become a two-way street; many firms have switched to IT outsourcing, while others have brought their outsourced IT functions in-house. Although numerous studies investigated the business value of IT outsourcing from various perspectives, little attention was paid to the direct comparison between IT outsourcing and IT insourcing with respect to value creation and firm performance. Our study aimed extending the extant literature by assessing the value-creating aspect of these two types of IT sourcing mechanisms. The results show that IT insourcing may have a greater strategic value than IT outsourcing with regard to developing IEBP. This finding suggests that the shared knowledge and smooth coordination between IT and business units, which can profoundly affect firms’ IEBP, are not commodities and cannot be easily purchased in the market, but rather should be developed in-house over a long period of time. Consequently, in order to achieve strategic advantages through IT investments, firms should be proactively develop IT-related resources in-house and should consider them an integral part of their core competences.
