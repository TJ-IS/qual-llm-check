# CONTROL OVER VIRTUAL WORLDS BY GAME COMPANIES: ISSUES AND RECOMMENDATIONS $^{1}$

Christophe Roquilly
EDHEC Business School, 58 rue du Port, 59046 Lille cédex, FRANCE {christophe.roquilly@edhec.edu}

Game companies use five components—four core components and one complementary one—in a 5Cs model to ensure the control and development of virtual worlds. A multidisciplinary review of the literature reveals that game companies make use of copyright, codes, creativity, and community to do this. They use the contract as a complementary component to reinforce their control over the four basic components and to compensate for the lacunae they present. In order to examine the extent to which game companies use the contract in this way, an analysis is performed of all contractual documents from a sample of 20 virtual worlds, providing evidence of general trends and emphasizing any differences between the virtual worlds in terms of the business and gaming models sought by each game company. An explanation is provided of why these contracts do not constitute a sustainable model for the game companies, given the high level of legal insecurity they present. Some basic recommendations can be made in order to improve the sustainability of the 5Cs model by modifying these contracts in such a way that they are enforceable and by matching their content with appropriate business and gaming models. This could lead to further studies aimed at providing answers to some of the intriguing issues affecting scholars and practitioners.

Keywords: Community, computer codes, contract, control, copyright, creativity, EULA, property rights, virtual world

## Introduction

Virtual worlds enable people to be someone else and even to take on different personalities at the same time (Castronova 2001, p. 11). Several authors have tried to define these virtual worlds (e.g., Barfield 2006; Mayer-Schönberger and Crowley 2006), with the following key phrases of particular importance: “sophisticated pieces of software,” “project an identity into a generated three dimensional reality,” “through the use of advanced computers,” “interactive computer simulation,” “which lets its participants see, hear, use and even modify the simulated objects in the computer-generated environment.” In the virtual world, items (armor, space stations, condominiums for avatars, etc.) can be reduced to a series of bytes and can change the physical medium on which they depend without their value changing in any considerable way (Gensollen 2007).

The history of online games and virtual worlds is not long but has it undergone an extremely rapid evolution (Mulligan and Patrovski 2003; Schwarz and Bullis 2005). If one refers to the studies carried out by Alemi (2007), three distinct categories can be identified. The first is simple online games, a highly disparate category despite its label. The MMOGs (massively multiplayer online games) form the second category, which is marked by the absence (or quasi-absence) of a pre-scripted story line and the fact that avatars controlled by the users are not (or not to a large extent) predetermined by the game developers. Virtual worlds such as Second Life, There, or Sims Online belong to this category. The avatars and their possessions are created by users, and it is the users who make and break this virtual world. The third category, the MMORPGs (massively multiplayer online role playing games), which submerge users in a pre-scripted environment of variable complexity, usually involve heroic fantasy or science fiction. Users customize their avatars using elements created and made available by the game developers. Both MMOGs and MMORPGs have in common the fact that they represent persistent worlds in which thousands of users continue to evolve their avatars. When users leave the digital world and turn off their PC, their avatar continues to exist, if only inscribed in lines of code. The world in which these avatars evolve is constantly changing, with other avatars being created, disappearing, and further developing their capacities. $^{2}$

The regularly updated statistics on Bruce Sterling Woodcock's weblog clearly reveal the current growth of digital worlds. $^{3}$ In 2008, more than 60 million users were recorded, and it is forecast that by 2011 four out of five Internet users will be part of a virtual world. $^{4}$ This increase in the number of users results in a corresponding increase in sales for game companies. The economic transactions that take place in digital worlds are not simply virtual, or internal to the digital environment. The economic flows between game companies and users, which can include an initial payment, monthly fees, etc., are set to triple by 2011, going from \$3.4 billion to \$11 billion, according to a study carried out by DFC in 2006. $^{5}$ The most popular MMORPG, World of Warcraft, generated revenue of \$1 billion in 2006 and \$1.5 billion in 2008, with a total of more than 11 million users. In 2006 and 2007, trading of virtual property approached a figure of \$1 billion. Growth in such transactions has led to the development of online purchasing platforms, sometimes auction-based (see mmobids.com or playerauctions.com), and specialist online sales (gameusd.com, ezgaming.com, IGE.com, Game4power.com). A digital island in Project Entropia was sold for \$26,500. $^{6}$

Worlds such as Second Life also provide a new source of revenue for various companies (Ives and Junglas 2008).

Virtual worlds are, therefore, much more than gaming environments; they also constitute a prosperous and competitive industry in which game companies must deploy a certain number of resources in order to construct a lasting and successful business model. As in numerous other industries, game companies need to exert a certain level of control over services they put at the disposal of users. For this reason, game companies decide whether or not to authorize users to take certain actions in their virtual worlds. Again like other industries, game companies use the contract to legally manage what they decide to authorize or prohibit. The contract constitutes a central tool by which to organize the mutual rights of game companies and users. It is designed to allow game companies to compensate the lacunae of technology and legal statutes, and to create a common set of rules for all users. This paper draws on an emerging issue in the field of information systems and management research (the key factors for the control and development of virtual worlds) and two controversial issues in the field of legal research (property rights allocation between game companies and users, and the legal validity of contracts of adhesion) in order to argue that the contractual models—end user license agreement (EULA) and terms of service (TOS)—used by game companies are neither efficient enough nor legally secure. First, a conceptual framework developed from a multidisciplinary review of the literature dedicated to the development of virtual worlds is proposed. Next, empirical evidence of the way in which EULAs and TOS are used to reinforce control by game companies over virtual worlds, according to the business model chosen, is provided. An explanation is given for why these contracts present a very high level of legal insecurity and do not, therefore, constitute a sustainable model that would allow the game companies to exercise legally sound control over the virtual world. Finally, some measures are recommended that would make the contracts enforceable and bring them in line with the business and gaming models chosen. The conclusions highlight some of the challenges facing researchers and practitioners in the domain of virtual worlds.

## Development and Control of Virtual Worlds

Our understanding of techno-social phenomena can be enriched by a multidisciplinary perspective (Orlikowski and Barley 2001). A review of the literature in the fields of law, economics, information systems and management allows us to identify five essential resources for the development and control of virtual worlds by game companies. We can refer to these resources as the 5Cs model: creativity, community, code, copyright, and contract.

## Creativity, Community, Code, and Copyright

Creativity is a key element in the development of any virtual world and the success it will have with the public. It can be expressed through a blend of artistry (quality of graphics and script in particular), technology (ergonomics of virtual world, usability, and security for users) and social interaction (ability to combine suggestions and generate ideas that will improve the virtual world). Creativity comes from the many different specialists working in a game company, and a company's ability to make the most of this can constitute a core competency (Cohendet and Simon 2007). Such creativity can also constitute an external resource when it comes from users, particularly from the most experienced among them (Thomke and Von Hippel 2002).

Community refers to the group of users connected to a virtual world. It can constitute a strong network when users share an underlying bond based on important dimensions (personal interests, loyalty to the product, in this case, the virtual world, etc.) (Rheingold 2000; Shankar and Bayus 2003). Users of virtual worlds do not correspond to the kinds of stereotypes suggested by the media, and what motivates them is variable (achievement, social advancement, immersion; see Williams et al. 2008). $^{7}$ Bartle (1996) identifies four user categories: achievers, explorers, killers, and socializers. Differences in what motivates these users, as long as there is no antagonism, do not impede the creation of a vibrant community. Indeed, game companies seek to construct communities that are as diverse as possible on the basis that there is greater motivation to join a virtual world when it has many different users (Shy 2001). This mechanism of network externalities, first identified by Leibenstein (1950), constitutes an extraordinary tool for competitiveness (Katz and Shapiro 1985). Furthermore, the expansion of a community makes it harder to coordinate user preferences and, therefore, harder to switch to another virtual world (Farrell and Saloner 1985; Liebowitz and Margolis 1990).

Codes are sets of instructions to a computer that determine whether users can or cannot perform certain actions within a virtual world. By developing and then controlling these codes, game companies determine the way in which their virtual world functions and the extent of the possibilities it offers. In a way, as Lessig (1999) points out, “code is law”

(see also Balkin 2006). Code can authorize users to make partial modifications (to an avatar, its attributes, or even to the virtual environment itself) or even create something entirely different such as a new virtual world (Arakji and Lang 2007; Hughes and Lang 2006). The more a game company employs permissive code, the more the user has control over the evolution of the virtual world. Codes also provide security for virtual property (Chen et al. 2005).

Copyright (or author's rights in civil law countries) can apply to a certain number of intellectual human creations—text, fiction, digital images, characters, building design, music, code (computer program, whether in object or source code)—where there is originality. Within virtual worlds, avatars and their accessories comprise both visual images and the software algorithms that are used to allow the avatar to evolve in its digital environment (Westbrook 2006). As shown by Fairfield (2005), avatars are eligible for dual protection under copyright, both as pictorial and literary characters. Copyright, therefore, allows game companies to legally protect the product of their employees' creativity, and even allows them to limit the creation of derivative works by users, given that the creator of an original copyrightable piece of expression is also given the exclusive right to authorize any derivative works based upon that original work (Baldrica 2007). Copyright is a major motivating factor for users in cases where a game company chooses a model for its virtual world whereby the (software) code offers users the possibility to contribute to the creation of that world's content. There are limits to these four elements, however, which explains why game companies need to rely on an additional resource: the contract.

## The Contract

Each of the four elements already outlined presents certain lacunae. With creativity, game companies choosing to develop virtual worlds that are densely scripted and rich with animation and characters must make significant investments. They are unable to ensure constant innovation, although some do regularly offer users extensions that provide access to new adventures. Of course, the size of certain virtual communities (in particular, World of Warcraft) benefits network externalities, but such large communities are at greater risk of heterogeneity and therefore potential conflict. Indeed, despite network externalities, users might be encouraged to leave a community if a virtual world fails to offer sufficient innovation. Code, meanwhile, is a powerful method by which game companies can determine what is and is not possible for users, but the use of overly restrictive codes that forbid various actions within the virtual world might diminish the appeal to participate in the first place. The creation of a virtual world that leaves users with sufficient freedom cannot, therefore, eliminate conflict between users or deviant behavior (Lastowka 2008; Mayer-Schönberger and Crowley 2006), as revealed by the many incidents to have occurred in Second Life. What is more, these codes can sometimes be circumvented, and they fail to prevent the reproduction of an image or text. Copyright, finally, only offers protection to game companies where it is a matter of copyrightable work, and it does not allow them to protect all types of information. The extent of the control that can be exercised under copyright is also limited by the doctrine of fair use.

In order to access a virtual world, users must enter into a contract with the game company. This contract allows the game company to reinforce the four elements already outlined, compensate for any lacunae they may have left, and lay down a certain level of control over the virtual world. It takes the form of an EULA and/or TOS, which are generally presented “as a graphical computer window that pops up when the user of the software begins running the program” (Glushko 2007, p. 520). However, most EULAs can be viewed on the relevant web site for a given virtual world, without ever having to install a program.

## Empirical Evidence from Twenty Virtual Worlds

In order to explore how game companies reinforce their control over virtual worlds using a contract, whether an EULA or a TOS, twenty virtual worlds were selected for analysis based on three criteria: renown (based on rankings produced by mmorpg.com, rpgrank.com, gameogre.com, and mondepersistants.com); age (providing a mix of more or less recently established virtual worlds); and type (with both MMORPGs and MMOGs).

## Basic Considerations for the Business Model

For each virtual world in the sample, an attempt was made to determine a few key points: launch date, number of subscribers or users, and economic model. As shown in Table 1, certain factors make it impossible to reach completely reliable data, although it is possible to learn more about certain elements. Three economic models can be identified: pay-to-play (with monthly fee), box-to-play (where the user pays to install the program and creates an account, but incurs no monthly charges), and free-to-play (free, except that users must pay for a premium version offering more game opportunities or for specific virtual items). Box-to-play is very much the minority model and is even disappearing. Analysis of the changes in user numbers over a period of several years tends to show that game companies that switch from pay-to-play to free-to-play do so in order to stimulate a virtual world with declining subscriber numbers. Finally, companies using free-to-play have a higher number of users than those on pay-to-play, with the exception of World of Warcraft, which largely dominates the market.

An analysis was conducted of all contractual documents (EULAs and TOS) that might be used to reinforce the four other elements, with the exception of any provisions concerning privacy policies or freedom of speech, which are not included in the scope of this paper. We can see how game companies contractually formalize their attempts to control the operation and development of their virtual worlds. Table 3 shows to what extent contract provisions are related to the four components (copyright, code, community, and creativity).

Looking at these two tables together reveals a general trend: game companies use the contract to complete and reinforce their control over the operation and development of the virtual world, as provided by the other four components. It is advisable to look beyond these simple statistics, however. Indeed, these tables reveal a certain amount of disparity from one virtual world to the next. Apart from the two encircled zones, differences in the positioning on the map are mainly due to alternative drafting of contract provisions, which do not radically change the model of control implemented by the game company. A major difference can be observed between MMOGs and MMORPGs. The trend in MMORPGs is not to authorize the sale of accounts and/or virtual items by users, with just a few exceptions (these are, in fact, the oldest virtual worlds, as shown in Table 1). The opposite trend is revealed in the case of MMOGs. This difference in the model can be explained primarily—if not exclusively—by the fact that in MMOGs the diversity and the richness of the content depends above all on the creativity of the users, whereas in MMORPGs it is the creativity of the game company’s staff that is the critical factor. In the latter case, the cost of investment in creativity is passed on to the users. Indeed, 87 percent of the MMORPGs in the sample require payment (for access rights, subscription, and in some cases extensions); the remaining 13 percent are free of charge either because they are new (launch phase) or are having difficulty attracting new users. All of the MMOGs in the sample are based on free access to the virtual worlds, but 80 percent of them require users to pay for a premium account if they wish to access to certain virtual items and/or sell items associated with their account. But game companies, when they do not prohibit sales of accounts or virtual items, nevertheless keep some control over the economic transactions, given that EULAs or TOS stipulate that transactions must be made on the platform made available and controlled by the game company.

The other main lesson that can be drawn from Table 3 and Figure 1 is that Second Life (virtual world number 13) uses an original model when compared to other virtual worlds, including MMOGs. Vacca (2008) describes Second Life as a “new model,” as against the “traditional model.” Indeed, the Second Life TOS is the only one to recognize user ownership of characters and items where they have been created by the users themselves. None of the contracts recognizes user copyright over virtual world content, with the exception of Second Life. The peculiarity of the Second Life contract is due to the fact that this virtual world develops primarily thanks to the creative input of users, which is boosted by the benefit of having copyright protection. But Linden Lab (creators of Second Life) keeps strong control over the community because the contract does not authorize any sale of the user account without Linden Lab authorization. This prohibition serves to reinforce network externalities by establishing a lock-in effect. Users are contractually forbidden from reducing their switching costs through the sale of their account (Castronova 2003). Even where EULAs or TOS are suboptimal, users will only leave a virtual world if the competing offers are clearly superior (Mayer-Schönberger and Crowley 2006). However, the lock-in effect seems to be reduced in the case of Second Life, given that users may sell their characters and items. Another provision in this contract nevertheless contributes to a reinforcement of this effect. Even if users have ownership of characters and items they have created, the level of control Linden Lab does have over codes is high. The Second Life TOS specifies that Linden Lab retains ownership of related data and that users do not own any data stored by Linden Lab on its servers, including any data representing or embodying any of their content. This means that, apart from a few exceptions, content created by a Second Life resident cannot leave that virtual world and cannot be resettled in another virtual world.

## Fragile Contracts

The analysis of the contracts in the sample shows how game companies seek to use the contract as a tool to reinforce their control over the virtual world, specifically over copyright, codes, creativity, and community. Contracts can be a useful and effective tool by which to structure a company's legal relations—especially between itself and its customers—provided that it offers a sufficient level of legal security. The problem is that the EULAs and TOS analyzed present more legal risk for the game companies than they provide security. Their allocation of property rights is highly contestable and they are designed in a way that makes them difficult to understand and disproportionate in terms of the reciprocal rights and obligations of the parties involved (game companies/users). The two major sources of legal insecurity, game companies' allocation of property rights and the unconscionable character of EULAs and TOS, are discussed next.

## Allocation of Property Rights

Except for the Second Life TOS, none of the contracts recognize user copyright or property rights over game content, account, personal avatar, or virtual items. Some game companies invoke the copyright regime to deny users all property rights. EULAs are almost always more pro-seller than the default rules of U.S. Copyright Code (Marotta-Wurgler 2007), but copyright cannot be used to legally protect all types of information or to prevent users from selling their virtual property (Stephens 2002; Garlick 2005). None of the contracts automatically authorizes derivative works produced by users. But provisions prohibiting distribution of derivative works by users are not necessarily excluded from the doctrine of fair use (Baldrica 2007). Moreover, some contracts deny users any property rights, even without an explicit legal basis for doing so. Finally, others do not contain any provision related to user property rights over the account.

The question of recognizing property rights in the virtual world is highly controversial in the field of academic research and has given rise to certain judicial or regulatory decisions that go against the contract provisions studied for this paper.

The highly controversial issue of property rights in virtual worlds. This controversy manifests itself in many ways, giving rise to diverse responses from legal scholars. Should real property rights be applied to cyber goods? Should a body of specific laws be created for cyber property? Should property rights over virtual items be allocated to game companies or users? Should an extensive application of the copyright system be developed? Table 4 summarizes the different viewpoints.

In the United States, legal scholars are divided as to how to manage the question of property rights in the virtual world. In Europe, the question has been subject to little debate for the moment. As discussed below, court decisions are beginning to emerge in other regions such as Asia, where certain countries have adopted specific provisions. These developments show that a denial of all property rights for users at the level of contract goes against the changes we are seeing at an international level.

The international context in terms of regulation and precedent may endanger EULAs and TOS. While there is only one known example of governments and legislators in Europe and in the United States paying attention to the question of property rights, there is a very clear trend in Asia toward favoring the allocation of property rights to users. $^{8}$

In China between 2003 and 2006, several courts (Chaoyang District Court, Guangzhou District Court, Beijing Court, and Ningbo Haishu District Court) made very similar rulings, stating that virtual goods are valuable and equal to tangible goods, that they are controllable despite their intangible nature, and that virtual items must be qualified as property. The Chaoyang Court concluded that the service provider should be responsible for users' property in the game. The first act came in December 2003, when a group of lawyers signed a proposal to the Law Committee of the National People's Congress, arguing that virtual property does have value and should be regarded as having the attributes of normal commodities, and should therefore be protected by law or by regulations. In June 2009, the Chinese Government declared that virtual currency cannot be traded for real goods or services but that this does not prohibit the sale or purchase of virtual items for real money. No further details were provided about the allocation of property rights. In South Korea, the situation remains ambiguous. Although the law does not clearly recognize users' property rights over virtual items, it does consider these items to hold value independently of the game company. In Taiwan, the Taiwanese Code recognizes that virtual property qualifies as electromagnetic records and should be considered moveable property in case of fraud or theft. Here, the right to control virtual property is acknowledged at the level of code and is granted to the owner of the code. In February 2009, the Minister for Justice in Vietnam argued in favor of legislating on the legal status of virtual goods, although it was not said whether property rights would be attributed to users.

By using a contract to retain all property rights, or by ignoring the issue altogether, game companies run the risk of weakening all of the contract provisions that oblige users to perform (or avoid) certain actions on the basis that they have no property rights over the virtual assets. The current trend in certain countries confirms this risk.

## The Unconscionable Character of EULAs and TOS

Whether in the United States, Europe, or Asia, the judicial authorities have implemented a system of sanctions for cases in which contracts signed between a consumer and a company are excessively disproportionate in terms of the distribution of rights and obligations. The EULAs and TOS analyzed for this paper present characteristics that make them clearly susceptible to such sanctions.

Contracts of adhesion, unconscionability, and abusive provisions: A considerable literature has focused on the enforceability of click-wrap/shrink-wrap agreements and contracts of adhesion (Conklin 2008; Harrison 1998; Hillman and Rachlinski 2002; Oakley 2005; Reuveni 2007). In the United States, a contract is considered as a contract of adhesion if several criteria are met: the contract appears on a preprinted form included in the installation materials; it is worded in a generalized fashion; it reflects the interests of the party with superior bargaining power; it is proposed on a “take it or leave it” basis; it concerns mass market environments (Fritzsche 2007). Such contracts have their proponents, who primarily advance the argument that individual negotiation of contracts is impossible on a large scale and contracts of adhesion allow standardization on a mass-market scale and provide users with information and a greater number of rights—at the lowest possible price—than if these users had relied solely on intellectual property rights (Gomulkiewicz and Williamson 1996). But even if contracts of adhesion can be enforced, this does not mean they systematically will be. The doctrine of unconscionability can be used to render certain contract clauses inapplicable. Unconscionability has two components: procedural and substantive. The procedural component is present as soon as one party has no bargaining power or meaningful choice. The substantive component comes from the terms of a contract, and is based on the fairness and obligations of the contract’s terms. Substantive unconscionability can, therefore, result from the particularly one-sided nature of a contract that creates an overall imbalance in the obligations and rights imposed by the bargain (Barnes 2005; Epstein 1975). In many civil law countries, and particularly in France, the “consumer code” empowers judges to examine whether a contract contains abusive clauses (i.e., those whose intention or effect is to create a significant imbalance, to the detriment of the consumer, between the rights and obligations of the different parties). Some Asian countries have similar provisions. In South Korea, consumer protection authorities can impose sanctions if a contract contains abusive terms of service.

EULAs and TOS for virtual worlds: An elevated risk of sanction by the courts. Most game companies seem unwilling to use EULAs and TOS as an effective instrument to inform users (some of which only provide patchy information, while others are at best unclear; contracts are not always easily accessible on the web site of the virtual world or game company). Aside from the Second Life TOS, all of the contracts in the sample are highly imbalanced in terms of the rights allocated to game companies and users respectively. The fact that it is the vendors who draw up the licensing contracts and use them to their advantage—to the detriment of users—thereby introducing considerable asymmetry, have been argued in the past (Jankowich 2006; Kayser 2006; Lemley 1995; Reuveni 2007; Sheldon 2007). Paradoxically, in the United States, Linden Lab is for the moment the only game company that has been taken to court for unconscionability, which occurred in 2007. The game company had frozen the account of one user and all of the assets he controlled because he had benefited from an exploit in the virtual world. The user then decided to take action against Linden Lab, which tried to avoid trial by arguing that the contract contained an arbitration clause. The court deemed the clause to be unconscionable on the basis that Linden Lab retained all powers, notably the ability to suspend or close accounts at its sole discretion. As Fairfield (2009) points out, by basing its judgment on the fact that Linden Lab had retained excessive control over the virtual world, when compared to its users, the court disabled the game company’s “contractual litigation shield.” In light of the risk that this dispute would go before a federal court, Linden Lab opted to settle, and the user’s account was reactivated.

Given that the analysis of the EULAs and TOS from 20 virtual worlds shows the Second Life contract to be less disproportionate than the others in terms of distributing rights between the game company and its users, it can be concluded that a similar dispute in relation to the other virtual worlds would not benefit the game companies. In 2001, a class action was taken against Sony, which wanted to end Everquest's commoditization by enforcing the EULA provisions, as users felt they should be allowed free usage of virtual items, given that the time and labor necessary to procure these items came from them. However, the action was later stopped for unknown reasons (Garlick 2005, p. 429). In 2002, BlackSnow Interactive sued Mythic Entertainment, claiming that the EULA that prohibited the sale of virtual items for real-world money was an anticompetitive mechanism, and it threatened to sue the operator of Anarchy Online in order to retrieve accounts that the operator had frozen for EULA violations. These actions were also abandoned because BlackSnow's entire operation disappeared without a trace, leaving behind the latest bills from their law firm.

In 2004, a group of South Korean consumers called on the Korean Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) to declare abusive certain clauses from the EULAs used by the game company NC Soft. In 2005, the KFTC ruled that numerous clauses should be deleted from the contract, in particular those that allowed the game company to permanently suspend or seize accounts of players caught engaging in real-money trade for the first time; refuse compensation for game time losses of up to four hours due to service outages, even where the company was at fault; suspend players' accounts for game-planning or operational needs; or terminate contracts without prior notice for trivial breaches by players.

As we have just seen, the legal environment is far from favorable toward the EULAs and TOS used by game companies. The low number of disputes seen so far might cause surprise, given the fragility of the EULAs and TOS, the tens of thousands of accounts closed by game companies each year for one reason or another, and the number of dissatisfied users who are expressing themselves on numerous Internet forums. Two explanations can be offered for this: first, a user, working alone, might feel that legal action would be too costly, and second, the community of users is too heterogeneous to organize itself as a group in order to enforce its rights (Kayser 2006).

## Some Recommendations to Improve the 5Cs Model

Without a contract, virtual worlds could be pure chaos (Castronova 2004). As explained earlier, contracts should make it possible to legally structure the relationship between game companies and users as well as the way in which a company manages the user community, the creativity of its personnel and of users, the copyright, and the computer codes that allow users to penetrate the virtual world. In their current state, EULAs and TOS have failed to create a legal framework upon which game companies can depend sustainably. Figure 2 shows the four conditions that would make EULAs and TOS less fragile (to allocate property rights to the users; to make the contract more conscionable and readable; to align the contract with the business model; to align the contract with the gaming model) and improve the 5Cs model.

This study suggests that the contract can only constitute an instrument by which game companies could provide an effective legal structure to organize their control over the 4Cs if it is enforceable and aligned with both business and gaming models.

## More Enforceable Contract Design

As shown in the previous section, EULAs and TOS suffer from a dual source of legal insecurity: property rights allocation and an unconscionable character. Even if game companies were to use the contract to commit certain acts against certain users (suspension or closure of accounts, denial of access to certain virtual items, etc.), the contract would still not be enforceable before the courts, who could order the reinstatement of accounts or items, the payment of damages to users, and even the deletion of abusive clauses from a contract.

There is a need to recognize users' property rights over virtual items. Maintaining the status quo in terms of property rights allocation does not appear to be a viable solution for game companies, given current regulatory and judicial trends in various countries. To predict that in the worst case scenario a judge will order the reinstatement of a user's account or virtual items would also be an error of judgment on the part of the game companies. Courts may also order compensation to be paid to a user, and this might exceed the real economic value (based on official or parallel transaction platforms) of that which was denied the user in the first place. While certain virtual worlds have millions of users, and even though the functioning economy (whether desired or not by game companies) that has developed around virtual items is experiencing rapid growth, the risk of a class action by dissatisfied users cannot be ruled out, especially as a majority of these users feel they have property rights. One study shows that 78 percent of Korean users who responded to a survey felt they should have property rights over the virtual items they had accumulated in the virtual world and thus take advantage of these assets as they saw fit (McInnes et al. 2004). As for the option of differentiating property rights allocation according to each country and their legal environment, this does not appear to be appropriate as it is a source of incoherency.

As a result, as demonstrated by Fairfield (2005) and Blazer (2007), the common regime of property should be applied to ensure recognition of property rights for users. Fairfield

indeed advocates property rights recognition at the level of code for virtual property, which Blazer defines as “persistent computer code stored on a remote source system, where one or more persons are granted certain powers to control the computer code, to the exclusion of all other people” (p. 141). Fairfield advances that virtual property shares three legally relevant characteristics with real-world property: persistence, interconnectivity, and “rivalrousness.” Avatars, virtual items, and users accounts are persistent and interconnected, and virtual items can in practice be traded on official or secondary markets. Concerning the criterion of rivalrousness, Fairfield explains that “rivalrousness, in the physical world, lets the owner exclude other people from using owned objects” (p. 1053).⁹ This means that URLs and e-mail accounts are rivalrous. Similarly, virtual world user accounts and avatars are rivalrous: they can only be used by one person. There is more room for debate concerning the items and accessories that avatars can accumulate, as a greater or lesser number of users can use given items, making this virtual property potentially semi-rivalrous. However, the more a virtual item has game value—and therefore in principle economic value—the more it will be rivalrous. The recognition of property rights over virtual items does not conflict with the intellectual property rights held by game companies over artistic creativity and software programs. Such rights are complementary. Ownership of a book is not ownership of the intellectual property of the content written by the author, and owners of virtual property have the same rights as the owner of a book: they cannot reproduce or distribute its content. Fairfield's assertion is supported by the work of De Soto (2000), according to which property rights are to be applied to any asset that is characterized by its latent economic and social qualities.

Contrary to what game companies sometimes contend, the recognition of users' property rights does not result in an increased risk that they will incur greater responsibility. As demonstrated by Fairfield (2009), game companies are fully responsible for any harm they cause to users, whether property rights have been accorded or not. Furthermore, such recognition does not automatically grant users absolute freedom in how they use these rights. A contract can prohibit the transfer of virtual items and accounts, as is already the case with plane tickets and theater seats, for example. It can also set limits on these rights, allowing game companies to preserve balanced virtual worlds and have sufficient latitude to modify the terms of their services and develop the characteristics of the virtual worlds when necessary (Bartle 2003; Castronova 2004; Vacca 2008; White 2008).

There is a need to make contracts conscionable and readable. EULAs and TOS, as analyzed for this study, present a flawed method of governance for virtual worlds (Gilbert 2009). They constitute a sort of trap in which users find themselves because, as highlighted by Kunze (2008, p. 110), "in the market of virtual realms, the user is helpless to shop around for better contract terms as long as all competitors are using similar clauses in their EULAs" (although Table 2 reveals the need to relativize this statement). In order to reinforce the legal solidity of these agreements and the real impact they have on users, game companies must take certain measures. First of all, they should give more transparency to EULAs and TOS, which should be clearly and easily available on game companies' websites. Secondly, and even though users will always have less bargaining power than the game companies, the latter should reduce substantive unconscionability by making their contracts less one-sided. The recognition of users' property rights over virtual items they control should help contribute to this. Furthermore, game companies should modify those clauses that allow them to end the agreement, close user accounts, or delete content (including user-produced content) at any time and for any reason, and provide for legitimate cases within the contract, as some game companies have already begun to do.

One recommendation is that EULAs and TOS be managed in a less administrative and legalistic way and instead be used to explain clearly to the community the values and respective rights around which it should be built. By making their contracts less obviously imbalanced and more comprehensible, game companies will create sound legal instruments that can provide them with sustainable and loyal control over access to their virtual worlds, and thereby reduce the risk of governments taking over the regulation of this domain.

Recommendation 1: Game companies should modify their EULAs and TOS by recognizing users' property rights over virtual items and by making these agreements less imbalanced and more comprehensible.

## Aligning Contract Provisions with Business and Gaming Models

As explained in the introduction, and as shown by Table 1, virtual worlds can be based on different business and gaming models. It is imperative that EULAs and TOS be in line with the model chosen by the game company and also have a design that makes them enforceable.

Aligning contract provisions with the business model. Two dominant business models currently coexist, with a third very much in the minority. The vast majority of game companies choose from between two models: pay-to-play and free-to-play (see Table 1). The first is used in heavily scripted virtual worlds, where users are above all required to develop “crafting skills” rather than any form of creativity that might result in a modification of the virtual world. According to Ondrejka (2006), when a user “only” advances a character in the virtual world by using the content created by the game company, he/she is “crafting” and not creating. As shown in Figure 2, where user creativity is limited to crafting in a heavily scripted virtual world, no copyright is attributed to users, except for derivative works such as “machinimas,” which are user creations made from avatars designed within a virtual world and which consist of video animations that transcend the virtual world in question. $^{10}$ These machinimas should be distinguished from screen shots, which involve a screen capture of a particular action at a given moment within the virtual world. They can be static or dynamic (Baldrica 2007). Contracts must be clear on this point, and game companies must not allow any confusion between the fact that they hold the copyright over the artistic content of the virtual world and the software program and the fact that the user holds property rights over virtual items. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 2, the more a virtual world is scripted and a user’s room for maneuver in terms of creativity is limited, the more a game company must use strict computer codes that will only authorize users to perform actions provided for within the script of the virtual world in question. As Lessig has written, “As the world is now, codewriters are increasingly lawmakers” (1999, p.60). Roche and Lester (in Mennecke et al. 2008) remind us that virtual worlds are 100 percent engineered environments and that nothing is random. Codes remain the most efficient solution to authorize (or not) user-created content. Meanwhile, the question of whether the pay-to-play business model, which corresponds to heavily scripted virtual worlds, should use a contract that allows the sale of virtual items is determined above all by the choice of gaming model, as will be explained later.

Table 1 shows that with just three exceptions—mainly from MMORPGs, whose game companies are seeking to stimulate their appeal—the free-to-play model has been adopted by MMOGs in which scripting is nonexistent and content is based either on cocreation by the game company and users or on exclusively user-based creativity. The contract must, therefore, encourage user creativity and be perfectly clear with regard to copyright. In a virtual world such as Sims Online, control over the story and the way in which the virtual world operates can be shared. As indicated by Will Wright in an interview with Pearce (2002), collaboration between users and the game designer is an important condition for the development of this virtual world. The contract must, therefore, organize coauthoring in the interests both of the users and the game company. If the development of the virtual world is exclusively (or almost entirely) based on user creativity, then the copyright should be held by these same users, as is the case in Second Life. This does not prevent game companies from making provisions in the contract that would accord them a license over this copyright in order to ensure the development and stability of their virtual world. As shown in Figure 2, the more a virtual world relies on user creativity rather than that of the game company, the more the copyright should go to the users and the more the codes will be open (i.e., allow for user creativity). Nonetheless, it should be recognized that the boundaries between crafting, cocreation that is shared by users and the game company, and user-created content are not easy to set—although these are the components that determine the allocation of copyright.

It appears logical that game companies whose MMOGs are based on the free-to-play model and whose users can create original content or purchase virtual land or other items to help their avatar prosper should authorize transactions on virtual items in the real economy. Where it is the users who create value within a virtual world, they should be allowed to profit from it. By combining the data from Table 1 and Figure 1, we can see that this is the case in all the MMOG contracts, except for Meez. $^{[11]}$ As for MMORPGs based on the free-to-play model but where users can purchase virtual items with real money from an items mall, the combination of Table 1 and Figure 1 indicates that the contract authorizes the sale of virtual items. This highlights the alignment between the business model and contract provisions chosen by the game company. However, it is regrettable that this alignment should be undermined by contracts that are difficult to enforce (see above). For this model not to be a failure, it is essential for game companies to offer their users a secure trading platform and rigorous methods for the sale of items (Oh and Ryu 2007) so as to discourage them from completing their transactions on external platforms, thereby diminishing the company's level of control.

Recommendation 2: Contract provisions should be aligned with the game company's chosen business model. In the free-to-play model based on user creativity or co-creativity (users/game company), copyright should be totally or partially attributed to users, who should be allowed to trade the virtual items they own for real money.

Aligning Contract Provisions with the Gaming Model. As we have just seen, virtual worlds based on the free-to-play model have one thing in common: users can sell virtual items they own on the real market. Table 1 shows that in the case of virtual worlds based on the pay-to-play model, some game companies have opted to authorize real economic transactions on virtual items, while others prohibit such transactions. This choice can radically alter the spirit of gaming. In the first scenario, a user can acquire a powerful avatar despite being a newcomer in a given virtual world; in the second, only gaming skills can in principle allow users to develop such an avatar. Game companies that opt for prohibition must formalize this choice in their contract, avoiding any legal considerations that might be widely contested. To argue for prohibition on the basis that users have no property rights offers companies no legal security. Nor is it judicious to invoke the copyright regime, as this cannot prevent users from selling their virtual assets (Garlick 2005; Marotta-Wurgler 2007; Stephens 2002). Justifying such a decision in the EULA or TOS on the basis that virtual items have no real-world value for users does not make it so (McInnes 2004). The best justification remains that such trading runs contrary to the spirit of gaming, and game companies should explain in the rules of conduct for their virtual world (to which the contract applies) that this is the reason why a limit is placed on the use of property rights over virtual assets controlled by users. The more users have a strong sense of belonging to a community that shares similar values (see the example of Guanxi in China: Lindtner et al. 2008), the more game companies will be able to provide themselves with an effective way to eliminate unacceptable behavior. In other words, the community should be globally hostile to a logic based on the real-money trade of virtual assets. This is particularly true when users cannot advance in a virtual world if they do not belong to a clan or a guild. Game companies can even rely on this kind of community to combat the practice of “gold farming.” Most MMORPGs have seen a rise in gold farming, which consists of playing for hours and even days at a time; once virtual items have been accumulated they are then offered to any users who might wish to procure them (Heeks 2008). Professional gold farming now represents a veritable and prosperous industry, often relying on cheap labor, mostly from China (Dibbell 2006). The new generation of “farmers” is made up of artificial agents (game bots) whose objective is sometimes to commit misdeeds in virtual worlds. These programs emulate certain user actions in the virtual world and are used to carry out repetitive tasks in the absence of users (the infamous Glider bot used in World of Warcraft is an example; see Chintapalli 2008). In January 2008, it was a user who, essentially on behalf of all WOW users, sued IGE on the grounds that by farming gold and spamming chat, it was diminishing the WOW experience. In August 2008, the user and IGE U.S. settled, but another company to which IGE U.S. sold its assets continues to farm gold.

The effectiveness of contractual clauses that allow game companies to prohibit real-money trade and close the accounts of users who infringe this restriction is, however, very limited, as revealed by the innumerable transactions being completed on certain web sites. There is a gap between what it is technically possible to do with virtual items and what is authorized by EULAs or TOS (Ruch 2009). The impact of community-wide hostility to this practice is far from negligible but may not prove sufficient. Game companies must, therefore, endeavor to develop computer codes that make real-money trade more difficult. Such codes could, for example, forbid separating virtual items from the avatar that owns them or transferring items from one account to another. It would then remain to be seen how one could prevent the transfer of accounts from one user to another. Some technologies have been developed to prevent bots from intervening in virtual worlds (Ahmad et al. 2009; Golle and Ducheneaut 2005) or to fight against the infamous CopyBot in Second Life, which allows users to make copies of virtual objects, including objects for sale. As for game companies that use the pay-to-play model and authorize real-money trade, they must rely on powerful computer codes and authentication procedures to ensure that transactions are secure and to prevent identity theft (Chen et al. 2004). The company's capacity to develop highly secure codes is also a way for them to avoid liability in certain circumstances (for example, in September 2009, Eros LLC took action against Linden Lab for copyright infringement and negligent interference with economic relations after designs for virtual goods had been copied with a bot program).

However, as pointed out by Hunt (2007), while code is law, this law can be circumvented. Another way for game companies to make up for their contract's lack of effectiveness in prohibiting real-money trade on virtual assets is to ensure a high level of creativity in the design of the virtual world. If this world is sufficiently creative, users will not feel the need to use game bots in order to circumvent repetitive and boring actions. If the game company does not want virtual items to acquire a real economic value, and prefers to prevent users from trading virtual items for real money, it can create a system of planned obsolescence (Grimmelmann 2006).

Recommendation 3: Contract provisions should be aligned with the gaming model. If a game company wants to avoid real-money trade, then its contract—even if it satisfies the conditions outlined in Recommendation 1—is not sufficient to discourage the prohibited behavior. Game companies should be able to depend on a community of users that is globally hostile to this behavior, codes that make trading difficult, and planned obsolescence that would eliminate the lasting value of virtual items.

## Concluding Remarks

Game companies aim to develop and control virtual worlds with four basic components—copyright, code, creativity, and community—as well as a complementary component: the contract. The analysis of the EULAs and TOS from 20 virtual worlds shows that the contract generally fails to guarantee game companies that the control they exercise over the 4Cs will be reinforced. The contracts examined for this study are based on the refusal to recognize users' property rights, even though the global trend runs in the opposite direction. What is more, their design makes them unconscionable or likely to contain abusive clauses, thereby making them unenforceable before a trial judge. With the virtual industry today worth billions of dollars and with an ever-growing number of users joining virtual world communities, the risk of legal action being taken by a group of dissatisfied users is on the rise. It is, therefore, essential for game companies to modify their EULAs or TOS in order make them more enforceable and comprehensible to the “reasonable man.” Future research might usefully seek to determine how game companies could develop a contract model with a unifying framework despite variations in the legal environment from one country to the next. It would also be interesting, both from a pedagogic and practical standpoint as well as for research purposes, to create a comparative framework by which to trace the evolution of EULAs, as suggested by Kunze (2008) and as already carried out in the case of EULA Wiki.

A contract should be aligned with the business and gaming models chosen. Depending on the business model, the level of creativity accorded to users via codes will vary, as will the question of whether or not they receive copyright. Future studies might investigate the boundaries that separate crafting from cocreation, and cocreation from user-created content, as it is these boundaries that determine the attribution of copyright. This issue appears all the more promising as current experimentation (such as between Linden Lab and IBM) seeks to develop interoperability between virtual worlds. Where real-money trade does not conform to the chosen gaming model, we have seen that the contract is insufficient to discourage such behavior. It is via codes and planned obsolescence that the solution is to be found as the contract only serves to provide a legal basis for the exclusion of users who have demonstrably engaged in these transactions. Research conducted on the level of control and security provided by computer codes should continue as should that which focuses on the way in which game design can encourage or discourage demand for virtual items (following on from studies by Hamari and Lehdonvirta, 2010). The adaptation of codes to the chosen business model and the adaptation of game design to the desired level of demand for virtual items are two key issues, whether for business, research, or virtual worlds. Indeed, they constitute a prerequisite for the future evolution of EULAs and TOS, which must be aligned with business and gaming models.
