## Abstract

Issues of power are inseparable from any organizational transformation associated with digital technologies. Still, the perception of power is typically one of overcoming resistance, involving restricted views such as unilateral domination and coercion. This precludes developing more nuanced accounts of entanglements between power and organizational transformation in the digital age. In this paper, we propose an alternative perspective on power, drawing on a novel, postmodern reading of Foucault that focuses not on traditional Foucauldian notions of disciplinary power, domination, or emancipation but on dialogue. We demonstrate the significance of this perspective through a “theory elaboration” study relying on a case of clinical transformation in a hospital setting driven by digital technology. Our findings illustrate how reactions to transformation initiatives can often be productive and even inventive exercises of power in their own right. Accordingly, through advancing a dialogical model of power, we argue that accounting for organizational transformation in the digital age demands a careful attention to four aspects: (1) power effects that emanate from incumbent discourses, (2) reversible relationships between power and resistance, (3) interdiscursive dynamics (instead of discrete, isolated discourses), and (4) the materiality and agency of information technology as a force in the power dynamics inherent to organizational transformation.

Keywords: Power, Dialogue, Discourse, Transformation, Interdiscursive Dynamics, Michel Foucault

## 1 Introduction

Power is often assumed to be unilateral in IS research. This goes hand in hand with the tradition that scholars in the IS field and beyond, by and large, tend to associate power with domination and the exercise of authority and control (Jasperson et al., 2002; Clegg et al., 2006; Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Fleming & Spicer, 2014). Particularly concerning organizational transformation associated with digital technologies, power has been predominantly (and often unwittingly) treated as a monologue, operating only in one direction. Occasions of effectuating change through digital innovations are still mostly portrayed as scenes of conflict between principals (i.e., “change makers”), who constantly remain in the position of proactive demanding, and other parties (mainly, “change recipients”), who perpetually stay in the position of reactive responding—whether resisting, obeying, or showing any other reaction in between.<sup>1</sup> Resistance, in particular, is usually framed as being in “diametrical” opposition to power (i.e., as the antithesis of power), often with a negative connotation— since it is generally considered a “barrier to” transformation and a refusal to accept the well-intended, strategically significant digital initiatives spearheaded by the leadership (Fitzgerald et al., 2014; Vial, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). Most importantly, resistance is widely viewed as an action rooted in no form of authentic discourse and with no particular productivity or ingenuity in its own right (the few exceptions include Hekkala et al., 2014; Hirschheim & Newman, 1988; Markus, 1983).

The perspective that dialogue emerges only when power is absent has long been questioned, as too has the position that power obstructs dialogue (Guilfoyle, 2003; Hermans, 2001; Sampson, 1993). The unilateral approach to power in IS research is thus becoming increasingly unsustainable in a world driven mainly by technologically infused dynamism that exhibits much less determinism and more multilateralism. This paper presents a dialogical understanding of power and contrasts it with the prevailing approach in the IS field, as identified above. In framing our alternative model of power, we draw from a specific, postmodern reading of Foucault where the key notion, in contrast to most Foucauldian studies of power in the IS field and beyond, is not disciplinary power, domination, or emancipation but dialogue. With the dialogical reading of Foucault, human beings are considered “active” ensembles of corporeal forces— embodying and enacting rival discourses or narratives— that engage in continuous reciprocation and dialogue leading to the perennial transformation of social orderings, including those manifesting closure/domination (Falzon, 1998).

The present work will, therefore, address the following question: “What theoretical insights can be derived from the dialogical reading of Foucault in relation to organizational transformation <sup>2</sup> in the digital age?” To empirically accomplish the latter, we employ the dialogical lens of power to analyze the case of a hospital setting undergoing transformation by implementing an ambulatory electronic health record (AEHR) system. The case analysis builds on a preexisting conceptual model— i.e., the “Foucauldian-based process model” of Leclercq-Vandelannoitte (2011). Our case analysis and theory elaboration efforts show that, from the power-relations point of view, dynamics that constitute transformation associated with digital technologies tend to be less about mere oppositions between sovereign power and individual acts of resistance as the mainstream approach to power suggests, and more a matter of evolving reciprocations among rival corporeal forces of discursive power—that is, an interdiscursive “dialogue of forces” (Falzon, 1998). Our theoretical account then creates the space to explore and discuss several theoretical and practical implications of this dialogical conception of power for the study and practice of transformation in the digital age.

## 2 Power and Transformation in the Digital Age

## 2.1 Some Background on Different Views of Power

Power is inherent to organizational and social life. Management and organizational studies scholarships have long been engaged with power from at least two distinct perspectives. On the one hand, episodic perspectives (covering the rational and pluralist views of power addressed by Jasperson et al., 2002) have been employed to deal with “the direct exercise of power … [relying] upon identifiable acts that shape the behavior of others” (Fleming & Spicer, 2014, p. 240). On the other hand, systemic positions have been leveraged to account for “power that is congealed into more enduring institutional structures … [which is] often less visible than overt and explicit acts of power” (Fleming & Spicer, 2014, p. 240). Episodic power may thus be recognized by the “faces” of coercion and manipulation (of processes/rules). In contrast, systemic power may be identified with the faces of domination (covering the interpretive and radical views of power addressed by Jasperson et al., 2002) and subjectification (Hardy & Leiba-O’Sullivan, 1998; Fleming & Spicer, 2014). Figure 1 seeks to illustrate these four faces of power.

Figure 1 demonstrates that power may operate at different levels, where institutionally deeper forms of influence encompass and engender surface mechanisms of power. This contention accords with the view expressed by Hardy and Leiba-O’Sullivan (1998, p. 460, emphasis original):

On the surface, power is exercised through the mobilization of scarce, critical resources, and through the control of decision-making processes. At a deeper level, power is exercised by managing the meanings that shape others’ lives. Deeper still, is the suggestion that power is embedded in the very fabric of the system; it constrains, how we see, what we see, and how we think…

The illustration in Figure 1 also implies that different views of power reside on a spectrum of sophistication, where accounting for the deepest macro-institutions associated with power (Leonardi & Barley, 2010) demands the most sophisticated view of power, which often concerns subjectification.

In general, the works of Michel Foucault can best represent the subjectification face of power (Hardy & Leiba-O’Sullivan, 1998; Fleming & Spicer, 2014). Nevertheless, this goes with the acknowledgment that Foucault (1966/1970, 1969/1972, 1963/1973, 1976, 1975/1977, 1976/1978, 1980, 1984/1985a, 1984/1985b) offers a body of thought with broad possibilities for interpretation and reflection. This specific trait of Foucault’s intellectual heritage has led to various readings and applications in the social sciences and management domains. For the purposes of the current paper, it is particularly relevant to briefly distinguish between two different readings of Foucault on power.<sup>3</sup>

A prevalent reading of Foucault in the IS literature tends to associate him with “critical” theory/thinking (Howcroft & Trauth, 2005; Myers & Klein, 2011). Critical research, in short, seeks to show how sovereign power operates behind the scenes to sustain the status quo (e.g., through the design and implementation of digital technologies) and how the subjugated must gain critical knowledge/awareness to challenge the status quo and to resist domination, with the goal of eventual emancipation from regimes of power (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2005; Hirschheim & Klein, 1994; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Stahl et al., 2011). Regarding Foucault as a figure in critical theory may be attributed to the fact that there is a focus on domination in Foucault’s middle-period works, such as Discipline and Punish (Falzon, 2010). This also appears to have led to efforts (e.g., Brooke, 2002a, 2002b) to advance

Foucault as an alternative to Habermas, widely recognized for his critical modernist stance (Cooper & Burrell, 1988).

An alternative reading of Foucault is essentially postmodernist (i.e., postmodernism as fragmentation<sup>4</sup>) and specifically concerns the subjectification face of power. With subjectification, the focus is not on transient conflicts over resources or decision-making (or nondecision-making) processes. Indeed, conflict is no longer considered a precondition for the operation of power (Hardy & Leiba-O’Sullivan, 1998; Fleming & Spicer, 2007). This reading of Foucault emphasizes that “a Foucauldian view of power differs from critical theory” (Hardy & Leiba-O’Sullivan, 1998, p. 459, emphasis original), relying on the postmodernist perspective maintaining that “in the place of the ‘individual,’ with a stable constellation of essential characteristics, stands the subject, a socially constituted category of analysis”— who is “embedded in the prevailing power relations and discourses” (Hardy & Leiba-O’Sullivan, 1998, p. 460). Furthermore, this reading of Foucault repudiates the idea that knowledge may lead to emancipation from regimes of power—for knowledge can never be separated from power (Mumby, 1992). Consequently, “freeing individuals from power relations”—which is a central theme in the tradition of critical theory (see Howcroft & Trauth, 2005, p. 3)—is not only infeasible due to the entwinement of power and knowledge but also of no inherent value because relations of power are not intrinsically evil (Willcocks, 2006).

## 2.2 Another Alternative View of Power: A Third Reading of Foucault

The postmodernist reading of Foucault outlined above, notwithstanding its significance (i.e., contesting the concept of sovereign power and advancing a “positive” account of power), has faced criticism that it ignores “a pervasive dialogical strand in [Foucault’s] thinking” (Healy, 2005, p. 84). This is where a third reading of Foucault has emerged in the literature (e.g., Coles, 1992; Falzon, 1998, 2010; Healy, 2005; Ingram, 1994; Thompson, 1999), grounded in the understanding that we are “from the start involved in a social dialogue of corporeal forces, the interplay of forces affecting and transforming one another” (Falzon, 1998, p. 38). We call this the dialogical reading of Foucault.

While the conventional postmodernist reading of Foucault indicates that Foucault’s “work illuminates the limitations of resistance” to enact alternative discourses against the prevailing discourse (Hardy & Leiba-O’Sullivan, 1998, pp. 459-460) or even criticizes him for downplaying the active subject by locating resistance within power itself and restricting agency (Willcocks, 2004), the dialogical reading appears to offer a more nuanced and dynamic interpretation of Foucault’s thinking “in which the interplay of power and resistance makes it possible to account for the emergence and transformation of social forms” (Falzon, 2010, p. 229, emphasis added). The dialogical reading thus entails what we may call an active variant of the subjectification view. In other words, here, the postmodernist subjectification view of power is maintained differently. First, postmodernism is interpreted through a dialogical lens (as opposed to the fragmentation lens), which recognizes that, in a plural society/world, different parties, living diverse local narratives, can and do influence one another and this does not necessarily distort or falsify the self-contained purity of any of those alternative forms of life (Falzon, 1998). Second, the perspective on power is that while we cannot “escape from power relations, and are inevitably influenced by our circumstances … to be in a power relation means that there is always the possibility of resisting and changing our situation” (Falzon, 2010, p. 233), where “one subject’s superiority over the other can never be guaranteed [forever]” (Thompson, 1999, pp. 200-201). That is why no social ordering or domination can be eternal. There will always be resistance, revolt, struggle, and renewed dialogue and transformation, leading to new forms of order and organization (Falzon, 1998; Healy, 2005). This view thus emphasizes that there is no opposition, for Foucault (1982, 1984b, 1984/1985a), between a conception of history that makes us “effects” and a vision of human freedom that makes us “actors”—that is, between the status of “historical objects” and the status of “subjects of history” (Revel, 2015).

On the one hand, in contrast to the critical modernist reading of Foucault, the dialogical reading does not consider freedom an ideal state to be attained once and for all. On the other hand, in contrast to the passive subjectification view, the dialogical reading does not negate the feasibility of freedom. Freedom constitutes a permanent project and an ongoing task where subjects continuously encounter and challenge various forms of totalization by engaging in strategic reciprocation and dialogue with the “other” forces (Falzon, 1998; Foucault, 1984a). The subject, thus, is shaped not just by being subjected to the prevailing regimes of power-knowledge (as argued under the passive subjectification view) but also by resisting those regimes.

Although not explicitly acknowledged, the dialogical reading of Foucault appears to have implicitly driven some influential studies of power in the management and organizational studies scholarships (e.g., Ezzamel & Willmott, 2010; Hardy & Thomas, 2014; Thomas & Davies, 2005). The key idea most of these studies promote is that power and resistance are mutually implicated—i.e., power always implies resistance, and acts of resistance (even the most passive ones) often involve an exercise of power with their own potential to bring about change (Hardy & Thomas, 2014; Kelly, 2009). In particular, Leclercq-Vandelannoitte (2011) shows strong sympathy for and interest in delineating the dialogical theme in Foucault’s thinking while bringing together all the crucial aspects of Foucault’s intellectual oeuvre in a single conceptual framework (as shown in Figure 2).

The model in Figure 2 highlights processes (see Table 1) that manage the dynamic relationships at the intersection of the symbolic and material worlds (cf. Ashcraft et al., 2009). It also enables one to think of organizations in the context of dynamically situated conditions of possibility and constraints (cf. Knights and Murray, 1994) constituted in discursive practices and embodied in material resources. Power relations are seen as the motor of organizational life (Deetz, 2003), such that “political activity” (through power relations) is the focal process through which organizations are constituted, stabilized, and transformed. For Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, “power-knowledge” provides a device to grasp the links between Foucault’s various conceptual entities. Overall, the model highlights “the dynamic political processes that combine material and symbolic elements into the constitution of organizations” (Waddington, 2012, p.104) and brings the relationships among technology, power, knowledge, discourse, discipline, and subject into sharper focus (Waddington, 2012). In the following sections, we take this model as the point of departure for our “theory elaboration” effort. Our goal will be to investigate the analytical similarities and differences that the lens provided by Leclercq-Vandelannoitte’s model may generate in our case study. This, in turn, offers the opportunity to revise and extend Leclercq-Vandelannoitte’s model to develop a dialogical view of power in an IS context.

## 2.3 Power and Transformation in IS Research

Historically, power issues are not unfamiliar or unaddressed in IS research, particularly in research on what is termed “IT-enabled organizational transformation” (Vial, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). In general, IS studies show that digital technologies introduce vast uncertainty in organizations and, by doing so, affect power relations and the positioning of organizational actors (Markus, 1983; Pfeffer, 1981; Silva & Fulk, 2012; Tushman & Anderson, 1986). Power issues appear at all stages of implementing digital innovations (Dhillon et al., 2011; Silva & Backhouse, 2003). Information systems are even presented as mirrors of the power relations that dominate the organization (Silva & Backhouse, 2003) or as opportunities to trigger the evolution of organizational structures and their respective power arrangements (Burkhardt & Brass, 1990). Additionally, there are IS studies that aim to investigate power issues associated with the transformative role of digital technologies in specific contexts and subject areas, such as social networks (Koch et al., 2013), health care (Doolin, 2004), and crowdfunding (Gleasure et al., 2019).

Among IS researchers who study power, certain theoretical lenses, such as Clegg’s circuits of power (Clegg, 1989), Lawrence et al.’s (2012) episodic versus systemic power, and the critical modernist Foucauldian approach to power (based on Foucault 1975/1977, 1980), have been widely adopted. Still, there are renewed calls arguing that some crucial aspects of power remain largely undertheorized or avoided in IS research (Jasperson et al., 2002; Leonardi & Barley, 2010; Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013; Marabelli & Galliers, 2017; Simeonova et al., 2020, 2022). These calls ask IS researchers to engage more deeply with issues of power, specifically regarding “the question of how technology and power relations are entwined” (Leonardi & Barley, 2010, p. 38; see also Koch et al., 2013; Simeonova et al., 2018), in order to move beyond the restricted, “power over” perspectives (Hardy & Thomas, 2014; Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013), where power is treated as a possessable commodity and a restraining/evil force (Ravishankar et al., 2013; Clegg et al., 2006).

The same restricted treatment of power is found in the more recent literature on the phenomenon called “digital transformation.” While the importance of critical reflection on this phenomenon has already been urged by such authors as Rowe (2018), few specific IS studies have directly investigated its power implications (let alone generate accounts based on progressive readings of Foucault or other prominent power thinkers). Indeed, several recent, often highly cited, articles that attempt to offer detailed conceptual accounts of digital transformation remain silent on power or address it only tangentially, even where they purport to draw on social theory perspectives (e.g., Hanelt et al., 2021; Hinings et al., 2018; Tana et al., 2023; Vial, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). Where power is addressed in the digital transformation literature, this is mainly through “oldschool” terms/notions such as resistance, inertia, domination, and control (e.g., Lanzolla et al., 2020; Vial, 2019; Wimelius et al., 2021).

The classic conceptions of power, however, would seem poorly suited to explaining contemporary transformations in organizations and societies, where the boundary between episodic and continuous change (Weick & Quinn, 1999) has become blurry, if not absent, and where, in a move toward malleable organizational designs, material and cognitive sources of inertia tend to erode or at least be mitigated (Hanelt et al., 2021; Besson & Rowe, 2012). Power-resistance relations seem to become fluidly reversible while unfolding endlessly. It is also increasingly perplexing, especially in complex digital projects/programs, “to say who ‘has’ power and who ‘lacks’ power” while “there is no clear-cut way of defining ‘whose power over whom’ is to be analysed” (Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013, p. 92). A reworked “analytics of power” (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2011) is thus indispensable to enable us to advance more constructive framings of power that transcend the conventional conceptions of domination, control, and negative resistance.

## 3 Methods

Central to our research design is a case study of a clinical transformation project (the OB-AEHR project), where a university hospital in the US implemented an AEHR system. The case study method is a robust methodological device that can provide researchers with fine-grained, contextual evidence to support the theorizing of power issues (Clegg, 2011). Our purpose, however, was not to generate a new theory or test a theory, as is typically done with case studies, but to engage in “theory elaboration” (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017; Lee et al., 1999). According to Fisher and Aguinis (2017, p. 441):

Theory elaboration is the process of conceptualizing and executing empirical research using preexisting conceptual ideas or a preliminary model as a basis for developing new theoretical insights by contrasting, specifying, or structuring theoretical constructs and relations to account for and explain empirical observations.

As stated, our theory elaboration draws on the Foucauldian-based process model of Leclercq-Vandelannoitte. While this model does not entail formal, testable propositions, it does involve a set of concepts structured as a preliminary model, which thus appears to be a suitable foundation for a theory elaboration study (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017; Lee et al., 1999). We conducted theory elaboration based on the “contrasting” approach in which one can examine how a theory “fits with or explains data collected from a different context” (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017, p. 445).

## 3.1 Data Collection

The data on the AEHR implementation case were primarily collected in 2014. To ensure an appropriate level of internal validity (Dubé & Paré, 2003), we relied on three different data sources: semi-structured interviews, several pieces of documentary evidence (including official agreements, meeting agendas, emails, meeting notes, and reports), and on-site observations. Initially, some potential participants were identified by reviewing the project’s documentation and two meetings with the project manager. The participant recruitment process continued using the snowball sampling strategy (Crabtree & Miller, 1992; Patton, 2002). The interviews were conducted with different actors holding various roles (see Table 2) and were audio-recorded and transcribed. The average interview time was about one hour, and interview notes were taken during and after the interviews. There were also followup email exchanges with some interviewees to clarify specific points and issues raised during the interviews.

## 3.2 Data Analysis

The data analysis comprised three main stages wherein we sought to develop specific, distinct forms of understanding. In the first stage, we developed chronological understanding by creating a narrative account (Eisenhardt & Bourgeois, 1988) of the chronology of events. This provided initial clues as to where in our dataset we might be able to locate elements and aspects (e.g., power-knowledge regimes and relationships, the enactment of discourses, enabling and constraining materiality, and various contextual conditions) relevant to Foucault’s thinking (notably, the dialogical reading of Foucault). We closely investigated the data in the second stage to develop a thematic understanding. We mostly pursued the spirit of “theoretical” (or deductive) thematic analysis (Boyatzis, 1998; Braun & Clarke, 2006). In theoretical thematic analysis, researchers analyze data for a specific research question where themes (i.e., repeated patterns of meaning) are generated in line with researchers’ preestablished theoretical or analytic interests resulting from their engagement with the literature before analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Finally, we conducted a contrasting analysis in the third stage to develop a comparative understanding. The interpretations and findings developed in the second stage prepared the ground to examine the analytical strengths and struggles of Leclercq-Vandelannoitte’s Foucauldian model when applied to the AEHR implementation case.

The next section describes the OB-AEHR project, involving elements and findings from our chronological understanding of the case. The second and third data analysis stages (i.e., developing thematic and comparative understandings) are then presented and discussed in Sections 5 and 6, respectively.

## 4 The OB-AEHR Project

In 2009, a university hospital in the US began a project to implement an AEHR system. The project’s primary goal was to achieve clinical transformation by bringing digital functionalities, including e-prescription, messaging, and patient charting, to the hospital’s outpatient (clinical) side. Before the project, most clinical workflows were paper based, especially patient charting. The most critical phase of the project, converting the patient charting workspace from paper to digital, started in 2010, during which more than 65 clinics in the hospital were scheduled to go live with the new system at different points in time. Our case study focuses on the AEHR implementation process in two clinics functioning under the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology (OB/GYN), hereafter referred to as the OB-AEHR project.

The OB-AEHR project started in September 2010 and ended in September 2012 (see Figure 3). There were three critical milestones during the project. The first pertains to the first “go-live” on July 13, 2011, when the first version of the OB ambulatory documentation system was announced to be ready for use.

Only about six weeks after the first go-live, A1 (the OB/GYN department chair) emailed all key actors involved in the project to suspend the use of the system. The email and the consequent suspension happened on August 24, 2011. The second-version system went live on September 18, 2012.

As shown in Figure 4, before the OB-AEHR project, the OB clinical workflows were organized around using a series of paper forms called American Congress of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) Antepartum Record, or as OB providers tend to call it: ACOG flow sheets. The OB providers used the ACOG flow sheets as the only tool for entering and viewing patients’ data. At the time, the OB providers also had limited interaction with a legacy EHR system, which clerks and nurses mainly used to place lab orders, retrieve lab and diagnostic results, and view in-patient charts.

During the first implementation round (before July 13, 2011), the OB-AEHR project began to propose a specific type of workflow setup, which was like an antithesis to the structured and longitudinal nature of the OB clinical practice shaped around the ACOG flow sheets. The implementation team was aware that the OB clinics workflow differed from that of most other clinics but decided, nevertheless, to deploy the default configuration of the AEHR system for the OB clinics as well. As shown in Figure 4, the configuration comprised three main components. The first component was an OB-specific charting tool. It was a data entry screen and, by default, should have been used by the nursing staff to document (to chart) patients’ different types of data, such as blood pressure, weight, disease history, etc. This tool was critical, especially during patients’ first visits, so the implementation team created customized versions for every medical department. The second component was an OB-specific noting tool, where the most important feature was that physicians could make and keep their own templates for writing notes. These templates could be customized according to physicians’ needs in any specific medical area/discipline. Also, the OB physicians could write their notes using prepopulated templates with data from the OB-specific charting tool. This feature, in particular, was considered by the implementation team to enhance productivity by generating comprehensive yet easy-to-complete clinical notes. Finally, the third component was a general ambulatory dashboard for all clinics. The primary function of the dashboard was to serve as an output page with all the patient-related data coming from different sources, including the OB-specific charting tool and the laboratory sections. In other words, the implementation team decided that all clinics should use/see the same dashboard.

From the perspective of the OB providers (mainly the OB physicians), however, the first version of the system had significant problems, the most important of which was the lack of an OB-specific ambulatory dashboard that could display all the OB-relevant patient data in one place and a longitudinal manner, just as the ACOG flow sheets used to do. According to the OB providers, OB-relevant data could not necessarily be found on the general dashboard because some symptoms, lab results, and diagnoses that are nonissues for other types of patients can be serious issues for obstetrical patients and needed to be highlighted or flagged on the dashboard page. Consequently, OB physicians rejected the first version of the AEHR system on the grounds of patient safety and persuaded their department chair (A1) to suspend its use. The second implementation round, therefore, aimed to focus on the specificities of OB clinical care. The result was a system that included an OB-specific ambulatory dashboard replicating most of the advantages of the ACOG papers. The second roll-out also brought some other new functionalities and changes in the other two tools (i.e., the OB-specific charting and noting tools).

## 5 Case Analysis: Developing a Thematic Understanding

The dialogical Foucauldian lens served as the primary guiding framework in developing a thematic understanding of our case, highlighting the plurality of discourses (i.e., forces), the materiality of discourses (i.e., corporeal forces), and the interplay between discourses (i.e., dialogue of corporeal forces). A further process of searching for, reviewing, defining, and naming themes (see Phases 3, 4, and 5 of thematic analysis in Braun & Clarke, 2006) permitted the development of narratives revolving around three thematic axes: (1) concurrent discourses, (2) discursive materialities, and (3) mediated reciprocation between discourses (see Table 3).

## 5.1 Concurrent Discourses

During the OB-AEHR project, a striking divergence transpired between the positions of the implementation team and the OB providers concerning ambulatory (or outpatient) care and the role of AEHRs in healthcare settings. The implementation team mostly maintained that the AEHR system is essentially a “natural” extension of the hospital’s inpatient EHR system, which had been in use for many years. From the implementers’ point of view, it was, therefore, now time for the outpatient side to fully adopt an EHR system to ensure compliance with “meaningful use” [the US government standards for the usage of EHRs in healthcare settings].

The OB providers (notably, the OB physicians), however, considered EHRs to be a tool built for inpatient medical care. As A12 (an OB physician) explained, EHRs “are great on the inpatient side, but they don’t understand that need for speed on the outpatient side. They are not built that way.” The physician added:

OB physicians also had issues with the idea of comprehensiveness due to their patient safety concerns, arguing that the AEHR system (particularly the first version) would increase the chance that doctors might miss crucial medical information because of high information overload. A7 offered a neat elaboration on the issue:

When it first went live, it was not usable for us. It seemed like it was just a push to get it out, like OK! We have to go EMR; we’re going to do it. And, it definitely was not ready. A big push to go electronic, and so it was like: OK! This is good enough; use it! But it wasn’t good enough. It was very cumbersome to get a snapshot of the patient. From a patient safety thing, it was easy to miss labs.

These two coexisting, indeed clashing, systems of thought were not just residing passively in the actors’ minds. As shown briefly in the chronology of events, the actors took specific actions based on what they— consciously or subconsciously—accepted as true and natural/normal about ambulatory (OB) care and AEHRs. In other words, the actors were governed in their actions and inactions by certain rules/systems/procedures (Hook, 2001), which worked to shape the way the topic of AEHRs could be “meaningfully” reasoned about, talked about, and acted upon (Hall, 2001, p. 72). From a Foucauldian perspective, these systems of thought may thus be viewed as discourses understood as “more than ways of thinking and producing meaning” (Weedon, 1987, p. 108; see also Hook, 2001) and as involving regimes that govern the true (i.e., knowledge) as they govern the natural/normal (i.e., power). Hence, as Hardy and

Thomas (2014) argue, “discourses are saturated with power relations that constrain and enable what individuals can think, say, and do” (p. 324). The OB-AEHR case may, therefore, be seen as an exhibition (or a performance) of at least two concurrent discourses, which we call comprehensiveness and regulatory compliance (henceforth, CRC) and workflow speed and patient safety (henceforth, WSPS).

## 5.2 Discursive Materialities

As stated earlier, before the OB-AEHR project, the ACOG flow sheets were the primary basis for the OB clinical workflows. Each page in the flow sheets was designed to keep certain types of data about OB patients over the course of, usually, 40 weeks involving multiple visits. A1 described the ACOG flow sheets as follows:

A true method that had worked for decades. Prenatal charts were designed from long evolution, and long experience. … [people] can complete them very rapidly for normal pregnancies. And where you [have] them, you know, if somebody walked into labor and delivery in an emergency, you could pull the important things out of that note, you know, in the time that took it walking to the patient’s room holding the chart.

Therefore, the ACOG flow sheets were pivotal in storing, structuring, and transmitting critical information to enable OB providers to undertake day-today clinical tasks. As such, they constituted a form of information technology. Indeed, during the interviews, A8 (a physician informaticist) explicitly described them as an effective electronic medical record (EMR) tool:

They [i.e., the OB providers] were a group that was already in the mindset that you do your work from a summary. … And this summary sheet is the key to all of EMR success. That’s the way electronic medica records are supposed to be used. … That ACOG sheet was really the EMR, just on a piece of paper.

For the OB providers, the ACOG flow sheets had two crucial advantages that contributed to the speed and safety of the OB clinical tasks. First, the flow sheets provided a convenient place for both entering and viewing patient data. Second, since OB clinical care is longitudinal rather than cross-sectional, the flow sheets served as a dashboard to view and monitor the evolution of the patient’s medical condition over time.

Adopting a “one-size fits all” approach to configuring the AEHR system, the implementation team imposed specific workflows that were very different from those that OB providers were used to. As A15 (a nurse informaticist) explained:

The rule of thumb was we’re gonna treat them [i.e., the OB providers/clinics] just as we treat everybody else. We were time-boxed into only having X amount of weeks to dedicate to OB. And, we were only, you know, given the guidelines from the project management office, we were only allowed to work on that solution for this amount of time. We couldn’t do anything special more than what we gave any other clinic.

A particularly problematic component of the system was the general-purpose dashboard page, as A4 (an OB physician) explained:

If I’m a patient and I have high blood pressure that goes on my problem list, so, the problem list would populate the OB chart. But, there are obstetrical problems that are really big problems but are not really medical problems that aren’t on the patient’s chart. So, that was getting lost in translation.

As Willcocks (2004) argues, different information technologies “increasingly mediate how power circulates, is exercised and what it produces” (p. 277). In that sense, the power effects of discourses may not only be enacted by nonhuman, material agents (Ashcraft et al., 2009; Hekkala et al., 2022) but also intensified through practices (Hardy & Thomas, 2014) performed with (and by) those agents. In the OB-AEHR case, the ACOG flow sheets embodied a particular powerknowledge regime, promoting the fast and safety-aware handling of patient data, or what we refer to as the WSPS discourse. By contrast, the AEHR system (particularly the first version) was the incarnation of a different power-knowledge regime that advocated comprehensive and compliant handling of patient data (the CRC discourse).

## 5.3 Mediated Reciprocation between Discourses

The OB-AEHR project was a significant occasion of conflict and divergence as well as interaction and convergence. In the beginning, some senior OB physicians were under the impression that the project was neither essential nor a priority for them to become involved as liaisons (or champions). A4, who later became the champion in the second round of the project, stated: “Truthfully, it felt like it should be more of a chore for some of the junior faculty, I confess.” It also appeared that most OB providers had little or no enthusiasm about the project. A2 described the situation in the following way:

I don’t think anybody was excited about going to an EMR. I think when [A1] put a stop to the OB, there was a resurgence of hope that we could just keep doing our paper charting.

However, the interactions among the actors took a dramatically different shape and nature after the first golive of the AEHR system on July 13, 2011. During the six-week use period (from July 13, 2011, to August 24, 2011), the OB providers’ initially apathetic reactions to the project were largely replaced by assertive—and even, in some cases, harsh—expressions about the AEHR system. It appeared that the OB-AEHR project suddenly became very crucial for the entire OB/GYN department. According to A2:

During that six weeks, [huh], I think all of us recognized shortcomings in the program that they had given us. There were a couple of faculty members [who said] that this was not a workable form and eventually said that it was a safety issue.

Although no longer challenging the legitimacy of the OB-AEHR project, the OB providers were concerned about how the AEHR system might negatively affect their clinical practice, thus manifesting their ACOG-centered worldview. According to A1, the “challenge of the system was to come up with something that wasn’t slower” than the paper charting method. In other words, the OB providers demanded a system “that could look like a paper prenatal chart, not because paper is better, but because a huge amount of thought went into that organization and that flow” (A1). Following the suspension of the AEHR system use, a new OB/GYN team championed the system and interacted with the implementation team during the second implementation round. The following account, offered by A4 (the lead OB physician in the second round), describes these new dynamics:

[We] spent so much time and energy really championing a good product. We kind of felt like [that] we have to believe it with every ounce of our body, and we have to sell it. The IT people can’t sell it. … The other thing that went terribly wrong in the first round is what was like, “OK! The EMR is starting tomorrow. Good luck.” And that was it. And I’m like no, no, no! You can’t because it slowed down our ability to actually take care of patients … So, we also took the strategy, which was a big change in the second round, of how we were gonna implement this.

Not only did the OB providers become vastly engaged in the implementation, but they also convinced the implementation team to make critical changes in the system so that it would replicate, as far as possible, the functionalities of the ACOG flow sheets. Eventually, the implementation team also seemed to be satisfied with the developments. As A10 (the hospital’s CMIO) said:

The technology that we handed them very poorly matched the way they practiced clinically. We usually want the computerized system, not as a replication of what used to be on paper, but in this case and in retrospect, it turns out that the docs were right, it wasn’t unrealistic, it wasn’t unfair in insisting that they can’t do their work if the system doesn’t replicate their ACOG paper format … the tool was wrong.

Notwithstanding the successful roll-out of the AEHR system on September 18, 2012,<sup>5</sup> the project was not, after all, a cliché success story. This is how A1 described the second-version AEHR system:

We don’t have a good system. You know, we, you know, we would love to come up with a great prenatal system that helped us make sure that we didn’t make errors, that let us spend, you know, time with patients instead of computers. We don’t have the resources to do that. Right now, we have impaired efficiency without tangibly improving quality or safety.

In complete contrast to A1’s assessment, A10 viewed the second-version AEHR system as follows:

I think it’s very good. If the docs still complain about it, I would be so frustrated because we spent so much time and energy on it. I have the sense, and I don’t want to know the truth if it’s not what I sense, that they very much like this system, especially compared to the first version.

In summary, the gaps, conflicts, and divergences, which remained salient even after the second roll-out, appeared to demand further rounds of interaction and political negotiation in the future. This entire process seems remarkable because it portrays the transformation scene as an enduring exchange occasion, where human and nonhuman agencies are constantly engaged in, so to speak, “give-and-take” undertakings. Through these undertakings, the OB providers, the implementers, the ACOG flow sheets, and the OB-AEHR system all achieved specific positions, goals, profiles, representations, or features at the expense of compromising on some other aspects of their identity, functionality, or comfort.

In the bigger picture, the plethora of “give-and-take” undertakings rendered a reciprocation field where the WSPS and CRC discourses appeared to be “talking” to or, more precisely, disrupting each other through their respective human and nonhuman embodiments and enactments. Before the OB-AEHR project, the WSPS was the dominant discourse within the OB clinical setting. The OB providers, the technology (i.e., the ACOG flow sheets), and the workflows were all in alignment with the mindset that workflow speed and patient safety are the ultimate true and normal/natural. The OB-AEHR project, however, instigated a competing “system of thought” (Fairhurst & Putnam, 2004), which began to challenge the “order” already established by the WSPS power-knowledge regime. The relationship between the two discourses developed into a situation of “mutual disruption,” with neither able to prevail, leading to a new negotiated, “reconfigured” order (as principally illustrated in Table 4).

## 6 Contrasting Analysis and Discussion

The themes identified and discussed in the context of the OB-AEHR project demonstrate the potential contribution of a dialogical model of power. In general, our contrasting analysis shows that Leclercq-Vandelannoitte’s model is robust enough to explain several essential aspects of the developments that took place in the OB-AEHR project, especially concerning contextual/historical (enabling and constraining) conditions, discourse-driven political activities, and the symbolic/ideational roles or effects of the material. Nevertheless, our analysis also reveals areas in which the dialogical reading of Foucault was too implicit and sometimes underdeveloped in Leclercq-Vandelannoitte’s model; hence, the model could be further refined.<sup>6</sup> In the following subsections, we briefly discuss how these areas of improvement allude to four distinctive features of the dialogical account of power that we advance in this paper in the context of organizational transformations that involve digital technologies.

## 6.1 The Power Effects of “Incumbent Discourses”

In her analysis of the discursive construction and transformation of organizational reality, Leclercq-Vandelannoitte (2011) appears to tie the constitution and dissemination of discourses exclusively to periods of implementing organizational change (i.e., the mobile technology deployment in the ABConstruction case), reflecting “the tendency to emphasize change over stasis” (Leonardi & Barley; 2010, p. 39), and to overlook the role played by “incumbent discourses.” Divorcing the implementation and use of digital technologies from preceding conditions, events, or decisions can thus prevent research efforts “from directly grappling with the role of power” (Leonardi & Barley, 2010, p. 38) as power and power effects become almost exclusively tied to those commonly viewed as “change maker” agencies (e.g., the leadership/administration, implementers, new digital technology, etc.). The dialogical view of power, however, enables us to recognize that incumbent discourses enacted by “change recipients” may also produce power effects, which can instigate reactions and adaptations among “change makers.”

## 6.2 The Reversible Relationship between Power and Resistance

Leclercq-Vandelannoitte posits that in contrast to “critical analysts, Foucault rejects the idea of predetermined social structures and dominant groups” (2011, p. 1253). The situation in the Sesame project<sup>7</sup> as the opposition between managers, who are always supposed to “impose” change, and employees, who are always assumed to “react” to change, however, is effectively presented as one of predetermined domination. To go “beyond the agency-structure dialectic to investigate dialogic qualities of existence” (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2011, p. 1253), it seems crucial to depart from this stereotypical unilateral conception of transformation—at least, as far as it is concerned with transformation in the digital age. Many experiences show that all involved parties, not just the intended change recipients, must often undergo significant changes. This, in turn, triggers a range of different individual and collective reactions (see Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Hekkala & Urquhardt, 2013; Rosenkranz et al., 2014). As a matter of fact, in our study of the OB-AEHR project, we encountered a nurse informaticist (A18) who used the term “resistance” to describe the implementers’ sentiments when they were unwilling to adopt a bespoke design/configuration for the OB clinics during the first roll-out.

In our view, the dialogical reading of Foucault requires us to develop a more progressive perspective toward the transformation phenomenon, recognizing the dynamics of the positions of change makers and change recipients. A dialogical understanding of power leads to a relational and situational conception of subjectivity, individual reaction, interiorizing, and resistance. Accordingly, individual reactions, subjectivity, and “technologies of the self” will not remain as ideas exclusively associated with those classically framed in the IS literature and beyond as users. The intended users are not necessarily subordinate to those who plan, administer, or implement transformations—especially in high-stakes settings such as health care. Our analysis of the OB-AEHR project clearly shows that as much as the physicians became objects of the power-knowledge forces emanating from the CRC discourse, the implementers also became objects of the power effects and discursive practices rooted in the WSPS discourse. Both sides displayed reactions, and both sides exercised discursive practices.

## 6.3 From Discrete Discourses to “Interdiscursive Dynamics”

Leclercq-Vandelannoitte’s model also seems limited in the sense that it only appears to account for how any discrete “discourse participates directly in the production and reproduction of organizational life through power-knowledge relationships” (2011, p. 1264). In other words, the model does not fully recognize that organizations may be constituted not simply through circumstances engendered by discrete, isolated discourses but through a remarkably more complex process—i.e., emergent reciprocations among competing discourses. In the OB-AEHR project (and even in the Sesame project in Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2011), one can observe how the organizational reality was produced through exchanges between the discourses introduced by the digital initiatives and the incumbent discourses.

The reciprocation transpiring between competing discourses constitutes what we refer to as interdiscursive dynamics. A more nuanced discursive explanation of transformation in the digital age should account for how interdiscursive dynamics can stimulate growth, development, and emergence. A dialogical view of power, therefore, would go beyond just emphasizing “the potential disunity” (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte, 2011, p. 1253) within a discrete body of discourse; it would also account for the generative dialogue between discourses.

## 6.4 The Materiality and Agency of Information Technology as a Force

In general, Foucault’s thinking has vastly contributed to the idea that techniques (or technologies) may well be considered material enactments of power-knowledge regimes (i.e., discourses) (Willcocks, 2004, 2006). In addition, the way the term “corporeal” is used in the context of the dialogical reading of Foucault appears to be readily transferable to the management and IS research domains, where the conceptions of “material” and “materiality” are associated with established research streams (Cooren, 2020; Leonardi, 2013; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, 2023; Schultze et al., 2020). Leclercq-Vandelannoitte presents a well-justified account in support of the argument that “the Foucauldian approach offers a valuable conceptualization of technology as simultaneously material and ideational” (2011, p. 1266). This is consistent with our findings from the OB-AEHR case. We observed that technologies (e.g., the ACOG flow sheets and the AEHR system) are ideational entities as much as they are material, and that may be considered, therefore, akin to contending that discourses (e.g., WSPS and CRC) are material entities as much as they are ideational. We recognize the importance of developing such a perspective about discourse and power, especially where the majority of IS and non-IS studies of power still show a strong tendency to favor the symbolic/ideational aspect of power over its material/tangible side involving bodies, sites, and artifacts, among others.

Still, the treatment of technology’s agency (cf. nonhuman agents in Ashcraft et al., 2009) seems absent in the Foucauldian processes proposed by Leclercq-Vandelannoitte (2011). The role of technology’s agency in producing effects within the individual, organizational, or social realms has long attracted researchers (Jones, 1998; Orlikowski, 1992; Woodward, 1958). Research addressing sociomateriality has paid particular attention to the entanglement of human and technological agencies (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008), technologies’ material agency exercised through their “performativity”— reflected in things technologies can do that humans cannot entirely control (Leonardi, 2011, 2013), or how the interaction between technology’s agency and human agency contributes to “varying and indeterminate routine performances” (Robey et al., 2013). Even beyond the sociomateriality research stream, we observe perspectives emphasizing the increasingly crucial and transformative role of technological agencies (Demetis & Lee, 2018). For instance, Baskerville et al. (2020) argue that the classical view of digital technologies representing physical reality is increasingly obsolete, and there is a growing theoretical need to address the fact that digital technologies are now creating and shaping physical reality.

In our view, the dialogical reading of Foucault can create a promising opportunity to invigorate the theoretical efforts to account for the agency of information technologies in power relations. The essentially dialectical conceptions of the “double mangling” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008), “ongoing double dance” (Jones, 1998), and “situated entanglement” (Orlikowski, 2005) of human and nonhuman/technological agencies already exist in the IS literature and beyond. However, such conceptions, especially in the context of the sociomateriality research stream, may be argued to be inadequate for taking full account of issues of power (Mutch, 2013; Kautz & Jensen, 2013), especially with the evolution of technologies towards greater “agency” (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Ågerfalk, 2020). The dialogical reading of Foucault can be leveraged not only to fully recognize and portray information technology as a category of material (or corporeal) forces involved in power dynamics—especially during occasions of transformation—but also to innovatively account for information technology as an “agentic artifact” (Baird & Maruping, 2021), a force that shows agency through engaging in strategic reciprocations with other rival forces, contributing to a complex political landscape where each of the participating forces behaves based on its own interests/goals.

## 7 Implications

To offer a clearer perspective regarding the implications of transformation in the digital age, researchers may often need to integrate different theoretical accounts and enact innovative research methods (Lanzolla et al., 2020; Rowe, 2018). In this paper, we seek to integrate a new reading of Foucault into the study of organizational transformation associated with digital technologies while innovatively engaging with an original and unconventional (at least in the IS field) theorizing approach known as “theory elaboration.”

## 7.1 Methodological Contributions

On the methodology front, we contribute by experimenting with and demonstrating how rigorous theorizing in IS qualitative research need not be limited to just inductive theory building or deductive theory testing, especially in situations where our theoretical understanding of an issue is insufficiently developed to be readily formulated and characterized as a full-blown theory (Hassan et al., 2022; Weick, 1995) or as a set of clear-cut, testable propositions. In general, pursuing theory elaboration in this paper is innovative in that it opens a new direction in how IS studies may engage with previous studies and theorizations. In addition, the specific “contrasting” approach we followed in the current study enabled us to not only draw on preexisting conceptual ideas but also meaningfully engage with data previously collected in a different context, all in a new inquiry setting of theorizing and reflection. Accordingly, it can be argued that the current study, through its execution of theory elaboration, also showcases a novel, methodologically legitimate path to approach the widely sought-after “cross-contextual” theorizing concerning the phenomenon of organizational transformation in the digital age.

Nevertheless, other than methodology, our dialogical perspective on power can also provide substantive insights and lessons for future research and for nonacademics whose interests are by any means linked to transformation in the digital age (and the surrounding power issues). Therefore, in the following two subsections, we address some major implications of our study for research and practice.

## 7.2 Theoretical Implications

The principal theme in our paper speaks to the idea that our proposed dialogical model of power allows for theorizing how the trajectory and outcomes of transformations are constituted by interdiscursive dynamics, where reciprocations among human and nonhuman forces—associated with rival powerknowledge regimes—may reinforce, undermine, and, or transform social/organizational conditions. According to Falzon (1998):

We are always in the midst of dialogue ... Even states of domination, where the other is temporarily silenced and overcome, remain ultimately subject to dialogue, to the resurgence of buried voices, to ongoing dialogue and transformation. (pp. 75-76, emphasis added)

One general opportunity worth investigating would be how “dialogue” may be understood and theorized as generative tension among a set of forces. This appears especially helpful to account for the emergent and often unintentional ways that transformation plays out, where commonalities and differences in values, norms, and worldviews create dynamics that drive ongoing, mediated reciprocations. This can also complement/enrich perspectives in the literature that emphasize “purposive cooperation among and between social actors (e.g., individuals or groups) who, united through shared values and norms, pursue a joint objective (i.e., instigate transformative change using IT)” (Tana et al., 2023, p. 1619).

Another opportunity would entail accounting for the interconnections between different faces or dimensions of power involving both micro and macro dynamics (see Figure 1), a topic not sufficiently addressed in the IS literature (Simeonova et al., 2020). The OB-AEHR project situation, for instance, showed how beneath the seemingly stable moments of order, control, or domination, there was an undertow of openended dialogue between rival discursive forces. The dialogical view of power may thus contribute to deeper investigations of how organizational transformation in the digital age needs to be accounted for as an “amalgamation of micro/local and macro/global” (Hanelt et al., 2021, p. 1178). Indeed, in our time, we can observe how deep, macro dialogues of forces— affected by global/external factors such as the constant emergence of novel digital technologies and narratives around them—go hand in hand with all the microtensions among local forces that transform everyday life/practice. With the dialogical model of power, while we can recognize that discourses may engage in certain macrolevel reciprocations, it is then often through the contingent interplay “of corporeal forces, from ‘below,’ as Foucault puts it, that specific forms of social arrangement and unity emerge” (Falzon, 1998, p. 40). We envisage that this perspective may also contribute to developing theoretical accounts that are eventually able to simultaneously address both the “transformation of processes/entities” and the “processes of transformation” (see Markus & Rowe, 2023).

While the IS literature now recognizes the possibility for sociotechnological transformation to take place seemingly episodically but situated in a broader continuity (Baygi et al., 2021), a third broad research opportunity appears to revolve around leveraging our proposed dialogical model of power to further examine the episodic yet continuous nature of organizational transformation in the digital age. In our case of OB-AEHR, while the project episode seemingly ended with the “complete” adoption of the AEHR system for daily clinical practice, our observations suggested that the covert, if not overt, tensions never ended. Indeed, with the two main stakeholder groups (i.e., the OB providers and the implementers) eventually holding quite contradictory assessments concerning the utility of the second version of the AEHR system, there remained a significant chance for continued revolt and transformation in the future. This is consistent with Hanelt et al.’s (2021) position that even episodic phases of transformation can trigger continuous changes. Drawing on the notion of dialogue as generative tension among forces, we envisage that future research might be able to develop more nuanced perspectives. For instance, an exciting avenue for research would be to account for how the very generative nature of the dialogue of forces may bring in or activate additional forces—not (directly) associated with the initially intended digital innovations—during the later stages of the transformation journey (e.g., consider how the substantive force associated with the ACOG flow sheets, not initially foreseen by the implementers, was activated and enacted, especially after the first rollout’s failure). Consequently, we may be able to achieve more nuanced theoretical imagery than that currently adopted in the literature, especially in research on competing logics—where the emergence of new competing logics is often deemed possible only at the beginning of a transformation process (see Mars & Lounsbury, 2009; Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2020; Thornton et al., 2012).

## 7.3 Practical Recommendations

The gist of the current paper for nonacademics is that, in line with the ideas discussed by Majchrzak et al. (2016), if transformation initiatives in the digital age aim to contribute to the improvement of societal and business conditions, power (and empowerment) issues should be addressed much more explicitly and transparently in practice. Our proposed dialogical model of power can provide a foundation for practitioners to augment their traditional mindset of “goals, task segmentation, hierarchies, and boundaries” (Majchrzak et al., 2016, p. 274) by acknowledging and managing tensions and appropriation moves. Furthermore, insofar as the dialogical understanding of power espouses an account of human agency where “an agent presupposes not an abstract will but rather the corporeal power or capacity to act in various ways” (Falzon, 1998, p. 38), we envisage that our proposed view of power may enable activists, who seek to make the transformative role of digital technologies more humane and fair, to move beyond just understanding/analyzing power relations toward modes of practice that incorporate a designoriented mindset about power. For instance, they could address issues such as: “How can democratic power (Haugaard, 2010) relations be achieved/designed while a setting is undergoing transformation with digital technologies?” or “What power interventions in relation to transformations in the digital age are likely to advance justice?” Finally, we can see the potential for the dialogical view of power to inspire practitioners to better manage the tensions around transformationrelated issues. In health care and other sectors, a pressing question is: “How can implementers’ understanding of the undeniable power complexity of transformation journeys be improved?” In our opinion, the most essential element would be for practitioners to recognize that they must ensure a healthy, continuous dialogue of forces. A rewarding transformation journey can often play out when chances remain open for all parties to respond continuously to each other. In practice, this often demands claiming “a space for lost voices” (Deetz, 1996) and ongoing empowerment of the powerless to enable them to attain sufficient creativity, confidence, and means to introduce new (or strengthen incumbent) logics and visions (i.e., forces) in the dialogue. Although, participation in dialogue should, of course, never be confused with “equal satisfaction with dialogue’s processes and outcomes” for all participants (Guilfoyle, 2003, p. 334).

## 8 Conclusion

We contend that organizational transformation in the digital age (with all its evolving, unpredictable, political, and negotiated nature) is located at the intersection of and generated from multiple discourses. With that understanding, the general spirit of our approach in this paper is to demonstrate again the relevance of the Foucauldian view and its interpretational capacity to inspire progressive theorizing on the intertwinement of transformation and power. In particular, we argue that the dialogical reading of Foucault appears promising as a way to move beyond the clichéd view of individual resistance against sovereign power, establishing, more sharply than ever, the perspective that “we are at all times … positioned in discourse, and [maybe] inadvertently, but inevitably, contribute to ‘macro’ dialogues between discourses” (Guilfoyle, 2003, p. 334).

In the context of transformations that involve digital technologies, our proposed dialogical account of power incorporates four distinctive features. First, it highlights the role of incumbent discourses. We contend that power effects should not be tied only to the “change makers,” and reactions should not only belong to the “change recipients.” We demonstrate that “change recipients” often produce rival power effects, which can instigate reactions and adaptations among “change makers.” This backs up the second feature, speaking to the reversible relationships between power and resistance. During transformation journeys, all sides both exercise discursive practices and display reactions. Hence, the third feature punctuates that transformation occasions are usually constituted not simply through discrete, isolated discourses but through emergent reciprocations among competing discourses. Finally, the fourth feature of our dialogical model of power emphasizes the materiality and agency of information technology as a force.

Nevertheless, this paper involves some limitations in that it is anchored in the work of Foucault, which is often complex and challenging to grasp and may sometimes even appear to be self-contradictory, notably because of the eclecticism of and evolutions in Foucault’s own thinking on power. In line with the Foucauldian problematization, this paper once again promotes the idea that science can only be developed through self-questioning, iteration, and elaboration. As Foucault points out: “There are moments in life when the question of knowing if one can think differently than one does and perceive differently to the way one sees is essential if we are to continue to look at or to reflect” (1984/1985b, pp. 8-9). Therefore, aligned with the drive to pursue innovative theorizing agendas in IS research (Markus, 2014; Rowe, 2012), we think of this paper, as the Foucauldian view invites us, not as a push toward an all-new theory accounting for universal truths but rather as a set of “original reflections” offering “new angles” and potential starting points to address key questions.

This contributed to specific power effects and, in turn, led to the emergence of OB providers' reactions and appropriation moves.</td><td>Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model appears to produce an account that does not adequately explain this part of our observations regarding the OB-AEHR project.The way Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model accounts for the developments/ circumstances in the OB-AEHR project seems to primarily rely on the presumption that different forms of power effects are only associated with the discourse enacted by those who are commonly viewed as the "change-maker" agencies (e.g., the leadership/administration, IT implementers, etc.). In other words, with that perspective, reactions and adaptations only belong to the "change recipient" side. However, the OB-AEHR case demonstrates that the CRC discourse, with all its affiliated constructions, practices, and enactments, was only one part of the big picture of organizational "becoming." Those who might be called, so to speak, "change recipients" (i.e., the OB providers) already were performing and reinforcing a rival power-knowledge regime (i.e., the WSPS discourse) with all its associated work practices, material enactments, and power effects.</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td>Process 3</td><td>"On the one hand, power generates consent, as it relies on knowledge and discursive practices which constitute individuals as docile subjects; on the other hand, it engenders resistance and discontinuity. For example, site foremen as organizational actors became objects of power-knowledge (DuGay, 1996) through the emergence of subtle forms of discipline, induced by discursive practices and embodied in new technologies. However, as they become objects of power-knowledge through the discipline of their behavior, they also can become subjects who construct and guarantee their ongoingidentity through power-knowledge relations. These relations enabled them to continue or even reverse the conditions of their subjection through ethics and care for the self (Jermier et al., 1994; Fleming &amp; Spicer, 2003). ... As Foucault shows, technologies of the self give people an opportunity to learn about the potential effects of disciplinary proceedings and consequently resist both them and their associated discourse.” (p. 1265)</td><td>The OB providers became objects of power-knowledge. The practices performed by the implementers and the AEHR system had strong disciplinary effects. In turn, the OB providers started to react to and disrupt the "system of thought" governing the AEHR implementation project and the ongoing subjection (or subjectification) threatening their identity.</td><td>Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model appears to produce an account that does not adequately explain this part of our observations regarding the OB-AEHR project.Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model seems inadequate on two grounds. On the one hand, the model appears to be relying on the presumption that organizations are constituted through only one (breed of) discourse(s) (typically advocated/enacted by change makers) at a time. In the OB-AEHR project, the OB providers were not just performing some "individual" practices or reactions; instead, their actions/reactions were primarily rooted in an established "system of thought" that already had its own manifestations and materialenactments in the OB clinical setting. This is what we refer to as the WSPS discourse. On the other hand, Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model also appears to overlook the fact that the implementers, in turn, also became objects of power-knowledge through the discursive practices and material enactments (e.g., the ACOG flow sheets) associated with the WSPS discourse. Consequently, the implementers displayed resistance against the effects of some nondesirable disciplinary proceedings. The implementers had to react to the crucial agency of the ACOG flow sheets and the requests and actions emanating from the OB providers.</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>Process 1</td><td>“The ABConstruction case shows that power-knowledge relationships are enacted in both symbolic and material conditions that drive organizational life (Knights &amp; Murray, 1994). These conditions are socially constructed through discursive practices, but they also involve and are embedded in material circumstances, resources, and consequences of action.” (p. 1266)</td><td>The power-knowledge relationships, governed by the WSPS and CRC discourses, were enacted by the ACOG flow sheets and AEHR system, among other human and nonhuman circumstances and resources. In other words, there were clear, significant consistencies between what was assumed as true and normal/natural under each discourse and what the discourses' respective symbolic and material conditions were affording people to do or not to do.</td><td>Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model appears to produce an account that adequately explains this part of our observations regarding the OB-AEHR project.</td></tr><tr><td>6</td><td>Process 1</td><td>“the Foucauldian approach offers a valuable conceptualization of technology as simultaneously material and ideational. Contrary to recent work that reproduces the materialist-idealist dualism (Aschraft et al., 2009, p. 28), a Foucauldian view provides insights into the behavioral and social technologies encoded and embedded in material technologies (Willcocks, 2004). ... These technologies appear as tangible incarnations that carry collective norms, values, and symbols and have material qualities to support the accomplishment of action, mediation of relations, and coordination of work (Ashcraft et al., 2009). At ABConstruction, technology ... structured behaviors and affected the bases of collective action” (p. 1266)</td><td>Doubtless, the physicality, features, and functionalities of the ACOG flow sheets and the AEHR system embody specific values, worldviews, and norms. In other words, they are ideational entities as much as material objects.</td><td>Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model appears to produce an account that mostly explains this part of our observations regarding the OB-AEHR project. However, it seems that Leclercq-Vandelannoitte's model does not adequately emphasize the agency of (digital) technology. Although Process 1 covers the idea that the material may carry ideational properties, there is no explicit indication of the agency of the material/technological (cf. Ashcraft et al., 2009) in the proposed process model.In the OB-AEHR project, the ACOG flow sheets and the AEHR system are evidently beyond passive material incarnations of ideologies or merely catalyst elements supporting action/relations/work. Indeed, the two technologies displayed agency in that they caused certain developments throughout the project and drove people to exhibit particular behaviors and reactions.</td></tr></table>

## About the Authors

Kaveh Mohajeri is an associate professor in the Department of Innovation, Entrepreneurship, and Information Systems at IESEG School of Management, France. He received his BSc degree in industrial management and his MSc in IT management from the University of Tehran, Iran. He also has a PhD in information systems from Virginia Commonwealth University. His research agenda primarily deals with studying issues of power associated with the digital transformation of organizations and societies and advancing novel methodological practices, focusing on the issues of research relevance and impact, computationally intensive qualitative research, and deductive theorizing. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Information Technology, and The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems, among other outlets. Before starting his PhD program, Kaveh worked as an entrepreneur and a project analyst for over four years.

Aurélie Leclercq-Vandelannoitte is a researcher at CNRS (French National Center for Scientific Research—LEM, UMR 9221, IESEG School of Management). She holds a PhD in Management from Paris-Dauphine University and has received three PhD national awards. Her primary research area focuses on the interplay among people, organizations, and information systems. She is particularly interested in new work practices and organizational forms enabled by the use of information technology. She relies particularly on the approach developed by French philosopher Michel Foucault. Her research has been published in leading journals in management, organization studies, and management information systems (e.g., Organization Studies, Organization, Journal of Business Ethics, Journal of Management Inquiry, Organizational Research Methods, Management Learning, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information & Organization, and Information Technology & People).
