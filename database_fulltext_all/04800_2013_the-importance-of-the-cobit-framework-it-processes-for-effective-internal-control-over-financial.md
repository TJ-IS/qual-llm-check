## A B S T R A C T

This paper presents the results of an international survey of IT professionals exploring the relationships between CobiT’s IT processes and financial reporting. Our respondents rated the importance of each of CobiT’s 34 processes from the viewpoint of effective internal control over financial reporting. Results revealed five processes that were deemed specially critical. We also find interesting patterns of results as a function of respondents’ employment type and geographic location. This survey of IT professionals shed light on the relevance of a widely used IT governance framework—CobiT—from the perspective of organizations’ internal controls.

## 1. Introduction

Top management of public companies in the United States is responsible for the design and operation of an effective system of internal control, as specified by the Sarbanes–Oxley Act; similar statutory requirements exist in other countries. Several highly publicized cases of corporate fraud, such as Enron and WorldCom, have resulted in increased attention to the importance of such internal controls and procedures.

In today’s business environment, organizations rely heavily on IT to control and to improve business performance; however, they can introduce risks which must be managed. Optimal methods of managing IT risks are prescribed in the ‘‘Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology’’ (CobiT) framework, released by the IT Governance Institute of the Information Systems Audit & Control Association. CobiT defines internal control as ‘‘. . . the policies, procedures, practices and organizational structures designed to provide reasonable assurance that business objectives will be achieved and undesired events will be prevented or detected and corrected.’’ The framework describes several specific IT control and security processes that an organization can use to enhance its ability to achieve its business goals and to improve internal control. Conformance to these control and security processes should theoretically result in a lowering of the risk associated with IT.

Information regarding the relative importance of the various IT control and security processes is important to CIOs in large organizations charged with the responsibility for IT governance and maintaining and managing the organization’s IT infrastructure [1]. Additionally, information about the relative importance of IT processes should be relevant to the planning and conduct of independent corporate audits. Tuttle and Vandervelde [11] reported that the major independent auditing firms use the CobiT framework in many of their audit engagements. Thus, an understanding of which of the framework’s IT processes are most critical in attaining reliable financial reporting has practical significance to the public accounting profession.

In our study, we investigated the relative importance of the various CobiT IT processes in the context of reliability of corporate financial information. The results should help organizations improve the reliability and timeliness of corporate information, improve stakeholders’ and other interested parties’ confidence in the reports, and lead to more effective and efficient audits of corporate financial information.

## 2. Background and research questions

## 2.1. Frameworks for internal control evaluation

Internal control and security is one of the top three concerns of IT management. Section 404 of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 requires management of public companies in the U.S. to evaluate the design and test the effectiveness of their internal controls over corporate financial reporting and provide a report, signed by the CEO and CFO, attesting to the effectiveness of those controls as of the end of each fiscal year. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) defined internal control over financial reporting as<sup>2</sup>:

‘‘A process designed by, or under the supervision of, the registrant’s principal executive and principal financial officers, or persons performing similar functions, and effected by the registrant’s board of directors, management and other personnel, to provide reasonable assurance regarding the reliability of financial reporting and the preparation of financial statements for external purposes.’’

The methods used by management to evaluate controls vary across companies, depending on the nature of the company and the controls being evaluated. However, rules related to the implementation of Section 404 of the Act require management to use a ‘‘suitable evaluation framework’’ in its evaluation of controls over financial reporting. Several possible frameworks exist and the SEC has not mandated the use of any one framework.

A framework used in evaluations of internal IT control is the Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (CobiT), released by the IT Governance Institute of the Information Systems Audit and Control Association. It is widely used by IT professionals in the United States and overseas to help manage and secure organizations’ IS and is being used increasingly by managers, internal auditors, and external auditors in the context of evaluating IT control systems [8,9,11].

The focus of the CobiT framework is on the management and control of IT. The framework specifies 34 key IT control and security processes (activities and risks) that must be managed for effective IT governance. The IT processes are organized into four domains, which correspond to the major areas of responsibility within IT: Plan and Organize (plan), Acquire and Implement (build), Deliver and Support (run), and Monitor (evaluate).

## 2.2. Research on CobiT usage and adoption

What types of organizations have adopted CobiT? To what extent has CobiT been adopted by these organizations? Where in the world is CobiT being used? Given the increased responsibilities on management to oversee and monitor the effectiveness of internal controls, these are relevant and timely questions. Since the CobiT framework is widely regarded as a valuable tool for providing standards and guidance on IT and information security [7,10], additional knowledge regarding how it is being used by organizations could assist managers in their efforts to improve their organization’s IT governance and controls. Jo et al. [4] examined the effects of several factors on IT organizations intentions to adopt CobiT as their framework for IT Governance. Factors included understandability of the CobiT framework, organizational changes needed to adopt CobiT, perceived benefits of adopting CobiT, external certification, and external support. Results of their survey of 100 Korean corporations revealed that only the availability of external support from CobiT consultants and CobiT-related seminars had any effect on adoption intention. The authors concluded that while CobiT has been successfully adopted in many other countries, it has been less well received in Korea, possibly due to lack of experts who can ‘‘customize it’’ to meet the needs of Korean organizations.

Focusing on firms in Taiwan, Lin et al. [5] explored external auditors’ evaluations of firms’ IT control structures and CobiT domains. Their results suggested that many firms suffered from ‘‘poor management commitment’’ and thus did not invest sufficient resources or training to be effective in implementing the CobiT framework.

Studies by Haworth and Pietron [3] and Wallace et al. [12] have addressed the relationship between compliance with the Sarbanes–Oxley Act and implementation of IT controls suggested by the ISO 17799 security framework, while Mishra [6] addressed the relationship between the Act’s requirements and the CobiT framework. Haworth and Pietron provide a good description of how the ISO 17799 framework’s components can help organizations comply with the Act’s requirements. The Wallace study explored the extent to which the ISO 17799 framework’s IT controls have been implemented by U.S. companies. Results revealed a significant relationship between training of IT employees and auditors and the extent to which controls have been implemented. Significant results were also observed for company type (public versus private), company size, and industry. Mishra presented a conceptual approach to applying the CobiT framework in order to comply with requirements of the Act in the context of software development.

Our study extended previous studies by focusing on IT professionals’ ratings of the CobiT framework’s IT controls and by examining the effects of several factors on those ratings, such as location, experience, and familiarity with the CobiT framework.

## 2.3. CobiT and financial reporting reliability

Tuttle and Vandervelde examined the extent to which the CobiT framework fits within auditing settings, specifically for operational, compliance and financial audits. They first had a panel of 12 experienced IT auditors rate the seven CobiT information criteria and four IT resources on four dimensions related to audit risk: complexity, client importance, client attention, and process risk. These assessments were used to compute ‘‘framework scores’’ for the 34 CobiT processes. Next, a total of 29 auditors directly rated each of the 34 CobiT processes in terms of the degree of risk to a typical organization associated with an unsatisfactory outcome of each process. A significant degree of association was found between the framework scores and the risk assessments, suggesting that the CobiT framework has ‘‘. . . significant (internal) convergence and consistency . . .’’. Additionally, they analyzed CobiT-related listserv postings by IT auditors and found that the CobiT conceptual model significantly predicted auditor behavior in the field, CobiT processes that had higher framework scores and risk ratings had a higher proportion of listserv postings for processes that had lower framework scores and risk ratings.

Although aggregated data from the CobiT usage surveys were informative, and while Tuttle and Vandervelde provided important evidence regarding the internal consistency of CobiT, little has been done to determine the utility of CobiT from the perspective of the reliability of corporate financial reporting. Therefore, we decided to address this gap in the literature by focusing specifically on how the 34 IT control and security processes in the four CobiT domains impacted the reliability of corporate financial reporting. Specifically, we focused on IT professionals’ assessments of the extent to which each of the 34 CobiT processes impacted the reliability of corporate financial reporting. We examined the effect of respondents’ experience in evaluating IT controls, the extent of their audit experience, familiarity with CobiT, the type of organization in which they were employed, and their country of employment. We had no a priori expectations of the magnitude or direction of the effects of these characteristics on IT professionals’ assessments. Therefore, we did not provide formal hypotheses, but posed the following questions:

RQ1: In the context of the reliability of financial reporting, what is the relative importance of each of the 34 IT control and security processes?

RQ2: To what extent do perceptions regarding the relative importance of the IT control and security processes differ between IT professionals employed in industry, government, and independent auditing firms?

RQ3: To what extent do perceptions of the relative importance of the IT control and security processes vary as a function of characteristics of the IT professionals?

RQ4: To what extent are various characteristics of IT professionals who are experienced in reviewing/evaluating IT controls significantly associated with other characteristics of the professionals?

## 3. Method

We conducted an Internet-based survey of IT professionals to obtain the data needed to address the research questions. Administration of the research instrument allowed us to include a number of experimental controls not possible in traditional paperbased instruments. The instrument first elicited demographic data about the participants’ country, age, gender, education, certifications, experience, type of work involving the review or evaluation of IT controls, and their level of familiarity with CobiT. Participants were then asked to rate, on a five-point Likert scale, the importance of each of the 34 processes in the CobiT framework, in terms of their reliability in financial reporting. Participants were also asked to indicate the 10 IT processes they believe are most important in reliable financial reporting and then to respond to several questions about the extent of their implementation of CobiT and their success with its implementation in their organization or for the client on their last audit engagement.<sup>3</sup>

Given the nature of the survey, it was deemed necessary to target IT professionals who were familiar with CobiT and had experienced the task of reviewing and evaluating IT controls. The assistance of ISACA headquarters in Chicago was obtained in soliciting the participation of ISACA chapters in the U.S. and internationally. Chapter presidents were sent an e-mail from ISACA encouraging them to participate in the survey. While some chapters chose to publish our solicitation in their local chapter newsletter, others forwarded our e-mail to their internal chapter members. Usable responses were received from 189 IT professionals from all over the world.

## 3.2. Participant experience and familiarity with COBIT

To assess participants’ extent of prior experience with evaluating IT controls, we asked participants to respond to the following question: ‘‘In the last two years, what percentage of your work hours related to the task of reviewing/evaluating information technology controls?’’ Fifty-two percent of the participants reported spending more than 50% of their work hours reviewing and/or evaluating IT controls. Only 9.5% reported spending less than 10% of their work hours reviewing IT controls. These data indicate that, on average, the survey respondents spend a considerable portion of their time on reviewing and evaluating IT controls.

We also asked each participant to rate the extent of their familiarity with CobiT using a five-point scale anchored with 1 (not familiar at all) and 5 (very familiar). Ninety-three percent of the participants rated their familiarity with CobiT as 3 or higher. The median rating was 4.0, and the mean rating was 3.9. These data suggest that the survey respondents could be deemed experts.

## 4.2. Relationships among IT processes in reliable financial reporting

In order to discern possible relationships among the 34 IT processes in terms of effective internal control over financial reporting, we performed a principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation based on participants’ importance ratings. Results of the factor analysis, which revealed some interesting relationships, are presented in Table 4.

Costello and Osborne [2] found that, in the social sciences, communalities ranged from 0.40 to 0.70, which they termed ‘‘low to moderate.’’ They further suggested that a good rule of thumb for the minimum loading of an item was a communality of 0.32. Accordingly, across the 34 IT processes, items with communalities higher than 0.40 were grouped to identify processes that were loading on the same factor. ## 4.3. Additional analyses—effects of demographic characteristics

4.3.1. Relationships among perceived importance of IT processes and employment type

To address our second research question, we used the GLM procedure in SPSS. Separate procedures were run using, as the dependent variables, the means of the CobiT IT processes within each of the six factors we had identified in our factor analysis. The independent variable in each GLM procedure was employment type.

Only one of the six factors—Factor 4, Technology processes—was found to be significantly affected by participants’ employment type. As shown in Table 5, participants employed in an independent auditing firm rated the importance of Technology processes higher, on average, than participants in industry or government. This may be attributed to the fact that respondents who work in an independent auditing firm often encounter an array of technological environments for different audit and feel differently about the importance of them than non-auditors who are familiar with only their own technology environment.

## 5. Summary, implications, and conclusion

## 5.1. Summary

A Web-based survey was conducted soliciting participation from chapters of the Information Systems Audit and Control Association from all over the world. Respondents were relatively experienced and were quite familiar with CobiT.

The process rated as most important for effective control over financial reporting was Ensure system security, followed by Manage changes, Assess risk, Manage Data, and Assess internal control adequacy. Subsequent factor analysis identified six discrete clusters of processes, labeled Key IT processes, Planning and IT management processes, Organization and relationships processes, Technology processes, Operations and facilities processes, and Independent audit processes.

The Technology processes cluster was the only one affected by the type of organization, with participants employed in auditing firms rating it more important than other participants. Technology processes was again the only cluster found to be significantly affected by any of the participant characteristics. Participants in North America rated it higher than participants elsewhere.

Correlations were found between three pairs of characteristics: extent of experience evaluating IT controls and geographic location, with participants in North America spending a greater percentage of their work hours evaluating IT controls than those in other locations; extent of experience evaluating IT controls and employment type, showed that participants employed in auditing firms spent more time evaluating IT controls than other participants; and familiarity with CobiT and extent of audit experience, showed that familiarity with CobiT was positively correlated with years of audit experience.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

Our results have important implications for managers responsible for ensuring effective internal control in IT-intensive organizations. Given limited resources, managers should focus their attention on the highest rated IT processes in the CobiT framework. From the planning domain, managers should ensure that risks of all kinds are adequately assessed throughout the organization. During acquisition and implementation, managers should address the process of managing IT changes. In the delivery and support services area, IT manager should ensure that system security issues are adequately addressed and also that the controls over application data are effective. Finally, in the monitoring domain, managers should focus on assessing the adequacy of internal controls on a regular.

Finally, managers in companies that operate outside North America and those heading internal auditing departments should consider placing greater emphasis on the task of reviewing and evaluating IT internal controls.

## 5.3. Limitations and conclusion

Given the multiple ways in which local ISACA chapters publicized the survey, and assuming that many chapters did not participate, the response rate and extent of non-response bias is unknown. The extent to which the importance ratings were affected by the length of the instrument was also impossible to assess from our results—the instrument could have invoked a ‘‘fatigue factor’’ for respondents.

Despite the explicit instructions given, it is possible that some respondents were not attuned to our focus on the effect of the CobiT IT processes on the reliability of financial reporting. Finally, the lack of a reference point or context could have resulted in bias of the importance ratings.

To conclude, this paper sheds light on the extent to which expert IT professionals judge the CobiT processes in terms of their importance for maintaining effective internal control over the reliability of financial reporting, which should be of significant interest and relevance to executives, managers, and auditors in virtually any organization.
