# Mixed results in strategic IT alignment research: a synthesis and empirical study

Magno Queiroz

Management Information Systems, Jon M. Huntsman School of Business, Utah State University, 3515 Old Main Hill, Logan, UT 84322–3515, USA

Correspondence: Magno Queiroz, Management Information Systems, Jon M. Huntsman School of Business, Utah State University, 3515 Old Main Hill, Logan, UT 84322–3515, USA. Tel: + (435) 797-5366; E-mail: magno.queiroz@usu.edu

## Abstract

The alignment of business strategy and IT has been a top managerial concern for decades. Yet despite much investigation, the effect of strategic IT alignment on organizational performance remains unclear, with mixed results reported in the literature. The purpose of this paper is to advance our understanding of mixed findings in IT alignment research. We first examine inconsistent findings reported in two streams of alignment research: the traditional firm-level IT alignment literature and the emerging literature into process-level IT alignment. We then empirically investigate whether firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment lead to different conclusions about the effect of alignment on performance. Using data from a survey of 120 firms, we show that firm-level IT alignment and process-level IT alignment yield different conclusions when testing the same theory under the same conditions. We also show that differences in firms’ strategic orientations can help explain these results. This research provides evidence that firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment are not interchangeable and that the choice of conceptualization can mean the difference between accepting and rejecting a theory. ## Introduction

One of the most widely accepted assumptions in the information systems literature is that organizational performance will be enhanced when information technology (IT) is aligned with business strategy (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Chan et al, 2006; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). While the enduring importance of strategic IT alignment (hereafter IT alignment) to both practitioners (Kappelman et al, 2013) and scholars (Yayla & Hu, 2012; Gerow et al, 2014a) has led to considerable investigation, the wealth of research has generated little synthesis of findings. Instead, prior studies report both significant and nonsignificant correlations between IT alignment and performance (Palmer & Markus, 2000; Byrd et al, 2006), indirect relationships that are mediated by organizational agility (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), and significant differences in results when alternative approaches are employed to conceptualize IT alignment (Cragg et al, 2002). It has also been argued that alignment could create rigidities and lead to a decline in performance (Tallon, 2003; Chan & Reich, 2007). While a recent investigation of this apparent alignment paradox supported the argument that alignment improves performance, our understanding of inconsistent findings reported in the IT alignment literature remains limited (Gerow et al, 2014b).

IT alignment is traditionally conceptualized as the extent of fit or congruence between business strategy and IT strategy (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). By employing this conceptualization, several studies have found that alignment has a positive and significant effect on performance (Chan et al, 1997; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Chan et al, 2006; Yayla & Hu, 2012). However, not all evidence concludes that alignment improves performance. For instance, Palmer & Markus (2000) did not find a significant effect, and went on to argue that ‘alignment has become institutionalized,’ and that it has ceased to be ‘a differentiating factor in firm performance’ (p. 257). Tallon (2008) cautions against this overly liberal interpretation, and proposes that a process-level perspective to IT alignment, as opposed to the traditional firm-level or organizationallevel view (the terms firm and organization are used interchangeably in this paper), could yield a better understanding of the role of IT alignment in explaining performance.

The process-level perspective conceives of IT alignment in terms of the links between the organization’s primary business processes that execute business strategy (i.e., supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, sales and marketing, and customer relations) and IT use, which are ‘process-leve manifestations of how firm-level strategies are executed (Tallon, 2008, p. 255). By employing this conceptualization, Tallon (2008) finds that alignment enhances performance. However, follow-up research reports a nonsignificant effect of process-level IT alignment on performance (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Extant research suggests that mixed results could be due to contextual differences between studies or the lack of control variables in the analysis (Chan & Reich, 2007). It is unclear, however, whether inconsistent findings of the effect of alignment on performance could be attributed to the different ways in which IT alignment has been conceptualized.

The purpose of this paper is to extend our understanding of inconsistent results in IT alignment research. We first examine mixed findings reported in firm-level and process-level alignment studies. We find that there is a paucity of empirical evidence as to the robustness of process-level IT alignment in capturing direct performance effects of alignment. We also find that several different conceptualizations of IT alignment and performance have been used to test the relationship between alignment and performance. This suggests that mixed findings could be a reflection of differences in conceptualization rather than differences in contextual conditions. To explore this argument, we then empirically investigate whether alternative conceptualizations of IT alignment lead to different conclusions when testing the same theory under the same conditions. Specifically, we investigate the following research question: can firmlevel IT alignment and process-level IT alignment yield different conclusions when testing the same theory under the same conditions?

This paper shows that firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment yield different conclusions when testing the relationship between alignment and performance. Using data from a survey of 120 firms, we show that firm-level IT alignment has a significant positive effect on performance, while process-level IT alignment, does not affect performance. We also show that differences in the strategic orientation of firms can help explain inconsistent results between firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment. While previous research suggests that these two conceptualizations of alignment yield consistent results (Tallon, 2008; Gerow et al, 2014b), our study shows that they are not interchangeable and that the choice of conceptualization can mean the difference between accepting and rejecting a theory. We discuss our findings in light of prior IT alignment literature, examine what could explain inconsistent results across IT alignment studies, and identify opportunities for future research.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section examines past empirical research on IT alignment and performance. Then, we describe the research method used to investigate whether different conceptualizations of IT alignment yield different conclusions when explaining performance. The next section discusses data analysis and presents our results. Finally, we assess the contributions of the study, its limitations, and opportunities for further research, before presenting our conclusions.

## Literature review

IT alignment has been the subject of extensive research over the past two decades (Chan & Reich, 2007; Coltman et al, 2015). In this section, we examine existing empirical evidence of the relationship between IT alignment and performance. To be eligible for consideration, a study must provide an explicit definition for IT alignment, test its effects on performance, and appear between 1995 and 2015 in one of the AIS Senior Scholars' Basket of Eight Journals (AIS, 2011), i.e., MIS Quarterly, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Research, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Information Systems Journal. Studies that do not investigate the effect of alignment on performance (e.g., Hussin et al, 2002; Avison et al, 2004; Newkirk et al, 2008; Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015) were excluded. After examining evidence, we discuss firm-level IT alignment and the emerging process-level perspective to IT alignment. A summary of the literature is provided in Table 1.

The literature has explored various ways to conceptualize IT alignment, measure the construct and investigate its performance implications. A review of the studies in Table 1 reveals four key observations. First, IT alignment has been typically conceptualized as a firm-level construct that captures the extent of fit or congruence between IT and business strategy. This construct is also referred to in prior literature as strategic integration or intellectual alignment (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Gerow et al, 2014a). It conceives of strategy as a holistic organizational-level (or organizational unit-level) phenomenon. This conceptualization has been employed in previous research to investigate the alignment between an organization’s IT and business strategy (e.g., Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Preston & Karahanna, 2009) and between the IT and business strategy of market-facing organizational units (e.g., Chan et al, 1997; McLaren et al, 2011). However, the literature has also employed a process-level perspective to IT alignment. This conceptualization captures the alignment between IT and the primary business processes that execute the business strategy (Tallon, 2008). It is similar in nature to Henderson & Venkatraman’s (1993) notion of operational integration as it focuses on the links between IT and business processes. Both operational integration and process-level IT alignment emphasize the internal coherence between business processes and the IT function. Yet they are not the same, notably because operational integration – which is also referred to as operational alignment (Gerow et al, 2014a) – captures the link between IT infrastructure and business infrastructure in addition to the links between IT and business processes.

Second, IT alignment has been measured both directly and indirectly. Direct measurements of alignment are based on Likert-type measurement scales (Yayla & Hu,

2012; Gerow et al, 2014a) while indirect measurements are usually based on one of three contingency fit approaches: a theoretically defined match between IT and strategy variables, a moderation score based on the interaction between IT and strategy variables, and a profile deviation score based on the absolute distance between actual alignment and an ideal alignment profile (Chan et al, 1997; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Coltman et al, 2015). The matching approach focuses on whether IT and business strategy have a shared goal and can be used to generate a binary alignment score, in which case a firm is either aligned or misaligned (Palmer & Markus, 2000). In contrast, profile deviation and moderation assess the extent of alignment. Profile deviation is calculated as the Euclidian or absolute distance from an ideal alignment profile, while a moderation score is calculated by multiplying the scores of the IT and strategy variables (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Tallon, 2008).

Third, the literature has employed many different performance indicators to test the relationship between alignment and performance. For example, previous studies have employed both self-reported and archival data to measure performance. Some of these studies investigate performance relative to competitors while others capture indicators of absolute performance. As the above discussion demonstrates, the literature has also employed different conceptualizations and measures of IT alignment to examine the effect of alignment on performance. These conceptualizations and measures are summarized in Table 2.

Fourth, the literature has generated mixed findings. Although some studies employing a firm-level conceptualization of IT alignment report a nonsignificant effect of alignment on performance most studies find a significant positive effect (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007; Yayla & Hu, 2012; Gerow et al, 2014a; Wu et al, 2015). In contrast, research employing a processlevel conceptualization is more inconsistent in its findings of the effect of alignment on performance. For example, Tallon (2008) finds a significant effect of process-level IT alignment on performance but Tallon & Pinsonneault (2011) find no significant effect. Also, in his study of the effects of alignment on the performance of firms in the banking industry, Tallon (2010) finds no significant effect of process-level IT alignment on the performance of large banks.

A recent meta-analysis by Gerow et al (2014b) indicates that IT alignment, despite differences in conceptualizations, is positively related to performance outcomes such as productivity, customer benefit, and financial performance. However, Gerow et al (2014b) note that some of their conclusions are based on a small number of empirical studies and call for additional research on the relationship between alignment and performance outcomes. As our examination of the IT alignment literature indicates, the firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment have generated mixed results. This suggests that a greater understanding of how these conceptualizations differ and how they compare when explaining performance might help explain inconsistent results in IT alignment research.

## Firm-level IT alignment

While most firm-level studies investigate the alignment between a given business strategy type and the firm’s IT strategy type (Chan et al, 1997; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Chan et al, 2006), researchers have also conceptualized alignment based on the organization’s pattern of deployment of IT resources to support the business strategy (Croteau & Bergeron, 2001; Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007). This approach places less emphasis on generic strategy types and focuses instead on the portfolio of IT applications needed to support the actual business strategy (Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007; Coltman et al, 2015).

As previously noted, firm-level IT alignment has been measured directly, based on measurement scales (Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Yayla & Hu, 2012; Gerow et al, 2014a) and also indirectly based on contingency fit approaches, in particular, matching, moderation, and profile deviation (Cragg et al, 2002; Hussin et al, 2002; Chan et al, 2006; Raymond & Croteau, 2009). These indirect measures have generated some inconsistent findings. For example, Cragg et al (2002) found a positive effect of alignment on performance by using the moderation approach but could not find a significant effect when matching was used. Byrd et al (2006) employed the same moderation and matching approaches. However, neither approach yielded a significant effect of IT alignment on performance.

Gerdin & Greve (2004) explain that distinct contingency fit approaches, e.g., moderation, profile deviation, and matching, are neither interchangeable nor complementary. Different fit approaches differ in terms of their underlying conceptual assumptions about what constitutes alignment and can generate inconsistent results even when applied to the same data. As an alternative to indirect alignment measures that have been criticized in past research (Iivari, 1992; Edwards, 1994; Meilich, 2006; Gerdin & Greve, 2004; Cheˆnevert & Tremblay, 2009), the literature has begun to employ Likert-type measurement scales to capture the state of firm-level IT alignment directly. A growing body of literature suggests that these measures are suitable for examining the performance implications of alignment (Newkirk et al, 2008; Bradley et al, 2012; Yayla & Hu, 2012; Karahanna & Preston, 2013; Gerow et al, 2014a). For instance, Bradley et al (2012) show that alignment affects business agility while Yayla & Hu (2012), Karahanna & Preston (2013), and Gerow et al (2014a) find that alignment enhances performance.

## Process-level IT alignment

The extensive literature into firm-level IT alignment reveals that alignment is an important antecedent of performance. However, researchers have recently argued that a process-level perspective to alignment adds greater realism by shedding additional light on the performance implications of alignment (Tallon, 2008, 2012). This emerging perspective is grounded in the widely accepted assumption that differences will often be found between intended strategy (what firms intend to do) and realized strategy (what firms actually do) (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985; Mintzberg, 1987). Realized strategy is enacted through business processes where the impact of IT is most likely to create value. The process-level perspective is consistent with the extant strategy literature, in which scholars argue that business processes are the building blocks of strategy and not generic typologies, plans, or structures (Stalk et al, 1992; Ray et al, 2004; Bower & Gilbert, 2007).

The process-level perspective views actual strategy as a series of business processes and operationalizes IT alignment in the processes that create value. For example, Tallon (2008) measures alignment across five primary value chain processes: supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, sales and marketing, and customer relations. He employs the traditional profile deviation and moderation approaches to operationalize process-level IT alignment. The empirical evidence in the evolving literature suggests that the process-level perspective is a promising avenue for further research (Tallon, 2008, 2010, 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). For example, by looking at how IT can support business processes that execute business strategy, Tallon (2008) finds that process-level IT alignment enhances performance.

However, follow-up research employing this conceptualization found no direct effect of alignment on performance (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Instead, it reports that the effect of alignment on performance is indirect only and mediated by organizational agility. Regardless, the lack of a direct effect of process-level IT alignment on performance is not consistent with the recent literature on firm-level IT alignment, which has generally found a significant direct effect of alignment on performance (Chan et al, 2006; Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007; Yayla & Hu, 2012; Gerow et al, 2014a; Wu et al, 2015). It is unclear, however, whether mixed results could be attributed to the different ways in which IT alignment has been conceptualized. For instance, we know little about how process- and firm-level conceptualizations of IT alignment compare when explaining performance. This suggests that further research is warranted to examine whether differences in conceptualizations affect theory testing.

## Research method

## Data collection

Data was collected from a sample of firms based in the United States (42%), Germany (40%), and Australia (18%). The firms contacted were randomly sourced from S&P Compustat and contact lists maintained by researchers at the Center for Information Systems Research at

MIT and the Center for HR Information Systems at Bamburg University. We identified a competent key informant as a chief information officer (CIO) or, if unknown, an IT executive of a key market-facing business unit [also referred to as strategic business unit (SBU)]. In this study, the unit of analysis was a key business unit within the organization rather than the organization as a whole (Chan et al, 1997; Chan, 2002). Given that an organization may consider itself a cost leader in some markets and a product leader in others, targeting key market-facing business units helps ensure meaningful assessments of strategic orientation (Venkatraman, 1989). This is in line with prior research examining a business unit as a single-business firm competing in a specific product-market space (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). From the 1200 firms contacted, we received 141 responses to our survey questionnaire, yielding an initial response rate of 12%. Of these responses, 21 were discarded due to incomplete data, resulting in 120 usable responses and an overall response rate of 10%. While low, this is comparable to other studies of IT alignment where respondents are senior executives (Kearns & Lederer, 2000, 2003; Bergeron et al, 2004; Gerow et al, 2014a).

The participating firms operated in a variety of sectors: financial services (19%), electronics (19%), manufacturing (17%), wholesale and retail trade (12%), energy (11%), and various other sectors (22%) including engineering and logistics. The median business unit in our data had 650 employees and accounted for 40% of consolidated sales. All respondents were senior managers, with 80% of respondents in a corporate CIO position and the remaining in a business unit IT executive role. On average, the respondents had been in the same position for 8 years and worked at the same organization for 12 years. To ensure validity of our research results, we analyzed our sample in terms of country of origin and respondent type. A multivariate analysis of variance indicated no significant differences in terms of respondent type and country of origin.

## Measures

We developed a questionnaire to collect data. All measures were refined using qualitative feedback derived from a pilot test of six senior executives and two academics at the Center for Information Systems Research at MIT. The measures are provided in the Appendix.

In accordance with a growing body of literature that measures firm-level IT alignment directly (Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Bradley et al, 2012; Yayla & Hu, 2012; Gerow et al, 2014a), we developed a measurement scale for firmlevel IT alignment. The scale captures the extent to which the IT portfolio enables the actual business strategy. To measure process-level IT alignment, we employed the profiledeviation approach described in Tallon (2008). This approach yields a robust and easily understandable measure of the distance between actual alignment and an ideal profile, which reflects the level of IT use that ensures perfect alignment for the current business strategy (Tallon, 2012). First, a baseline ideal IT use profile was defined to specify the extent to which the IT portfolio should ideally be used in critical areas of the value chain. The IT use profile provided by Tallon (2008) was employed. Second, strategic orientation was measured to compute IT use profiles. Third, an ideal IT use profile was computed for each firm based on its strategic orientation and the baseline ideal profile. Fourth, the extent to which IT is actually used to support the primary value chain processes was measured. Finally, the absolute deviation between actual IT use and ideal IT use was computed for each process. Following Tallon (2008), the individual scores were averaged to create an index for process-level IT alignment.

As shown in Table 1, past literature has measured performance in many different ways. Following Powell & Dent-Micallef (1997), we devised a five-item scale containing questions about market share, revenues, revenue growth, and profitability relative to competitors. This approach is consistent with prior IT alignment research investigating relative measures of performance (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Yayla & Hu, 2012; Gerow et al, 2014a). Other studies that use this scale have reported its robustness (Kim et al, 2011). We also collected archival data on return on assets and return on equity for a subset of firms in our sample to cross-validate our measure of performance. Finally, three control variables were used to account for differences in firm size, industry type, and ownership structure.

## Data analysis and results

The data analysis was conducted using partial least squares (PLS), a structural equation modeling technique that uses a principal-component-based estimation approach (Chin, 1998). While covariance-based structural equation modeling is well suited to confirming well-established theories (Hair et al, 2012), prior research suggests that PLS is advantageous when analyzing predictive research models (Wetzels et al, 2009; Hair et al, 2011).

## Measurement validation

In accordance with the prior literature, we investigated non-response bias, common method bias, and convergent and discriminant validity. To investigate nonresponse bias, we analyzed our sample using the extrapolation procedure in Armstrong & Overton (1977). No significant differences were found between early and late respondents (Wilks’ lambda = 0.97; F = 0.62; N/S), suggesting that non-response bias is not a major concern. To assess common method bias, we applied Harmon’s one-factor test (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). No dominant factor emerged. However, as Podsakoff et al (2003) note, the one-factor test is relatively insensitive and they strongly recommend designing the questionnaire itself to reduce common method bias, albeit injecting a note of caution that scale validity should not be sacrificed for the sake of reducing this bias. We applied procedural recommendations for questionnaire design, including protecting respondent anonymity and separating the scale items used to measure constructs that were part of our study by blocks of questions relating to other constructs not part of this study. Within the blocks relating to the modeled constructs, some items had the directionality of their scales reversed to encourage careful answering. As Podsakoff et al (2003) note, these steps should further alleviate concerns with common method bias.

We also investigated the reliability, internal consistency and discriminant validity of each measure included in the study. To assess the reliability of each reflective measure, we examined how each item related to the latent constructs. All the loadings for the measures in the study are significant (p \ 0.01) and load more highly on their intended construct than on others. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. ## Results of additional analysis

When we consider the above results in light of prior literature, two interesting insights emerge. First, Tallon (2008) finds that the relationship between process-level IT alignment and performance varies between firms pursuing different strategic orientations. Specifically, he finds that process-level IT alignment is positively correlated with the performance of firms pursuing customer intimacy and product leadership (i.e., firms with emphasis on differentiation through customer focus or innovation), but it is not correlated with the performance of firms pursuing operational excellence (i.e., firms with emphasis on low cost and efficiency). Similar mixed results have been reported in firm-level IT alignment studies (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Chan et al, 2006). This suggests that the strategic orientation of firms could help explain inconsistent findings between firm- and processlevel conceptualizations of IT alignment.

Second, Gerow et al (2014b) find that in some circumstances indirect fit-based measures of alignment are associated with lower alignment-performance correlations when compared to direct measures of alignment (i.e., Likert-type measurement scales). While the use of different types of measures to operationalize firm-level IT alignment has generally yielded consistent findings, i.e., that alignment affects performance (Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007; Karahanna & Preston, 2013; Wu et al, 2015), existing literature suggests that the use of different types of measures to operationalize process-level alignment might yield inconsistent findings. For example, Tallon & Pinsonneault (2011) employ an indirect fit-based measure to operationalize process-level IT alignment. They find no significant effect of alignment on performance. In contrast, Gerow et al (2014a) employ a direct measure to operationalize the alignment between business and IT infrastructures and processes. They find that this process-level IT alignment construct has a significant positive effect on performance.

We conducted additional analyses on our data to investigate whether (a) the strategic orientation of firms and (b) the way process-level IT alignment is operationalized can help explain mixed results. Specifically, we tested the effects of firm-level IT alignment and process-level IT alignment on the performance of firms pursuing different strategic orientations. Following Tallon (2008), we classified the firms in our sample according to the amount of points respondents allocated to each strategic orientation (see Appendix). In this way, we classified our sample as follows: 70 firms were pursuing differentiation (product leadership and customer intimacy firms) and 50 were pursuing operational excellence. Then, we tested two separate regression models in which the performance of firms pursuing different strategic orientations can be affected by alignment. The two models estimated the effects of firm-level IT alignment and process-level IT alignment, respectively. We tested these models by using the PROCESS macro for SPSS with 1000 bootstrap samples (Hayes, 2013).

In addition, since the IT use construct (used in this study for calculating the process-level IT alignment index) can itself be thought of as a type of process-level or operational alignment (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Gerow et al, 2014b), we tested a third regression model in which the performance of firms pursuing different strategic orientations can be affected by process-level IT use. The IT use construct is measured directly through a Likert-type measurement scale. This allows us to examine whether an indirect measure of process-level IT alignment and a direct measure of process-level IT use yield consistent findings. The results of additional analysis are shown in Table 4.

## Discussion

When discussing the development of theory in the IS discipline, Weber (2003) states that ‘The most fundamental components of a theory are its constructs’ and that ‘the ways we conceptualize them are likely to have an important impact on the contribution to knowledge we make via our theory’ (p. vii). By investigating how firm- and processlevel conceptualizations of IT alignment explain the same performance construct under the same conditions, this study shows empirically that distinct conceptualizations of alignment can generate inconsistent findings when testing the same theory. It also shows that differences in the strategic orientation of firms can help explain these findings. An understanding of mixed results in IT alignment research is important not only for assessing what we know and don’t know about the performance implications of alignment but also for enhancing the cumulative development of IT alignment theory.

## Implications for research

Our contribution to research follows a model-theoretic approach to knowledge accumulation, which is the dominant perspective in the IS discipline (Weber, 2003; Harris et al, 2013). According to this perspective, theories allow researchers to develop context-specific models that capture phenomena, while empirical research is undertaken to investigate testable claims about those phenomena. Thus, empirical research is not designed to prove whether a particular theory is true or false as proposed by the law-statement perspective to knowledge accumulation (Harris et al, 2013). Instead, it contributes to knowledge accumulation by building on what is already known to demonstrate that particular phenomena or relationships between phenomena fit in a particular context. Accordingly, the current study does not claim that a given conceptualization of IT alignment will always lead to accepting or rejecting the theory that alignment affects performance. Instead, it argues that differences in conceptualization can mean the difference between accepting and rejecting a theory. The model-theoretic approach is visible in Burton-Jones & Straub’s (2006) reconceptualization of the prominent ‘system usage construct. They recognize that changes in conceptualization affect the conceptual meaning of the phenomenon being studied and can ultimately affect theory testing.

This study shows that the firm-level conceptualization of IT alignment and the emerging process-level conceptualization lead to different conclusions of the effect of alignment on performance. According to the modeltheoretic approach to knowledge accumulation, this result is explainable. Specifically, it indicates that a particular conceptualization of IT alignment is not better or worse. It allows for a deeper appreciation of how differences in conceptualizations influence theory testing and the need for researchers to justify their choice of a conceptualization (Suddaby, 2010). Since construct conceptualization delineates the phenomenon of interest, alternative conceptualizations of IT alignment capture distinct phenomena and therefore can lead to different conclusions when testing a theory. This helps to explain the mixed results reported in the IT alignment literature, and enhances our understanding of the way different perspectives to IT alignment (i.e., firm-level and processlevel perspectives) influence performance.

We also show that mixed results between firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment can be due to differences in firms’ strategic orientations. In particular, depending on sample characteristics (e.g., most firms in the sample pursue operational excellence or differentiation), these conceptualizations can generate either consistent or inconsistent findings of the effect of alignment on performance. While the two conceptualizations yield consistent findings for firms pursuing operational excellence, they generate inconsistent findings for those firms pursuing differentiation through product leadership or customer intimacy. This implies that strategic orientation plays an important role in our understanding of the effects of IT alignment and the conditions under which different conceptualizations of IT alignment generate mixed results.

When we examine the effects of firm-level IT alignment on the performance of firms pursuing different strategic orientations, we also find mixed results. Specifically, the effect of firm-level IT alignment on performance is significant for firms pursuing differentiation, but it is not significant for firms pursing operational excellence. This provides further evidence that differences in the strategic orientation of firms can help explain mixed results in firm-level IT alignment studies (Sabherwal & Chan. 2001: Chan et al. 2006).

Another factor that might help explain mixed results is the proliferation of several different measures of performance. As shown in Table 1, there is variation not only in the performance indicators used but also the types of measures, i.e., self-reported, archival, relative to competitors, and absolute. Similar findings have been reported in the management literature. For instance, in a review of the operationalization of organizational performance, Richard et al (2009) found that among 213 papers identified as including a performance variable, 207 different measures of performance have been used. They conclude: ‘What stands out from this examination is the lack of clarity in the theoretical definition of performance and the absence of methodological consistency in the formulation of the construct(s) used.’ (p. 719) …‘Stated simply, we may not be measuring the performance to which managers are managing’ (p. 721).

The above discussion suggests that inconsistency in the way performance is operationalized affects our ability to understand the link between alignment and performance. For example, IT alignment studies have employed four different types of measures to operationalize performance: self-reported measures of performance relative to competitors (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Cragg et al, 2002); self-reported measures relative to the industry performance average (Yayla & Hu, 2012); self-reported, absolute (i.e., not relative), measures of performance (Palmer & Markus, 2000; Byrd et al, 2006); and archival, absolute, measures of performance (Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007; Karahanna & Preston, 2013). An important implication of the proliferation of different types of performance measures is that it is difficult to compare and contrast results across studies. Also, our examination of the literature indicates that different measures of performance are not interchangeable and can lead to different conclusions about the link between alignment and performance (Yayla & Hu, 2012).

## Limitations and suggestions for future research

When assessing the contributions of our study, it is important to note its limitations – and also the opportunities for further research. The two alignment constructs investigated in this study can be operationalized in different ways, and further research could examine whether alternative operationalizations yield similar findings. For example, this study employed the profile deviation approach proposed by Tallon (2008) to generate an index for process-level IT alignment, where individual business processes are weighted equally to create a proxy measure for alignment. Future research could consider the extent to which different strategies place greater importance on certain processes and less importance on others to create a weighted index for process-level IT alignment (Tallon, 2008, 2012). A key step toward creating a weighted index for process-level IT alignment is to develop weighting schemes that can account for differences in strategic orientations and other organizational and industry contingencies that can affect the relative importance of business processes across firms.

Given that the effect of process-level IT alignment on performance is not significant, future research might seek to revisit or extend this conceptualization to enhance its ability to explain performance. Tallon et al (2016) are currently investigating this issue. They propose that the notion of digital options, whereby IT resources are held in reserve by organizations while they wait for a suitable market opportunity, can extend our understanding of process-level IT alignment and its performance implications. Further research could examine the mechanisms through which organizations leverage digital options and whether they facilitate synergistic relationships between IT and business processes. Existing literature suggests that the potential of resources such as IT to improve performance is conditional on functional complementarities or cospecialization between IT and other organizational resources (Wade & Hulland, 2004). The notion of cospecialization considers alignment as a multifaceted phenomenon based on the mutual relationship between distinct parties (Teece, 1986, 2007). As IT resources become increasingly embedded within business processes, cospecialization could provide a valuable perspective for investigating the effects of IT alignment at the process level.

It is also important for future research to investigate other variables that could help explain mixed results in IT alignment research. As suggested above, different types of performance measures (e.g., self-reported relative, selfreported absolute, archival absolute) might generate inconsistent findings of the relationship between alignment and performance. However, our understanding of how IT alignment relates to different types of performance measures and the conditions under which these measures yield inconsistent findings is still limited. Another factor that might help explain mixed results is variance in environmental uncertainty. The literature has only recently started to investigate the role of environmental uncertainty on the link between alignment and performance. Notably, Yayla & Hu (2012) show that the effect of firm-level IT alignment on financial performance is significant when uncertainty is high, but it is not significant when uncertainty is low. Future research could investigate whether firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment yield consistent or inconsistent findings under varying conditions of environmental uncertainty.

Our synthesis of the IT alignment literature and empirical study suggest that the relationship between alignment and performance is more nuanced than one might think from the early literature. To further our understanding of the IT alignment phenomenon, researchers might seek to develop theories that account for the different roles of process-level IT alignment and firm-level IT alignment in creating value for firms. Given that firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment are not interchangeable, future research could investigate whether these conceptualizations play complementary roles in the nomological net leading to firm performance. In doing this, it would be important to understand whether the pathways through which process-level IT alignment and firmlevel IT alignment affect performance differ. For example, Tallon (2010) shows that process-level IT alignment has a significant positive effect on a process-level measure of IT business value, which in turn improves firm performance. He also shows that this process-level outcome of alignment (i.e., IT business value) completely mediates the effect of process-level IT alignment on firm performance. What stands out from this discussion is that the mechanisms through which different conceptualizations of IT alignment impact performance can vary, which might help explain why firm-level IT alignment and process-level IT alignment lead to different conclusions about the effect of align ment on performance.

## Conclusions

The literature has conceptualized IT alignment in many different ways to test theories about the effects of alignment. While those conceptualizations differ in the scope of the phenomenon being captured, the literature has yet to ask whether and how changes in conceptual ization affect theory testing. The primary motivation of this study was the recognition that mixed results reported in the IT alignment literature might be due to differences in conceptualizations – which, if ignored, can ultimately affect the cumulative development of theory. Our study shows that firm- and process-level conceptualizations of IT alignment are not interchangeable and that the choice of conceptualization can mean the difference between accepting and rejecting a theory. It also shows that differences in the strategic orientation of firms can help explain mixed results. We hope that this paper will further debate on the importance of explaining mixed results in IT alignment research and building a cumulative body of knowledge.
