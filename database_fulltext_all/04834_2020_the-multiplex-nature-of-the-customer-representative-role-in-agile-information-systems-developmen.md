# THE MULTIPLEX NATURE OF THE CUSTOMER REPRESENTATIVE ROLE IN AGILE INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT<sup>1</sup>

Likoebe M. Maruping

Center for Digital Innovation, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303 U.S.A. {lmaruping@gmail.com}

Sabine Matook

UQ Business School, University of Queensland,

Brisbane, QLD, 4072 AUSTRALIA {s.matook@business.uq.edu.au}

It is well acknowledged that close collaboration with the customer serves as the lynchpin to ensuring that agile information systems development (ISD) teams produce the right software within mutually agreed targets. In several agile ISD methods, this emphasis on close collaboration is enacted through the role of a designated customer representative (CR). The agile ISD literature has recognized the behaviors in this role to be inherently complementary and contradicting in nature, presenting a challenge to whoever occupies the role and hampering their ability to add value to the project. How do CRs manage these challenges and why do they do so in a particular manner? Unfortunately, there has been little theory to answer these questions. In this research, we explore and theorize about this phenomenon by leveraging role multiplexity as a theoretical lens in making sense of the behavior of CRs in agile ISD. Results suggest that the CR role is multiplex, exhibiting multiple manifestations with different orientations. We develop a theoretical model that articulates the instantiation of these role manifestations and the mechanisms that enable the CR role to remain intact while managing these challenges. The theoretical model highlights the CR role in agile ISD as being dynamic and multi-oriented.

Keywords: Agile ISD, software development, customer representative, customer participation, role theory, multiplexity, elasticity, case study, qualitative research

## Introduction

Agile information systems development (ISD) methods advocate the active and continuous participation of customers in the ISD process to create software that meets customer needs (Matook and Maruping 2014; Ramesh et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2012). The close collaboration equips the ISD team to understand the customer’s specific needs in creating a relevant functional system (Markus and Mao 2004; Mathiassen et al.

2007). In prominent agile methods such as eXtreme Programing (XP) (Beck 1999) and Scrum (Schwaber and Beedle 2005), this close, ongoing collaboration is instantiated in the designated role of customer representative (CR) (Fowler and Highsmith 2001; Larman 2004). The CR is tasked with con tinuously contributing to the creation of software outputs; typically in the form of providing requirements to, and approving changes made by, the agile ISD team (Beck and Andres 2004; Koskela and Abrahamsson 2004; Martin et al. 2004; Matook and Maruping 2014).

This research note zooms in on the specific phenomenon of CRs who are expected to manage numerous role expectations that are complementary and contradictory in nature. We draw on role theory (Biddle 1979; House and Rizzo 1972; Kahn et al. 1981) and introduce the novel theoretical concept of role multiplexity (De Luque and Sommer 2000; Leonardi et al. 2016; Rogan 2014) to the domain of agile ISD. Role multiplexity recognizes that one individual may occupy a plurality of roles that have various orientations (De Luque and Sommer 2000). By introducing role multiplexity, we broaden the concept of role conflict (Merton 1957) to incorporate orientations of complementary and contradicting expectations that emerge as challenges for the individual occupying a respective role.

It is widely recognized that a CR has to manage a large number of expectations from the agile ISD team and the customer (Cao et al. 2009; Hoda et al. 2011; Ramesh et al. 2010; Vidgen and Wang 2009; Wang et al. 2012). On one hand, the CR needs to ensure that the customer receives a software solution that returns value on the investments made by meeting their business needs. On the other hand, the ISD team relies on the CR for inputs about the problem domain, prioritization of requirements regarding customer value and potential risk areas, and acceptance of requirements to confirm they have been met (Ghobadi and Mathiassen 2015). At the core, we theorize these role expectations as assisting the agile ISD team to understand and prioritize customer needs and as assessing how well the needs are met by the software created. These expectations essentially require CRs to handle different and opposing orientations—one toward the agile ISD team and the other toward the customer.

The current literature recognizes that the assisting and assessing expectations unfold as CR challenges due to their inherent complementarities and contradictions that hamper the CR’s ability to maximally contribute to agile ISD work. Various empirical studies have reported on CR challenges by depicting the CRs as being unresponsive, unavailable, or unable to juggle the role expectations (e.g., Hoda et al. 2011; Ramesh et al. 2010). Complementarities pose a challenge because, as prior research notes, although assisting and assessing are complementary in nature (with assessing setting forth the criteria that will be used to evaluate deliverables so that assisting can be directed toward achieving those quality standards), it cannot be taken for granted that they are being leveraged in a value-adding manner. Furthermore, execution of assisting and assessing behaviors manifests as contradictions with regard to representing the interests of different parties within an ISD project (Ghobadi and Mathiassen 2015; Mähring et al. 2008; Wiener et al. 2016). Indeed, contradictions can stand in the way of CRs’ value-adding responsibility despite existing complementarities. This paradox puts CRs in the position of needing to balance their management of contradictions and complementarities in ways that enable the role to add value to the ISD project.

In short, the agile ISD literature has clearly defined the CR role and its challenges (Beck 1999; Martin et al. 2004; Matook and Maruping 2014; Wang et al. 2012) and has established a good understanding of the assisting and assessing challenges faced by CRs (Matook and Maruping 2014). However, several phenomenologically important aspects remain unresolved in this literature. First, there is currently no theory to explain why the CR role is enacted in very different ways by occupants of the role. Second, there is very little theory to explain how CRs go about addressing the role challenges. Third, the theoretical mechanisms by which CR role occupants maintain coherence in the role’s meaning and purpose remain unknown. Consequently, this research seeks to augment understanding of the CR role and is motivated by the following research question: How and why do CRs in agile ISD adopt particular approaches to managing the complementarities and contradictions they experience in their role?

To answer this question, we draw upon, and integrate, two theoretical concepts that speak to different enactments of roles and to the dynamics of maintaining coherence in the purpose of roles. We leverage role multiplexity as a lens to shed light on behaviors of individuals who occupy multiple roles of varying orientations (De Luque and Sommer 2000; Leonardi et al. 2016). As these varying orientations can test the coherence of the CR role, we draw upon the theoretical concept of elasticity (Kreiner et al. 2015) to articulate how the role adapts to situational demands while remaining intact. Together, these two theoretical lenses have the potential to help address the important and, as yet, unresolved issues that motivate our research question.

In this research, we inductively build theory (Eisenhardt 1989) through a multisite embedded case study of agile ISD teams. The framework elaborates the unfolding process by which CRs come to adopt a particular approach to balancing the role challenges. We advance the notion that even with a clear definition of what it means to be a CR in agile ISD, the specific instantiation of that role can vary (i.e., take shape in multiple forms) and is situated within the conditions experienced by the role’s occupant. Here specific instantiations are shaped by the challenges associated with assisting and assessing. These responsibilities reside within the same role and yet differ substantially in their orientation and are potentially opposing, rendering the CR role multiplex. This CR role multiplexity is viewed as an essential dialectic in that multiple, different role manifestations threaten to tear apart the meaning of the CR role by rendering it incoherent and yet these same manifestations are also fundamental to fulfilling the challenges associated with assisting and assessing that are the primary responsibility of the role.

## Conceptual Background

## Agile ISD Methods and the CR Role

Agile ISD is an iterative and incremental approach to software development (Baskerville et al. 2001; Highsmith and Cockburn 2001; Maruping et al. 2009; Sarker and Sarker 2009) that advocates active and continuous participation of customers in the process (Matook and Maruping 2014; Nerur et al. 2005). With such participation, ISD teams and customers are able to achieve and maintain timely feedback loops, continuous contribution to the software, and a shared understanding of priorities (Chakraborty et al. 2010; Ghobadi and Mathiassen 2015). The agile method achieves a close ISD team-customer partnership through the role of CRs—a concept known in Scrum as product owner and in XP as on-site customer (Wang et al. 2012).<sup>2</sup>

Drawing on prior research (Beck and Andres 2004; Koskela and Abrahamsson 2004; Martin et al. 2004; Matook and Maruping 2014), we offer the following definition of CRs: In agile ISD, a CR is an individual from the customer sphere (e.g., department or company) who closely collaborates with the ISD team by providing requirements and other development-relevant information, and has decision-making authority over requirements regarding approval and change requests. The CR can be a real user who uses the software once it is implemented (Beck and Andres 2004) or a proxy for end-users when they are external to the organization (Highsmith 2004). In the case of in-house ISD projects, the CR can be a representative from the user community, a product manager, or product champion with domain expertise from the department that requested the software (Mohammadi et al. 2008). Original conceptualizations describe the CR as being co-located with the ISD team (i.e., 100% availability) (Beck and Andres 2004). However, in practice, the CR role has manifested in different forms; either fully co-located, partially available, or not co-located at all (Martin et al. 2004).

The agile ISD literature has developed an extensive list of expected CR behaviors (Beck 1999; Sillitti et al. 2005). Some of these behaviors are similar to those already identified in the traditional ISD literature on user participation (Baroudi et al. 1986; Ives and Olson 1984; McKeen et al. 1994; Olson and Ives 1981). For instance, acceptance testing by the CR is recognized in traditional ISD as beta-testing (MacCormack et al. 2001). Other behaviors are somewhat unique to agile ISD. For example, CRs are responsible for prioritizing story cards for each iteration (Sharp and Robinson 2008). Considering the extensive list of potential CR behaviors, CRs face challenges in remaining responsive to the ISD team and the customer (e.g., Hoda et al. 2011; Ramesh et al. 2010).

Based on the existing literature, we classified the CR behaviors into two broad categories that capture the expectations on the CR: (1) assisting behaviors by providing information and making decisions (e.g., Hoda et al. 2011; Ramesh et al. 2010; Sharp and Robinson 2008), and (2) assessing behaviors by testing the software for acceptance (e.g., Cao et al. 2009; Conboy 2009; Vidgen and Wang 2009). Table 1 provides an overview of the assisting and assessing behaviors attributed to CRs in the literature.

In sum, a review of prior research emphasizes the importance of the CR role for agile ISD given the added emphasis on customer collaboration. The rich portfolio of CR behaviors grouped as assisting and assessing provides a window into the challenges associated with the CR role. However, little theory has been dedicated to understanding the CR role and why and how it is enacted in particular ways. We turn to role theory with a focus on the concept of role multiplexity to gain insight.

## Conceptual Foundations

## Role Multiplexity

Role multiplexity has its roots in role theory. Role theory is concerned with the study of patterned behavior (Biddle 1979, 1986). It seeks to understand situated behaviors of a focal actor who is embedded within a social system (Biddle 1979; Turner 1974). Role theory has been used to study phenomena in a variety of business disciplines including marketing (Heide and Wathne 2006) and management (Gittell and Douglass 2012). For IS research, Appendix A provides an overview of studies that applied role theory to understand emergent roles, associated role behaviors, and resolution of role contradictions. Open systems theory provides the foundational thinking for role-theoretic research. In essence, an entity (e.g., work group) depends on the environment to provide systems inputs that in a subsequent transformation process are changed into outputs to be released back to the environment (Kahn et al. 1964). Critical in this theoretical framework is the emphasis on human patterns of behavior that ensure the system’s goal achievement. In complex systems, the patterns can be explained through the concept of role multiplexity; that suggests a dynamic coexistence of multiple roles with different orientations (Gluckman 1962; Leonardi et al. 2016). In an organization, employees in work groups enact work roles with prescribed in-role behaviors and associated duties (Wickham and Parker 2007). The role expectations are formed by others in the organization and are explicitly formulated or learned behaviors (Floyd and Lane 2000). Early conceptions of role multiplexity capture the possibility that actors can occupy multiple roles of various role orientations (De Luque and Sommer 2000). Thus, one person may hold multiple roles in relation to others in the surrounding envi ronment depending on situational demands (Verbrugge 1979). More recent conceptualizations of multiplexity suggest instead that multiple types of relationships can exist between the same two entities (Dhanaraj and Parkhe 2006; Rogan 2014). Indeed, Shipilov et al. (2014, p. 450) define multiplexity as “the presence of multiple types of relationships between the same actors.” Viewed in the context of roles, this conceptualization of multiplexity suggests that a single role can adopt multiple types of relationships with other members of a role set. It embraces the idea of relational pluralism, which is related to, but distinct from, multiplexity. Relational pluralism is the extent to which an entity, derives its meaning and action potential from different kinds of relations with other entities (Shipilov et al. 2014). We adopt this contemporary view of multiplexity as it serves as a useful vehicle to understand how different relational orientations toward the same entity can coexist within a single role.

A role may adopt different orientations that unfold as being complementary and contradictory at the same time, creating role challenges for a role actor (House and Rizzo 1972). Role complementarities manifest as role challenges because the role actor may be unclear about how to best leverage the benefits of supplementing behaviors. When subject to a contradictory role challenge, a role actor is confronted with conflicting demands such that meeting one core expectation can only be done by failing to meet another core expectation (Perrewé et al. 2004). The role contradictions are often found in social systems due to differing values and interests of the role set (Friedman and Podolny 1992).

## Elasticity

To the extent that the meaning of an entity is indeed derived from different kinds of relations with the same set of other entities (Shipilov et al. 2014), one challenge associated with multiplexity is that it poses a threat to the meaning of a role— particularly when the underlying relational orientations are simultaneously complementary and contradicting. The theoretical concept of elasticity is useful in thinking about how the meaning of a role can remain intact under such circumstances. Elasticity in management research refers to the socially constructed capacity of an entity to stretch and grow while still maintaining its core behaviors and identity. Kreiner et al. (2015) aptly use the allegory of a rubber band, the boundaries of which can stretch or contract while the rubber band remains intact. In a similar way, elasticity allows for the boundaries of a role to dynamically stretch and adapt to situational demands while maintaining its purpose.

## A Multiplex Perspective on CRs in Agile ISD Projects

Prior research has shown that CRs face challenges in performing their role resulting from the assisting and assessing behaviors (e.g., Hoda et al. 2011; Ramesh et al. 2010). Per the definition of multiplexity, assisting and assessing represent different types of relational exchange between the same two entities—the CR and the agile ISD team. Viewed through the lens of role multiplexity, assisting and assessing represent two role-theoretic positions among which multiplex roles may manifest that are of varying orientations based on the complementary and contradictory expectations. CR role complementarities and contradictions may unfold because the assisting and assessing behaviors may supplement each other while simultaneously creating conflicts for the person serving as CR.

The expectations of performing assisting and assessing are a source of complementarity for CRs. Within an agile ISD project, CRs perform a number of behaviors as prescribed by the agile ISD method (see Table 2). For example, CRs make comments on requirements regarding their specification, clarity, priority, implementation and acceptance—all of which are part of assisting and assessing behaviors (e.g., Koskela and Abrahamsson 2004; Ramesh et al. 2010; Sharp and Robinson 2008). The assessing can be complementary to the assisting because the CR behaviors of assessing bring to light the need for further assisting behaviors (Ramesh et al. 2010). Through CR testing of the software product (assessing), incor rect conceptions and misunderstandings about requirements are exposed that point to the need for further explanations of requirements by the CR (assisting). In turn, assisting can be complementary to assessing because the inputs the CR provides are done with the evaluation criteria in mind (Martin et al. 2004). In the ISD process, the CR may reprioritize requirements (assisting) while considering their alignment with business needs. It should be noted that CRs may not be fully aware of the complementarity of their behaviors including lacking awareness of which behaviors are complementary to each other. As a consequence, CRs are challenged in taking full advantage of the complementary nature of their behaviors.

The expectations of performing assisting and assessing are also a source of contradictions for CRs. Integrating role theory with the expected CR behaviors (see Table 1), we posit that ISD teams and their customers impose fundamentally different demands on CRs that mount as conflicting voices for the role. The assisting-assessing conflict is due to the duality in serving as quality assurance and objective assessor of the project’s outcomes while simultaneously being the primary source for inputs and guidance of the software production process (Ghobadi and Mathiassen 2015; Mähring et al. 2008). According to game theoretic views (Montgomery 1998), assisting requires cooperative behaviors of the CR with the ISD team where the CR potentially grows closer with the team; but when assessing the software, the CR’s primary motivation is utility maximization and is expected to be a distant assessor of the team’s work, as one would find in arm’s length business relationships. ## Research Methodology

We conducted a multiple site embedded case study to inductively build theory on the CR role and the role challenges (Eisenhardt 1989). Case study research utilizes multiple perspectives and data sources to inform the researcher’s understanding of phenomena and “emphasize the rich, realworld context in which the phenomena” exist (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007, p. 25). We position this research as being exploratory, rather than confirmatory or descriptive (Dubé and Paré 2003; Yin 2014), and seek to build theory from case research in Eisenhardt’s sense with a positivist mindset.

## Research Design

The selection of the case study sites was based on the principles of case similarity and case variability (Kirsch 1997; Orlikowski 1993). We sought similar cases by selecting established agile ISD teams (i.e., teams that use the agile method for their development) with an in-house customer. Our focus on in-house customers was informed by the original context within which this class of methods was conceived. We anticipated that this would give us a more representative view of CRs and their behaviors because (1) the person comes from a business unit within the same company, (2) will likely go back to the same unit, and (3) transition into being a user of the delivered system. There was variability in the companies’ industry, the ISD project and the customer departments. We deemed these variations as relevant, but not extreme enough to prevent us from making cross-case comparisons while seeking to build new theory on the role of CRs in ISD projects. ## Data Collection

This section is presented in the spirit of Sarker et al.’s (2013) principle of transparency to contribute to the credibility of the research by providing “details about where, when, how, and from whom data was collected” in the text and via appendices (p. xiii). Data was collected via semi-structured interviews with a pre-developed guide (Weiss 1994) that is included as Appendix B.

Two experienced researchers interviewed 31 individuals in four agile ISD teams within the two companies (see Table 4).

All interviews were audio recorded and professionally transcribed, resulting in a total of about 650 pages of singlespaced text. This data was supplemented with extra materials that served as additional sources of evidence to strengthen the validity and robustness of our findings (Dubé and Paré 2003), including examinations of project documentation (more than 150 pages of PDF files and PowerPoints) and real-time observation of ISD team activities (e.g., participating in four stand-ups and in four pair programming sessions).

Several measures were taken to increase the rigor of our data collection thereby aiming for high quality and accuracy during the data collection process. We were particularly sensitive to the four principles of flexibility, non-direction, specificity, and range (Flick 2006; Sarker and Sarker 2009).

In analysis step 1, two researchers familiarized themselves with the data and first impressions were captured in handwritten notes. In step 2, data were formally analyzed. Therefore, data were decomposed into meaningful text segments, a process Tesch (1990) refers to as decontextualization. We used two coding techniques—descriptive coding and process coding (Saldana 2009)—to label the data segments. In step 3 (i.e., recontextualization) (Tesch 1990), we assembled the various data segments into higher-order categories based on the theoretical concepts as outlined in Table 1. As a final analysis step, we needed to further aggregate the codes about the CRs’ behaviors into higher order themes.

We sought to understand the CR role and how assisting and assessing were handled. First, the CR role behaviors identified in data analysis step 3 were compared with our preexisting knowledge (see Table 2). Through the comparison, it became clear that CRs perform additional behaviors over and above those reflected in the literature to date. Second, we aimed to understand why CRs perform these “other” behaviors, which we labeled as unique CR role behaviors. To better understand these unique behaviors, we turned to the surrounding conditions as they had an enabling role in facilitating the behaviors, but also in overcoming the CR contradictions and leveraging the complementarities. We identified factors in the surrounding conditions of a particular CR to inform the emergence of the unique CR behavior. Through this integration, we became aware of different ways in which the CR handles the assisting and assessing challenges.

In a similar creative process, all unique behaviors were identified as manifesting particular CR roles. We identified three behaviors that represent their own role manifestations (argues with team à deterrence; suggests own ideas à immersion; delegates responsibilities à removal). One behavior was split into two roles (hiding information from the customer à ally; hiding information from the team à guard) because the hiding was done in two different ways, toward the team and the customer. Finally, a number of behaviors were performed with the intent of keeping the team working on the project during difficult moments. These were combined into one behavior and captured as one role (motivating à leadership). Together they reflect the multiplex nature of the CR role.

Finally, we redirected our attention to the assisting and assessing complementarities and contradictions. Based on the findings regarding how the role manifestations balance the complementarities with the contradictions, we gained clarity about how CRs leverage the benefits of assisting and assessing while at the same time managing the contradictions between the two. This insight gave additional meaning to actions taken by CRs and consequently, we sought to understand how the elements of the environment helped shape the role enactment.

As urged by Sarker et al. (2013), we sought to enhance the study’s credibility by ensuring rigor regarding the data analysis and presentation of findings. Therefore, we employed various strategies that contribute to the trustworthiness of the research as presented in Appendix D (for details, see Creswell 2009; Denzin and Lincoln 2000; Flick 2006).

## Results

## Overview CR Role Behaviors

The results present the CR behaviors that were grouped into the three categories of (1) assisting, (2) assessing, and (3) unique CR role behaviors which are behaviors that fell under neither the assisting nor assessing category and are novel CR behaviors in relation to what is known in the literature. Next, we present the unique CR role behaviors due to their novelty. In Appendix E, we provide an overview of all CR behaviors. The unique CR role behaviors are the first component of our theoretical framework to explain how CRs manage the role challenges.

## Unique Behaviors Reflecting the Multiplex Nature of the CR Role

A first unique role behavior was the active approach of one CR (Spar\_T1) who made suggestions and followed up on how to implement software features. The CR recalls:

I’d propose a lot of ideas, and a lot of them were accepted, so we’ve done a lot of the bits and pieces in terms of change along the way.

The CR even engaged in developers’ tasks when the ISD team was struggling with the implementation of requirements; such that the CR “started working with the developers to figure out how we can actually get around this.”

A second unique behavior was the CR’s refusal to accept incorrectly or incompletely implemented requirements. Failure to implement requirements as specified can cause delays in the project schedule. In this case, the team argued that a feature was implemented per specification and that the CR now wanted the feature implemented differently. The CR, however, believed they had spelt it out clearly in the card, and they [ISD team] kept arguing that: No, it’s a change. And we say: no, it’s not.” The CR fought for it to be treated as a bug as opposed to a change request because “the implication appears to be if it’s a change, it has to get reprioritized, if it’s a bug it gets fixed, because that card is already prioritized.”

A third unique behavior shown by some CRs is that of motivating the ISD team. The CR needs to motivate and convince the team of the importance of certain features for the business against the ISD team’s belief that implementing these features would not provide any value to the software and hence, is not necessary. The CR from Werk\_T1 explains:

But it’s up to me to reassure them that there is a good commercial reason for it, and that we need to stay on target and focus on that. Even though they might think that their logic is: But if we fix this, it will be easier to fix that—but I have to change their mindset to convince them that commercially we are going down the right path.

A fourth unique behavior is the delegation of various expected assisting and assessing behaviors to the ISD team. This is contrary to what is detailed in the ISD literature. The CR from Werk\_T2 assigned various requirement-related behaviors (elaboration on requirements, detailed testing) to the ISD team. The CR is comfortable doing so because he/she has

first and foremost … trust in the project manager, so I [CR] give a very big leash and he [PM] constantly does the right thing with that leeway, he’s like the lynchpin in terms of that trust and that communication.

A fifth unique behavior is the removal of roadblocks that impair the team’s progress in a way that exceeds the ISD teams’ expected behaviors regarding CR support for resources. When performing these behaviors, the CR fulfils a strong motivational duty (CR Werk\_T1):

I will personally ensure that that works from the other angle—the commercial angle. I think they thought I was crazy, but we delivered it quite to their surprise.

A sixth unique role behavior performed by a number of CRs is the hiding or withholding of information from different targets. In Sparkasse, both CRs were withholding information from the customer:

There’s been another problem that’s come up, just this afternoon which we will have to find some other way. We may end up hiding the information just so that we are not showing something which is incorrect, we just won’t show it at all. (CR Spar\_T2).

The CR justified the withholding of information because of the team’s ability to address the problem in the next iteration. Conversely, the CR from Werk\_T1 was withholding business domain information from the ISD team:

Sometimes they don’t really understand the commercial reason for it, and that commercial reason might be something that we can’t disclose to them, something that we may have promised at a higher level that they might not be privy.

In such situations, the CR needs to manage requirements and their implementation without explaining why these requirements are necessary.

Taken together, these behaviors represent specific manifestations of the multiplex CR role. They vary in the orientation of the CR toward the interests of the ISD team versus the customer. The behaviors also reflect the stretching of the CR role to encompass behaviors that are not seen as the purview of CRs in agile ISD. Although they are outside of the CR role definition, these behaviors are enacted in fulfillment of the role’s purpose.

## Conditions Surrounding the Multiplex CR Role

We also identified a number of factors in the three categories, which capture the surrounding conditions that serve as the second component for our theoretical framework on how CRs manage the role challenges. Appendix F provides an overview of the surrounding conditions.

## Organizational Factors

CR’s Responsibility for Project Delivery and Success: CRs believed they had responsibility for project delivery and success; and only Werk\_T2’s CR believed the responsibility lies with the ISD team. Werk\_T1’s CR explicitly states:

I have a responsibility to our CEO, the Board, and our shareholders, to ensure that we will deliver a project that we say we will deliver.

Sparkasse’s CRs share full responsibility for project delivery and perceive that their departments hold them accountable for project success.

Support for the CR from the Customer Department: The support the CRs receive from their departments is multifaceted and includes the exemption from departmental duties, the ability to physically move their desk to co-locate with the ISD team, and the option to access other resources from the department. In Sparkasse, the CRs are co-located full-time and released from their daily duties, with expectations to still perform some departmental duties. In Werkstatt, the CRs are expected to perform their normal departmental job duties in addition to the CR role.

## Personal Factors

Types of CR Knowledge: Across all four sites, the CRs possessed extensive knowledge of the business, with years of experience in their respective fields. Differences emerged regarding the CRs’ technical knowledge and agile knowledge. In Sparkasse, the CRs received formal training about agile methods prior to their assignment to the team; something that was not done in Werkstatt. Regarding the technical knowledge of the CRs, the CR in Werk\_T2 puts it this way: “I’m not tech savvy, I probably will never be completely tech savvy”; which is also true for the CRs from Spar\_T2 and Werk\_T1; however, Spar\_T1 CR has “the IT background and understand how it will work and how it does work.”

CR’s Understanding of Membership in the Team: Each CR has a different view of whether or not they are a member of their ISD team. Both CRs in Spar\_T1 and Spar\_T2 felt they were full-fledged team members. Spar\_T2’s project manager also confirms that “the CR feels part of the team.” In contrast, Werkstatt’s CRs do not feel like part of the team. Werk\_T1’s CR creates some distance from the ISD team when stating: “I think I’m more of a stakeholder” but then goes on to say “but I think that’s probably, the real answer would be that … I’m very much part of that team to make sure that that project is delivered.”

## Interpersonal Factors

Communication and Interaction Frequency: The proximity of the CRs to the ISD teams, either with desks side-byside (as was the case in Sparkasse) or on the same floor allows for extensive communication and high interaction frequency. The CR of Spar\_T2 states:

We talk all the time, constantly. And I sit right next to the [project] manager, we have a very close working relationship and talk constantly about what’s going on.

The high communication frequency resulted in better understanding of why CRs make certain decisions.

Time Commitment of the CR to the Project: The CRs’ time commitment to the projects varied, depending on their departmental duties, but also on their personal commitment to the projects. For example, despite not being released from any departmental tasks, Werk\_T1’s CR committed a lot of time to the project:

Lots of conversations, lots of one-on-one meetings, lots of emails so they keep on track, after hours, texting the team after hours assuring them that we were on the right path, and that we were getting great, positive feedback that this was going to happen. And encouraging them and making them believe that it was going to happen.

Werk\_T2’s CR only devoted limited time to the ISD project due to other commitments.

## Discussion

This research aims to build theory on CR roles in agile ISD and how those who occupy the role handle the complementarities and contradictions so as to add value. To this end, we draw upon our results to develop a theoretical framework that integrates the complex relations between the performed behaviors and the surrounding conditions to explain the resolution of the contradictions and the leveraging of complementarities.

## Elasticity of Multiplex CR Roles

Drawing on the theory of role multiplexity, the results suggest that adaptive challenges trigger the manifestation of a CR role that manages complementarities and contradictions that emerge between assisting and assessing the agile ISD team and the customer. Each of the six identified CR role manifestations illustrates the multiplexity of being a CR by reflecting specific modes of managing the complementary and contradictory nature. Indeed, the CR can transition into and out of a given role (i.e., the role is multiplex) depending on the situational demands of the surrounding conditions. As such, the occupation of one multiplex CR role is momentary and persists only until the adaptive challenge reaches a satisfactory resolution. This finding highlights an important insight about the CR role in agile ISD that has hitherto remained untheorized. Namely, that the CR role is not a static, monolithic concept, but rather, in its multiplex form, is dynamically emergent and multi-oriented.

Role multiplexity posits that a role can manifest in different shapes (i.e., the six role manifestations) with different role orientations (i.e., complementary and contradictory). We enrich role multiplexity theory with the concept of elasticity (Kreiner et al. 2015) to further theorize the unfolding of the complementary and contradicting orientation of assisting and assessing. Elasticity captures how much the contradictions can stretch the CR role (leading to a broader CR identity) without breaking it apart because the complementarities reign it in. The elasticity of multiplex CR roles illustrates the dialectic between assisting and assessing as one side stretches while the other side holds it together. This elastic nature of the multiplex CR role is illustrated in Figure 2.

We theorize about multiplex CR role elasticity in two ways. First, we consider the elasticity between the multiplex CR roles. The role of CR in agile ISD can stretch to accommodate a plurality of manifestations—six of which we identify—that expand (and potentially threatens to dilute)

what it means to be a CR. However, the role manifestations simultaneously serve to strengthen and maintain the very purpose of the CR role in balancing the interests of the agile ISD team and the customer. The “between-role” elasticity is exemplary of the multiplexity of the CR role whereby different role orientations can dynamically coexist (Leonardi et al. 2016). Second, we consider the elasticity within a specific CR role manifestation. Each role manifestation represents a specific approach to managing the assisting and assessing that stretches and holds the CR role together simultaneously. The elasticity is evident in the ability of CRs to expand and contract their orientation toward assisting or assessing by leveraging complementarities and handling contradictions in the face of different adaptive challenges.

## CR Role Enactment and Transition in Agile ISD Projects

Drawing on Burton-Jones et al.’s (2015) suggested approach to combine process and variance theoretical perspectives, we develop a theoretical framework that encompasses sequences of events that lead CRs to enact particular role manifestations due to triggering events in agile ISD. Specifically, we leverage this approach to illuminate whether events exert influence in the process from triggers to responses to challenges (process perspective) based on the state of the surrounding conditions in which the CR operates (variance perspective) (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). We then develop propositions related to six CR roles that elaborate how they handle role challenges. We conclude with a discussion of the multiplex nature of the CR roles.

## Theoretical Framework

Our theoretical framework outlines a cycle in which CRs manage role challenges that emerge in an agile ISD project (see Figure 3). The process begins with a triggering event Î that prompts CRs to seek resolution of a challenge Ï which facilitates their transition into one of six multiplex role manifestations that culminate in a particular response to the challenge Ð. The probability of transitioning into any one of these CR roles is shaped by the properties of the state of the surrounding conditions. Once the CR is able to resolve the challenges, they transition out of the occupied CR role. The cycle begins anew when another triggering event emerges. Thus, our theoretical framework represents an ongoing cycle into and out of CR roles depending on the nature of the triggering event and the surrounding conditions’ properties. In this regard, it encompasses elements of process and variance perspectives, per Burton-Jones et al. (2015).

## Multiplex CR Role Manifestations and Resolution of Contradictions

We elaborate the six CR role manifestations that articulate how CRs balance the role challenges associated with complementarities and contradictions between assisting and assessing in agile ISD. The role manifestations are distinct from each other in their orientation toward leveraging the complementarities versus tackling the contradictions. Next, for each CR role, we identify the specific orientation and elaborate its relationship to resolution of the challenge of balancing. We develop propositions that explain how each of the six CR roles, together with the surrounding conditions, culminate in resolution of the challenges that become salient with triggering events. Table 5 identifies the triggering event, salient surrounding conditions and transitions out of each of the six CR roles.

Leadership Role. In the leadership role, the CR’s actions are geared toward leveraging the complementarities between assisting and assessing. In leveraging the complementarities, the CR places an emphasis on assessing which elastically stretches the orientation in the direction of the customer. This is primarily due to the triggering condition being related to the delivery of the next release of the software. That is, the likelihood of the ISD team incorporating agreed upon features for the next release may be threatened and the CR, as part of their obligation to the customer, needs to ensure that these targets are met and that roadblocks do not impede progress. The features that will serve as the basis for assessing the deliverable become the focal lever for the CR to employ.

CRs in the leadership role address the challenge of balancing complementarities and contradictions by highlighting the necessity of achieving established targets for the next release. They engage in motivational behavior to encourage ISD team members to persist in working to successfully implement requirements for the next release. This may be borne of the CR’s sense of responsibility for the project’s success. For example, based on the specific objective being assessed, the CR will appeal to ISD team members for extra effort by calling them after hours, especially during difficult times.

Motivating ISD team members is intended to align their focus with the customer objectives that will be assessed. That is, the CR begins with what the customer wants assessed for the current release and uses this to channel the ISD team’s focus through motivation. In this way, the CR is leveraging the complementarities between assisting and assessing because, by emphasizing the objectives and motivating the ISD team to achieve them—while ensuring no impediments are blocking the team—the CR does not need to invest additional effort in assisting. This elasticity enables the CR role to fulfill its purpose. Incidentally, by placing the emphasis on leveraging the complementarities, the contradictions remain intact but are no longer an impediment to the value added by the CR. Taken together, we offer the following proposition:

Proposition 1: CRs enacting the leadership role achieve balance in their role challenges by leveraging the complementarities between assisting and assessing through an emphasis on assessing. A sense of responsibility for project success informs CRs’ transition into this role manifestation.

Ally Role. The ally role addresses the role challenges by tackling the contradictions between assisting and assessing. A particular emphasis is placed on assisting the ISD team. The CR role orientation elasticity stretches toward the agile ISD team. This is due to the triggering condition of the software lacking features that can easily be incorporated into the next release. The CR’s objective in this case is to buy time for the ISD team to incorporate said features. As part of their obligation to help the ISD team deliver a successful project, they align themselves with the team at the interface with the customer. This becomes the focal lever that CRs in this role employ.

In the face of the trigger elaborated above, the CR manages the contradictions by allying with the ISD team and directing showcases to emphasize completed features while deemphasizing (“hiding”) incomplete features. In doing so, the CR addresses the contradiction by moving from the conflicted middle and siding with the ISD team. The move from the conflicted middle is facilitated by the CR’s relationship with the ISD team. Specifically, the CR is socialized to the ISD team through social events that help build bonds with team members. This creates a strong sense of belonging that leads the CR to see themselves as a member of the ISD team rather than as an outsider. By moving from the conflicted middle and aligning with the ISD team, the CR adds value by affording them additional time to implement expected functionality while also demonstrating steady progress to the customer. Hence, our second proposition is:

Proposition 2: CRs enacting the ally role achieve balance in their role challenges by aligning themselves with the ISD team through an emphasis on assisting. Socialization with the ISD team informs CRs’ transition into this role manifestation.

Immersion Role. In the immersion role, the CR addresses role challenges by leveraging the complementarities between assisting and assessing. Here, the complementarities are leveraged by placing a particular emphasis on assisting the ISD team. The CR role is elastically stretched toward the agile ISD team. Similar to the triggering condition for the leadership role, this is primarily due to the delivery of the next release of the software being threatened. As part of their obligation to the customer, the CR needs to ensure that these targets are met. In contrast to the leadership role however, in the immersion role the CR shifts their orientation toward assisting rather than assessing as a way to leverage complementarities.

The CR addresses the role challenges by self-immersion as a full participant in the development work. For example, the CR engages in GUI design and wire framing as a way to contribute to the development effort. On the surface, this might appear to escalate the contradictions between assisting and assessing; yet, it actually leverages the complementarity between them. Implementation of design requirements is performed with an understanding of the design expectations in the customer domain. That is, the CR implements the design with the customer’s assessment standards in mind and the CR’s in-depth understanding of the customer design expectations informs their development work. Therefore, the CR can develop to the necessary standards for assessment, thus ensuring that, at least in the area of their contributions, the deliverable will be up to standard and pass acceptance tests. Transition into this role manifestation is only possible because the CR has the requisite technical knowledge that enables them to engage in the necessary development work. The contradiction remains intact but leveraging the complementarities enables CR to add value through their contributions. As such, we posit:

Proposition 3: CRs enacting the immersion role achieve balance in their role challenges by leveraging the complementarities between assisting and assessing through an emphasis on assisting. Possession of technical development knowledge informs CRs’ transition into this role manifestation.

Guard Role. The guard role seeks to balance the role chal lenges by leveraging the complementarities and ultimately benefiting from them. The assisting and assessing contradiction serves as the foundation for the CR to perform the guard role and there are no efforts made to remove the contradiction. The CR intensifies assisting and assessing because they are required to withhold information for strategic management reasons. This behavior elastically stretches the CR role orientation in both directions. Withholding is done to maintain the team’s attention on implementing features desired by the customer. The assisting manifests as providing additional requirements, clarification, and various feature specifications while being careful to maintain confidentiality of top management’s business rationale behind the requirements. Moreover, the CR performs extensive assessing that can be perceived as being much more stringent because of undisclosed information. The assessing is focused on filling the gaps created by withholding the information. The CR focuses on meticulously assessing those requirements for which they cannot provide justification (without revealing confidential information) regarding their necessity.

Without good justification, the ISD team struggles to fully commit to creating the functionality. Intensifying assisting and assessing might be expected to further escalate the contradictions. Yet, our results suggest that the secrecy is seen as facilitating an effective CR role by focusing on the complementarities between the two behaviors. In fact, using the complementarity between assisting and assessing, the CR is pushed to devote extra care to explaining the requirements, answering all developer questions, and providing feedback whenever possible in order to deflect any possible inquiries about confidential matters. Through the intensified assessing, extra care is devoted to the software, especially relating to undisclosed information. Consequently, these requirements are implemented more effectively compared to those for which the team was given a business reason. Overall, the software outcome is improved. Thus, we suggest:

Proposition 4: CRs enacting the guard role achieve balance in their role challenges by leveraging the complementarities between assisting and assessing through an intensifying of both. Alignment with the customer informs CRs’ transition into this role manifestation.

Deterrence Role. The role contradictions are the main focus of the deterrence role, which seeks to tackle any conflicts head on. The elasticity of the CR role stretches it in the direction of the customer. In the deterrence role, the CR goes into direct confrontation with the ISD team over the quality and accuracy of deliverables, particularly if they are at risk. The CR has a strong sense of responsibility for the project, thus evoking a willingness to fight for desired outcomes and expected levels of quality. The confrontational behaviors emerge as part of the assessing because acceptance testing surfaces the failure to implement agreed-upon deliverables. The CR insists on the implementation of requirements as set forth in the original story card, refusing to accept the ISD team’s attempt to move it to a later iteration as this may delay the entire project. In this role, contradictions are reduced because emerging tensions and potential sources of disagreement are immediately raised and addressed. Indeed, the contradictory side of CR challenges as being in the center of assisting and assessing are used as a stimulant for the CR to do superior work in the role. The exhibited (i.e., expanded) assessing pushes the ISD team to elevate the level of quality at which prioritized functionality is to be delivered. Thus, the direct conduct of the assessing orients the ISD team toward the high level of expectation regarding implementation of requirements. Consequently, we propose:

Proposition 5: CRs enacting the deterrence role balance their role challenges by aligning themselves with the customer through an emphasis on assessing. Knowledge of agile ISD processes informs CRs’ transition into this role manifestation.

Removal Role. In the removal role, the CR manages the role challenge through a focus on the contradicting aspect of the role and decides to address the contradictions by exhibiting less assisting and assessing behaviors. The elasticity of the CR role contracts inwards and thus, does not lean towards one side or the other. The removal role is triggered because the CR is not released from the daily business tasks. The CR allows activities to occur without prior approval and assumes that the ISD team will fill in any decision gaps. The contradiction is managed by creating distance from the source of the tension. Assisting and assessing become conflict-free for the CR as there is little involvement at the requirements level. In fact, the CR is only involved in thinking about the big picture regarding system functionality and assesses more complete outcomes. When CRs occupy the removal role, the ISD team has to rise to the task without the CR’s input when assisting is needed while the ISD team is also on their own regarding the assessment of outputs based on the requirements the team defined. The CR only engages in the ISD process for highlevel issues (e.g., approval of re-prioritized larger business features, not requirements) during assisting and assessing. It is this high-level, on-demand involvement of the CR in the project that reduces the role contradiction. Overall, our last proposition is:

Proposition 6: CRs enacting the removal role balance their role challenges by distancing themselves from the contradictions through less assisting and assessing. Competing work demands on their time informs CRs’ transition into this role manifestation.

## Multiplex Nature of CR Roles

As the preceding propositions suggest, each of the six CR role manifestations represents an approach to resolving the challenges that emerge with a triggering event. Although each of these CR role manifestations is distinct, the multiplexity underlying our theoretical framework allows for a CR to occupy any number of them at any given point in an agile ISD project. In this regard, a swinging pendulum is a useful metaphor for envisioning a CR’s transition into and out of the six CR roles according to situational demands. Consequently, the CRs do not occupy one static role with a fixed set of properties. It can be thought of as a complex and multifaceted aggregation of various roles (i.e., the six roles we identified) into a dynamic whole. Indeed, each CR role constitutes one sixth of a holistic understanding of customer representation in agile ISD projects. For instance, a CR can transition into the leadership role when motivational threats emerge and transition out of this role and into the ally role when the ISD needs additional time to implement a feature. This transition process clearly demonstrates that our framework conceptualizes the CR role as being dynamic in nature. The theoretical framework is shown in Figure 3.

## Theoretical Contributions

Although there is a basic understanding that the core activities of CRs in agile ISD are simultaneously complementary and contradicting, theoretical insight about how CRs address this issue and why they employ particular means is lacking in the literature. This research makes three key theoretical contributions. First, this research advances understanding of how CRs manage the challenges associated with their work. Although it is broadly understood that the work of CRs involves inherent complementarities and contradictions, there is no theory to explain how they manage these challenges and why they do so in particular ways. By theorizing the CR role in agile ISD as being multiplex,<sup>3</sup> we identified six role manifestations that represent particular instantiations of the role and are aimed at managing the challenges. Our research is the first to conceptualize the varied ways in which CRs behave in resolving the contradictions and leveraging the complementarities experienced when executing their core responsibilities in agile ISD. This multiplex view represents an important shift in thinking about the CR role in that it moves us from viewing the role CRs inhabit as a monolithic concept to one that is dynamically emergent and multi-oriented.

Second, this research theoretically enriches the multiplex conceptualization of the CR role in agile ISD by integrating the concept of elasticity between and within multiplex CR role manifestations. By theoretically conceptualizing the elasticity between multiplex role manifestations, we explain how discordant approaches to enacting the CR role actually help to maintain the coherence of the role while simultaneously threatening to tear it apart. Elasticity allows for the boundaries that constitute the CR role to constantly stretch and contract while remaining intact. Further, by theoretically conceptualizing elasticity within role manifestations, we explain how CRs manage the role challenges by constantly stretching and contracting their emphasis on assisting and assessing in agile ISD. On the whole, this is a noteworthy shift in thinking about the CR role as being dynamic and constantly morphing. It suggests that part of functioning in agile ISD requires the CR to be agile in enacting the role.

Finally, by developing a theoretical framework that hybridizes process and variance perspectives, we provide theoretical meaning to why CRs come to adopt particular approaches— explicated via the CR role manifestations—to managing role challenges. Specifically, the framework identifies triggering conditions that may lead a CR to transition into one of the role manifestations. It further identifies surrounding conditions that inform whether a CR transitions into one CR role manifestation or another, given the nature of the triggering conditions. By outlining this framework as a sequence of events and states, we provide theoretical insight into the temporal unfolding of CR behavior. This result is an important theoretical development given our current limited understanding of why CRs act as they do in particular situations in agile ISD. Our framework enables researchers to sort through some of the underlying reasons for CR behaviors that appear to be outside of the assisting and assessing that are commonly understood.

## Implications for Practice

Our research clearly demonstrates that, in practice, there are discrepancies between customers and ISD teams in their role expectations for the CR. It would be beneficial for members of the role set to collectively voice their role expectations and attempt to reach some agreement about appropriate role behaviors. Furthermore, this research enables managers to give careful thought to the necessary skills required to perform the role behaviors (e.g., ability to analyze a situation and make decisions on the fly, being naturally proactive when role tensions emerge). Finally, our findings suggest that complementarities in assisting and assessing represent a useful lever for addressing CR role challenges. Consequently, there is benefit to thinking holistically about the role responsibilities and how they help CRs navigate potentially competing expectations of the customer and the ISD team.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

As with any research, our research design is not perfect and includes some limitations that must be acknowledged. Our case study data were collected exclusively from Australian firms. Although this allowed us to account for any crossnational cultural differences, it may have implications for the conclusions that can be drawn across other national contexts. Yet, we have no reason to believe that our findings have been biased by the choice of country. Interviewee bias and researcher bias are possible because interviews are our main data source. The measures taken to improve the rigor of our qualitative study enabled us to limit any potential bias (Dubé and Paré 2003; Paré and Elam 1997; Sarker et al. 2013). As we mentioned in our description of the case study site selection, we focused on settings involving in-house customers. In such settings, the nature of connections between CRs and ISD teams maybe closer-knit compared to study contexts involving an external customer. CRs from external customers may not have the same shared history and rapport with an ISD team as CRs from in-house customers. The findings need to be interpreted with this caveat in mind. A natural extension of the current work would be to evaluate the CR roles that emerge in the context of an external customer with potentially different role behaviors.

These limitations notwithstanding, this research opens several avenues for future research on CRs in ISD. While we show the triggers for CRs to transition into a role based on events or conditions, future research should draw on the identified CR roles to examine how transitions into and out of a role happen over time. Variations may manifest in the early and late phases of an ISD project. This temporal perspective could also provide additional insight about whether and how the CR’s approach to managing role challenges affects their transition back into their respective domain upon project completion. It would be insightful to know whether the CR role manifestations might endear CRs to their department of origin, alienate them, or open up new opportunities.

Although our findings present a number of surrounding CR conditions, we treat each as being equally important in understanding the CR role. Future research is encouraged to examine the interplay between CR competency (technical, agile, business knowledge) and socio-psychological forces that bind CRs closer to the team or cognitively impair the CR in fulfilling his or her role.

Her research interests are in understanding human behavior in relation to the creation, adoption and use of IT artifacts. Methodological Guidelines for Data Analysis: Threats and Mitigation\*

<table><tr><td>Data Analysis</td><td>Mitigation and Illustration</td></tr><tr><td>1. Credibility: Findings make sense, are credible, and can be believed (Lincoln and Guba 2000)</td><td>We did peer-debriefing to scrutinize our findings with outsiders to our research by asking colleagues for their feedback with the aim of “exploring aspects of the inquiry that might otherwise remain only implicit within the inquirer’s mind” (Lincoln and Guba 1985, p. 308).Their comments stimulated us to seek additional explanations on how CRs navigate the role conflict. In addition, we engaged in triangulation across organizational members through multiple formal and informal meetings with senior executives in the case companies, and also conducted a workshop with each team to discuss the preliminary insights and our understanding of the cases (Flick 2006).</td></tr><tr><td>2. Dependability: Research process was consistent across the cases and findings with the same informants could be repeated (Lincoln and Guba 2000)</td><td>Data coding followed a coherent, traceable, and iterative process, was undertaken by multiple researchers who did both; hand-coding and software-supported coding (NVivo10), and intermediate coding results were extensively discussed and scrutinized.We produced an ‘audit trail’ that documents the research process from data collection through to the drawing of conclusions and stored related materials in a cloud-based solution accessible to all researchers involved (Flick 2006).</td></tr><tr><td>3.
