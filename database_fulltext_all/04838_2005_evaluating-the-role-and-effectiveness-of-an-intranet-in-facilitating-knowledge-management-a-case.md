# Evaluating the role and effectiveness of an intranet in facilitating knowledge management: a case study at Surrey County Council

Walter Skok<sup>a,\*</sup>, Caroline Kalmanovitch<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Kingston Business School, Kingston University, Kingston Hill, Kingston upon Thames, Surrey KT 27LB, UK <sup>b</sup>Social Services Department, Surrey County Council, UK

Accepted 25 April 2004

Available online 8 December 2004

## Abstract

The research study discussed here examined the role and effectiveness of intranet technology in the process of creating and managing knowledge for the Social Services Department of Surrey County Council, which is one of the largest local authorities in the UK. Based on an analysis of the literature in the field, we devised an intranet evaluation model (IEM) using both technical (hard) and human (soft) factors. It made use of an epistemological framework to elicit user mental models from across an organisation via a survey.

The model was able to identify gaps, mismatches and failings in the knowledge management efforts. These were summarised in an easily understandable diagrammatic form, using knowledge evaluation maps; these showed the gap between the current and desired intranet roles for the different user groups within the council.

The paper concludes by demonstrating how factors, such as the different mental models of the user groups can determine the effectiveness (or otherwise) of an intranet in managing organisational knowledge. It also contains recommendations of services that need attention in the council operations and suggests how the IEM could be used as a consulting tool for organisations seeking to evaluate their own knowledge management work on a continuous basis. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. A review of knowledge management literature

The literature on this multifaceted and dynamic subject shows that it is a complicated and sometimes contradictory topic, capable of being analysed from a number of different perspectives.

## 1.1.1. Understanding the dynamic nature of knowledge

There has been much discussion of the definition of knowledge. Whilst some have suggested it is not productive to attempt a definition [3], others feel that managers need to be sure of the meaning if they are to effectively evaluate their knowledge management projects [8]. Some researchers have given their definition, but in most cases they have merely equated information with knowledge and subsequently used these terms interchangeably [13,19]. Whilst some take knowledge as a given entity, concentrating on the management aspect, Nonaka and Takeuchi [20] argue that the processes by which knowledge is created, through the interaction between tacit and explicit knowledge forms, is the key issue. They refer to tacit knowledge as that which does not take a linguistic or numeric form (broken down between cognitive tacit, i.e. values, beliefs, and emotions, and technical tacit, i.e. know-how) and explicit knowledge as an objective form of knowledge that can be linguistically/numerically represented.

From a holistic view, some researchers favour the IT perspective and the notion that knowledge management emerged in the 1950s alongside IT developments, while others emphasise the people issues, suggesting that knowledge is an essential ingredient of civilisation. This distinction in the strategies for managing organisational knowledge is encapsulated by Hansen et al. [11] who discuss codification, a standardised approach that emphasises capturing, codifying, and re-using, as opposed to personalisation, an approach that maximises individual interaction in order to share tacit knowledge.

Such debates appear to be a classic symptom of knowledge management and may serve to prolong the confusion. Gourlay [9] sums up this complex situation by suggesting that none of these arguments actually advances our understanding of knowledge and that whilst there are many ways to define knowledge, e.g. know-how, learning, and intelligence, and classify knowledge, the bottom line is the need of managing it better or perishing.

Given this, it is not surprising that managers, having rushed to adopt the latest knowledge management solutions, are now struggling to justify their decisions.

## 1.1.2. Using epistemologies to critique knowledge management theories

Von Krogh and Kleine [35] suggested that the most effective means of bringing order to this rapidly growing body of seemingly contradictory literature was to avoid an attempt at a universally acceptable definition of knowledge and concentrate instead on structuring theories according to their research assumptions (epistemologies), in order to gain a practical understanding of the nature of knowledge (Table 1). Whilst they acknowledged that most researchers’ work spans more than one epistemology, they suggested that these epistemologies should be viewed as a continuum: cognitivistic–connectionistic– autopoietic. Furthermore, that the epistemologies could be broken down within their own divisions. For instance, there is a split in the cognitivistic epistemology between the mainstream cognitive sciences i.e. artificial intelligence with an IT orientation, and the situated cognitivistic division which has more in common with the autopoietic epistemology.

## 2. The role of an intranet in knowledge management

## 2.1. The changing role of the intranet for managing knowledge

Advances in technology have facilitated the recent growth in systems designed for managing organisational knowledge. Supporting technologies include intranets, document management systems, information retrieval engines, relational and object oriented databases, electronic publishing systems, groupware and workflow systems, push technologies and agents, help-desk applications, brainstorming tools, data warehousing, and mining tools [21,30].

Oz [22] identified a number of ways that an intranet can enhance productivity:

 sending and receiving internal email;

 collaborative processing;

 allowing access to ‘organizational memory’ residing in databases;

 providing personal and departmental web pages;

 permitting group communications;

 providing product and company information.

However, unless closely controlled, the intranet may also transfer false knowledge, misinformation, and propaganda.

There have been many debates on the role and function of an intranet in facilitating knowledge; Hendriks and Vriens [14] emphasised the need to move away from an engineering perspective to examine more specific challenges within an organisation, while Wood and Varey [38] viewed the role of an intranet as an information tool, a communication tool, or a combination of both. Truch [33] also demonstrated how the intranet’s role varied according to the corresponding user epistemology.

## 2.1.1. Cognitivistic view of an intranet

The artificial intelligence branch sees an intranet as a culturally/socially neutral tool for representing information through the combination of explicit information, i.e. information management, as opposed to knowledge management [34]. An intranet would therefore encompass activities such as the acquisition, organisation, storage, retention, distribution, and presentation of information.

An intranet is viewed as a cost-effective, standardised, technological solution to deal with this information chaos, i.e., the central building blocks for robust infrastructures to help facilitate knowledge flows within an organisation and identify trends and connections based on facts and figures that the human mind could not process.

## 2.1.2. Connectionistic view of an intranet

This epistemology looks towards an intranet as a tool, not just to acquire, store and disseminate information for their own group, but also assist in making the right connections between different groups through the communication of the rules and procedures required to locate hidden organisational information. Thus an intranet driven by the connectionistic epistemology is essentially an information tool designed around community groups who share the same interpretation processes.

## 2.1.3. Autopoietic view of an intranet

Supporters of the people oriented epistemology, which can be aligned with cognitive theory, believe that the mainstream cognitive trap of viewing the human mind as a computer has already been experienced via artificial intelligence efforts in the 1970s; they cite the fact that 70% of BPR projects, with their overemphasis on IT at the cost of human involvement and commitment, have failed to deliver expected outcomes [18]. They argue that an intranet cannot deal directly with certain forms of knowledge.

Thus, this epistemology places increasing emphasis on an intranet as a communication tool to facilitate the direct conversion/creation of both contingently and inherently tacit knowledge via internalisation and externalisation processes, and indirectly, via socialisation, encourage knowledge sharing through the provision of data that enables individuals to identify relevant staff for making contact.

## 3. The intranet evaluation model (IEM)

## 3.1. Importance of evaluation

The importance of evaluating an organisation’s investments in IS/IT is highlighted by the productivity paradox [36], where large-scale expenditure appears to provide elusive benefits. This has resulted in a body of literature in the area [6,7,37], including an update of the seminal 1992 work on measuring IS success by DeLone and McLean [5].

For knowledge-based systems, Common Kads [25] is argued to be the most widely used methodology and is a high level approach to support the development of knowledge management systems. Magrill and Brown’s TEAM methodology [17] assesses existing intranet applications by considering performance enhancement within business and technical domains. While Leung [16] reported that many investors were disappointed with the performance of their intranets and suggested a set of quality metrics based on ISO standards.

Diagramming aids for reporting on evaluations allow senior management to gain an overall perspective of the current situation in order to identify and prioritise areas for improvement; e.g., Importance-Performance maps have been used for IS success measurement within the leisure industry [28] and Kiviat diagrams are used as part of an evaluation model for executive IS [27].

None of these evaluation models and techniques considered the different user mental models for handling knowledge within their work areas. We therefore decided to devise an approach that made use of features of such models but aimed to establish an intranet’s role and effectiveness against user epistemology. We felt that it should aid in making recommendations on whether an intranet, as a knowledge management tool, should be expanded, revised, or removed.

## 3.2. Intranet evaluation model (IEM)

An intranet evaluation model (IEM) was devised to provide a formal evaluation structure, ensuring that critical elements are not inadvertently missed, and potential political/personal bias is minimised. The IEM (Fig. 1) consisted of a framework and a process.

## 3.2.1. The IEM framework

The evaluation framework aimed at eliciting the users’ mental models across the entire organisation. In doing so, it allowed users to input ideas about a solution, as opposed to having a solution imposed upon them, whilst also helping the organisation identify priorities and focus the intranet knowledge management efforts by highlighting gaps, mismatches, and informing the decision making process. The framework makes reference to an organisation’s architecture and its resource issues: client information, service provision, stakeholder information, business processes, environmental information, staff information, and organisational memory. These must be reflected in the accompanying evaluation process to make sure that key areas and issues were not omitted. From a technology perspective, the key determinant of success is often whether people are willing and able to use the technological tools to contribute their knowledge to systems and access the contribution of others.

## 3.2.2. The IEM process

The framework is operationalised via a two-stage evaluation process. The first involves the application of Soft Systems Methodology principles [1], covering key influences, external pressures, identifi cation of stakeholder groups, naturally occurring human networks, and areas of conflict. The results from this stage can provide a deeper understanding of the issues and areas of conflict pertaining to a specific organisation in order to maximise the effectiveness of the IEM. The second part (Fig. 2) consists of establishing research objectives, classified into four sections that examine the current situation, human and technology aspects, and future developments.

## 3.2.3. Guidance on the application of the IEM process

Although an essential aspect of the IEM is its flexibility, allowing individual organisations to customise the model to suit their particular circumstances, there are a number of key issues that are central to the IEM robustness and validity. These are:

 It must be clearly aimed at assessing an intranet as a strategic performance enhancing knowledge investment, covering current performance and potential future developments.

 As an intranet is essentially pull technology, the views of the end users are more important than in most other studies.

 The intranet is just one of a number of competing information/communication vehicles for facilitating knowledge, thus the IEM needs holistically to explore the organisation’s architecture.

 The IEM must ensure coverage of both human and technological perspectives in order to evaluate the epistemological tendencies.

 The IEM must not be applied in isolation, so the results should feed into the organisation’s strategic plans.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Justification

The IEM’s aims can be achieved through the application of a predominantly phenomenologically orientated evaluation strategy, utilising aspects of the Soft Systems Methodology approach to help understand an organisation’s situation and a questionnaire to elicit a wide range of views. A pilot study can be employed to elicit key trends in respondents’ qualitative answers in order to produce a structured and cost-effective questionnaire, while still allowing respondents to express their own perceptions, experience, and interpretations. The responses from this stage can be followed by a series of personal interviews enabling issues to be clarified and explored in more detail.

This evaluation approach ensures essential organisational-specific issues, such as setting, interdependencies, complexities, idiosyncrasies, and context specific observations, can be captured in a controlled manner without automatically imposing a set of pre-formulated expectations.

The continuous development of an evaluation model, as opposed to a stand-alone questionnaire, provides a valuable on-going learning opportunity for an organisation. By transferring control of the IEM to in-house administrators, it also makes sure that the evaluation can be readily replicated and that the results are comparable.

## 4.2. Interviewer bias

The major weakness of a phenomenological strategy is in managing and reducing potential interviewer bias. If the administrators of the framework are employed by the organisations under review, a neutral stance must be maintained or researchers should be open about the assumptions they bring to the work. It may be expected that the closing of questions through a pilot study should act, to a certain degree, as a controlling mechanism.

The key issue is the administrator’s ability to draw on inquiry by observation through their status within the organisation. This can help address the limitation of qualitative responses, through the researcher’s ability to understand any situation in a way not entirely possible by others using only interviews.

## 4.3. Sample frame/selection

The following illustrates the steps required to ensure an adequate sample frame:

1. Establish population size from a reliable (up-todate) source.

2. Identify sample size (at least 10% of the population) and calculate the population proportion and confidence interval.

3. Apply a stratified sampling technique to the population sample according to the identified stakeholders.

4. Calculate sampling error on the respondent sample.

## 4.4. Non-sampling errors

Due to the complexities of the information being requested by the IEM, data validation and cleaning become critical. The use of a statistical package to store and handle data queries, as well as to identify areas of discrepancies in respondents’ replies, is essential. The use of check questions is also an intrinsic aspect of the IEM, otherwise the validity of the results could be skewed by inconsistent responses. The non-responding population can be analysed via identified stakeholder categories using unique identifier codes, to ensure that they are not significantly different from the sample population. For quality control reasons, respondents should be asked to identify their job title and location, enabling full control of the stakeholder classification process to rest with the researcher.

## 5. Surrey Social Services Department (SSD)

## 5.1. Background

The County of Surrey is situated in the south east of England and borders London; thus it combines some of the features of outer London, with some areas of rural remoteness. It is the fifth largest local authority in the UK, serving a local population of just over 1 million residents with a wide range of statute bound community, education, environmental, and social care services. The funds to support these are set at the beginning of the financial year and come from a combination of Central Government funding and a local council tax, thus resultant service demands during the year must be met within extremely tight budgetary restrictions.

In the area of social care alone, the authority employs approximately 3000 staff to commission and provide services to protect and support those in need of care and assistance, enabling them and their families to retain or regain their independence and take responsibility for decisions affecting their lives. In real terms, this strategic intent translates into the need to support approximately 23,000 vulnerable and disadvantaged or disabled people using 6 key business targets: promoting independence, improving life chances, protecting vulnerable people, making communities safer, supporting carers, and working better. This is achieved through a network of seven service areas all working to satisfy an increasing demand for services against a limited resource. Four service areas (The Directorate, Commissioning, Resources, and Inspection and Regulation) deal with the strategic direction and development of social care, whilst the remaining three (Children’s Services, Adult Services, and Local Services and Community Care), fulfil operational roles through the provision of services to the local community, taking into account the unique pressures that their specific client areas experience.

In addition, the external operating environment for the authority is highly complex (legislatively and politically) and constantly changing, with all UK authorities working to both a national framework of strategic imperatives as well as local priorities and pressures. With regards to knowledge management, central government has highlighted this specific issue within the public sector with The Green Paper on the National Grid for Learning, Downing Street’s Seminal Paper: Our Information Age—The Government’s Vision, and the Department of Trade and Industry’s: Converging Technologies—Consequences for the New Knowledge-driven economy.

A holistic analysis of Surrey SSD encompassing all stakeholders views (Fig. 3) provides an overview of the organisation’s current situation, encompassing the external influences, internal operating structures, processes, cultures, and climate issues reflecting the viewpoints, notional activities, and outcomes at a variety of different organisational levels. It illustrates the main issues facing Surrey SSD with regard to knowledge and highlights some potential barriers to effective knowledge management. This initial analysis indicated that staff are generally unhappy about the organisation’s current architecture, infrastructure, processes, and culture.

## 5.2. Surrey SSD’s knowledge management programme and intranet application

Surrey can be classified as a knowledge organisation in the sense that its main assets are intellectual ones and the organisation is adopting a focus on knowledge workers and knowledge management strategies [31]. Managing knowledge is the key for the organisation’s survival and, as such, should be a vital determinant of the strategic position of their business. However, whilst the organisation has employed a Knowledge Manager and part time Chief Knowledge Officer, both of whom, against the current trend are based in the IT Department [23], it was considered that the knowledge management programme is in its infancy. The Chief Executive viewed the installation of an intranet as a key strategic development to assist the department in managing and growing its knowledge resources, and becoming a knowing organisation, allowing it to manoeuvre with intelligence and creativity in order to deal with the information chaos [2].

Surrey SSD’s intranet has been established since 1998, with a connection installed in each SSD service location. However, it was set up initially as an information tool providing basic information on the department’s services and it did little to address or alleviate the issues shown in Fig. 3. Web pages were created and posted on the site, but this was accompanied by little co-ordination or advertising.

## 6. Analysis

## 6.1. Analysis of intranet use within Surrey Social Services Department (SSD)

The application of the IEM provides an essential means for effectively evaluating the intranet’s role in achieving the required outcomes. This has enabled meta knowledge, that is knowledge about how the organisation creates and manages this intangible resource, to be gathered and analysed. The result has been a practical evaluation of the strategic and operational role of an intranet, based on 49% of those respondents across the Surrey SSD (Table 2).

## 6.1.1. Surrey SSD’s current intranet role

The organisation, whose management’s premise resulted in a heavy strategic reliance on technology in their knowledge management strategy, initially adopted a single solution cognitivistic orientated approach, which placed the emphasis upon the management of explicit knowledge as a fixed entity. This resulted in the intranet being implemented predominantly as an information tool, designed for the acquisition, storage, retrieval, distribution, and presentation of information; the aim being to prevent the organisation from both re-inventing the wheel, whilst also leveraging knowledge for increased value. When this approach was not seen as delivering the required outcomes (Fig. 3), the organisation looked to develop the intranet as a communication tool, without implementing an effective study and evaluation of user needs and requirements. This two-stage strategic response is typical of organisations faced with developing a knowledge strategy to maximise returns on knowledge assets [26].

## 6.1.2. The effectiveness of surrey SSD’s current intranet role

The results from the IEM evaluation (Fig. 4) provided detailed insight into the effectiveness of the current intranet role in facilitating knowledge flows across the organisation. It also highlighted the possible reasons for the failure of the intranet in its current form.

6.1.2.1. Objective one: to establish key strategic business targets. The seven services of the Surrey SSD are prioritising the organisation’s targets according to their own worldview and operating environment, while indicating a connectionistic epistemology with services forming community groups. This provides an example of a major mismatch between user needs and the single intranet solution. In addition, there is some confusion relating to organisational and individual targets, suggesting that individual aims are in danger of being pursued over and above the organisational aims.

6.1.2.2. Objective two: to establish information required to achieve organisational targets. Threefifths of the organisation’s knowledge required to support the six key targets take an explicit form, whilst two-fifths take a tacit form, broken down evenly between contingently and inherently tacit knowledge. The mix between explicit and tacit knowledge changes throughout the services in line with their differing epistemological orientations. Areas of particular vulnerability for the organisation have been identified as Local Services, Children’s Services, and front line staff, all of whom display high proportions of tacit, particularly inherently tacit knowledge that cannot be directly expressed. Thus with the current intranet role there is a danger that a substantial proportion of the organisation’s knowledge may be ignored.

6.1.2.3. Objective three: to establish current organisational setting. A holistic evaluation of the organisa tion’s architecture indicated that the formal structures, infrastructures and culture were viewed as neither effective nor ineffective in facilitating knowledge flows. The degree of this dissatisfaction generally differed in line with the services’ epistemological orientations. Thus, those supporting a more autopoietic epistemology expressed a greater degree of disquiet at the current situation than those supporting a cognitivistic approach (to which the current architecture was orientated). The exception to this finding was Adult Services, which, despite its strong cognitivistic orientation, rated the architecture as highly ineffective. The outcome was that any knowledge management tool, not just an intranet, was in danger of being ineffective as the appropriate support from the organisation’s architecture was not present. This presented a major barrier to the transformation of Surrey SSD into a knowing organisation and specifically the effective development of any knowledge management tool.

6.1.2.4. Objective four: to identify an intranet’s strategic role. Whilst the organisation appeared to hold the premise that technology, in particular the intranet, was the key strategic tool in its knowledge management strategy, the analysis indicated that this did not hold true across the services. In line with the services’ epistemologies, they all displayed differing degrees of reliance on people and IT channels for facilitating knowledge. Thus the organisation’s current approach, in which the intranet holds a strong strategic role, is largely mismatched with the identified needs and requirements, highlighting the need to provide multiple channels to facilitate the flow of knowledge throughout an organisation.

6.1.2.5. Objective five: to establish an intranet’s operational role. There is a mismatch between the organisation’s view of the intranet as a single solution information tool and the actual requirements of the services, which differ depending on their epistemological orientations. Thus, whilst those services supporting stronger cognitivistic epistemologies (e.g. Adult Services) are theoretically better matched with the current intranet application as an information tool, those supporting autopoietic tendencies (e.g. Children’s Service and the Directorate) would be better supported by an intranet designed as a communication tool. Also, those supporting aspects of both cognitivistic and autopoietic epistemologies (e.g. I&R, Commissioning, and Resources) would benefit from an intranet which acts as both an information and communication tool. This mismatch between the existing intranet and service requirements manifests itself in a low perceived effectiveness and use of the intranet, despite widespread access to it.

6.1.2.6. Objective six: to identify areas for improvement. Aware that the current intranet was ineffective, Surrey SSD intended to develop its knowledge management strategy by adapting the existing intranet from an information tool into a communication tool. However, in line with the theory, desired intranet development varied in line with the services’ epistemologies. By ensuring they have the opportunity to use an intranet in a way that supported their epistemological mix, its impact may be enhanced.

Analysis indicated that all seven services place differing priorities on future developments, thus highlighting the danger of attempting to apply a single solution approach to the intranet. Fig. 5 compares the service’s epistemological orientations against their varying priorities for future developments, illustrating relative consistency between desired developments and their identified epistemology. In addition, when the issue of content was explored, there was a direct correlation between a service’s prioritisation of the organisation’s targets and the knowledge content, and form (i.e. data or information) required from the intranet.

## 7. Recommendations for Surrey Social Services Department (SSD)

We recommended that Surrey SSD revisit their intranet application. By separating the existing single departmental site into naturally forming community groups (e.g. service areas) and developing them according to their individual needs and knowledge requirements, it was expected that the main issues relating to the intranet (Fig. 4) could be relatively easily addressed.

## 7.1. Knowledge evaluation (KE) maps

The results from the application of the IEM are presented as a set of seven knowledge evaluation (KE) maps (Fig. 6); these graphically contrast the existing intranet solution and planned developments against the needs and requirements of the services. Thus this highlights the areas where reconciliation is needed in order to increase the effectiveness of the intranet in facilitating knowledge.

Consider the structure of each map. Five areas were identified as key factors in providing a comprehensive view of the perspective taken by each of the service areas. For each factor, the survey results were plotted along the epistemological continuum in order to show the current and desired intranet roles. First, the intranet’s strategic role provides an indication of the importance attached to people and IT channels, thus providing an overall benchmark as to an intranet’s role against a wider suite of KM tools. The intranet’s operational role was then plotted according to the design of the intranet as either an information tool, communication tool, or a combination of both. The knowledge classifications identified within the services were then plotted according to their orientation towards either tacit or explicit knowledge. The services’ underlying epistemological orientations were then identified, and finally, the services requested development of the intranet in terms of its communication/information capability were plotted.

The set of KE maps outline the current intranet role, whilst also highlighting the user requested developments against the organisation’s existing intranet. The difference between the two elements illustrated where an effective change management programme should concentrate, whilst also providing an indication of the scale of change required to achieve the desired outcome. Thus, whilst it can be seen that The Directorate, Commissioning, Children’s Services, and Local Services require relatively major change, Adult Services, whose desired profile related closely to the intranet’s existing profile, required little direct change (Resources and I&R are of less priority). The findings for Adult Services illustrate that effective knowledge management required more than sophisticated KM tools. This demonstrated the importance of the wider architectural issues to support and foster a knowledge sharing culture, as well as content issues.

This finding also highlighted the limitations of the KE maps in that they had a narrow focus on the KM tool under review (i.e. at present they have no mechanism for providing an indicator of the wider architectural or content issues). It is therefore essential to combine the KE maps with a wider contextual analysis to gain a true holistic perspective.

The KE maps also explored the anomalies between service requirements and theory (e.g. Children’s Services, which identified the need for both communication and information facilities from an intranet, may benefit more from the communication capabilities of an intranet).

These recommendations require careful implementation and a sufficient support structure (human and financial). Table 3 provides a structure for an effective change management programme for an intranet development strategy for Surrey SSD.

## 7.2. The intranet evaluation model (IEM)

In general, the IEM, when combined with a wider Soft Systems Methodology analysis, proved to be a robust and valid IT evaluation tool capable of evaluating the role and effectiveness of an intranet in facilitating knowledge flows.

Despite some weaknesses highlighted in the initial pilot, the results have been successful in both confirming existing knowledge management and IT theories, whilst also providing an application of knowledge management theories to a practical problem. The pilot and subsequent analysis indicated areas where questions could be removed from the evaluation process questionnaire in order to ease the burden upon respondents.
