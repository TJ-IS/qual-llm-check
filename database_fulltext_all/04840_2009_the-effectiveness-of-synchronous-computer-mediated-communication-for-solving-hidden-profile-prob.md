# The effectiveness of synchronous computer-mediated communication for solving hidden-profile problems: Further empirical evidence

David S. Kerr <sup>a,1,2</sup>, Uday S. Murthy <sup>b,2,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> University of North Carolina at Charlotte, United States <sup>b</sup> University of South Florida, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 10 March 2007 Received in revised form 2 April 2008 Accepted 16 December 2008 Available online 30 January 2009

Keywords: Computer-mediated communication Media richness theory Synchronous communication Hidden-profile tasks Group decision making Information exchange and recall

## A B S T R A C T

We experimentally compared the effectiveness of face-to-face (FTF) and synchronous computer mediated communication when using a chat tool in solving hidden-profile business problems. In such problems, information critical to its solution is dispersed among team members and they must share it to solve the problem. Unlike prior research using hidden-profile tasks, our study used a real-world business-oriented task, established real rather than ad hoc teams, and imposed a time constraint on them. Hypotheses derived from media richness theory were found to be supported, with the results revealing that computer-mediated teams using the chat tool were less successful in exchanging and processing information than FTF teams and were thus less successful at solving the hidden-profile problem. The results also showed that, when operating under a time constraint, FTF was preferred over computer-mediated communication due to the relative immediacy of feedback and multiplicity of cues available in the FTF setting, as media richness theory predicted.

## 1. Introduction

Computer-mediated communication (CMC) systems are now widely available for supporting teamwork in organizations. CMC tools have allowed professionals to form and work in ‘‘virtual teams’’ that are not bound by time and space [15]. There is ample empirical evidence that groups using them outperform face-toface (FTF) meetings in brainstorming and similar tasks [20,21]. However, there is mixed evidence about their efficacy in group problem solving—tasks that require members to exchange information, process it, and solve the problem [1,11].

One type of group problem-solving task is the ‘‘hidden-profile’’ task, in which individual team members possess only part of the information needed to provide a superior decision, but they can pool and process all the needed information [18]. Such tasks require team members to process both commonly held information (i.e., information known by all members) and uniquely held information (i.e., information initially known by only one member). Teams of business professionals are often confronted with hidden-profile problems. In hidden-profile situations, it is essential that information be shared with other members of the team. Essentially, the team members must convey information to the others and then the team as a whole must process it to arrive at an optimal decision. This setting is analogous to many team-based settings where professionals perform some work individually and then meet to solve problems and make decisions.

One study of hidden-profile problem-solving performance compared teams meeting FTF with those using two forms of CMC—a bulletin-board based tool and a chat tool [14]. Computermediated teams using the bulletin board tool exhibited the best performance. There was no significant performance difference between the teams communicating FTF and those using the chat tool. The lack of a difference warranted further investigation because:

1. failure to find a significant difference did not imply that they were equally effective,

2. professional service teams are likely to meet synchronously when making a decision; therefore use of chat tools seemed more natural than bulletin boards,

3. it was not obvious why CMC via a chat tool was not better than FTF communication, since instant messaging has become so popular in organizations [2,4].

Thus our study was intended to extend CMC research by investigating the performance of FTF teams with those using a chat tool when they operated under a time constraint. Employing media richness theory, we further hypothesize that the leaner medium of CMC would cause teams to exchange and process less of the uniquely held information necessary for solving hidden-profile problems, resulting in CMC teams being less successful.

## 2. Background, theory, and hypotheses

There is evidence that performance of a team is superior to that of an individual: members provide different viewpoints, pool information and check for errors. This is particularly important in the discovery of hidden profiles in team decision-making tasks.

Prior studies using hidden-profile tasks have found that teams interacting FTF often fail to process all of the uniquely held information, leading to sub-optimal decisions. However, such groups may be less prone to overlook uniquely held information if they believe that their task has a correct answer or when members are told who has additional relevant information [16]. However, it has also been observed that simply forewarning members of the existence of uniquely held information does not lead to greater success [19].

## 2.1. Effects of CMC on hidden-profile tasks

There are three major information-related steps involved in solving hidden-profile tasks, as shown in Fig. 1.

First, all relevant uniquely held information must be exchanged, i.e., each team member must share uniquely held information with the other team members. Since team members initially are not aware of which items of information are known only to them (uniquely held) and which items are known by others (commonly held), this step involves the exchange of both uniquely and commonly held information. Second, team members must process the information exchanged and recognize what information is uniquely held. In a hidden-profile task, this information should evoke additional information exchange. Thus, information exchange becomes an iterative process. Once members have exchanged all relevant uniquely held information, they can proceed to the third step: to solve the problem.

A team’s ability to solve hidden-profile problems might be improved by using CMC, since it mitigates some of the process losses that occur in FTF team interaction. A key advantage is that the technology allows simultaneous parallel input of information by all members of the team. A second advantage of CMC is that all participants’ comments may be automatically stored in a log, enabling participants to refer to them later. This is a form of collective memory that facilitates group decision-making [10]. It allows CMC participants to access exchanged information readily. However, it is possible that uniquely held information could be buried in the group memory: participants might become so engrossed in inputting their own comments that they do not devote adequate time to processing inputs of others. Several previous studies have examined the effects of CMC solving hidden-profile tasks. Dennis [5] investigated groups working on a task in which each participant received different but not conflicting information. The participants had to combine the information to identify the best solution. He found that groups interacting FTF exchanged only a small portion of the available information and thus made a poor decision. Groups using CMC exchanged about 50% more information, providing sufficient material to enable all groups to identify a good decision. However, only one CMC group chose the best. Dennis suggested that anonymity and delayed feedback in CMC reduced the credibility of new information.

Another study [6] examined information exchange and decision-making processes in small groups interacting verbally or with a CMC system. Using a group size of six, the study had groups attempt to solve a hidden-profile task in which group members had information that needed to be shared. Both computer-mediated and FTF groups exchanged only a small portion of the available information and made poor decisions. Even when information was shared, CMC groups were less likely to use the shared information.

Another study compared GSS and FTF groups in their exchange of information in a requirements elicitation task [17]. GSS groups used a brainstorming tool (synchronous chat) within Group-Systems. Although both groups exchanged a large percentage of the shared information, the GSS groups were more effective in exchanging unshared information. It was observed that FTF groups discussed shared information sooner and unshared information later than GSS groups. However, the focus of the study was on information exchange (of shared and unshared information) rather than the ultimate solution to a hidden-profile problem. Furthermore, the study used ad hoc groups rather than established ones.

Mennecke and Valacich [13] conducted an experiment varying both group history (i.e., established vs. ad hoc groups) and the level of computer support (i.e., CMC vs. FTF) for a hidden-profile task. Contrary to expectations, they found no significant difference in information sharing performance between the groups and that established groups discussed fewer of the unique items than ad hoc groups.

More recently, researchers have examined the relative effectiveness of alternative modes of team communication in hiddenprofile tasks requiring domain knowledge in addition to the exchange of unique information. For instance, Murthy and Kerr [14] compared FTF to CMC using a bulletin-board tool, and also using a chat tool for a problem that required knowledge of accounting processes. In all three modes, teams were given as much time as they needed (no time constraint). The teams using the bulletin-board tool significantly outperformed teams using the chat tool or communicating FTF. There was, however, no performance difference between teams using FTF and chat tools.

## 2.2. Theory and hypotheses

Media Richness Theory [3] states that communication media differ substantially in the way they convey information; e.g., faceto-face communication is rich while CMC is leaner. Richer media help in resolving uncertainty (when there is not enough information to solve a problem) or equivocality (when there are multiple and possible conflicting interpretations of it) [7]. Thus, per media richness theory, CMC can be effective for low equivocality tasks but high equivocality tasks require information-rich communication.

![](/api/attachments/878X42RH/fulltext/images/0448e9bbb1915eaf81ac235539e7b8004e23b2cfefe9e36f05e2e98863270bb0.jpg)  
Fig. 2. The resulting research model for our study.

Four factors influence media richness: its ability to transmit multiple cues (e.g., voice and facial gestures), immediacy of feedback, language variety, and the personal focus. FTF communication provides immediate feedback and many cues but CMC is better suited when rich communication may actually hinder participants by their being distracted by cues extraneous to the task.

We had decided that our experimental task would require domain knowledge (of accounting procedures) and involve discovering a hidden profile under a time constraint. We believed that such a task was highly equivocal and should benefit from immediacy of feedback and multiplicity of cues available in a rich communication medium. Moreover, because the teams were established ones and needed to recall and discuss accounting procedures, a rich communication medium should be best.

However, it was also possible that CMC teams using a chat tool may be more able to exchange information efficiently than FTF teams due to the parallel communication possible under conditions of low task equivocality [9], though CMC teams were likely to be less able than FTF teams to distinguish between uniquely held and commonly held information. Consequently, given the commingling of uniquely held and commonly held information in hidden-profile situations, CMC teams were expected to be less effective at identifying, exchanging, and processing the uniquely held information crucial to solving the problems (Fig. 2). Accordingly, we hypothesized that for our task involving exchange of uniquely held information in interacting teams operating under a time constraint:

H1. Teams using a CMC tool will be less effective at exchanging uniquely held information relative to teams interacting FTF.

The immediacy of feedback inherent in FTF communication should allow such teams to quickly identify and process uniquely held information, especially when operating under a time constraint. One method of discerning the relative effectiveness of information processing by team members is to examine their recollection of information, since information effectively processed is more salient and more easily recalled [8]. Prior studies have deemed the recall of information to be an appropriate proxy for effectiveness of information processing [12]. This lead to our second hypothesis:

H2. Individuals in teams using a CMC tool will exhibit lower information processing proficiency, as evidenced by recall of information, than individuals in teams interacting FTF.

When faced with hidden-profile-type problems requiring discussion and deliberation, teams are faced with a choice of communication mode—either FTF or some form of CMC. To solve a hidden-profile task, team members must both exchange and successfully process uniquely held information. The first two hypotheses deal with the processes involved in solving hiddenprofile problems. Following media richness theory, teams using a leaner communication medium such as a CMC chat tool should be less successful at solving hidden-profile problems than teams using FTF communication. Accordingly, we posited:

H3. Teams using a CMC chat tool will be less successful at solving hidden-profile problems than FTF interacting teams.

H3a. Team problem-solving success will be positively associated with the exchange of unique information.

H3b. Team problem-solving success will be positively associated with information-processing performance.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Participants

An experiment was performed using 128 students taking a masters degree. They were enrolled in a corporate auditing course and had been formed into teams of four at the start of the semester. Students were assigned so that all teams were as similar as possible along four dimensions: (1) aptitude (measured by students’ overall grade point average), (2) employment history, (3) membership in student organizations, and (4) gender (no allmale or all-female teams).

Prior to the experiment, all students had received training on using CMC and had worked together in their teams, both face-toface and using CMC, on various course projects unrelated to our experiment. Thus, the teams were established rather than ad hoc. Participants were told that a portion of their course grade would be based on their performance on the experimental task, which served to motivate them to take it seriously.

## 3.2. Design and task description

The independent variable for our experiments was the participants’ communication mode while performing the hidden-profile task. The two modes were FTF and CMC. Teams were randomly assigned to one of these two treatment conditions with the requirement that there be, as closely as possible, an equal number of teams in each. Fifteen teams used a CMC environment, while seventeen used FTF.

Two sheets of written materials were provided to each participant at the beginning of the experiment. The first sheet contained the following background information, description of the task, and instructions:

You are one of four staff-level members of the audit team performing the annual independent audit of Hush Corporation’s financial statements. During the course of the audit, you, along with the other members of the audit team, have worked diligently reviewing the client’s accounting and control policies and procedures, interviewing employees, and reviewing a large number of financial transactions that occurred during the period under audit.

One financially material problem item remains unresolved: Hush Corporation has begun to receive past-due notices from one of its suppliers. The past-due letter states that Hush Corporation owes \$150,000 for invoice No. 54321. The corporation’s records show that a check for \$300,000 was mailed for that invoice. The vendor insists that the check has not been received and threatens to turn Hush Corporation’s account over to a collection agency.

Investigation of the loss has determined that only four people had knowledge of this particular transaction—Ms. Spring, Mr. Summer, Ms. Autumn, and Mr. Winter. One member of the audit team interviewed Ms. Spring, another member interviewed Mr. Summer, another interviewed Ms. Autumn, and another interviewed Mr. Winter. You should assume all interviewees answered truthfully. In addition, you, and the other members of the audit team, have reviewed the supporting documents for this transaction.

Notes summarizing your interview and review of supporting documents are shown on the next page. You should assume that all notes are correct. Your team’s task is to discuss the notes made by each team member and determine answers to the following four questions:

What happened to the \$300,000 check? (2) How much money is owed and to whom? (3) What specifically needs to be done to fix the financial error? (4) What specifically needs to be done to fix the system that allowed the error?

The second sheet contained ten audit notes that were needed by each student when performing the task. Nine of them were common to all team members, but each team member’s sheet had one statement that was unique to that team member. All four unique statements were necessary to complete the case correctly. Although participants were told that their interview sheet was the product of one interview and that other team members’ sheets were the products of interviews with other client employees, participants were not informed that their sheets contained a combination of commonly held and uniquely held information. An example of an audit notes sheet is shown in Appendix A.

## 3.3. Pre-treatment test

The pre-treatment phase of the experiment began as each participant received his/her written description of the task, instructions, and audit notes. Each participant was allowed sufficient time to read the task description and instructions and study the audit notes. They were then told to place the task description and audit notes face down on the floor under their chair. Each was then given a sheet of paper and asked to list as many of their audit notes as they could recall. These recall sheets were then collected from each participant, and they were told to retrieve their task description and audit notes from under their chairs.

## 3.4. Hidden-profile task

Immediately following the pre-treatment phase, teams separated to perform the hidden-profile task. Teams in the FTF condition were seated around tables in separate conference rooms and monitored via closed-circuit televisions. The entire FTF meeting for each team was videotaped. One member in each team was designated as the team scribe to write the team’s solutions to the case. Before beginning their discussions, participants were told that a large pad of paper was available for the team’s scribe to write notes and that it should be positioned so that each member could see it at all times. Participants were not allowed to show their original list of audit notes directly to teammates.

Teams in the CMC condition used the WebBoard system developed by Duke Engineering and O’Reilly and Associates, Inc.

It supports same time/same place, same time/different place, and different time/different place discussions. All participants had prior experience using the system with their teammates and had received a written summary about using the software; also assistance was available for anyone needing help. One member in each CMC team was also designated as the team’s scribe. Written instructions were provided to the scribe indicating how the team’s solutions were to be submitted in the WebBoard system. Participants were reminded that all of their typed comments would be recorded and saved electronically.

Each team member in the CMC condition was assigned to a computer terminal in a large computer lab, with other team members dispersed throughout the room. In effect, the setting simulated a ‘‘same time/different place’’ setting, since verbal interaction was not allowed. The WebBoard system allowed team members to offer their comments simultaneously. The WebBoard program split each user’s computer screen so that the user could read the comment he or she was typing as well as see other team members’ comments. The WebBoard session log captured each team member’s typed comments, as well as each team’s scribe’s solutions to the case.

All teams were told they would have 30 min to work together to complete the task. A time constraint was imposed to allow us to investigate the impact of time pressure on CMC teams compared to FTF teams in successfully sharing the unique and common information and solving the hidden-profile problem. The amount of time given to participants – 30 min – was based on pilot testing of the task; CMC teams had taken approximately 39 min on average in completing it.

## 3.5. Post-discussion test

As each team completed the case, the experiment’s facilitators collected the case materials. Each participant was then given a sheet of paper and told to list as many of his or her own audit notes, as well as audit notes learned from other teammates. Participants then completed a debriefing questionnaire, were thanked for their participation, and dismissed.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Effect of communication mode on information exchange

Hypothesis 1 addressed the exchange of uniquely held information items, comparing the performance of teams using a CMC chat tool and FTF. The number of information items exchanged was determined by examining the videotape transcripts for FTF and the WebBoard session logs for CMC. A Mann– Whitney U-test was then employed, with communication mode as the independent variable and the number of uniquely held information items exchanged as the dependent variable. Descriptive statistics and U-test results are presented in Panels A and B of Table 1, respectively, arranged according to communication mode.

Teams interacting FTF exchanged more of the uniquely held information items, with 71% of the FTF teams conveying all four uniquely held items but only 33% of the CMC teams doing so. Mann–Whitney’s U-test revealed that the distributions of teams conveyance scores across the two communication conditions were significantly different $( U = 6 0 . 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Thus, the results of the test support Hypothesis 1 that FTF interaction was superior to CMC for the exchange of uniquely held information when a time constraint was imposed.

Analysis of the total number of items exchanged again revealed that FTF teams exchanged a greater number of information items, with 41% of the FTF teams conveying at least 12 of the 14 possible items, while none of the CMC teams exchanged 12 or more items (see Table 2). The Mann–Whitney U-test indicated a significant difference between FTF and CMC teams’ distributions of conveyance scores for both commonly held and uniquely held information $( U = 5 7 . 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results were also consistent with Hypothesis 1 and supported the results based on the analysis of uniquely held items only.

## 4.3. Team problem-solving performance

Hypothesis 3 addressed the degree of success in solving hiddenprofile-type problems, comparing teams using a CMC and interacting FTF. Problem-solving success was determined by finding, for each team, which of the following ‘‘hidden’’ facts were discovered: (1) \$150,000 was owed by the client to the supplier, (2) the check was made payable to the wrong company, and (3) the check was mailed to the wrong state. Problem-solving success was measured as the number of these that each team was able to discover (a scale from zero to three). Teams’ answer sheets were analyzed by two coders working independently. Analysis of interrater reliability revealed a very high level of agreement between coders (a correlation of 0.949).

Descriptive statistics for the problem-solving success dependent variable are shown in Panel A of Table 4 arranged according to teams’ communication mode. A Mann–Whitney U-test was used with teams’ communication mode as the independent variable and the number of hidden facts discovered as the dependent variable. Results of the U-test are presented in Panel B.

FTF teams showed greater problem-solving success rates than CMC teams, with 71% of the FTF teams discovering all three hidden facts while only 33% of the CMC teams discovered all three. A Mann–Whitney U-test showed that the difference in FTF and CMC teams’ distributions of success scores was significant (U = 65.0, p < 0.01). These results supported Hypothesis 3, which predicted that FTF interaction would be superior to CMC in solving hiddenprofile problems when a time constraint was imposed.

Hypotheses 3a and 3b predicted that team problem-solving performance would be positively associated with informationexchange and information-processing performance, respectively. To examine these hypotheses, we regressed problem-solving performance on information exchange and information processing performance. In addition, there was a significant relationship between the processing of unique information and problem-solving performance $( F = 1 0 . 0 , p = 0 . 0 4 ,$ Adj. $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 8 2 )$ ). These results were consistent with Hypotheses 3a and 3b.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Our study compared the performance of FTF and computermediated teams in a hidden-profile auditing task. Unlike most of the prior CMC research, established rather than ad hoc groups were used. The results of research using ad hoc groups may not generalize to established groups, which are typical in auditing and consulting firms. Moreover, our study used a task that required participants to use domain-specific (accounting) knowledge. Considering the additional cognitive load, results of prior research may not generalize to such settings such as ours. An additional contribution was the imposition of a time constraint: professional auditors and consultants typically work under strict time budgets. We found that imposing a time constraint heightened the advantage of FTF interaction over CMC.

In light of the time constraint, the parallel communication feature of CMC may have caused in information overload, slowing participants’ ability to process information effectively. However, though CMC teams input information in parallel, they do not receive immediate feedback. In FTF environments, when a team member presents a unique fact, other members often identify it as unique and thus all know it as unique. Thus, parallel communication tends to reduce immediate feedback and increase information overload.

## 5.1. Limitations

Many of the usual limitations of laboratory experiments apply to our study. Accounting students are generally considered to be acceptable surrogates for staff auditors. The task used in our study seemed to be appropriate for student participants and it was unlikely that professional experience would have had any different effect.

## 5.2. Implications

There are several implications, particularly for professionalservices firms that have implemented or are considering implementing group support systems for their professionals. It seems that firms should consider employing FTF interaction in solving possible hidden-profile tasks with time pressure. For such tasks, critical uniquely held information may be obscured in a sea of information when using CMC. This occurs as team members simultaneously input their information without adequate processing and consideration of information submitted by others. In an FTF environment, relevant uniquely held information may be more salient, allowing it to be processed more effectively.
