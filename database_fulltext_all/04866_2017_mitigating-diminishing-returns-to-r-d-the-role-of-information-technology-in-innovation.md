# Mitigating Diminishing Returns to R&D: The Role of Information Technology in Innovation

T. Ravichandran,<sup>a</sup> Shu Han,<sup>b</sup> Sunil Mithas<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Increasingly, firms across many industries are grappling with the challenge of declining returns to research and development (R&D). In this study, we investigate if and how information technology (IT) can help firms deal with this problem. We theorize that IT systems can help firms to cope with the complexity and ineficiency in managing innovation when R&D investments scale. We explicate the mechanisms through which IT enables firms to cope with the challenges they face when scaling their R&D and we posit that IT will mitigate the diminishing returns to R&D. Using archival data for 161 firms from 1991 to 2003 we find support for our conjecture that IT mitigates the diminishing returns to R&D. The mitigating efect of IT is stronger for firms facing greater complexity in their R&D activities, as measured by inventor intensity and geographical and technological complexity. We interpret and discuss these findings and their practical and theoretical implications.

## 1. Introduction

Almost all senior executives understand the need for innovation to ensure a firm’s survival and growth and invest considerable resources in research and development (R&D) to discover new products and processes; many express dissatisfaction with the return on their R&D investments (Knott 2012). Prior research attributes the low returns on R&D to firms’ lack of capabilities that enable R&D eforts, such as collaborations across R&D silos, engaging customers in innovation activities, exploiting external ideas, managing huge amounts of data, managing project portfolios, and aligning R&D strategy with business strategy (e.g., El Sawy et al. 2016, Jaruzelski and Dehof 2010, Leiponen and Helfat 2010). In particular, firms feel the pain from diminishing returns to R&D, as reflected in comments such as “spending more on R&D won’t drive results” (Jaruzelski et al. 2011, p. 1). Diminishing returns is a significant challenge faced by organizations as they seek ways to make their growing innovation eforts more productive (David et al. 2010). It is no surprise then that there is increasing attention in the practitioner literature to the problem of declining R&D productivity in several industries including pharmaceuticals, automobiles, and semiconductors (Abaogye et al. 2014, Barr 2015).

Increasingly, firms use information technology (IT) and digital platforms to manage complexity in organizational processes and improve productivity of knowledge work within firms and their ecosystem of customers and partners (Huang et al. 2018, Joshi et al. 2010, Kohli and Melville 2009, Saldanha et al. 2017, Teo et al. 2007). While studies have examined the efects of IT on many core organizational processes and established linkages with the improvements in productivity, profits, customer satisfaction, and Tobin’s q (Dedrick et al. 2003; Mithas et al. 2012, 2016; Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien 2005; Tafti et al. 2013; Tambe and Hitt 2012; Ho et al. 2017), research aimed at examining the efects of IT on knowledge intensive processes such as R&D and product development is still in a relatively nascent stage (Nambisan et al. 2017). Case studies of firms support the conjecture that IT can influence innovation capacity and outcomes through IT-enabled or IT-embodied innovations. For instance, AstraZeneca, a major player in the pharmaceutical industry, is relying on its IT to redesign its innovation model. They are targeting technologies to “facilitate spontaneous conversations between stakeholders. . . to share huge data sets and images. . . to support collaboration both within the walls of the enterprise as well as with outside partners” (Hickins 2012). Wyeth, a biotechnology and pharmaceutical company that was acquired by Pfizer, also invests significantly in IT to support its innovation activities (Mandviwalla and Palmer 2008). These investments enable virtual teams from diferent business units around the world to collaborate in research and to develop new drugs (Carr 2008). Recent empirical studies also attest to the role of IT in creating capabilities to manage R&D. Joshi et al. (2010) uncovered the efect of IT applications on firm innovation arguing that IT-enabled knowledge capabilities can enhance various types of absorptive capacities of firms, which in turn facilitate inventions and new product introduction.

Aside from these studies, we have a relatively limited understanding of how and the extent to which IT contributes to the innovative outcome of a firm, and whether IT can address the challenge of declining R&D productivity. Because of the central role of innovation in firm growth, it is imperative to systematically theorize and empirically examine the role of IT in influencing R&D productivity. This paper aims to do so by examining the efects of IT on the diminishing returns to R&D. To the extent marginal returns to the investment diminish as firms increase their R&D investment (Acs and Audretsch 1988, Graves and Langowitz 1993), we test whether IT investments influence innovative outcome by reducing the ineficiencies and complexity that arise when firms scale their R&D eforts.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

Our review of prior work suggests that although there has been progress in understanding the role of IT in innovation, the extent to which IT mitigates diminishing returns to R&D has not received as much attention. Therefore, by focusing on the role of IT in scaling R&D operations, our work complements extant research that relates to the efect of IT on innovation (e.g., Kleis et al. 2012, Saldanha et al. 2017). Table 1 shows an illustrative summary of related research and the unique contributions of this study.

Although innovation manifests in many forms, we focus here on R&D activities that are usually considered inventive activities through which technological knowledge is created and scientific discoveries are made. We view innovation as a combinative process through which diferent streams of knowledge are recombined to create new knowledge (Nerkar 2003, Schilling and Phelps 2007). This perspective posits that innovation arises from combining novel knowledge streams with existing ones, or novel ways of combining existing knowledge streams. Thus, to innovate, firms need to either integrate existing knowledge streams in a diferent fashion, or search for and identify novel knowledge streams which can be integrated with existing knowledge. Integration of different streams of knowledge usually requires the communication and collaboration among people with different backgrounds.

Studies in economics, strategy, and R&D management report that the marginal return to R&D decreases as R&D intensity rises (Graves and Langowitz 1993, Lin and Chen 2005). Increasing R&D staf expenditure and increase in R&D staf have both been associated with declining marginal returns (Graves and Langowitz 1993), a trend that is seen across many industries (Acs and Audretsch 1988). Moreover, studies that find smaller R&D units generating higher patents per dollar attest to the diminishing returns witnessed by firms when they scale their R&D (Bound et al. 1984). Collectively, these studies document the diminishing returns to R&D investments.

The reason for the diminishing returns to R&D is that larger R&D operations require greater capabilities to manage the complexities in search and coordination, thus incremental R&D investments are likely to be inefectual unless coupled with appropriate levels of matching capabilities in other areas (Argyres and Silverman 2004, Henderson and Cockburn 1994). Often, R&D growth entails geographical dispersion, and firms locate R&D in diferent regions of a country or the world to leverage the local spillover efects (Lahiri 2010). There appears to be a trend toward geographic dispersion of R&D; studies suggest that the share of R&D done ofshore increased from 45% to 66% between 1975 and 2005 (Golbruner et al. 2006). Interestingly, studies have found that distributed R&D is negatively related to a firm’s innovation ability (Furman et al. 2010, Leiponen and Helfat 2010, Singh 2008). Singh (2008) notes that “potential gains from access to diverse ideas and expertise from diferent locations are, on average, ofset by dificulty in achieving integration of knowledge across multiple locations” (p. 77). Moreover, because more than 50% of R&D budget is spent on human resources, growth in R&D is frequently accompanied by an increase in the number of knowledge workers involved in R&D.<sup>1</sup> As R&D units grow, they also experience functional diferentiation through specialization, which can create rigidities that impede creativity, collaboration, and knowledge sharing within the R&D unit. Thus, as R&D investments increase, unless matched with adequate capabilities to manage the complexities, dysfunctions of the type we articulated above are likely to dampen R&D productivity.

The idea that R&D can have an inverted U-shaped relationship with innovation is consistent with additive benefit/cost arguments (Haans et al. 2016), in that although the benefits (or innovation in our context) from R&D may be increasing linearly, the diseconomies or costs (or reduced innovation) may escalate rapidly following an exponential or convex curve because of coordination and collaboration dificulties. Therefore, after subtracting convex costs from linear benefits, one may end up with an inverted U-shaped relationship between R&D and innovation. We extend this argument further and explain how IT may moderate this inverted U-shaped relationship leading to a potential flattening of the curve.

## 2.1. Role of IT in Managing R&D

Because of the knowledge and communication intensive nature of R&D, we argue that IT can be a significant enabler in managing R&D, and mitigate diminishing returns to R&D. We argue that IT investments are complementary to R&D investments, and this complementarity manifests strongly at higher levels of R&D investments. The complementarity perspective posits that two types of resources are complementary to each other when the marginal return of one resource increases with the level of the other. Thus, the value of having both resources together exceeds the sum of the value of having each resource individually. For instance, conducting basic research is considered complementary to conducting applied research (Arora and Gambardella 1990). Similarly, IT is considered complementary with organizational practices such as incentive pay and human resource analytics (Aral et al. 2012), and customer involvement (Saldanha et al. 2017) as the value of these business practices are enhanced by IT.

We argue that IT complements R&D resources by overcoming the challenges in scaling R&D eforts by enabling better information processing capacity, better communication among organizational members and units, and better integration of knowledge. First, innovation activities require firms to have information processing capacity. The knowledge involved in innovation is usually complex, such as the design of a component in a car, the method to produce a certain chemical compound, or a certain DNA sequence. Storing, processing, and transferring such knowledge is costly and error prone. Moreover, research and discovery has become data driven and data intensive. Simulations and modeling are an inherent part of most R&D activities and many discoveries are driven by mining of large-scale data. For example, in the biotechnology and pharmaceutical industries, data intensive approaches are replacing traditional lab experiments in the discovery stage of the innovation process. Similarly, many design activities are impossible to perform today without the software and IT tools that enable complex modeling. Thus, as R&D becomes more data centric, the role of IT in R&D activities has become very central.

Second, innovation usually results from interactions among individual scientists, especially scientists from diferent but complementary specialty areas. Novelty emerges when they interact with each other, share ideas, and discuss possibilities. Yet, the communication channels are shaped by organizational structure, culture, and incentive mechanisms (Henderson and Cockburn 1994). Maintaining a communication infrastructure that harnesses the interactions of individuals can be challenging and the lack of communication channels can hinder innovation.

Finally, integrating knowledge requires individuals/groups to work closely together. The coordination of multiple parties, especially when these parties are from diferent backgrounds, is critical for innovation and yet dificult to do (Carlile 2004). By providing the information-processing capacities, coordination capabilities, and a flexible communication infrastructure, IT can enable firms to deal with the complexities of managing R&D and thereby make R&D efort more productive, particularly at higher levels of R&D.

## 2.2. IT as a Facilitator to Reduce Diminishing Returns to R&D

We next propose that the complementarity relationship between IT and R&D is such that IT can mitigate the diminishing returns to R&D at higher levels of R&D.<sup>2</sup> We explicate two mechanisms: scalability of IT-based communication infrastructure and the use of IT to overcome the trade-ofs among eficiency, scope, and flexibility of knowledge integration.

First, we argue that IT-based communication infrastructure is more scalable and can better accommodate new demands from a growing R&D operation (Mandviwalla and Palmer 2008). R&D activities are intangible and knowledge-based, yet labor intensive. Therefore, when firms scale up their R&D spending, it is likely to result in an increase in the number of employees engaged in R&D eforts. Link and Rees (1990) report a 0.65 positive correlation between R&D spending and the number of R&D employees (significant at the 0.01 level) in their sample. As firms scale up their R&D operations, they are likely to have a larger organization, and thus need to accommodate additional communication channels—between new members and existing members—in their communication infrastructure. The number of additional channels can increase dramatically with the size of the R&D organization depending on assumptions about nature of knowledge exchanges (Reed 2001). In other words, the larger the R&D organization, the more communication capacity it needs to add, and unless the communication infrastructure can accommodate a growing demand, the benefit of new R&D investment cannot be fully realized. For an IT-based communication infrastructure, adding more members to an existing infrastructure is likely to incur low marginal costs; while a non-IT-based communication infrastructure is likely to take more time and efort to build additional communication channels. Similarly, as R&D organizations grow, a greater variety and volume of information needs to be transferred along communication channels, and IT-based systems can accommodate such increased complexity with ease. For R&D activities, the complexity of knowledge being integrated is particularly high. For example, integration between product development phases requires exchange of engineering drawings, parts descriptions, bills of materials, and machine tool configurations among design engineers, manufacturing engineers, and manufacturing analysts. These interaction procedures are people intensive, time-consuming, and error prone. IT facilitates knowledge integration by reducing the cost and improving the quality and speed of information transmission. For example, a centralized database allows the parties to share information in real time, which enables significant reduction in communication costs and increases the quality and timeliness of knowledge integration.

Second, IT can help to avoid the trade-ofs among eficiency, scope, and flexibility of knowledge integration associated with larger R&D operations. The knowledge-based view of firms posits that firm capabilities come from their ability to integrate or combine diferent streams of knowledge (we use “integrate knowledge” and “combine knowledge” interchangeably in this paper). Grant (1996) suggests three attributes of knowledge integration: eficiency, scope, and flexibility. Eficiency refers to how productive firms are in integrating specialized knowledge, scope of integration captures the breadth of knowledge integrated, and flexibility of integration depicts the extent to which the new stream of knowledge can be encompassed in the integration.

It is dificult to achieve eficiency and integrate broad streams of knowledge in a flexible manner at the same time, especially in a large R&D operation. As R&D operations grow, certain communication structures such as groups based on disciplines, projects, product components, or locations are usually deployed and become institutionalized (Henderson 1993). For instance, R&D organizations of large pharmaceuticals are organized first by therapeutic classes, then by research programs within a therapeutic class, and then by diferent research projects within a research program (Henderson and Cockburn 1994). While creation of specialist or geographic groups makes it more eficient to integrate knowledge within a group, it may hinder the integration across groups. Integration across groups is critical for the scope of integration because members of the same group tend to have overlapping knowledge bases, and members from diferent groups have a higher chance of possessing novel knowledge elements. Other organizational structures and mechanisms that support the eficiency of knowledge integration, such as the frequent use of certain communication patterns, may also create rigidities because they could keep organization members from obtaining and integrating new knowledge that comes through other communication patterns (Leonard-Barton 1992, Levitt and March 1988), thereby limiting the flexibility of knowledge integration. Henderson and Clark (1990) argue that the rigidity resulting from deepened communication patterns can deter new patterns of interactions among components, which is the source of architectural innovation achieved by flexible integration of knowledge streams.

Prior research has suggested that there appears to be a “trade-of between forming dense clusters to facilitate rapid exchange and integration of knowledge, versus forging links to create short paths to a wider source of knowledge” (Schilling and Phelps 2007, p. 1115). The trade-of also manifests in the Gold et al. (2001) proposition that optimization of knowledge sharing within a functional area can make the sharing of knowledge across a firm suboptimal. Similarly, the economics literature argues that cohesiveness within groups leads to hoarding of information, less creativity, and “notinvented-here” syndrome, which contribute to diminishing returns to R&D (Graves and Langowitz 1993).

The trade-of between eficiency and the breath and flexibility of knowledge integration can be alleviated by the use of information technologies that can enable a flexible or fluid communication infrastructure. Such flexible communication infrastructure supports various expected or unexpected connections among organization members, and thus enable them to reach beyond boundaries (e.g., geographical, function, group, project, or discipline boundaries). A communication infrastructure enabled by IT can help organization members to form or maintain weak ties with much ease and less costs (Feldman 1987). For instance, a person can post a question on the electronic forum to a large electronic distribution list, and the people who read and reply to the question do not necessarily need to know those who post questions (Huang et al. 2018). IT and digital platforms can help to link people “in the absence of acquaintance, physical proximity, group membership, a history of prior relationship, and demographic similarity” (Constant et al. 1996). Connections enabled by IT (e.g., community of practice, Usenet groups, Listservs) make it possible for scientists to share information and know-how, or stories of personal experiences with each other. Guira and Ravichandran (2013) argue that IT facilitates knowledge flows among alliance partners and thereby enhances learning through both enabling weak ties and through serendipitous discovery of complementary knowledge.

More recently, IT-enabled communication infrastructure including social networking tools, has helped to broaden the connections among organizational members. Such infrastructure can enable the organizing of ad-hoc meetings and spontaneous interactions among organizational members in addition to scheduled meetings. These ad-hoc meetings and spontaneous interactions increase the chance of integration of knowledge from seemingly unrelated sources, which can widen the scope of knowledge being integrated. The ease with which such new communication patterns are enabled in firms, allows firms to integrate knowledge with greater flexibility. For instance, P&G claims their innovation strength is linking the most impossible knowledge together. They use an internal website called “InnovationNet” that acts as the global “lunchroom” where researchers throughout the world can trade information and make connections across the company (Sakkab 2002). Likewise, the Tata group in India uses an “InnoVerse” platform for facilitating knowledge exchanges (Mithas and Arora 2015). An IT-enabled communication infrastructure also supports sharing of knowledge via knowledge repositories and portals. This can increase scientists’ awareness of fellow scientists’ work, enabling the opportunities for collaboration across traditional boundaries. Communication infrastructure enabled by IT can foster spontaneous interactions and create opportunities for crossfertilization of knowledge across project, functional, departmental, geographical, and disciplinary boundaries without adding much cost burden on the organization, making it possible to maintain a high level of integration eficiency and scope and flexibility at the same time.

Furthermore, IT can greatly facilitate coordination among people coming from diferent backgrounds. Common language is a prerequisite for coordination between specialists (Grant 1996). IT can help with such coordination through creating a common language (Anthony et al. 2008). For example, advanced computer aided design (CAD) systems help to create a common “technical grammar” for experts from diferent areas in their eforts to design an aircraft (Argyres 1999). Furthermore, the IT artifact itself often serves as a boundary object (Carlile 2002); the artifact that allows people with diferent knowledge bases to communicate or interact with each other or even see the others’ perspective. The boundary objects are increasingly digital in nature because the digital version provides richer information (such as hyperlinks or 3D models), are more flexible, and are easy to share and manipulate (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013, Levina and Vaast 2005, Pawlowski and Robey 2004).

Based on the above arguments, we propose that IT intensity will mitigate the diminishing returns to R&D intensity in a way that marginal returns to R&D decrease at a lower rate for firms with higher IT intensity. Since it is not any one IT application or system, but the overall capacity that is created through IT for communication and to manage the trade-of between eficiency and the breadth and flexibility of knowledge integration, the overall IT spending of a firm complements its R&D spending in a manner that the diseconomies of scale to R&D is likely to be mitigated by a firms’ IT intensity.

In other words, although the relationship between R&D and innovation outcome is likely to have an inverted U-shape because of diminishing returns, this inverted U-shape is flatter for firms with higher IT intensity. Our theorizing is consistent with the recommendations of Haans et al. (2016) in that we consider both the benefits and costs of scaling R&D. On one hand, R&D is the major input of innovation, thus more R&D can lead to more innovation. On the other hand, the complexities and rigidity escalate rapidly as R&D scales up, resulting in the diminishing returns to R&D. The role of IT lies in enabling a scalable and fluid communication infrastructure that can cope with the complexities and rigidity, thus flattening the inverted-U shaped relationship depicting returns to R&D investments.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Data

We compiled the data to test the research model from several secondary sources. We obtained the IT data from InformationWeek, data on R&D investments, and other financial and industry information from the Compustat database, and patent and citation data from PATSTAT (Patent Statistical Database: 2011 version) compiled by the USPTO (United States Patent and Trademark Ofice) and by the European Patent Ofice (http://www.epo.org/searching/subscription/ patstat-online.html).

The theoretical population for this study is firms that engage in patenting. The InformationWeek Survey includes firms from both manufacturing and the service sectors. Because the R&D and patenting practice of service firms could be very diferent from those of the manufacturing industry, we carefully examined the data to screen out firms from industries with no history of patenting. For example, firms in some service industries such as telecommunications, software, and IT services engage in patenting, those in other industries do not. We dropped firms from industries with no patenting history such as utilities, wholesale, cable services, retail, insurance, hospitals, hotels, amusement parks, and personal services in constructing our sample for this study.

## 3.2. Variables

We measure firm innovation outcome using citationweighted patent count.<sup>3</sup> A patent examiner, who is an expert in the technology domain, is responsible for ensuring that the proper prior art is cited when evaluating the patent application. Forward citation measures the number of times a patent is cited by later patents. To the extent that it serves as a knowledge foundation of later technologies and discoveries, the number of forward citations of a patent signify the impact (Kotha et al. 2011, Rosenkopf and Nerkar 2001), importance (Hall et al. 2001), and quality (Lahiri 2010) of the innovation. Phene et al. (2006) used the number of citations as a way to identify breakthrough innovations. Forward citation has been shown to relate to firm market value (Hall et al. 2005) and expert evaluation (Albert et al. 1991).

Because the propensity to cite may vary across years and technology fields, we adjust the raw citation counts adopting the semistructural approach used by Hall et al. (2001) that give weights to citations based on the year of the patent application and the technology field that the patent belongs to. Following Hall et al. (2001), who defined six main technology categories for patents (Chemical, Computers and Communications, Drugs and Medical, Electrical and Electronics, Mechanical, Others), we used these technological categories of patents and extracted such information from the USPTO patent database. Although the adjusted citation count is a continuous variable, it resembles a count variable because of the presence of many zeros and big numbers, hence we round it to integer numbers.

Our focal explanatory variables are IT and R&D spending. We use IT data from the annual survey conducted by InformationWeek (IW) from 1991 to 2003. From 1991 to 1996, IW published a list of 500 companies and their IT budgets in a special issue of the magazine every year and we obtained the data on IT spending from 1999 to 2006 through a nondisclosure agreement.<sup>4</sup> Since patent data from the USPTO is only available until 2006 and because we use a three-year lag between IT investment and patent application, we can only use our IT data until year 2003. IW data have been used previously in academic research (Banker et al. 2011, Bharadwaj 2000, Mithas et al. 2005, Tafti et al. 2013). We compiled the R&D spending data from the Compustat database, and use R&D spending as a percentage of sales as the proxy for R&D intensity.

We accounted for relevant control variables in our models, while also maintaining parsimony. We include the level of diversification (Herfindahl Index of sales) and firm age as control variables as both variables have been shown to relate to firm innovation (Garcia-Vega 2006). We extracted data of sales from diferent SIC segments and firm age from the Compustat database. We also use year and industry dummies in the regression to further account for the diferent propensity to cite patents across years and industries.

After matching the firms listed in InformationWeek with firms in Compustat and the patent databases, we have 822 observations with 161 unique firms between 1991 and 2003 in the final data set. We have an unbalanced panel with about four observations per firm on average. About 19% appear twice, 15% appear three times, and 14% appear four times. Table 2 shows the frequency distribution of the firms in our sample. Among all sample firms, approximately 10% have sales greater than \$25 billion, 19% have sales between \$10 and \$25 billion, 24% have sales between \$5 and \$10 billion, 45% have sales between \$1 and \$5 billion, and 2% have sales less than \$1 billion.

We find that both IT intensity and R&D intensity have positive and statistically significant correlations with the citation weighted patent counts.

## 3.3. Empirical Models and Econometric Considerations

Considering our goal in this study, we follow the convention in testing diminishing returns to R&D (Graves and Langowitz 1993), where patent count is used as the dependent variable instead of the logarithm of patent count. We use a negative binomial model because of overdispersion in our dependent variable (the standard deviation of citation weighted patent count (4,824) is much larger than its mean $( 2 , 2 2 { \bar { 7 } } ) ) . ^ { 6 }$ In the base model, we include R&D intensity, and the quadratic term of R&D intensity as predictors and account for year efects, industry efects, firm age, and level of diversification (Griliches 1979, 1998; Jafe 1986; Pakes and Griliches 1984).

To test the efects of IT on innovation outcome, we add IT intensity and the interaction terms involving IT intensity and squared R&D investment $( \mathrm { I T } \times \mathrm { R } \& \mathrm { D } ^ { 2 } )$ We examine the coeficient of $\mathrm { I T } \times \mathrm { R } \& \mathrm { D } ^ { 2 }$ terms to test whether the inverted U-shape is flattened by the use of IT (Haans et al. 2016). Because R&D expenditures take time to yield results in terms of patents, it is important to incorporate time lags in the model. We apply a threeyear lag between the time of IT and R&D investment and the time of patent application.

We follow the convention that relates flows of IT investments with other flow type measures such as profitability using yearly flows of patents and citations (Aral and Weill 2007, Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Rai et al. 1997, Tafti et al. 2013). Recent studies have argued that flow measures avoid measurement errors involved in aggregating capital stock (Chwelos et al. 2010) and following this logic, Kleis et al. (2012) preferred a flow type measure in their recent work linking IT with intangibles such as patents. We use flow measures for both IT and R&D investments.

We have an unbalanced panel data for the period between 1991 and 2003. We used fixed-efects models because they yield consistent parameter estimates even when the independent variables correlate with the omitted time-invariant firm characteristics. We used the random-efects models as a robustness check and obtained broadly similar results.

We considered reverse causality issues (i.e., firms with more patent citations investing more in IT) and our analyses suggest that reverse causality is not a serious concern in our setting because we lag our IT variable by three years compared to the outcome variable (in other words, our outcome variable filing of patent applications occurs three years after the IT investment). To further rule out concerns due to reverse causality, we regressed IT at year t using citation count at year t <sup>−</sup> 2 while controlling for firm age, diversification, R&D investment, and year and industry fixed efects, and found the efect of citation count on IT to be insignificant. We considered endogeneity issues and our key findings are unlikely to be driven by endogeneity issues. Generally speaking, endogeneity because of any common factors that may drive our focal variables and outcome variables is less of a concern when models contain interaction terms and quadratics. Note that the mere presence of endogeneity does not always bias the coeficients of interest significantly. For example, the work of Tambe and Hitt (2012) suggests that “efects of endogeneity on IT productivity estimates may be relatively small” (p. 599) and the bias may even be negligible for studies that use aggregate measures of IT spending or more complex models as is the case in our setting. Key estimates, based on the three-way complementarity among IT, external focus, and decentralization, are less subject to bias than our main efect estimates because any biases that afect the complementarity term must be present only at the confluence of all three of these factors, but not when factors are present individually or in pairs (p. 851).

There may also be a concern that high intercorrelations among IT and R&D may drive our findings. Because our models already account for any correlations and overlap among IT and R&D investments as these variables appear on the same side of the regression equation, our parameter estimates remain unbiased (Kennedy 1998).<sup>7</sup> Note that prior published studies also show an overlap in IT and R&D measures (e.g., Kleis et al. 2012). We examined correlations among IT and R&D further and studied the definition of R&D expenses in the Compustat manual, the accounting policy on R&D expenses from the Statement of Financial Accounting Standards (SFAS No. 2), and the accounting policy on software expenses in the Statement of Financial Accounting standard (SFAS No. 86).

Although the Compustat manual states that R&D expenses include “software development expenses,” our reading of SFAS No. 2 and No. 86 suggests that these software development expenses apply mostly to IT companies for whom the cost of software development can be expensed (rather than capitalized) until technological feasibility has been established for the product. However, we believe that non-IT companies are unlikely to expense software development costs as a part of their R&D expenses. Although firms can capitalize some part of their IT related to R&D, the Sandino and Kaplan (2001) review led them to note that “in the U.S., . . . few major companies are choosing to classify any portion of their software development costs as meeting the standard for capitalization” (p. 7). Nonetheless, even if some firms include part of the hardware or software used in R&D as part of their R&D expenses, we do not believe it undermines our analysis because the correlations among independent variables are already accounted for in the regression analysis. Note that our IT data come from chief information oficers (CIOs) or other senior IT executives hence it may not include some IT expenditures that may be part of R&D, and prior studies also note that IT expenditures in departments outside the centralized IT organizations may not be large enough compared to the overall IT budget that CIOs report (Im et al. 2013). Because our theorizing is based on the notion of IT for coordination and collaboration, and not IT for R&D per se, we do not believe that incidental correlations between IT and R&D in our study are a cause for serious concern.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Main Findings

The importance of innovation for firm growth and renewal has focused managerial attention on how to prime their innovation pump. R&D has traditionally been an important driver of novel ideas and technologies for firms. While firms allocate significant resources toward R&D, declining R&D productivity has been a major roadblock in enhancing innovation outcome for firms. Our goal in this study was to investigate if and how IT can mitigate the diminishing returns to R&D investments.

Using archival data on citation-weighted patent counts, IT investments and R&D investments for 161 firms from 1991 to 2003, this study provides several new insights. First, we find that IT intensity mitigates the diminishing returns to R&D, meaning that marginal returns to R&D decrease at a slower rate for firms with higher IT intensity. Our results not only support the findings in related studies that IT has a role in enabling innovation (Huang et al. 2018, Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012, Saldanha et al. 2017) but also highlight the role of IT in mitigating diminishing returns to R&D. This finding underscores the strategic importance of managing overall levels of IT investments in driving innovation. A major implication for allocating discretionary R&D and IT expenditures based on our finding is that firms should use a balanced approach in resource allocation to achieve productive outcomes. Overinvesting in R&D without concomitant IT investments are unlikely to yield the outcomes in terms of patents and, creating the necessary IT infrastructure to handle the information, search, and coordination needs to facilitate scaling of R&D operations is key for innovation success.

Second, our additional analyses indicate that the role of IT is particularly important for large, complex, geo-

## 5.2. Contributions and Implications

Our findings contribute to research on IT business value, particularly on the efect of IT on scaling and growth. While prior findings show that firms with a superior IT infrastructure can grow more eficiently (Duncan 1995, Mitra 2005), these studies focused primarily on core operational aspects of firms and not as much on the intangible aspects such as innovation and product development. Our study adds an important additional finding that firms with an IT-intensive infrastructure can scale knowledge intensive processes such as R&D. In addition, our results suggest that information technologies can help larger firms to create communication patterns that mimic those of small and entrepreneurial firms. Other studies suggest use of organizational practices to keep larger organizations innovative, such as use of agile practices for software development (Kude et al. 2015), providing time and flexibility to employees to experiment with new ideas (Mims 2015), job rotation, and involving customers, complementors, and partners in innovation activities (Foerderer et al. 2016, Huang et al. 2018, Saldanha et al. 2017). It would be interesting for future research to investigate the complementarity between IT and other practices to keep organizations innovative by leveraging newer approaches that can also uncover more complex patterns of configurations (El Sawy et al. 2010).

Our research also contributes to the literature on R&D management. This line of research has focused on the returns to R&D spending, to assess whether there are diminishing returns to R&D, and whether firms overspend on R&D (Acs and Audretsch 1988, Graves and Langowitz 1993, Link and Rees 1990, Mank and Nystrom 2001). Limited attention, however, has been paid to the mechanisms that can enhance R&D productivity. Lack of capabilities and presence of rigidity have been commonly referred to as the reasons behind diminishing returns. Our results suggest that use of IT can be one of the mechanisms that will mitigate diminishing returns. Hence, it would be useful for such studies to take the efect of IT into consideration when examining the efect of R&D spending. For instance, Chan et al. (1990) examined the stock market response to R&D spending announcement in high-tech versus low-tech industries and found that the abnormal return is positive for high-tech firms and negative for low-tech firms. It would be interesting to examine whether the results are similar for high-IT versus low-IT firms. Moreover, it would be interesting to examine the efect of R&D spending announcements that also mention the use of IT.

Our findings have important managerial implications. First, the efect of IT investments on innovation should help dispel doubts about the strategic value of investing in IT. Second, an indirect implication of our results is that IT investments can contribute to revenue growth by allowing firms to come up with new patents, products, and services. To the extent that ITenabled revenue growth is more promising than ITenabled cost reduction for firm profits (Mithas et al.

2012), managers should allocate resources among IT projects to facilitate innovation that difer with respect to these two objectives, although we recognize that some innovations can be dual focused in that they may enable both revenue growth and cost reduction simultaneously (Mithas and Rust 2016).

Third, for managers who oversee R&D activities, this study suggests that using IT to manage knowledge is important, especially for more R&D intensive operations. Because flexibility is critical in a fast-changing environment, managers in such industries should pay more attention to their IT investment, which might help them adapt to changes quickly.

Fourth, from a top management or board perspective, IT investments should receive significant attention in governance and resource allocation processes because they influence the innovation capacity of firms. CIOs can use the findings to develop a business case and justification for investments in IT based on their linkages with other projects and impact on intangibles such as patents or improvement in employee engagement and customer satisfaction (Fichman et al. 2005).

Finally, from a policy perspective, given that prior work has shown the value relevance of IT in financial markets and to the extent that IT investments have efects similar to that of R&D investments, U.S. firms listed on public stock exchanges should be required to disclose their IT investments and risks associated with them, just as they are required to do with R&D, for further transparency and signals for more eficient financial markets. Our suggestion for firms to disclose their IT spending and its detailed breakup will not only facilitate a better understanding of discretionary expenditures of firms but also inform investors just how well companies are managing their intangible resources because they do influence firm profits and risk as documented in other studies (Bharadwaj et al. 2009, Dewan et al. 2007, Kim et al. 2017, Mithas et al. 2016, Tian and Xu 2015).

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Although this study provides useful insights, it has some limitations that are inherent in our research design and data availability. First, this study uses data on relatively large firms, which limits the generalizability of our findings when it comes to smaller firms. Second, although we used lags in citation-weighted patent counts to avoid reverse causality and, a fixedefects model to account for potential endogeneity, further research is needed to establish causality. Third, while this study explored the role of IT intensity in innovation, more granular data on specific IT systems and applications can provide further insights (Liu et al. 2013).<sup>11</sup> Also, there remains a need to understand the role of IT human capital in enhancing the innovation capacity of firms. Following prior research that suggests that high-skilled foreign-born workers make significant contributions to innovative activities of firms (Hunt and Gauthier-Loiselle 2010, Kerr and Lincoln 2010), we call for studies to explore the role of IT professionals, including foreign-born workers, in enhancing the innovation capacity of firms. Fourth, while we studied the synergetic efect of IT and larger R&D contemporaneously, it would be interesting to examine how IT investment influences the size of R&D over time. As suggested by Im et al. (2013), the relationship between IT and firms size manifests over time, and a similar efect might exist between IT and R&D size. Moreover, the efect of IT on R&D size could be determined by multiple mechanisms. IT can both reduce the coordination costs within the organization (allowing for a larger R&D organization), and the costs of sourcing innovation externally (allowing for more open innovation) as well. In addition, IT may reduce the R&D workforce by digitizing some R&D activities even though we believe this efect to be probably weak in the context of R&D given that R&D needs scientists and engineers with advanced degrees with specific expertise. It would be very interesting to tease out which of these mechanisms are salient and how they influence R&D outcomes and productivity.

Finally, future studies should examine the role of IT in other innovation-related outcomes, such as inimitability of innovation, speed of difusion of innovation, and whether innovations are industry leading or lagging (Leiponen and Helfat 2010, 2011; Srivastava et al. 2013). The role of IT in the generation of radical innovations or appropriating gains from such innovations can also be very useful themes to examine in future research (Dedrick et al. 2010; Linden et al. 2009, 2011).

In conclusion, our goal in this study was to understand how IT enhances firm innovation efectiveness. We argued and found that IT intensity mitigates the diminishing returns to R&D intensity, and such efects are stronger when firms face greater complexity in their R&D activities. This study makes important contributions to research in both information systems and R&D management. The findings also provide actionable insights to IT executives, R&D managers, and chief executive oficers on the levers to manipulate in their quest to enhance the innovation outcome of their firms. To the extent that innovation is one of the critical ingredients for survival and success in increasingly competitive markets, firms need to pay attention to the management of their IT resources and digital platforms to become more innovative.
