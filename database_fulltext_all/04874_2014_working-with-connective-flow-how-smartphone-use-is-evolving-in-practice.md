# Working with connective flow: how smartphone use is evolving in practice

Kristine Dery<sup>1</sup>, Darl Kolb<sup>2</sup> and Judith MacCormick<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Sydney, NSW, Australia; <sup>2</sup>University of Auckland, New Zealand; <sup>3</sup>University of New South Wales, NSW, Australia

Correspondence: Kristine Dery, Work and Organisational Studies, University of Sydney, N406, Institute Building H03, Darlington, Sydney, NSW, 2006, Australia. Tel: +61409 849 099; E-mail: kristine.dery@sydney.edu.au

## Abstract

Smartphones, those handheld devices that connect us via telephone and the Internet to virtually everyone and everything in the world, are becoming an integral part of everyday life. While there are significant individual and collective benefits from being more connected, there are also concerns associated with ‘always on’ work practices. This paper reports on a two-phase case study of smartphone users in a global financial services firm comparing the use of smartphones and their impact on work over time. We found that mobile communication technology practices have evolved within a relatively short (5-year) period of time as users seek to manage connectivity across work and non-work spaces. Disconnecting from work is no longer possible, nor desirable, for many users, who exercise choice (agency), switching between work and nonwork interactions to regulate the connective flow across multiple connective media.

## Introduction

As global competitive pressures drive organizations to work around the clock and around the world (e.g., Friedman, 2005; Sirkin <sup>et</sup> <sup>al</sup>, 2008), many organizational members <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ‘shut off’ work at the end of the local working day (Perlow, 2012). Not surprisingly, as competitive pressures outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm mount and technologies inside the <sup>fi</sup>rm allow, workers <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves faced with the decisions of if, when and how much to ‘switch off’ from work to participate in family, community and other non-work activities, including sleep, rest and relaxation. Organizations, of course, tend to happily support and endorse employees who ‘go the extra mile’. Indeed, many industrial sectors romanticize ‘work hard/play hard’ organizational cultures (Casey, 1999), which further complicate the psycho-social boundaries between work and non-work. As mobile communication technologies make work increasingly independent of location, the decision to work or not often takes place in non-work settings, that is, in a playground, in a car, at the ski resort or beach, or sitting at a café or kitchen table. In summary, around the globe a multitude of workers are using mobile technologies that allow them to stay connected to work rather than ‘shutting off’ their computers and going home as they might have done in the pre-Internet era.

At present, throughout much of the world, over half of all handheld devices are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as smartphones. Accordingly, connecting to the Internet via smartphones has exploded exponentially in recent years and collectively represents the fastest growing sector of Internet use (Smith, 2010). Smartphones combine telephone voice and text functionality with

Internet data applications such as email, web search and social networking as well as increasingly pervasive location-based services. The technical connectivity afforded by smartphones puts massive communication possibilities into the palm of the user’s hand. The smartphone also constitutes the site where we decide between work and non-work activities.

A growing challenge for both individuals and organizations is achieving a state of connectivity that supports both work productivity and personal well-being (Kolb <sup>et al</sup>, 2012; MacCormick <sup>et al</sup>, 2012). Connective choice (i.e. if, when and how much to connect) is situated within the age-old antinomy (Leonardi & Barley, 2010) of individual free will (agency) <sup>vs</sup> normative social pressures (structures) (Giddens, 1984; Orlikowski, 1992). Individuals can choose if, when and how much to connect, but their choices are made in relation to others, for example, work colleagues, suppliers, customers or family members. Connective choices affect personal and social outcomes. Too much independence may produce poor collective results, while too much engagement with others may cause individual burnout. While mobile phone users cannot escape making connective choices, the manner in which they deal with mobile connectivity appears to have changed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly over a relatively short period of time.

This paper reveals how professionals employed by a global bank engaged with mobile technology (smartphones) to manage their connectivity with work and non-work activities. In particular, we addressed two main research questions. First, how do smartphone users enact choice (agency) in a world of near-constant connectivity? Second, we were interested in how respondents in the same organization changed their behaviours and attitudes over time. To address these questions, we examined the practices of the smartphone users in the research division of a global bank (known as ‘Urbis’ to protect the identity of the organization and the interviewees) using data collected from a series of semi-structured interviews conducted at two points in time, roughly 5 years apart. These data were analysed to gain insights into how smartphone users’ behaviours, attitudes and choices evolved over time.

## Smartphones and work

While smartphones are by de<sup>fi</sup>nition mobile, the revolutionary element of smartphones is not their mobility, but rather their enhanced connectivity that users employ and rely on (Castells <sup>et al</sup>, 2007). Of course, smartphones are not objective, neutral tools. Nor do they necessarily have a deterministic impact on all users. Users ‘enact’ the features of technology in ways that satisfy their particular needs and interests (Orlikowski, 1992). Therefore, we would expect widely different behaviours to be evident as users adopt smartphone technology to manage both time and space to meet their individual work and nonwork demands. As users interact with their smartphones they not only use the technology to support their work demands, they also reshape work around what the technology can do. For example, a user can provide customers with real-time information and feedback at times and in places that were previously not possible. It is this evolving technology-in-use (Orlikowski, 1992) that is of particular interest and the focus of this research.

Before the advent of the smartphone, those who used mobile phones for work purposes generally reported that such technologies improved their work productivity, but also increased the pace of life (Chesley, 2010). This quickening pace of life means our attention to those around us can be compromised as face-to-face relationships compete with a growing number of ‘screens’ that also compete for attention (Atchley, 2010; Carr, 2010; Gregg, 2011). In many contexts, the mobile handheld device often takes precedence over face-to-face encounters and ‘screens’ of all sorts have been described as having an addictive quality (Turkle, 2011). For some, extreme volumes of email have become a symbol of professional importance as a proxy for a high workload (Barley <sup>et al</sup>, 2011). Patterns of addictive behaviour were <sup>fi</sup>rst associated with the BlackBerry, which became notoriously referred to as the ‘CrackBerry’ (Mazmanian <sup>et al</sup>, 2005; Funtasz, 2011). Despite such addictive attributes, Mazmanian and colleagues found that BlackBerry users nonetheless reported that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts far outweighed the down-sides of the device (2006).

Matusik and Mickel (2011) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three types of smartphone use, namely ‘enthusiastic,’ ‘balanced’ and as ‘trade-offs’. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly for our purposes here, they suggested that, compared to other work technologies, when it comes to the use of handheld devices, organizational norms and pressures play a less dominant role than users’ individual choices of when and how to use these connective technologies. Similarly, Wajcman <sup>et al</sup> (2008) found the affordances of mobile connective technologies to serve personal constructions of self and other social needs at least as much as organizational needs. And, more recently, Mazmanian and colleagues found smartphone use to ‘paradoxically’ lend a sense of personal autonomy, while simultaneously controlling individuals’ work behaviours (Mazmanian <sup>et al</sup>, 2013). All these authors also call for more and better theoretical explanations of mobile technology use. This paper offers an exploration in that direction.

## Connective choice

The questions of <sup>if</sup>, <sup>when</sup>, <sup>how</sup> and <sup>how much</sup> to use smartphones brings us to the fundamental issue of choice and ‘free will’. The notion of free will emerged during the Renaissance (14th – 17th centuries) and lives on today in the form of human ‘agency’ (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). Within his structuration theory, Giddens identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a ‘duality’ between agency and structure in society, wherein individuals’ actions may be somewhat idiosyncratic, but are nonetheless constrained and enabled by social structures, for example, the norms that guide and shape behaviour (Giddens, 1979, 1984). Others have explored the psychological capacity for individuals to make choices and to impose those choices on the world (Bandura, 2001) or act with ‘autonomy’. Autonomy in work environments is generally associated with job satisfaction, higher motivation and lower burnout.

The concept of human agency has been applied more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to technology, that is, exploring the extent to which individuals are more or less free to enact the technology in different ways. Moreover, when such freedom is enacted, both anticipated and unanticipated consequences can occur (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). The implication is that technology is not the sole determinant of social change, but rather it is employed at the discretion of the human agents. As Leonardi & Barley (2010) proclaim in their recent review of the social construction of socio-technical systems, technological determinism is well and truly defeated in organizational science. Rather, it is generally accepted that social and organizational change occurs over time as users enact technology in response to their local experiences and personal and social needs. Ultimately, the practice of technology becomes embodied within the practice of the individual, which is recursively re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the practices of the organization and/or society.

The notion of agency is particularly applicable to mobile devices, where the organization’s technology resides literally in the hands of the individual user, thereby allowing and prompting the user to make choices regarding its use for personal or organizational purposes. Cousins & Robey (2005) applied the concept of agency to describe how mobile bankers’ choices to adopt (or not) handheld devices (e.g., Palm Pilot) demonstrated various elements of Emirbayer & Mische’s (1998) typology of human agency: iterational (habitual aspects informed by the past), projective (capacity to project into the future and inform new ways of working) and practical-evaluative (capacity to contextualize past habits and future projections in the moment). Although all the mobile bankers in Cousins and Robey’s study were offered smartphones, each individual exercised agency (choice) of when, why and how to use the device. Ultimately, smartphone technology is implicitly or explicitly designed to enhance human choice or freedom (agency) by offering the user the prime feature of ubiquitous connectivity to manage their work and nonwork spaces according to his or her individual needs.

While individual choice is inherent in the mobile workforce, it is not without some cost as well as bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to users. Empirical evidence suggests that users who embrace mobile technologies often <sup>fi</sup>nd increased connectivity to be destructive to their well-being (Mazmanian <sup>et al</sup>, 2013). Some commentators (Faragher, 2006; Meall, 2006; Rockwell, 2006) argue that the capability of smartphones to connect an individual to work at all times reduces the ability of some users to separate work and non-work spaces, thereby reducing choice. Rather than the positive and desired consequences of increased engagement with the organization, users become exhausted, cynical and disengaged (MacCormick <sup>et al</sup>, 2012). Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence from peers and colleagues also constrains choice to some degree, partly through improvised learning among peers (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) and partly from institutional and professional norms (i.e., long hours in professional services <sup>fi</sup>rms). Fulk (1993) proposed that the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of peers, in particular related to organizational communication expectations, largely shapes the way in which users enact a given technology. It is not surprising then that many smartphone users do not always feel free to disconnect from their organizational responsibilities and thus experience stress and potential burnout (Besseyre de Horts <sup>et al</sup>, 2012; Mazmanian <sup>et al</sup>, 2013).

## Duality vs flow

Borrowing the notion of duality from Giddens’ (1984) structuration theory, Kolb (2008) has suggested that connectivity represents a duality of connects and disconnects. Such a duality portrays an image of the smartphone user making intermittent contact (connecting) with others, then disconnecting, re-connecting, and so on. A duality lens suggests that users learn when to connect and when to disconnect in order to maintain the right amount of connectivity, for example, to more or less ‘balance’ their work and personal lives. In their ‘requisite connectivity’ model Kolb <sup>et</sup> <sup>al</sup> (2008) suggest that having the right kind and amount of robust and reliable connectivity for the task or purpose at hand creates the threshold condition (‘requisite connectivity’) at which distributed work can be accomplished. Applying this model, when users engage with smartphones and other mobile technologies they are striving for the requisite technical and social connectivity needed to achieve desirable outcomes. In their study of global virtual teams, Kolb et al (2008) found that teams will try to make regular adjustments to avoid states of hypo-connectivity (too little connectivity) and hyperconnectivity (i.e., too many emails, phone calls, meetings, etc.) in order to be productive. Once the requisite connectivity threshold has been achieved, members may transcend to another state, which has been referred to as ‘connective <sup>fl</sup>ow’ by several authors (Middleton, 2007; Kolb <sup>et al</sup>, 2008; Wajcman <sup>et al</sup>, 2008).

We found these earlier conceptualizations of <sup>fl</sup>ow were helpful but limited when it came to interpreting our data. In general, our empirical evidence suggests that managing connective <sup>fl</sup>ow is more nuanced than Kolb’s duality. In our discussion below, we explore how we had to reconceptualize the <sup>fl</sup>ow metaphor in light of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. It is important to note that we did not initiate this study with the intent of challenging Kolb’s duality, nor Kolb and colleagues’ conceptual framework around connective <sup>fl</sup>ow. In the context of smartphone use, however, we found the application of a duality of connects and disconnects relatively easy in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews, but more problematic in the second round. The contrariness of our data vis-à-vis duality led us to re-consider whether connective <sup>fl</sup>ow is a better conceptual model or metaphor through which to interpret our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

It must also be noted that the term ‘connective <sup>fl</sup>ow’ is not to be confused with the psychological state of ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’ identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Csikszentmihalyi (1990). The psychological state of ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’ is associated with an optimal balance between challenge and competence, which can lead to a heightened sense of well-being (in part from increased levels of dopamine) and the loss of a sense of time passing (‘time <sup>fl</sup>ies’). It is often associated with high-performance athletes, but can be achieved by anyone who gets totally absorbed in a challenging pursuit. While it is certainly conceivable that someone experiencing connective <sup>fl</sup>ow might experience some of the elements associated with psychological <sup>fl</sup>ow, the two metaphors are distinct and certainly not interchangeable. Connective <sup>fl</sup>ow draws upon the transmission model of communication, wherein messages and signals are exchanged (<sup>fl</sup>ow) between sender and receiver. Another way to think of connective <sup>fl</sup>ow is in the words of one of our study participants below, who referred to the <sup>fl</sup>ow of connectivity being like a ‘tap’ to regulate the volume of connectivity pouring into one’s life.

Overall, while the smartphone has been recognized as a game-changing technology, theorizing about its use is in its infancy. From the neurological threats associated with technological over-stimulation from multiple ‘screens’ and fragmented attention (the myth of ‘multitasking’), to toxic work cultures that threaten core social institutions of family and community, we understand smartphones to be both a blessing and a curse. The literature has extended socio-technical and sociomaterial theories to mobile technologies and these are generally applicable and helpful. But the literature does not delve into the micro-choices that present themselves in mobile technologies. Paradox abounds. While mobile phones represent independence for the user, never has large-scale social networking and normative social pressure been more acute. Given the lively interest and uptake of smartphone technologies, our intent was to empirically explore and theoretically expand our understanding of their use.

## Research method

Our study was motivated by two main questions. First, how do smartphone users enact choice (exercise agency) to connect and disconnect in a mobile-enabled world. Second, we were interested in how responses to these questions changed over time. An interpretative epistemology was selected for this study as we sought to focus on the use of smartphones and the subjective meanings attributed to the technology by our interviewees. This approach was found to be especially helpful in that it challenged the idea that technologies and technological artefacts have a pre-given and <sup>fi</sup>xed meaning. Instead it supports the position that the opinions of interviewees can only be understood in the context of individuals and groups comprehending, interpreting, using and engaging with the technologies (Dery <sup>et al</sup>, 2006), while also being mindful of the strategic, industry or national context. This approach advocates two essential assumptions: <sup>fi</sup>rst, that smartphone users create their own meanings as they interact with the capabilities of the technology and, second, that context can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the creation of such meanings. This perspective is consistent with the perspective adopted by Orlikowski and Baroudi who contest that ‘the design and use of technology in organisations, in particular, is intrinsically embedded in social contexts, marked by time, locale, politics, and culture. Neglecting these in<sup>fl</sup>uences may reveal an incomplete picture of information systems phenomena’ (1991, p. 12).

A qualitative single-site case study design (Berg, 2009) was adopted and data were collected at two time points in order to understand how users understand the evolving practice of mobile connectivity. Small-scale qualitative case studies such as this are also evident in other studies such as Frissen’s (2000) work on the use of information communication technologies in busy households and are useful in their capacity to examine phenomena in depth and enable a more complex level of analysis. By collecting data soon after the initial implementation of smartphones and then again 5 years later (following the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis), we were able to construct our study that provided the opportunity to examine how users’ approaches to the technology had changed over time (Pettigrew, 1990). Selecting meaningful time periods for longitudinal research is not an exact science, although clearly ‘when one puts pen to paper will in<sup>fl</sup>uence what one can see and say’ (Pettigrew, 1990, p. 274). We selected a small sample case study (from within the research division of a global <sup>fi</sup>nancial services company) and studied it over two time periods: the <sup>fi</sup>rst timed with the implementation of the smartphones to get an initial picture of the technology in use, and the second 5 years later when smartphones were well embedded into work practices. This time lapse is recognised by Pettigrew (p. 273) as a ‘social construction of time’ where events make up our experience of change and create natural breaks in time. As Urbis emerged from the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis (GFC) in 2011 there was a sense that they were re-orienting into a new time period and thus it seemed like an appropriate stage to conduct the second round of research to enable the inclusion of both real-time and re<sup>fl</sup>exive (hindsight) experiences of smartphone use.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted focused on the user experience of the smartphone (e.g., the RIM BlackBerry and Apple’s iPhone) and its impact on participants’ jobs, including the ability they felt they had to construct and manage their time on work and non-work activities. The <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews were conducted in 2006 and the second round in 2011, with as many of the same interviewees in both rounds as possible. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant redundancies in the bank over this time period, together with natural attrition. meant that some of the emplovees from round one were no longer with the bank in round two of our data collection. Where this occurred, we interviewed an executive in the same or similar role. On-site interviews in the Urbis Research Divison (URD) of<sup>fi</sup>ces enabled the researchers to gather contextual information pertaining to the work environment (Cresswell, 1998; Yin, 2003) through observation of general of<sup>fi</sup>ce material, activities surrounding the work of the employees and experiencing the general ‘feel’ of the of<sup>fi</sup>ce. This was important to situate the research and provide context for our interpretive analysis. A minimum of two researchers conducted, participated and took notes during each of the interviews. The interview data were supplemented with material, such as policy documents, emails, websites and annual reports deemed to be informative and relevant (Neumann, 2000), together with follow-up emails and discussions with participants. Table 1 summarizes the data sources. Interviews were recorded and the transcriptions coded using a combination of NVivo, manual coding and spreadsheet analysis to group data according to themes (Roulston <sup>et al</sup>, 2003).

Four research themes were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that provided insights into how the users understood the requirements for connectivity and the way in which they were able to manage their work in a connected context. These themes were: (1) smartphone capabilities (skills, hardware, applications, data access and search), (2) requirements for connectivity (expectations from industry, organization, clients, colleagues and other stakeholders to be connected), (3) reconstruction of work time and space (such as mobility, access, <sup>fl</sup>exible work practices that enable the user to enact the smartphone capabilities to construct new ways of working across time and space), and (4) managing boundaries (balancing personal and work objectives). These themes enabled us to group the data to gain insights into what technology was being used, how it was being used, and what the impacts were on the location and intensity of work.

## The case study: URD

Urbis is a leading global <sup>fi</sup>nancial services provider headquartered in the U.S. with a presence in over 100 countries employing in excess of 100,000 people worldwide. As an early adopter of BlackBerrys, the focus of the organization was to be equipped with technology to meet the needs of an increasingly mobile workforce, working across the globe. The Head of IT Architecture and Development described their position in this way,

We are committed to the continuous development of new services and creating convenient access to more business applications.

Urbis envisioned the BlackBerry as more than a mobile email device and was motivated in their adoption by the opportunity to manage connectivity more effectively in an increasingly virtual and mobile world. Initially the BlackBerry was only distributed to the senior levels of management and others on a ‘needs only’ basis with criteria typically built around mobility (such as travel requirements, client demands). However, in a short period of time the demands of executives across the organization led to widespread adoption of the devices. These early adoption patterns are interesting as they frame some of the strategic thinking behind the uptake of the technology prior to the momentum that occurred as the smartphone technology exploded and was quickly absorbed by business in general as one of the basic ‘tools of the trade’.

Urbis employees number more than 2000 in Australia in a wide range of roles. However, the focus of our research was the Research Department, employing 80 staff based in the Australian Head Of<sup>fi</sup>ce. The Research Department provided comprehensive economic, equity, quantitative and <sup>fi</sup>xed income research. The quality of their research consistently ranked them at the top of their <sup>fi</sup>eld. In Australasia, this group undertakes stock market analysis on 200 listed companies, representing coverage of 90% of the combined Australian and New Zealand Stock Exchanges, as measured by market capitalization. Analysts had a wide range of backgrounds spanning chartered accounting, <sup>fi</sup>nance, treasury, funds management and industry. A key to their competitive advantage was being able to rely on connectivity with overseas colleagues to make valuation comparatives as well as to track the global span of a number of Australasian companies in terms of both their operations and end markets.

The work was divided according to industry with analysts specializing in knowledge of all stakeholders and their corporate activities within each sector (e.g. Telecommunications, Mining, Healthcare). Analysts were expected to be leading experts in their sectors and available at all times to meet the needs of clients, worldwide colleagues, industry advisors and media. This is a very competitive, high performance work environment where the value of individuals is determined by their ability to access, analyse and communicate critical knowledge to investors in a timely manner. Connectivity is, therefore, critical to success in this area of the business.

Data were collected in two phases: the <sup>fi</sup>rst in 2006 when the BlackBerrys had recently been implemented, and the second at the end of 2011, which was post GFC and a time of recovery and cautious regrouping. In order to analyse the changes in behaviours over time the data have been presented according to the broad themes identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the analysis: smartphone capabilities, requirements for connectivity, reconstruction of work time and space, and work/non-work boundary management.

## Phase 1 of interviews (2006)

At the time of the initial interviews, 2006, Urbis had an experimental perspective on the role of the smartphone and were in many ways trialing how it might add value to their operation. URD were very early adopters within the company with the management and senior analysts recognizing early opportunities to improve performance through better communication technologies. Phase 1 data are presented below according to the themes listed above.

Theme 1: smartphone capabilities BlackBerrys were initially offered to all senior management. The BlackBerry was largely restricted at this time to email delivery (i.e., no Internet search or data capacity) and interestingly most devices did not enable mobile phone capabilities. In this sense, the term ‘smartphone’ was somewhat of a misnomer for these devices, for they were used as a telephone relatively infrequently. Most users in our study initially retained their existing mobile phone either because their BlackBerry was not voice enabled or because they chose to manage separate devices for personal reasons. The head of research publications (URD) expressed a commonly held view when she said that,

A lot of times I’m on the phone and doing the email at the same time because I have to then review the email, talk through, and approve. I can still do that. I’ll talk it through with a few people. I could have an earpiece and then typing at the same time. I could do that but I just <sup>fi</sup>nd I like to have it separate still. One day I might combine but I have the Motorola phone and I like that phone. … a second reason why I won’t combine. I don’t have to and I have the choice to say it’s not work so I’m not going to bring that. But if I combine it it’s kind of with me all the time.

A small number of senior management declined the technology for fear of being constantly available for work, retaining their existing mobile phone and continuing the practice of distributing their mobile and home numbers only for emergency contact requirements. They maintained a reliance on desktop computing for all other data requirements. Email-enabled devices were also made available for those outside of the senior management team who could present a business case for the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of increased <sup>fl</sup>exibility and mobility (e.g. corporate travel was typically used as justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for requesting a BlackBerry).

Initially, a series of learning and development seminars were offered to train BlackBerry users on how to integrate the BlackBerry into better time planning. These sessions were designed to ‘develop a certain sense of ruthlessness about how to manage your time’ (Urbis Time Management educator). This was accomplished to a large degree by opening up the ‘black-box’ of the technology and educating users on how to manage the BlackBerry in ways to gain ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies from the effective management of email. Further understanding of the capabilities and use of Black-Berrys was, however, largely left to the individuals and thus there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variation in competencies and usage of the device. The head of research publications explained how typically expertise was developed,

Because I was one of the early adopters and I have to train others and I have to evangelise the use of

BlackBerry to everyone, I make sure that I know everything that I need to know about BlackBerry.

Generally the capabilities of the technology (e.g., mobile email) were used to enhance <sup>fl</sup>exibility of work time and space. Choices for executives centred around the decision on whether to adopt the technology, whether to replace the existing mobile phone or enhance it, and the acquisition of skills to access its capabilities. The BlackBerry was largely acknowledged as a technology to enhance existing management practices by accessing and responding to information faster.

Theme 2: requirements for connectivity While there was a wide range of work behaviours evident in URD, it was generally considered that the increasing organizational demands for higher responsiveness and performance had resulted in the need for increased levels of connectivity within and beyond the workplace. Thus, any technological artefact that generated greater levels of connectivity was valued by URBIS generally, and particularly in URD where customer responsiveness was critical.

Its really just increasing demands. Obviously out on the streets it’s just an increasingly competitive society; much greater pressure, globalization … I think was historically much easier for an analyst to turn the mobile phone off, than it is to actually say well I didn’t look at the BlackBerry. (Senior Executive Urbis)

On one hand, Urbis recognized the need for work-life balance and had an HR policy to support those principles. On the other hand, there was an expectation that employees would <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to maximize their organizational connectivity to meet corporate goals. Managing connectivity was considered both desirable and integral to organizational performance and thus a requisite skill for a successful Urbis executive. While the additional duration of workplace expectations was accumulating collectively among colleagues and customers, there was also signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant general recognition that extended hours were largely driven by individual personalities for whom BlackBerry connectivity made it possible to stay connected with work.

There is work<sup>fl</sup>ow throughout the whole night so I use the BlackBerry a lot throughout the night to monitor work<sup>fl</sup>ow. (Research Publications Manager URD)

This manager’s job in URD required work to be published overnight for release the next day and while the manager had always been required to be contactable afterhours, she identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that the BlackBerry had extended her ability to be more connected and consequently her desire to be engaged with work for longer in the evenings and during holidays than before.

… when it’s with you and you are seeing it all the time it’s so tempting. People will be tempted to actually send it (emails) to you and expect you to respond. (Research Publications Manager URD)

Taken together, it was also widely recognized that while these demands to be connected to the work place outside of working hours were not new, they were exacerbated as a result of the increased connectivity enabled by smartphones. Corporate policies and training designed to improve time management seemed to have little impact on the extended hours worked by employees in URD. Global customer demands, workload and performance demands appeared to drive industry-wide norms and expectations of long working hours. While the BlackBerry was highly valued as a tool for liberating users from the restrictions of physically having to be in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, there was a general acknowledgment that duration of work was exacerbated by the increased expectations to be connected.

Oh it extends it a lot. I’ll work to about nine, nine-thirty … but I’ll keep <sup>fl</sup>icking back and forth on the BlackBerry until about midnight. As soon as I wake up, I’ll look at it again. (Business Unit Manager, URB)

Generally there was an acceptance among our sample of executives that the BlackBerry itself was responsible for the increased levels of expectations around connectivity. While it was always a requirement to be available by phone outside of the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, the new requirement was to be available by email. There was little evidence of choice being legitimately enacted. Moreover, there was a sense that being connected was an intrinsic requirement of the job and largely beyond the control of the individual. Disconnecting manifested itself largely as resistance, with users deliberately letting the BlackBerry run out of battery, leaving it ‘by mistake’ in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, holidaying in places with no service, forgetting the charger and other acts of deliberate sabotage or benign neglect. Users typically had a love–hate relationship with the technology, often acknowledging the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of increased connectivity, while resenting the 24/7 intrusion of work and therefore seeking the opportunity to legitimately or illegitimately disconnect.

Theme 3: reconstructing work time and space Along with increased requirements to be connected, the mobile nature of work beyond the of<sup>fi</sup>ce building was another common theme across all interviewees. This was often related to the global nature of the work in URD with interviewees re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on regular requirements for contact with colleagues, customers across time zones.

Being a global matrix organization you need to talk with people who are starting their day over the other side of the world … a lot of the conference calls that I need to participate on happen in the middle of the night. I have remote access and I work every evening at home. (HR Manager, Urbis)

Therefore while there was a generally reported extension to the working day, this was generally viewed positively when <sup>fl</sup>exibility allowed a better balance between work and family time. There was overwhelming evidence in the interviews supporting the value of the technology to liberate users physically from the of<sup>fi</sup>ce while still maintaining connectivity with the workplace. The research publication manager at URD describes this degree of connectivity as,

In the past I logged on using remote access at home a lot more … you tend to just sit there and never get out. So I <sup>fi</sup>nd that I am actually there sitting with my son, checking my Blackberry when it blings, but I am there with him. If I am on my computer I wouldn’t be able to do that so the BlackBerry is <sup>fl</sup>exible. You can go anywhere with it. I could go and pick up my son earlier and know that I can still check my email while waiting for him in the car or whatever.

While there was some reference to being less tied to the of<sup>fi</sup>ce space during the day as a result of mobile technology and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when travelling, mobility was largely framed around being able to leave the of<sup>fi</sup>ce and continue working from home or while engaged with non-work activities.

Theme 4: managing boundaries Urbis had a commitment to work/life balance through both corporate policy and rhetoric. All senior managers were eager to point out the need for executives to ‘switch off’ and the company’s respect for personal time. However, while they acknowledged the importance of responsible management in preserving executives’ right to non-work space there was an expectation that the high levels of reward in the industry came with certain obligations, including pushing work boundaries.

Privately we consider it a Faustian pact. We’ve got analysts here who earn you under \$2 million a year in our team. And they don’t get that by working a <sup>fi</sup>ve day eight hour week. So yes, we’re really committed to work life balance. But we’ve got a guy next door who’s the top rating insurance analyst; he works six days a week. And he works 18 hours a day. And he chews up associate support staff, a couple a year, because they just can’t keep up the pace. That’s just the nature of the guy. He’s probably going to blow up one day. (Department Head, URB)

Managing boundaries was considered to be the responsibility of the individual. While policies at URBIS were in place and referred to in discussions more generally around work/life balance, there was an unspoken understanding that the ability to manage boundaries in ways that ensured maximum return for the company (and the executive) was the objective. Thus the BlackBerry was thought to be a potentially valuable tool in URB to implement personalized boundaries if and when required.

## Phase 2 of interviews (2011)

In the second round of interviews, conducted in 2011, the experiences and expectations of users were very different. Urbis had been badly impacted by the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis with severe staff and budget cuts worldwide. URB was under increased pressure to perform and they had been through several rounds of redundancies and re-hiring. Further performance-based redundancies were anticipated during the interview phase. While the business climate was more grim, responses to our inquiry were more optimistic regarding smartphone use. Each theme is examined below and compared with the results in Phase 1.

Theme 1: smartphone capabilities In Phase 2 all senior management and all analysts carried URBIS-issued Black-Berry smartphones. The most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change was the importance placed on the security of data resulting in security <sup>fi</sup>rewalls that severely restricted the functionality of the device for private use. While all of the interviewees in URB had email access on their Urbis BlackBerry, most had no additional web-based capabilities,, such as social media, on their corporate-issued smartphone. There was restricted access to industry-related web-based systems based on role requirements and most still had no phone connectivity on the BlackBerry.

Just as corporate IT policies for mobile technologies were becoming or remaining cautious and conservative, employee expectations for connectivity were increasing exponentially in both work and non-work domains. The rise of social media, increased availability of publically available on-line data, the exponential development of mobile applications and the explosion of smartphone capabilities meant that employees demanded access to a mobile data-enabled lifestyle. Thus the restrictions on the Urbis BlackBerry resulted in most interviewees carrying at least one additional smartphone (personally owned and serviced) and often a tablet computer (e.g., an iPad). The toolkit of mobile devices had become an URB and industry norm rather than an add-on gadget.

I see no need to disconnect and I juggle between two devices. My iPhone is 95% private use … . I can choose to look at the BlackBerry … . I think that is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for those more senior that have a single device. (Senior URB Analyst)

The division of use between devices was, however, complex and speci<sup>fi</sup>c to individual preferences. While some users maintained a separation between work (Black-Berry) and non-work (personal smartphone), others used their personal devices for work-related activities, such as accessing web-based documents (such as industry reports, newspapers), social media (such as LinkedIn), SMS and phone functionality. An associate analyst summed it up as:

Work won’t pay for the calls and the technology, but will load the apps to make you fully connected all the time.

As distinct from Phase1 of our study, the interviewees in 2011 were proactive in their choice of mobile technologies and capabilities. They were prepared to self-fund their technological toolkit in order to get the connectivity and capabilities required and desired/or to perform at the highest levels, both personally and professionally.

Theme 2: requirements for connectivity In general, in Phase 2 higher levels of connectivity had become established as both the desirable and normal state as users functioned in a highly inter-connected, competitive, global industry. In contrast to our earlier interviews, most interviewees in 2011 felt they had a right to disconnect from work. Enacting their freedom was still no mean feat. The organization still demanded (and got) its considerable share of individuals’ time. All reported checking their BlackBerrys when they got home at least once, and more often if something important was happening in other markets. The director of research noted,

It is interesting that they say they choose whether or not to be connected. Yet when London calls them at 3am with a legal issue and they have their phones switched off, they then complain that their report wasn’t released. I say well what do you expect … you need to be available.

This lack of freedom to disconnect was still evident at both times across most of the users, suggesting that it is not a state peculiar to any particular individual or subgroup, but rather that there was a propensity for users in URB to surge from being connected to being over-connected, depending on what was happening around their work and/or home life. Despite (for the most part) the positive intentions of users to serve the organization, their behaviour at times was consistent with Whyte’s (1956) description of ‘non-well-rounded men’ as they became over-connected with work and described resentment and resistance to the duration and intensity of their work activities.

Notwithstanding organizational and industry norms, individual users had varying tolerances to differing levels of connectivity with work, that is, states of connectivity are ‘in the eye of the beholder’ (Kolb <sup>et al</sup>, 2012). For some the concept of constantly looking at their smartphone was positive and they described withdrawal symptoms when parted from their device or having to endure a system breakdown. As a research analyst explained:

I don’t have speci<sup>fi</sup>c times when I shut off from work. Maybe it is because I am younger. I don’t really mind. I’m quite keen to advance my career and stuff. I’m quite happy to work the hours that I do. I don’t think that it’s invading too much into other areas of my life … . I don’t have that many commitments outside of work.

Most respondents did not report feeling over-connected all the time, but many reported signi<sup>fi</sup>cant levels of resentment at the intrusion of work into their non-work space as they negotiated the increased duration and intensity of work. This level of work intensity and connectivity at times resulted in negative feelings toward the workplace and the work itself as the user became more stressed by the high levels of connectivity.

In <sup>fi</sup>nance work seems to be more than life … there doesn’t seem to be much balance … if you have started as a graduate you slip into that little slot and you just work that way … . I choose to disconnect because my partner is only home once a fortnight … if that changed I might feel differently. Even though I do it, I feel guilty because no-one else in my team does it. … it creates anxiety for me when I disconnect. (Associate URB Analyst)

Theme 3: reconstructing work time and space Contrary to earlier trends, there appeared to be less focus on the capability to be mobile in the second phase of interviews with all participants reporting long working days in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce (average 7.30 am – 8 pm) and more focus on being available and connected when required. Rather than the executive becoming more mobile and able to work in alternative workspaces (as anticipated and celebrated in Phase 1), work had become more mobile and simultaneously more of<sup>fi</sup>ce-bound. In addition to the long days (and nights) at the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, working at home, while on annual leave, as well as multi-tasking across multiple devices had become the URB standard. While there was a general understanding that work in itself had become more mobile through smartphone technology, there was also an acknowledgement that this did not necessarily create more <sup>fl</sup>exibility for the executive.

I took a break this year to attend a wedding overseas and that was not a good idea because everyone else was working. I won’t do that again. I will take my breaks when my clients break. (Senior URB Analyst)

The need to con<sup>fi</sup>ne annual leave to match common breaks taken by customers (e.g. the Christmas holiday period) was expressed by most of the interviewees. In general, the affordances (Norman, 1999) of mobility of smartphones had become much more associated with connectivity than the capability to create or experiment with <sup>fl</sup>exible work spaces and practices.

Theme 4: managing boundaries In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of this research many interviewees identi<sup>fi</sup>ed distinct boundaries between work and non-work. Their discussions around mobile technology were frequently framed around their perceptions of these boundaries and, in particular, their understanding of work centrality. However, by the second phase of the study, boundaries had become much more <sup>fl</sup>uid with work and non-work activities seamlessly interacting in the same spaces, albeit with different priorities at different times. Interviewees reported a more fluid approach to boundaries as they juggled work and nonwork more seamlessly, in part through their interaction with multiple mobile devices. Individuals had different approaches to prioritizing work, but work centrality appeared to be less about the need for boundaries and more about engaging with the technology to manage activities in ways that suited individualized needs.

When I get in the taxi to go home I typically will have the Blackberry on silent and I switch to the iPhone. In that way I can keep my eye on email but I am more actively engaged with Facebook, sms and other social media to contact friends. (Junior URB Analyst)

Technologically enabled boundaries were deployed to manage the separation of work and non-work while maintaining the ability to interchange easily between these spaces anytime, anywhere. While work and nonwork activities were typically distinct, the spaces in which these activities were enacted became more <sup>fl</sup>uid (e.g.work carried out at home, and non-work activities completed during work time).

In summary, as an exploratory study of mobile technology use over time, our thematic analysis revealed a shift in users’ attitudes, feelings and behaviours associated with smartphones. In both timeframes, there was praise for the affordances of the devices mixed with resentment at the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty the mobile device represents in terms of ‘leaving work behind’. The normative use of smartphones, however, evolved over time, wherein the business Black-Berry was supplemented with the more personalized iPhone, allowing users to switch between devices as a means of segmenting work with non-work life. Such device-switching behaviour and other patterns of ongoing regular usage suggest to us that users are <sup>fi</sup>nding it easier to regulate the ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’ of connectivity. While at times participants reported exercising binary on-off connective choices, these behaviours were positioned at the extreme ends of the connectivity continuum. More commonly participants’ choices tended to be more nuanced around managing ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’ <sup>vs</sup> switching on and off. We found that connective <sup>fl</sup>ow was managed according to volume (how much) a user desired as well as directionality, that is, with whom one wanted to connect, for example, work colleagues or family members. We now focus our discussion on the implications of viewing and managing connectivity as ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’.

## Discussion: managing flow

As reported above, the interviewees in our sample reported a wide range of behaviours and views on the role of the smartphone technology in their work and personal lives. They generally associated positive attributes such as mobility, <sup>fl</sup>exibility and enhanced connectivity to these handheld devices. However, while they recognized their right and need to moderate their connections with work, they experienced challenges in doing so. The following discussion explores how the changes of behaviours in smartphone users over time might be interpreted through the metaphor of connective <sup>fl</sup>ow in an effort to further develop the theoretical base for future connectivity studies.

Smartphone users in our earlier interviews predominantly referred to their use of smartphones as a connective duality of switching the device ‘on’ and ‘off’. Switching a mobile device on and off could be seen as part of the resistance normally associated with the adoption phase of technology implementation. In agency terms, this re<sup>fl</sup>ects Emirbayer and Mische’s <sup>fi</sup>rst and third variations in the triad of human agency, namely iterational (habits informed by the past) and practical-evaluative (demands and contingencies of the present). In the earlier phases of BlackBerry deployment within our <sup>fi</sup>nancial services <sup>fi</sup>rm, users struggled with their new devices in conventional mindsets of ‘leaving’ work behind (as in leaving an of<sup>fi</sup>ce), even though they understood (and generally appreciated) the fact that these mobile devices were useful primarily because they allow users to ‘take work with them’.

Choices around early deployment of the BlackBerry seemed heavily constrained by pre-existing notions of work practices, or habits. Therefore, rather than enacting the technological capabilities of the BlackBerry to devise new ways of working, pre-existing patterns of work emerged in more exaggerated forms. The technology itself became synonymous with a lack of control over connectivity, and by extension with work itself. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews, participants’ problematization of mobile technology was framed around these concerns, that is, the inability to maintain boundaries between work and non-work. By contrast, during the second round of interviews, responses illustrated more of the practical-evaluative dimension of agency, whereby interviewees framed their choices to connect or disconnect as part of their routine day-to-day practice. In our <sup>fi</sup>rst interviews, connecting was seen as an obligation of work, while disconnecting represented the enactment of personal boundaries. As such, disconnecting was characterized as a form of personal control and counter-balancing mechanism against the organizational norms of near-constant connectivity. Over time, however, as smartphones had become a common feature of day-to-day life, users found ways to enact connective choices, less as a reaction to oppressive work demands, and more as a practical, ongoing facet of contemporary life.

Overall, the evidence in this study suggests that the smartphone has come to represent freedom from work as much as it represents work. The users’ choices were framed less around disconnecting from work, but rather were focused on the monitoring and re-direction of the <sup>fl</sup>ow of connectivity between work and non-work (e.g., personal) activities. In our <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews, users were balancing their BlackBerry use against other aspects of their lives, at times evoking the term ‘CrackBerry’ to describe the connective-addict. By contrast, in the second interviews, not only were interviewees switching back and forth between their company-issued BlackBerry and their personal iPhone, they were framing their usage, not so much as on/off, but as a more <sup>fl</sup>uid connective <sup>fl</sup>ow. One URD Analyst speci<sup>fi</sup>cally likened connectivity to a tap: ‘It is never totally turned off but the <sup>fl</sup>ow changes as required’. We define connective flow as a dynamic series of moderated connects and occasional disconnects that <sup>fl</sup>uctuate as circumstances, communication volumes and contexts change. Connective <sup>fl</sup>ow requires levels of human agency that enable actors to regulate the quantity and/or connective media to suit their circumstances and choices to be more or less able to connect with others. In this study we investigated connective <sup>fl</sup>ow as an alternative metaphor to the duality of connects and disconnects. In some circumstances, the ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’ may be switched completely off, but in most cases there is a trickle, if not a torrent, of visual screens, voice and face-to-face stimuli coming our way, <sup>fl</sup>owing past us. We can seldom avoid or opt out of the stream of connectivity, but we must moderate its <sup>fl</sup>ow in order to avoid the consequences of becoming overloaded and overwhelmed.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support this viewpoint. The way in which users de<sup>fi</sup>ned ‘disconnecting’ from work shifted over time – from a binary notion of connects and disconnects to a more <sup>fl</sup>uid notion of connectivity. Rather than a dualistic view of connectivity (i.e. switching on/switching off), users deployed multiple strands of connectivity (including multiple mobile devices) that they juggled (almost literally) in order to manage their <sup>fl</sup>uxuating requirements for data and discussion. They engaged in a set of practices that allowed them to increase connectivity in one strand, while decreasing connectivity on another strand. For example, having completed their work for the day, they might engage with social media on their iPhone, while still keeping an eye on email activity on their BlackBerry. These shifts in activity and connective strands occurred across various mobile technologies depending on the nature of the activity itself, the demands for data or dialogue, and the personal preferences of the user.

As described above, connective <sup>fl</sup>ow is not the same thing as the psychological state of <sup>fl</sup>ow. Like the psychological variation, however, the notion of ‘connective <sup>fl</sup>ow’ is similarly an attractive metaphor for high-performing lives (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). New metaphors can help us understand phenomena that are evolving at a rapid rate. Noticing how our participants had changed their stance between our <sup>fi</sup>rst study and the second, we believe our conceptual understanding and language also needs to adapt as quickly as necessary to account for such rapid changes in work and society. Through our interviews, we have witnessed the smartphone going mainstream. It is no longer just a tool for the corporate knowledge worker, but it is rapidly becoming the tool that binds the family, work and life together. We saw knowledge workers less concerned about connecting and disconnecting and more focused on getting the right amount and kind of connectivity for the purpose or task at hand (Kolb <sup>et al</sup>, 2008). Managing the <sup>fl</sup>ows of connectivity afforded by mobile technologies embodied in smartphones will no doubt be a challenge, but it is one that few of us can avoid. While the metaphor of connective <sup>fl</sup>ow may not be the <sup>fi</sup>nal resolution to our theoretical understanding, we believe it is a step in the right direction.

## Conclusion

We began our research wondering if, when and how smartphone users connected and disconnected to work, exercising personal choice within the context of an intensely competitive global industry. What we found in our <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews was that, as expected, executives who had BlackBerry smartphones struggled to disconnect and found themselves hyper-connected much of the time. They saw the merits of the device, but acknowledged its addictive qualities and passively or actively sought ways to free themselves from too much connectivity. Five years later, it was evident that these same smartphone users had changed their attitudes and behaviours. Indeed, many had two devices, a BlackBerry for work and an iPhone for personal use. But, more importantly, their adaptation to mobile connective technologies was to perceive them not as something to ‘leave behind’, but rather something that was always with you, but which nonetheless had to be regulated much like water from a tap.

We are not the <sup>fi</sup>rst to apply the notion of <sup>fl</sup>ow to connectivity (see Kolb <sup>et al</sup>, 2008). However, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to offer empirical evidence of users managing <sup>fl</sup>ow. Importantly, our empirical data pushed the concept of <sup>fl</sup>ow further than previously applied. We discovered it more appropriate to apply the metaphor of <sup>fl</sup>ow as controlling connectivity through self-regulation, of keeping the valve open, in order to get the right (appropriate, desired) amount of connectivity for the situation. In contrast to Kolb <sup>et al</sup>’s (2008) suggestion that <sup>fl</sup>ow is an <sup>ideal state</sup>, we suggest it is a <sup>continuous</sup> <sup>process</sup> in the management of dayto-day connectivity. Although careful not to con<sup>fl</sup>ate the two notions, that is, psychological <sup>fl</sup>ow and connective <sup>fl</sup>ow, we nonetheless see linkages between ‘healthy’ onthe-run connective practices and physical and psychological well-being, something that is beyond the scope of this paper, but certainly a worthwhile connection to explore. From our theoretical stance, ‘Crackberry’ addicts were not so much <sup>addicted</sup> to the device as they lacked practice regulating connective <sup>fl</sup>ow, something many more of us have now mastered, though seldom perfectly (Mazmanian <sup>et al</sup>, 2013).

In a collocated, pre-mobile world, it was natural to come and go from work. Essentially, the affordances of communication technology stayed in one place and the worker could leave them behind. With the introduction of the smartphone, it was only natural to perceive them as a work/non-work boundary, where the contest was framed as who connected and disconnected from whom or what. Focusing on tensions around boundaries, however, does not adequately explain how users in our more recent study had adapted their use of mobile devices. Studying smartphone use over time, we discovered three shifts in practice, each with theoretical implications. First, a persistent (‘push’) stream of (mostly) work-related email, coupled with its addictive qualities, gave rise to the ‘CrackBerry’ syndrome, to which early adopters responded by setting up varying ways and means to ‘disconnect’ from the technology and thereby also disconnect from work. Five years on, in our second round of interviews, we found handheld email being signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly upstaged and expanded by handheld applications (‘apps’) for personal use. Given the attraction and popularity of the iPhone and the fact that the BlackBerry remained the of<sup>fi</sup>cial corporate platform, the response of many of our interviewees was to have two devices, thereby separating work from personal computing through <sup>material</sup> means, that is, separate handheld devices for work and ‘play’. Besides the irony that increased technical integration within smartphone devices brought less integration in practice (i.e., two devices instead of one), the <sup>fi</sup>rst big lesson (or strong reminder) from this longitudinal study is that, when it comes to mobile devices, material differences matter (Kallinikos, 2005; W. J. Orlikowski & Scott, 2008).

Second, while we did <sup>fi</sup>nd connecting and disconnecting to be key themes in our <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews, we encountered a shift over time in the way users managed their smartphones. In general, in the second phase of our study, we found users managing the connective <sup>fl</sup>ow of their mobile connectivity, meaning users do not so much connect and disconnect as they regulate an ongoing ‘<sup>fl</sup>ow’ of messages, media and data. Having two smartphones, that is, one for work and the other for personal use, allowed users to shift the connective <sup>fl</sup>ow from one device to another, depending on the context (e.g., time of day, competing interests or social or personal needs), but they tended <sup>not to disconnect totally</sup> from either work or nonwork communication. We believe that managing connective <sup>fl</sup>ow represents a fundamental shift in thinking about mobile computing practices, the implications of which may be seen in future research.
