# CAUSAL INFERENCE GROUNDED IN CAUSAL DIAGRAMS: BENEFITS, LIMITATIONS, AND OPPORTUNITIES FOR THE INFORMATION SYSTEMS FIELD<sup>1</sup>

Ali Tafti Department of Information & Decision Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of Illinois Chicago, Chicago, IL, U.S.A. {atafti@uic.edu}

Galit Shmueli Institute of Service Science, College of Technology Management, National Tsing Hua University Hsinchu, TAIWAN {galit.shmueli@iss.nthu.edu.tw}

We join the important effort of embracing diverse views on causality reflected in a prior MIS Quarterly Editor’s Comment (Mithas et al., 2022b). Specifically, we aim to expand the discussion around a major causal framework and toolkit that, we believe, is largely missing and needed in empirical studies in the field of information systems: that of causal diagrams and structural causal modeling (SCM). Being relatively new, the SCM framework has faced resistance from economists that has only recently begun to soften and, for this reason, remains largely absent in econometrics textbooks and in the curriculum of most information systems PhD programs. In contrast to viewpoints emphasizing the dichotomy between SCM and econometrics or potential outcomes approaches, we explain how SCM can serve as a complementary layer of identification and communication that aligns with such proven design and analysis frameworks. We discuss the current limitations of the SCM framework and opportunities for new research.

Keywords: Causality, causal diagrams, directed acyclic graph (DAG), econometrics, structural causal modeling (SCM)

## Introduction

Establishing sound causal inference has become an exceedingly important prerequisite to publishing empirical work in MIS Quarterly (MISQ) and other premier journals in the field of information systems (IS). Even when an empirical paper avoids making causal claims, a subtext of causality is almost always present in the theorization, motivation, and analysis. There’s good reason for the emphasis on causality in empirical research: Causality is understood as central to many conceptions of theory (Gregor, 2006). Causality ties phenomena together, at least in how we make sense of things, in what is “to us the cement of the universe”—a phrase attributed to the 18thcentury philosopher David Hume (Mackie, 1974).

The important role that causality plays in quantitative IS research is reflected in a prior MISQ Editor’s Comment editorial: “Causality Meets Diversity in Information Systems Research” (Mithas et al., 2022b). Mithas et al. (2022b) mention some “unwelcome consequences” of the explosion of attention to causality, including “endless checks that reviewers sometimes ask authors to conduct or that authors think they must include” (p. iii). They also mention a second unwelcome consequence, namely, “too many researchers overspecialize in their understanding of causality, focusing on one view without appreciating the value of others” (p. iii). The editorial calls for appreciating diverse views of causality and presents three views for “thinking about causality” (p. iv).

We appreciate Mithas et al. (2022b) and interpret their editorial as an invitation for further discussion. This Issues & Opinions article contributes to this important effort of embracing diverse views on causality by expanding the discussion around a major causal framework and toolkit that we believe is largely missing in empirical studies in IS (in addition to most other business research fields): that of structural causal modeling (SCM). The SCM approach by Pearl (2009) offers a system that (1) helps represent the causal story, (2) provides answers to identifiability questions, and (3) provides guidance on how to identify the effects of interest in a way that fits well with other prevailing approaches. According to Pearl (2010), the SCM framework combines features of structural equation models (SEM) used in economics and social science, the potential outcomes framework of Neyman (1923/1990) and Rubin (1974), and the graphical models developed for probabilistic reasoning and causal analysis. While Mithas et al. (2022b) touched upon a few aspects of SCM, we contend that SCM is highly compatible with all “three perspectives on causality” (p. iv) of their taxonomy, including the path analytic, potential outcomes, and configurational perspectives. In that respect, SCM offers a more holistic framework than suggested by the Mithas et al. (2022b) editorial.

Ph.D. programs in IS have recognized the need to train students who aspire to do empirical research in causal inference methods.<sup>2</sup> However, SCM remains largely absent in econometrics textbooks and in most IS Ph.D. programs—in part because it is relatively new and perhaps in part because it has faced resistance from economists, a resistance that has only recently begun to soften.<sup>3</sup> We explain what benefits can be gained from incorporating SCM practices into empirical research. Our focus here is on confirmatory causal studies where the theory is already set. SCM has made many powerful contributions to causal discovery, machine learning, and data recovery, among other applications (Castro et al., 2020; Peters, Janzing & Schölkopf, 2017; Mohan & Pearl, 2021). However, this commentary focuses on how SCM can complement econometric studies in terms of causal identification, which has been a major hurdle for publishing empirical studies in premier IS journals such as MIS Quarterly.

Our intended contribution is to bring to light how SCM can be used by authors and reviewers of research to facilitate studies of causal phenomena in IS as a complement to the prevailing econometrics and potential-outcomes frameworks. This Issues & Opinions article is not intended to be a pedagogical work. Nevertheless, we discuss some SCM ideas and principles to support our points, provide a glossary of terms in Table A1 of the Appendix, and refer readers to further resources. We should remind readers that this piece merely expresses our point of view; as we acknowledge that there are different valid viewpoints, this commentary is not intended as the final word but rather as an invitation to learn more about SCM and exchange views on its usefulness and limitations for advancing IS research.

## Compatibility of SCM with Econometrics

The SCM approach is similar to and compatible with practices of mainstream econometrics (e.g., Angrist & Pischke, 2009) and the statistical analysis of experiments (e.g., Imbens & Rubin, 2015). As in traditional causal econometric studies, SCM begins with a research question of interest and tries to articulate in a causal framing the question: What is the effect of a treatment (or a set of treatments) X on an outcome of interest Y? Next, some questions are asked about how the treatment and outcome may be confounded, for example: What is the source of correlations between X and Y that would not be explained by the causal effect of X on Y? Are such correlations due to variables that are observable or unobservable? Do the unobservable sources of confounding exhibit variations that cannot be proxied or modeled with observable variables? If so, what sources of exogenous variation can be found that directly affect only X? Can an experiment be devised, and what would an ideal experiment look like? These are some basic questions that should be asked while devising a design for empirical investigation.

A second important way that the SCM approach is compatible with traditional econometrics is in the conceptual support provided for conceiving experiments. Econometricians are trained to think about what sources of exogeneity can be exploited to identify the causal effect of interest. The SCM approach supports this in the sense that such sources of exogeneity can be explicitly represented in the causal diagram. Moreover, SCM has a formal notation devoted to such experimental or manual interventions, namely, the do(.) operator. Similar to the explicit causal language used in a causal diagram, the do(.) operator is helpful in clarifying what variables are observational—e.g., X—vs. interventional— e.g., do(X). The do(.) operator is used within a do-calculus that can convert certain causal queries containing do(.), the experimental quantity, into statistical estimands that quantify a causal effect using conditional probabilities or expectations.<sup>4</sup> These causal queries are based upon a set of conditional independence assumptions implied by a causal diagram (Pearl, 2009). SCM and its do-calculus also offer a language and formalism for estimating a causal effect using observational data—as if a randomized experiment had been conducted—given that the causal diagram itself implies that a certain requisite set of conditional independence assumptions holds true. Econometricians can incorporate the process of formulating a causal estimand using Pearl’s do-calculus as a preceding conceptual step before converting such estimands into the parametrized econometric models with which they are familiar. While that extra step would be seamless to the thinking process that econometricians are already wellequipped to engage with, it also provides a clearer and sometimes stronger theoretical grounding for their identification strategy.

A third compatibility is with the potential outcomes (PO) framework by Rubin (1974), which underlies propensity score matching and other causal inference methodologies. The SCM framework incorporates counterfactual notation used in the PO approach. For example, E(Y<sub>X=1</sub>|X=0) expresses what the expected outcome would be under X = 1 (e.g., with treatment) given that what we actually observe is the outcome under X = 0 (e.g., no treatment). Such counterfactual operators—staples of the PO framework—are readily incorporated into SCMbased models; see, for example, Chapter 4 in Pearl et al. (2016) and Chapter 8 of Pearl and Mackenzie (2018).

## Benefits of Causal Diagrams in IS Research

By supplementing econometric techniques with causal diagrams, some important econometric concepts become more intuitive, and common conceptual errors in the design, implementation, and evaluation of research can be avoided. We describe several such uses next.

## Improve Your Causal Storytelling and Literature Understanding: SCM Highlights the Theoretical Components in a Visual, Parsimonious Way

Writing a good causal paper requires clearly conveying its focal causal story. Authors can achieve a clear exposition of their causal story and a better understanding of the literature they rely on by using causal diagrams. In its simplest form, a causal diagram uses nodes and single-headed arrows, where arrows reflect causal relationships between nodes. When the causal diagram does not contain any cycles, it is called a directed acyclic graph (DAG). The SCM approach typically involves the exercise of specifying a causal diagram. This, in itself, is a valuable exercise because it forces a clearer articulation of the research question and fosters engagement with the theoretical mechanisms of interest. Such engagement is more easily avoided when the thinking stays at the level of associations or correlations between certain variables. We may observe coincidences between events, conjecture the existence of associations between certain phenomena, and measure correlations between variables, but once we delve into the reasons for such coincidences, associations, or correlations, we get into causal theories.<sup>5</sup>

When considering the theoretical mechanisms of interest in a research question, we also need to think about the level of aggregation at which those mechanisms could be observable. This is why we must contemplate what nodes and arrows we need to represent in a causal diagram. This is akin to the process of constructing a story. The story has a background— which, to a certain extent, we must justify— but at a certain point, some concepts are taken as primitive according to common understanding. In the process of articulating the story, one confronts questions arising from any theory-driven research: How does this phenomenon of interest play out in the real world? Who are the actors? What are their objectives? What decisions or conundrums confront the actors, and what are their alternatives? What are the potential impacts of the decisions or policy?

We found that questions such as these can help us not only in writing our own papers but also in better understanding the literature that we rely on and cite. Drawing causal diagrams for relevant causal studies (that may not themselves present such diagrams) can also improve our understanding of those studies and help us identify potential opportunities and derive new insights. Tafti and Shmueli (2020) illustrated this approach, drawing a causal diagram to represent the theoretical arguments presented by Bapna et al. (2016), which studied the effect of anonymous browsing on matches in an online dating platform. If one is only interested in the average treatment effect (ATE) of the randomized treatment variable, then such causal diagrams might not add much insight—a point emphasized by Imbens (2014, 2020). However, scholars conducting randomized experiments are often interested in leveraging covariate (or auxiliary) variables to generate insight beyond the overall treatment effects. By drawing causal diagrams explicitly representing a set of background theoretical arguments made by a randomized experiment study (and thus largely avoiding assumptions that are not already implied by the study’s theoretical arguments), Tafti and Shmueli (2020) demonstrated how additional theoretical insights can be generated. Another recent work in the IS field utilizing DAGs for randomized experiments is Park et al. (2024), who demonstrated how covariates in experiments can be instrumental for generalizing or transporting the causal inferences to other settings in which only observational data is available.

## Clearly Express Your Assumptions and Hypotheses: SCM Provides an Explicit Representation of Causal and Correlational Relationships

Many current research papers are written in a way that may contribute to conceptual errors in the design or analysis of empirical studies. To “play it safe,” such as when it is known that causal claims are tenuous, researchers often choose to stick with the language of associations. Associational terms in which the causal relation is left unspecified include associated with, correlated with, increases (decreases) with, observed in conjunction with, coincidental with. These terms tend to be abstract and can be used generically in many contexts. By contrast, causal terms are more context specific and descriptive, tending to employ active verbs. For that reason, it seems that there are many more terms in the English language that describe causal relations than associations, likely because one can embed specific verbs characterizing both why and how something is happening. Causal terms allow for various levels of abstraction. While some are abstract and applicable to many contexts (e.g., causes, leads to, contributes to) others are more context specific (e.g., stimulates, encourages/discourages, incentivizes, accelerates, facilitates, undermines, nurtures, fosters, creates, inflates/deflates, pressures, impedes, excites, depresses, deprives). Causal terms are more effective for explaining a hypothesis than terms that describe associations because causal terms can better describe the specific mechanisms that drive a story or theory. Causal terms also help clarify how to analyze a system of variables. By contrast, language that is confined to stating associational relationships results in theoretical discussions that come across as vague and sometimes intractable, with sentences that can be exceedingly awkward or difficult to parse. Rewriting such text with causal language forces a certain clarity of thinking that often resolves such issues. Using a causal diagram forces us to convey our hypothesis/claim clearly. When we say “A is correlated with B” do we mean in the sense of the top or bottom diagrams in Figure 1? Selecting the appropriate diagram dictates our usage of causal vs. correlational language.

Our point is therefore the following: Beyond achieving clarity of exposition, when authors clearly state the causal aims of their research and their causal background assumptions, it is easier to understand their strategy of causal identification—that is, the identification of a causal estimand of interest and a procedure for quantifying it (Lundberg et al., 2021)—and interpret the empirical findings. This allows scholars to explore the reasons why the causal effect might be confounded. The causal diagram shown by Yang et al. (2024) is a particularly effective example; the authors use their diagram to illustrate the meaning of a set of econometric assumptions and to make it very transparent how those assumptions could possibly break down.

To the extent that the underlying theory driving a relationship is of interest, the causal effect is implicated in the motivation and should not be obfuscated with the language of associations. If the researcher is not sure which causal scenario is the right one, they can explore, compare, and contrast the implications of alternative causal scenarios (more on this below).

More Easily Detect and Estimate Identifiable Effects: SCM Provides a Visual Aid for Causal Identification and Estimation

SCM allows the researcher to figure out which effects are identifiable and provides the estimands for the identifiable causal effects (Lundberg et al., 2021). In other words, SCM tells us which causal questions can be answered and how to answer them. One way in which SCM departs from the thinking process familiar to most econometricians is that, in SCM, the sources of confounding are not expressed as correlations but rather as causal relations involving nodes representing unobservable variables (e.g. U); for example, X ← U → Y means that unobservable variable U is assumed to affect both the treatment X and the outcome Y. Rather than just asserting that an unobserved source of heterogeneity exists or that an error source is correlated with both the treatment and outcome, the source of endogeneity is represented explicitly in terms of its causal underpinnings. This causal relationship can also be expressed with a dotted double-headed arrow in place of U, e.g., X ← - - - → Y (see Fig. 1 bottom). This is the simplest form, but there could be other more complex forms as well.

Consider the following system of nonparametric structural equations, based on an example from Morgan and Winship (2015, p. 32):<sup>6</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{X} = \mathrm{f} _ {1} (\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{C}) \\ \mathrm{B} = \mathrm{f} _ {2} (\mathrm{U} 2) \\ \mathrm{A} = \mathrm{f} _ {3} (\mathrm{U} 1, \mathrm{U} 2) \\ \mathrm{F} = \mathrm{f} _ {4} (\mathrm{U} 1) \\ \mathrm{M} = \mathrm{f} _ {5} (\mathrm{X}) \\ \mathrm{N} = \mathrm{f} _ {6} (\mathrm{X}) \\ \mathrm{Y} = \mathrm{f} _ {7} (\mathrm{F}, \mathrm{G}, \mathrm{M}, \mathrm{N}), \end{array}
$$

where all variables except U1 and U2 are observable. The goal is to measure the causal effect of X on Y, given a dataset generated by the seven structural equations above, which are nonparametric in the sense that the functional forms are unspecified. Most of us, even those well-trained in econometric methods, would probably feel bewildered trying

to make sense of a system of seven structural equations without any kind of visual aid. In trying to formulate a reduced-form model to estimate a causal effect, authors would likely encounter the following kinds of critiques in a review process that is uninformed by SCM: (1) Variables U1 and U2 (both unobservable) are correlated with both the treatment (X) and outcome (Y); hence this is a source of unobserved heterogeneity. (2) Any of the variables A, B, C, F, G, M, or N could be sources of endogeneity or estimation bias if they are subject to measurement error or sampling bias, even if they are all observable. (3) Variable C is likely to be correlated with both the treatment (X) and outcome (Y) and hence might not be a valid instrumental variable. This is a nonexhaustive list of possible threats to identification,<sup>7</sup> and reviewers will typically identify many others. Then, it becomes incumbent on authors to address each of those threats separately, often resulting in what can become a seemingly interminable set of robustness checks. Rather than trying to confront the bewildering set of potential threats to identification, following the approach by Pearl and Bareinboim (2014) we suggest that authors be allowed to state their licensing assumptions in the form of a causal diagram.<sup>8,9</sup> The causal diagram in Figure 2 is a visual representation of the seven structural equations above. This system admits three basic strategies for identification of the causal effect of X on Y, that become clear with the aid of the diagram.

Three strategies for causal identification (see Morgan and Winship pp. 30-33): (1) The method of blocking the backdoor paths, i.e., conditioning on variables along paths that lead from X to Y where there’s an incoming arrow into X. Here we can block the two backdoor paths that go through F (highlighted in yellow and pink) by either conditioning on F or on the combination of A and B. (2) The front-door adjustment method using nodes M and N. (3) The instrumental variables method using node C. Although this is the identification strategy among the three that is heavily favored in our own research community, it is the only one that requires imposing additional functional form assumptions, in particular the linearity assumption that is given in almost all econometric textbooks (see Pearl, 2009, p. 90 for further discussion).

## Avoid Unhelpful Characterization Instrumental Variable Requirements

We have encountered authors or reviewers making statements such as the following: “According to Angrist and Pischke (2009), a good IV should satisfy two requirements, that it is correlated with the endogenous variable (relevance), but uncorrelated with any other determinants of the dependent variable (exclusion restriction).” This statement is clear and accurate, but it can be hard to fully appreciate and apply all of its nuanced implications. It is common to see reviewers inadvertently object to the validity of a study’s IV by stating: “The instrumental variable (IV) is correlated (associated) with the outcome.” By itself, this latter statement is not very helpful to authors, because, in fact, valid IVs will usually be correlated with the outcome. There is an alternative way of understanding the validity requirements of instrumental variables using causal language: The effect of the IV on the outcome should be entirely mediated by endogenous independent variables, and their effect on the outcome should not occur through any causal paths that circumvent the set of variables in the model nor should they be directly influenced by any unobserved causes of the outcome variable. Although this textual description of the required conditions is lengthy, it can be accurately conveyed with simple causal diagrams that result in a much more intuitive understanding. Figure 3 shows two variants of classic IV models: an unconfounded IV (A) and a partially confounded IV (B).

Importantly, causal diagramming allows for the representation of more nuanced variants of IV models such as generalized instrumental variables—see Figure 4, (C) and (D) (van der Zander et al., 2015), and they can also illustrate invalid IV models—see Figure 4, (E). In (C), the instrumental variable Z1 is indirectly influenced by an unobservable U that also affects the outcome, but that influence is blocked by an observable node Z2; hence Z1 conditioned on Z2 is the valid instrument for that system (Brito & Pearl, 2002; van der Zander et al., 2015). In an IV regression model, one would condition upon Z2 in both first and second-stage IV regression models; that is, one would include Z2 as both an instrument and a regressor. In (D), Z1, the exogenous cause of X, is unobserved; but Z2 is an observable proxy for Z1 that can stand in as a valid IV. The use of causal diagrams allows us to distinguish these scenarios from models such as (E) in which no valid instrument exists.

The machinery for estimating most types of IV models is already in the econometrics texts, and standard statistical software such as R or Stata can be used. But the use of causal diagrams provides conceptual guidance on which instruments and models are valid, and on how to use them to provide the desired causal estimate.

In these particular examples, it can be seen how the language of correlations or conditional independencies may not provide sufficient intuition to evaluate the validity of an instrumental variable. Rather, causal language can provide a more intuitive and grounded understanding of the assumption structure from which the econometric definitions based on correlations and conditional independencies can flow. That is, the correlation structure can be inferred from the causal diagram, but the diagram cannot necessarily be inferred from the correlation structure. Logically, this implies that there is information in the causal diagram that is not conveyed in the correlation structure. See also, for example, the use of causal diagrams in Liu et al. (2022) to illustrate identifying assumptions. Researchers can better develop their intuition by first specifying how the IV is causally situated with respect to other variables in the system, from which one can (as needed) more easily deduce the compatible econometric conditions for the validity of the IV.

Demystify and Address Endogeneity Concerns: SCM Shifts the Focus from What We Cannot Do (“Threats to Identification”) to What We Can Do (“Licensing Assumptions”)

In PO and mainstream econometrics approaches, causal inference often begins with a set of assumptions given in the form of conditional independence statements. These are sometimes also called exclusion restrictions. The SCM approach also uses conditional independence assumptions for some tasks, but those can be derived from a structural model represented by a causal diagram. In that sense, the causal structure logically precedes the conditional independence statements. Unlike in the PO framework, exclusion restrictions in the SCM framework are understood to be the set of arrows that are missing or excluded between certain pairs of variables. That set of missing arrows forms a system of background assumptions (i.e. licensing assumptions), that might be justified in part by prior literature, common knowledge, experiential knowledge, or a basic set of facts that we take for granted.

While the causal diagram clarifies and helps isolate the threats to identification, it also provides a set of licensing assumptions that enable causal identification. To illustrate how causal diagramming can be beneficial in demystifying the problem of endogeneity, consider the following example, adapted from Hayashi (2000). This example invokes rational expectations econometrics to demonstrate the distinction between predetermined regressors and strictly exogenous regressors, a distinction that Ph.D. students often struggle with. Suppose we would like to model the annual pricing of information technology capital (ITC) as an autoregressive process at any given year t:

$$
I T C (t) = \alpha + \beta \times I T C (t - 1) + e (t),
$$

where ??(??) is independent white noise. Under large sample theory, ?? can be estimated because ITC(t-1) is predetermined in this model, that is, uncorrelated with the error term ??(??). However, ?? cannot be estimated under finite sample theory because ITC(t-1) is not strictly exogenous.

What does it mean for the price of the prior year’s IT capital to be predetermined yet not strictly exogenous? Like with all traditional econometrics texts, Hayashi’s (2000) exposition leans squarely on the language of correlations and conditional independencies. Hayashi (2000) provides a proof with several clever algebraic manipulations to show that even though ??(??) is a white noise process, it is correlated with the IT capital of subsequent years, which is hence not strictly exogenous. Even when students can follow the proofs, the distinction between predetermined and strictly exogenous comes across as nuanced and unintuitive. But if we start with a set of simple causal processes giving rise to the same equation, the concept becomes transparent. As Figure 5 shows, because there is no directed path from the current year’s error ??(??) to the prior year’s IT capital ITC(t-1), it follows that these two variables are statistically independent, which is equivalent in econometric terms to saying that “IT capital is predetermined in the autoregressive model,” And because there exists a directed path from ??(??) to any subsequent year’s IT capital (ITC(t), ITC(t+1), etc.) it follows that those variables are likely correlated with each other; hence, in econometric terms, “IT capital is not strictly exogenous in the autoregressive model.”

As this example illustrates, from a pedagogical point of view, it is much easier to begin the exposition with a causal diagram and make statements about correlations or statistical independencies based on it. Drawing the causal role of error terms allows for a clearer interpretation and intuition for some basic concepts in econometrics in addition to understanding the hypothesized effects. A precise specification of which variables are influenced by the error term makes a significant difference in whether and how the hypothesized effects can be estimated.<sup>10</sup> By the same token, when reviewers confront authors with endogeneity critiques in a form that is nebulous and difficult to address, authors can use the SCM approach as a more tangible pathway to address endogeneity concerns.

## Look at the Bigger Picture: SCM Helps Explore Alternative Causal Scenarios

Sometimes multiple alternative sets of background assumptions or causal scenarios may be valid. It may not always be possible to rule out competing scenarios, and rather than commit to one scenario, the researcher can explore the implications of multiple scenarios. As Pearl (2014, p. 473) advised: “anchoring one’s analysis in specific causal scenarios does not imply commitment to validity of those scenarios. It implies willingness to explore their ramifications, evoke critique of one’s assumptions, and to understand which variants of each scenario are critical for identification and for choosing the correct estimator.” That is, whenever there are some competing background stories, one can endeavor to explore the causal effect of interest in each of them and, hence, to compare how the effect of interest varies under each scenario.

Park et al. (2024) illustrated how this can be done: The researcher can focus upon (and justify) one main model that best fulfills the dual need for parsimony and realism—with just enough complexity to be realistic and viable but not more complex than that. The researcher can then consider a set of alternative causal scenarios (see Figure 4 in Park et al., 2024) and then present empirical results based on each alternative diagram, allowing scholars to explore the resulting range of estimates and consider the theoretical trade-offs involved with each alternative scenario’s assumption set. This is not altogether different from the spirit in which we see tables of alternative regression model specifications presented in econometric studies, except that causal diagrams can help avoid the “kitchen sink” approach to displaying many regression specifications and provide guidance on which control variables should be included in a model (e.g., see Cinelli et al., 2022). Causal diagramming provides authors with a way to specify a set of causal background assumptions explicitly—helping to clarify the research question and the theoretical mechanisms of interest, as well as the units of analysis, measurement, and data collection process.

## Maintain Transparency and Rigor in Causal Identification

An informed use of SCM can be empowering to authors who struggle to justify their causal inferences. Reviewers often challenge such inferences with statements such as: “The independent variable is endogenous.” This statement is nebulous because it does not explain the causal sources of endogeneity. Moreover, in many causal scenarios, there are independent variables that are endogenous yet the system admits causal identification (see the “More Easily Detect and Estimate Identifiable Effects” section above). Instead, scholars can help clarify the issue by explaining which variable(s) might jointly influence the treatment and outcome and, specifically, why that is a problem. By making assumptions more explicit through the diagram, it becomes easier to understand those assumptions and to detect whether they contain a serious conceptual flaw. If there are any flaws in the identification strategy, the causal diagram allows scholars to find and communicate them more easily. By the same token, reviewers might also find that explicitly drawing out the causal mechanisms through which endogeneity can arise is a more constructive way of guiding authors. Authors can use the process to clarify the limitations of their study, and reviewers can offer specific and constructive points of feedback. This process can help facilitate constructive engagement between authors and reviewers.

Causal diagramming is a useful conceptual tool that can help researchers find and articulate the possible sources of endogeneity in a study. The practice may sometimes feel burdensome, especially if a study’s identification strategy is not well conceived in the first place, but it can often contribute to rigor while maintaining clarity of the study’s causal identification strategy.<sup>11</sup> If the process of drawing a causal diagram leads to the finding that more robustness checks are needed, we think it is probably because some implicit assumptions of the model have come to light and not because additional new assumptions are being imposed.<sup>12</sup>

## Limitations, Challenges, and Opportunities

Although SCM offers numerous advantages in clarifying and devising a causal identification strategy, there are also several limitations, challenges, and opportunities for future research to make SCM a more useful tool for applied empiricists. Here, we provide a summary of three broad categories of SCM limitations, many of which are discussed in Imbens (2020). Rather than disagreeing with the points made by Imbens (2020), we will discuss some mitigating considerations that still make causal diagrams useful, despite those limitations; further, we will discuss how those limitations represent opportunities for future research and development.

## Missing Statistical Machinery

The first broad category of limitations, and perhaps the one that presents the most important and challenging research opportunities, is the need for methods of statistical inference and validation for many applications of SCM. It can be challenging to decide which among multiple causal diagram possibilities most accurately and parsimoniously represents a particular real-world empirical setting that is being studied. Although there are some basic validation methods that involve statistical tests of conditional independence, research on validation methods for causal diagrams remains at a relatively nascent stage of development, and sophisticated and robust methods are not yet in wide use.<sup>13</sup> One possible mitigating practice is to produce estimates for multiple plausible causal diagram scenarios (as mentioned above in the “Look at the Bigger Picture” section, via the Park et al., 2024, example).

Park et al. (2024) provide a discussion of further opportunities for research on statistical inference and validation, and in particular causal discovery—the integration of data-centric and theory-based methods to discover the causal structure representing the data-generating process of real-world data sets. In comparison to applied econometrics practices guided by the potential outcomes framework, the SCM literature does not offer much guidance for statistical estimation and inference; thus, while an SCM solution can deliver direct quantitative estimates of the magnitude of causal effects, what is needed are some robust methods that produce reliable standard errors and statistical models for estimation and hypothesis testing. In many cases, a causal diagram’s identification strategy converts easily to a linear regression solution,<sup>14</sup> but in some cases, such as the transportability solutions presented by Park et al. (2024), they do not. Hence, it would be of great benefit for the applicability of SCM to empirical hypothesis testing if Pearl’s do-calculus can be extended to derive standard errors; to the best of our knowledge, this remains an open research problem for which a solution would be tangibly impactful and useful.

## Lack of Functional Form Notation

The second broad category of limitations has to do with the difficulty of integrating functional forms into causal diagrams. Causal diagrams are fundamentally nonparametric constructs—i.e., since causal arrows do not specify parametrized functions, SCM solutions are typically nonparametric. Although functional form constraints could, in theory, be added to causal diagrams, this is often not done. The nonparametric form has both benefits and drawbacks. Avoiding functional form constraints has benefits in terms of flexibility, especially because functional form assumptions are not needed in many SCM solutions (in particular, models that are solved by Pearl’s do-calculus). But Imbens (2020) provides an extensive discussion on the drawbacks of this feature, making the point that many methods— in particular, instrumental variables models, regression discontinuity designs, and models requiring monotonicity—require the integration of functional forms for estimation. We also point out that SCM has a sparsity of notation for integrating functional forms into causal diagrams in general, and this extends to the use of interaction (moderating) hypotheses or multi-item constructs, both of which are afforded special notation in covariance-based and partial least squares (PLS)-type path modeling (Tafti & Shmueli, 2022). On the other hand, scholars using causal diagrams can always invent new notational conventions as the need arises, as has been done with special symbols for selection bias and measurement errors in the work of Bareinboim and Pearl (2016). Moreover, researchers have demonstrated the versatility of causal diagrams, even with minimal notational innovations, to represent concepts of quasi-experimentation such as regression discontinuities and matching or propensity score designs (Steiner et al., 2017; Mithas et al., 2022a), fixed-effects and random-effects panel models (Kim & Steiner, 2021), or in modeling different kinds of interaction effects (VanderWeele & Robins, 2007; VanderWeele, 2009). The SCM literature readily incorporates the counterfactual notation in potential outcomes, which can be seen in SCM papers on causal mediation (e.g. Pearl, 2014), causal attribution (Pearl, 2009; Pearl et al., 2016),<sup>15</sup> the development of generalized instrumental variables (Brito & Pearl, 2002), and partial identification models (Balke & Pearl, 1997).

Another related limitation of causal diagrams is the difficulty of expressing simultaneous equations with such diagrams, due to the lack of functional form notation. Nevertheless, Imbens (2020) demonstrates how the process of attempting to create a causal diagram to represent the simultaneity of general supply and demand systems revealed an insight that functions serve as the primitive nodes in such systems. But because the nodes in common causal diagrams are typically variables and not functions, Imbens (2020) expresses the opinion that causal diagram structures do not provide much insight for such simultaneous equation systems in general. On the other hand, there are certain settings in which causal diagrams can be useful for modeling supply and demand when there are interactions among multiple products—in which variables can still serve as primitive nodes—as demonstrated by Athey et al. (2019), who use causal diagrams to illustrate an identification strategy developed by Wang and Blei (2019) that leverages multiple causes. If future research can improve our understanding of the properties of causal diagrams in which primitive nodes are functions rather than variables, this could plausibly lead to more useful applications of causal diagrams in systems of simultaneous equations.

Another limitation related to the lack of functional form notation is that of distinguishing between constructs and measures and depicting multi-measurement (multi-item) constructs. In many IS studies, especially those involving surveys, the distinction between construct and measure has a theoretical significance. We would want the causal diagram to depict that separation faithfully, similar to diagrams used in path modeling. This relationship between constructs and measures is an example of where SCM departs from path modeling, as used by researchers employing PLS-PM and covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) approaches, and one that calls for further research.<sup>16</sup>

## Scarcity of Applications

The third broad category of limitations has to do with the current scarcity of useful applications, especially for SCM solutions that provide new causal identification strategies in settings where mainstream econometrics and potential outcomes frameworks do not. Imbens’s (2020) commentary, highlighting this point, casts skepticism on the value of several causal scenarios in which SCM has produced innovative causal identification solutions—the front-door path (FDP) model, the “napkin problem,” and the M-form model.<sup>17</sup> SCM leverages the greater precision in assumption structure afforded by specifying directed causal relations between pairs of nodes, rather than starting with conditional independence statements. In these particular causal scenarios, among others, SCM is able to provide solutions for causal identification that the PO-based framework has not. The point Imbens (2020) makes is that realistic economic scenarios represented by such causal diagrams have not yet come to light. As Imbens (2020) explains, Pearl’s texts have made only passing and unconvincing attempts to justify their validity for the settings in which they were being applied, and most of his examples are presented as “toy models” rather than realistic applications. Although many more realistic applications could be discovered in the future, Imbens (2020) argues that such a development would be analogous to a “hammer looking for a nail.” By contrast, other methodological innovations from the field of psychology or aligned fields have transferred readily and been adopted much more quickly and broadly in economics; those methods include regression discontinuity design (RDD) and synthetic control models.

There is no doubt that more research is needed to explore useful applications of SCM, particularly regarding its innovative identification methods. More work that bridges SCM theory and practice needs to be done to explore such applications. That gap between theory and practice is more pronounced in SCM compared to the prevailing econometrics and potential outcomes frameworks, the latter of which have paid greater attention to empirical estimation solutions and their statistical challenges. Consider how much intellectual energy has gone into finding credible instrumental variables in applied economic settings. As any applied empiricist would attest, it is quite difficult to find and justify valid instrumental variables in many real-world settings. But the field of economics has devoted a great deal of training to the task, and applied empiricists have invested a great deal of time to precisely such a task. As a result, applied economics and aligned fields of scholarship have developed a whole subindustry devoted to finding instrumental variables in clever places. It is plausible that not many economic applications of SCM’s innovative models have been found— only because not many applied empiricists have been looking for them. This is a chickenand-egg problem, so to speak, and perhaps this will change as awareness and attention spread.

Nevertheless, despite some debate on the usefulness of SCM’s innovations in causal identification, there is general agreement about the expositional benefits of using causal diagrams alongside traditional methods. SCM methods will not replace applications in which econometrics and potential outcomes have proven useful, but they can serve as a useful layer of identification and clarity that complements existing methods. Although, broadly, there is agreement on this point, Imbens (2020) argues that the expositional value of causal diagrams is primarily to visualize and manage complexity, especially when we are dealing with complex systems of variables.<sup>18</sup> A recent example is Liu et al. (2022), who use causal diagrams to illustrate counterfactual estimators for causal inference with time-series crosssectional data.

Imbens (2020) argues that such complex systems have lost importance among applied empiricists, who have turned their attention to experiments. If one is only interested in the overall treatment effect (the average treatment effect, ATE on predetermined variables), the only assumption required to produce consistent estimates is that the treatment is randomized and any complications such as noncompliance are accounted for. But, as mentioned earlier, causal diagrams can be useful for generating insights beyond the overall treatment effects of a randomized experiment or for insights into complications that arise in its implementation (Tafti & Shmueli, 2020)—or for demonstrating how covariates in a randomized experiment can be instrumental for generalizing or transporting the causal inferences to other settings where only observational data is available (Park et al., 2024).

We believe that there are many opportunities for IS scholars to further explore examples in which SCM can be useful for applied settings, including experiments. For example, digital platforms pose many possibilities for complex models and experiments in which a mediating variable is shielded from confounding effects or influenced by certain errors or stochastic manipulations in a system that can be mapped, as occurred in the causal diagram depicting the experimental context in Yang et al. (2024). Such a scenario would map quite naturally to the front-door-path (FDP) model, which is an innovation brought to us by the SCM framework.<sup>19</sup> Exploiting this phenomenon may be easier than trying to find a fully exogenous instrumental variable in such settings. Hence, contrary to suggestions that FDP models are unrealistic in real-world settings, digital platforms may offer abundant opportunities to explore the usefulness of such models.

As Imbens (2020) acknowledges, many of the limitations could be due to the nascent stage of development of SCM’s applications. After the publication of Imbens (2020), more recent studies have emerged exploring useful applications of SCM in IS or related fields, as shown in Table 1.

More efforts along those lines could help to bridge the gap between the theoretical foundations of SCM and practical applications in IS and the related fields of management, social sciences, and economics. The primary benefit of SCM, which is generally supported by Imbens (2020)—with a few qualifications—is its expositional benefits; that is, in many cases, causal diagrams can help to elucidate the assumptions behind a causal identification strategy. Our commentary above emphasizes this aspect, which is largely uncontested by Imbens (2020).

## Conclusion

SCM provides a new way of thinking about causality that is complementary to traditional econometric methods. Table A2 in the Appendix provides a list of key points and supporting examples that we leveraged in this commentary. Despite limitations, SCM can be used alongside traditional methods as a complement to enhance the rigor and transparency of causal identification methods. SCM is especially useful in what Mithas et al. (2022b) describe as the diversity in the landscape of causality relevant for IS research, which includes subcommunities using various approaches for conducting quantitative causal research (econometrics, path modeling, statistics, machine learning, etc.). The simplicity and transparency of causal diagrams can improve communication and understanding across researchers using different causal methodologies. In this sense, a picture is worth “a thousand words,” and perhaps also worth many equations. SCM does not replace other methods, but it provides a beneficial conceptual framework and aids communication between authors and reviewers. It can improve research practices, prevent common errors, help to focus on relevant concerns in the review process, and open new research opportunities central to the IS field.

A relatively small investment of time to learn about the basics of causal diagramming and their interpretation will yield large benefits to authors and reviewers of causal empirical IS research. Using SCM enables greater clarity in the design, implementation, and evaluation of empirical research. SCM can help researchers improve their causal storytelling and understanding of the literature by highlighting the theoretical components in a visual fashion. It clarifies the researcher’s causal identification strategy by providing an explicit representation of causal phenomena, assumptions, and hypotheses. Finally, SCM helps researchers engage with alternative causal scenarios, strengthening generalizability. Together, these benefits provide authors with more robust tools for developing, presenting, and arguing for their research design and implementation. Ideally, both authors and reviewers should have a basic understanding of the SCM approach, enabling a synergetic scenario such as that depicted in Figure 6.

Where can scholars start to learn about SCM? A set of basic principles for causal identification can be learned in a short time. The Book of Why by Pearl and Mackenzie (2018) is a good place to start; from there, one can learn a great deal from the text commonly referred to as the Primer, by Pearl et al. (2016). We outline a few of the basic features of causal diagramming here, but readers can find more background information in Tafti and Shmueli (2020).

Which IS research communities can benefit from incorporating SCM? We believe SCM can empower researchers who use survey data and observational data, researchers who conduct experiments, and, of course, researchers using mixed methods. The machine learning subcommunity can combine SCM’s identification backbone with methods such as causal forests (Wager & Athey, 2018; Imbens & Athey, 2021), thereby expanding the causal empirical toolkit beyond standard econometric methods. A wider embrace of the SCM approach by our field can help to reorient our research towards the questions that we want to ask, especially those at the core of the IS discipline and its development of theory, where it is difficult to avoid some degree of theoretical complexity.

Gatekeepers of the field of economics and some other standard bearers for causal inference have been reluctant to embrace the SCM framework and its body of related work. However, at least one new econometrics textbook (Cunningham, 2021) has embraced this approach, and leading economists such as Imbens (e.g., Imbens, 2020), Heckman (e.g., Heckman & Pinto, 2015), and the late Halbert White (e.g., White & Lu, 2011; White et al., 2011) have recognized and begun to explore its importance. The fields of epidemiology and biostatistics, in part due to the influence of renowned biostatistician James Robins, have already embraced the approach more fully and with productive results. Insofar as the IS discipline positions itself as a cutting-edge and fast-moving field—closer to the field of computer science, and less burdened by certain dogmas that may prevail upon other social science fields—the IS community is wellpositioned to build on the opportunities afforded by this way of thinking about causality in various ways that will advance the central themes of IS. The community of IS scholars should not wait for a new generation of econometrics textbooks to lead the way in this new kind of thinking.
