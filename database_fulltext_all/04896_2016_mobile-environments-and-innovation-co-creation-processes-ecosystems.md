# Mobile environments and innovation co-creation processes & ecosystems

Tracey Y.T. Wong <sup>a</sup>, Gabrielle Peko <sup>a</sup>, David Sundaram <sup>a</sup>, Selwyn Piramuthu <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Information Systems, University of Auckland, Owen G. Glenn Building, 12 Grafton Road, Auckland, New Zealand <sup>b</sup> ISOM Department University of Florida, 351 Stuzin Hall, Gainesville, FL 32611-7169, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 17 September 2014 Received in revised form 19 May 2015 Accepted 17 September 2015 Available online 28 September 2015

Keywords: Innovation co-creation Mobile devices

## A B S T R A C T

The process of value creation was traditionally driven almost exclusively within the firm. Product design and production were performed with minimal input from consumers, and the role of the consumer was witnessed only at the end of the product development process. Co-creation is generally considered by innovative firms that seek to achieve or maintain strategic competitiveness in the marketplace as a highly valuable progression in open innovation. Although extensive research results exist on innovation cocreation between firms and consumers, a coherent understanding of the application of such co-creation in mobile environments is missing in the literature. We consider opportunities that mobile technologies introduce to further develop co-creation. Based on an extensive literature review of innovation cocreation processes and ecosystems, we identify the key problems, issues, and requirements. We then develop an innovation co-creation framework with two levels of abstraction to organize the enablers, capabilities, and characteristics. We then suggest a roadmap with four stages for realizing a mobile innovation co-creation ecosystem.

## 1. Introduction

The success of an enterprise lies in the development of new products to meet consumer demand and to deliver them profitably in the marketplace [43]. Innovation is one of the primary ways firms can differentiate their products and services from the competition and thus retain sustainable growth. In the past, it was possible for organizations to survive with very limited amount of innovation. They focused on simply updating products to a minimum level that maintained their competitiveness in the market [59,101]. However, due to factors such as changes in consumer tastes, ever-shortening product lifecycles, competitive movements, technological advances, and globalization, there is an increased need for firms to improve their response to changing markets [9,16,20,37].

According to a survey conducted by Accenture, two-thirds of executives identified innovation as one of the five most critical factors required for companies to succeed and sustain a competitive advantage. However, the same survey found that only one in eight executives feels strongly that their companies excel at achieving innovation [44]. Benkler [7] argues that the best innovative solutions lie not within firms but with consumers. A recent survey conducted by the Economist Intelligence Unit [23] suggests that consumers are becoming increasingly important sources of innovation across all regions, industries, and company sizes. Skills and insights from the ultimate users of output help firms develop relevant products that well suit consumer needs [40]. By taking full advantage of the collective creative power in both current and potential consumers, firms have the potential to lead existing markets and create new ones within a relatively short period [102].

Innovation co-creation has recently gained popularity among businesses as a fundamental source of competitive advantage. Although the idea of creating two-way dialogues with key stakeholders is not new, the availability of powerful Web-based tools make it relatively easier to start conversations and gather input rapidly from a large number of participants [79]. Many of the world’s leading companies, such as LEGO [64], Starbucks [77] and Nike [108], actively use innovation co-creation platforms and communities to engage directly with consumers to generate new ideas [78]. However, these enabling tools have predominantly been isolated to the traditional desktop environment [99]. Facilitated by advances in communication technology as well as the general availability and increased openness to two-way access between firms and consumers, there is a clear transformation of consumers from passive observers to active participants. While companies have successfully used brands and social media to target consumers, consumers have evolved to use social media to target companies and their brands in constructive ways that lead to positive product development contributions.

The emergence of mobile technologies presents great challenges and opportunities for the creation of business value [19]. Mobile technologies have provided users with the ability to communicate with other parties and access information independent of location and time constraints [52]. One significant outcome of this increased empowerment is that consumers now desire to play a greater role in their interactions with companies [40]. As a result, there arises the question of how the development of mobile technologies will transform the enterprise and its consumer-centric processes, such as innovation co-creation [6].

In most cases, innovation as well as the generation of ideas is known to be location-independent, where the innovators or idea generators do not necessarily delve into their creative or innovative side in a laboratory or company setting. It is difficult to time innovations and ideas, which have a rich, long history of occurrence when and where they are least expected, and especially in locations that are outside of one’s traditional ‘‘work’ environment. Given this innovation dynamic, it is time-critical for such ideas to be heard and shared with relevant people as and when they occur. The traditional work environment, although not necessarily known to provide the most conducive atmosphere for innovations and ideas to be generated, fails to support innovation instances that occur outside of the work environment to the fullest possible extent. With the extensive availability of mobile technologies, it is only natural to make use of these technologies to facilitate idea generation and innovation whenever and wherever they occur, with the removal of location-based constraints. When consumers become joint innovators as well as idea generators for companies, there is a further need to incorporate these consumers and their ideas into the innovation co-creation process. The innovation process does not place any location-based restrictions, and neither should the technology that is used to facilitate this process. We therefore argue that mobile technologies are an essential part of innovation cocreation.

Despite the large volume of published research that considers innovation co-creation, discussion and studies on the opportunities that arise with the introduction of mobile technology are absent in the literature. We attempt to address this gap in the existing literature. Based on an extensive literature review of innovation co-creation processes and related ecosystems, we identify the key problems, issues, and requirements. We then develop an innovation co-creation framework with two levels of abstraction to organize the enablers, capabilities, and characteristics. We then suggest a roadmap with four stages for realizing a mobile innovation co-creation ecosystem.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: We provide an extensive review of innovation co-creation in Section 2. We then discuss the effect of mobile technologies on the innovation co-creation process based on our synthesis of existing literature in Section 3. We also develop a framework and an associated roadmap with the incorporation of mobile technologies in Section 3. We conclude the paper with a brief discussion on innovation cocreation as it relates to mobile organization-customer interactions in Section 4.

## 2. Related literature on innovation co-creation

The traditional innovation process was linear and sequential, in which firms internally developed new ideas and tested them in the market [58]. This structure gave firms the ability to undertake activities in an orderly manner, thereby minimizing uncertainties related to each and enhancing total task effectiveness [104]. Consumers were generally passive participants in the innovation process, acting only as validators at the end stages of the product development lifecycle [58].

However, the process of innovation has changed. The concept of co-creation has transformed the traditional closed innovation process into a parallel and open one [35]. Today’s new products must satisfy multiple consumer needs in terms of quality, function and price to be successful. To this end, innovation activities must take place simultaneously, with information being processed from various sources as products are conceived and designed. Linear innovation models that undertake tasks in a sequential manner are therefore inappropriate for this purpose [66].

Prahalad and Ramaswamy [74] define co-creation as ‘‘an active, creative and social process based on collaboration between producers and users, initiated by the firm to generate value for [consumers].’’ Successful new product development requires two types of information: (1) information about consumer needs and (2) information about how to best address these needs. Typically, consumers are the most knowledgeable about the first type of information, whereas providers are the best source for the second type [63].

We consider literature in this area and the key concepts discussed in this literature in terms of innovation co-creation. Specifically, we consider literature that covers the general theme of consumer and the co-creation process as well as the co-creation ecosystem. With respect to the former, we consider the role of consumers in the innovation process, their characteristics, as well as the benefits and risks of co-creation.

## 2.1. Co-creation and the consumer

## 2.1.1. Consumer roles in innovation processes

Co-creation is found in all stages of the innovation process, including idea generation, design, engineering, and test and launch. The level of involvement of the firm and consumer varies across different innovation processes and depends on the context and degree of task complexity. As a result, consumers take on various roles based on the purpose of the collaboration. In practice, the term ‘‘co-creation’’ is often generalized and refers to instances of consumer input that do not necessarily involve the joint creation of value [96]. The different roles that consumers play in the development of new products, based on their level of influence and autonomy in the innovation process, include (in increasing order of consumer involvement) insight provider (e.g., [96]), customizer (e.g., [71]), ideator (e.g., [11,39,46,92,93]), co-creator (e.g., [74]), and creator (e.g., [32]). Because these are only roles that are played by consumers, these roles can be switched as often as necessary as a given situation dictates.

Consumers who are insight providers generally have a low level of influence and are sourced by companies to help them find solutions, provide insights, identify needs, and evaluate ideas. Unlike insight providers, customizers have the direct influence to adapt and select product features during the final stages of product development, though only to the extent that is allowed by the companies of interest. As a group, ideators provide creative input to firms, with the resulting intellectual property being owned and used by the company for further inspiration and innovation. Cocreators work with companies to create new products, with their involvement spanning all phases of the innovation process. Online communities are perfect for this role because frequent real-time two-way communication is necessary in this context. Creators initiate and drive the innovation process as independent value creators.

## 2.1.2. Exploring the co-creative consumer

In innovation co-creation, consumers actively engage with the firm at all stages of the product development lifecycle [63]. They are able and willing to provide ideas for new goods and services that fulfil needs not yet met by the market or are better than what is currently being offered. In addition, they are now able to easily communicate these ideas to the firm through the Internet [40]. As important influencers as well as a source of innovative ideas, consumers are now considered a primary source of value creation and competitive advantage [97].

The literature identifies several characteristics that differentiate the co-creative consumer from the passive consumer. These characteristics include those that determine who are lead users (e.g., [94]) and those with necessary technological skills (e.g., [24,74]), analogous market knowledge (e.g., [38]), cooperative skills (e.g., [18]), and pro-active attitudes (e.g., [75]).

A growing number of consumers are seeking a more active role in the creation of products they use [36,81]. Forrester Research [27] found that 61% of all U.S. online adults are open to co-creating across a wide spectrum of applications and industries. With such wide-ranging interest in participation, firms are keen to actively involve consumers in the process of improving product offerings [80]. Detailed information on what motivates co-creative consumers to participate helps firms determine how to best attract consumers to co-creation processes. The literature identifies several consumer motives for co-creating, which include enjoyment (e.g., [29,31,71), empowerment (e.g., [95]), interaction (e.g., [24]), self-expression (e.g., [1]), peer recognition (e.g., [24,29]), altruism and moral responsibility (e.g., [72,87,90,98]), information-seeking (e.g., [96]), and control (e.g., [24]).

## 2.1.3. Benefits of innovation co-creation

By successfully creating and maintaining an active dialogue with consumers, firms enjoy various improvements in business processes and performance. eYeka [25] suggests three main benefits of innovation co-creation for firms:

2.1.3.1. Acceleration of innovation cycles. Co-creation has the potential to increase the effectiveness and speed of new product development. According to a survey conducted by the Boston Consulting Group [10], even though a majority of senior executives listed innovation as one of their top three priorities, a significant percentage felt that lengthy development times was one of the biggest challenges to achieving innovation. By facilitating knowledge sharing and experiences, the number of available sources for new ideas is considerably enhanced [82]. Co-creation can also lower the cost of product testing. Rather than wait for physical prototypes to be created, firms can test virtual prototypes with consumers using virtual worlds that provide a media-rich environment [47,61].

2.1.3.2. Reduced risks of market failure through closer consumer engagement. Co-creation largely concerns communication with consumers to learn and understand their future needs. This continual generation of insight increases product relevance for consumers [30]. New goods and services developed through innovation co-creation are more profitable than those developed with traditional market research techniques [107]. Franke and Piller [28] found that the willingness of consumers to pay for a selfdesigned product was twice that of the willingness to pay for the best-selling product of the same quality. Co-creation by consumers results not only in the creation of a product but also in an experience that facilitates the formation of an emotional connection to the product. Therefore, co-creation as a compelling exercise can improve the trust and retention of consumers with the firm [3,60,73,105].

2.1.3.3. Build-up of an innovative organizational culture. By removing the organizational boundaries that prevent the firm from being connected to its environment, co-creation can help generate a better innovation culture [8]. The acceleration of innovation cycles reduces research and development costs and provides quick and visible successes. In turn, employees are encouraged to collaborate with consumers to gain different perspectives and seek a higher level of innovation in their work [17,25].

## 2.1.4. Risks of innovation co-creation

Although co-creation is highly valued for its numerous benefits, the literature also mentions several risks and disadvantages that must be kept in mind when implementing innovation co-creation processes.

2.1.4.1. Product success is not guaranteed. There have been instances in the past in which unsuccessful products were the result of co-creation [12,33,100]. Bughin et al. [13] note that it is unknown whether the idea that groups can develop better products than individuals applies to all types of products. Cocreative consumers can also diminish value and damage a firm’s reputation. For example, Amazon.com’s attempt to change its business model offended many of their co-creative consumers, who then mobilized to protest, putting the firm’s reputation temporarily at risk [45].

2.1.4.2. Increased costs of coordination. Co-creation can actually delay new product development when additional process uncertainties resulting from co-creation are not properly managed [61]. Furthermore, additional effort and resources are needed to actively include consumers in the new product development process [49], which is especially true for virtual communities where appropriate online toolkits must be provided by the firm [28]. The increased interaction with consumers might also interrupt internal new product development processes [31].

2.1.4.3. Inappropriate use of information. Information gathered from virtual consumer environments might not be appropriate for co-creation. For example, the development of a new product could be driven by the needs of an extremely visible and vocal group of consumers who do not necessarily represent the majority of consumers [31,61]. Kohler et al. [47] suggest that more traditional research techniques should be applied in this context to ensure a representative sample and validate the results of co-creation. In addition, consumers may still have difficulties articulating and communicating their needs for future products [47]. They often align themselves to existing product solutions and as a result may only provide ideas for incremental innovations [31]. There is also the possibility that consumers in virtual environments pretend to be someone else or even intentionally harm co-creation tasks [47].

2.1.4.4. Legal and privacy issues. One common issue associated with co-creation concerns copyright and intellectual property rights for the development of consumer ideas [31]. This issue can lead to potential legal claims, such as in software or information companies, where consumers are heavily relied upon for content [45]. Consumers are also increasingly concerned about privacy issues when sharing their personal information with firms, particularly through the Internet [45,73]. Finally, increased transparency of business processes as a result of co-creation might decrease the firm’s competitiveness. Competitors may be informed on or gain access to confidential information regarding new product development [31,61].

## 2.2. Innovation co-creation ecosystem

Prahalad and Ramaswamy [74] argue that a space for cultivating potential co-creation experiences in which individuals jointly create value at multiple points of interaction must be established as the foundation of co-creation. The success of cocreation is founded upon the quality of consumer-firm interactions. Participants must initiate rich dialogues among themselves to exchange and generate knowledge to realize shared objectives. Therefore, it is essential to offer an open and transparent environment where participants feel comfortable and possess the necessary capabilities to provide input [67]. In co-creation, the appropriate experience environment in which participants participate in an active dialogue to achieve results must be created. Successful co-creation initiatives tend to share several characteristics in common that facilitate this process:

## 2.2.1. Inspiring participation

Most people want to contribute to the products and brands that surround them, but there must be a trigger for them to actually participate in co-creation tasks [50]. To this end, the contributors must first be inspired through an understanding of the firm, why their help is needed, and what will be done with the results. Next, access must be given to anyone with the potential to be beneficial to the process [67]. Moreover, all participants should be willing to share their skills and resources to achieve a common goal [74].

## 2.2.2. Selecting the best

For co-creation to be successful, the best possible ideas and set of people to maximize the value contributed should be selected [70]. For any co-creation project, one of the biggest challenges is to filter and find ideas that bring new value to the initiative, especially when a large number of potential ideas have been generated [34]. All participants play a crucial role in finding ideas through rating and commenting mechanisms [48]. When determining the most influential people in the co-creation process, characteristics such as leadership, proven expertise, creative thinking, peer recognition, and communication abilities must be considered [67]. The set of characteristics considered is the same regardless of the (mobile or non-mobile) environment because ultimately what matters is not the location from which ideas are shared but rather the sharing of the ideas themselves.

## 2.2.3. Connecting participants

There must be a structured way to enable participants to work together and generate constructive dialogue [67]. The initiator of the co-creation process should actively engage, listen, and be encouraging to unlock the potential of a group of contributors [68]. Furthermore, when participants are physically separated and thus cannot meet face-to-face, connection through technologyenabled networks must be allowed to facilitate productive interactions [35].

## 2.2.4. Mutual benefits

Fair value must be provided to both the initiator and contributor within co-creation processes. This compensation may be extrinsic (e.g., monetary value) or intrinsic (e.g., recognition in the community as a key contributor or an invitation to join more initiatives) [13]. Most importantly, all participants must be kept informed of the latest developments and progress [67].

## 2.2.5. Continuing development

As previously discussed, collaboration can only be classified as co-creation when it involves long-term engagement between parties. Constant feedback and learning must be facilitated through open communication and frequent status updates [67]. As a result, participants can continue the conversation, build on ideas, and initiate new developments [78].

Based on our review of published literature, as discussed above, we consolidate and classify (Table 1) the various traits of participants in the process into enablers, capabilities, characteristics, and goals of innovation co-creation. The enablers form the foundation of the necessary ecosystem that allows innovation cocreation processes to take place. Capabilities refer to the operational mode of the interactions between all participants in the innovation co-creation process. Characteristics refer to the low-level features that together define the various capabilities. Goals elucidate the processes that lead to the achievement of the end result, which is an end product of concerted effort realized through an ensemble of several characteristics, capabilities, and enablers.

## 3. Mobile technologies and innovation co-creation

Due to today’s fast-paced world and the ability of the Internet to instantaneously connect people worldwide, participants of innovation co-creation tend to be distributed and rarely situated in fixed locations [14]. There is a need for a way to allow these members to collaborate with one another even when they are physically on the move. Mobile technologies have the potential to enable firms and consumers with the ability to not only stay in the loop on what has been done but also to input ideas and provide feedback anytime, anywhere using only their mobile devices.

Following the definition advanced by Campbell et al. [14], mobile technologies are devices that include mobile phones, laptop computers, or PDAs and have (1) wireless connectivity and (2) some collaboration-enabling software application. By extending activities into the wireless medium, mobile technologies allow users to have continual access to information, which in turn provides greater flexibility in communication, information sharing, and collaboration [86]. Compared to traditional forms of collaboration, mobile technologies offer several unique features, including portability (e.g., [56]), reachability [55], localization (e.g., [51]), identification (e.g., [51]), and accessibility (e.g., [14]).

We now consider mobile technologies and related characteristics from the perspective of innovation co-creation, followed by a discussion of our proposed innovation co-creation framework and then the associated roadmap.

## 3.1. Mobile technologies

## 3.1.1. Impacts on firms and consumers

Over the past decade, the continual spread of mobile technology adoption has had a significant effect on the way firms and consumers do business with each other in terms of people, process, and technology. Mobile technologies have played an important part in recent attempts to re-engineer business processes to increase focus on core activities rather than on individual members. Enterprise mobility can solve unique business problems by taking the business process to the consumers, who add rich content to business information [85], thereby affecting the strategic management and competitive advantage of firms [4,57].

3.1.1.1. Impact on people. Employees are already extensively using mobile technologies for personal purposes outside of the organization. The use of mobile applications for business purposes is thus a logical progression of this trend. Employees can access the same resources online as they could in a traditional office. Therefore, they can have more flexible schedules and perform work as needed. As a result, management’s focus is shifted from monitoring attendance to evaluating results and achieving productivity [2].

Mobilization has also affected how people work with each other and how teams are organized. Virtual teams bring together geographically dispersed people to work towards a common goal, allowing members to gain greater status awareness and real-time collaboration with mobile technologies [15,26,56].

From the consumer’s perspective, mobilization signifies higher service quality and thus increased consumer satisfaction. For example, mobile technologies allow for queries to be made from (almost) anywhere in the world. This flexibility provides consumers with real-time access to accurate inventory information independent of their physical location when such queries are submitted. With remote status monitoring and order modification, consumers can effectively manage their order placements at any location [2]. Mobile consumers can also benefit from improvements in technical support and customer care. Because employees can now directly access expert collaboration networks and diagnostic tools from any location, they can respond more quickly to inquiries from customers [88].

3.1.1.2. Impact on process. Mobilization has changed the way firms support internal operations and manage their production processes, primarily in terms of information flow. Because status information is dynamically captured and monitored, this change has enabled more precise asset management and real-time reporting [83]. Mobile technologies have also altered the business process at the task level. Work is now centralized and redistributed to available resources, introducing improvements to the way tasks are assigned and performed [22]. Real-time monitoring has reduced downtime, and mobilized workflows have decreased time to completion. With real-time access to information, managers can make better decisions faster [2].

Mobilization has also changed the way firms work with business partners, leading to the enhancement of adaptive business networks. The integration and interdependence of firms in an adaptive business network enable real-time information flows across organizational boundaries, which allows partners to collaborate effectively and achieve greater innovation [84]. With the synchronization of mobile workforces and central information systems, firms can provide higher value to global markets [42].

3.1.1.3. Impact on technology. With the emergence of mobile technologies, information systems have been enhanced to accommodate mobile business models [2]. The unified communications environment has provided users with a single access point for online services such as e-mail, instant messaging, and file storage. This system supports the capture and transmission of information throughout the business process [103]. Mobile technologies can also be used to highlight the location and availability of individual workers and resources, thus improving operational efficiency [14].

Mobilization has also changed the way in which employees interact with core business systems. Mobile applications enable the storage and integration of data across a variety of sources, which affects how business information is secured and used [62]. Authentication protocols must be set up to provide the appropriate level of security at the network, application, server, and device levels [2].

## 3.1.2. Mobile collaboration

The proliferation of mobile technologies has led to a profound change in the way people communicate, collaborate, and make decisions. With the speed and functionality of accessing the Internet using mobile technologies, remote access to business information can be achieved. Basic Web 2.0 technologies can be integrated into new communication channels that maximize the user’s responsiveness and agility to work with virtual communities and make informed decisions [2]. With such real-time and rich data sharing, consumer inputs can be incorporated into business processes in a timely manner [53].

Mobile technologies can enhance operational efficiency by distributing information to the remote workforce and providing new channels over which to interact with consumers. The success of customer relationship management (CRM) lies in the ability of the firm to communicate continuously with consumers on an individual level and provide differentiated value [65]. It is also important to provide CRM activities through channels that consumers are also interested in using to interact with the firm [89]. Mobile technologies can be used to manage the coordination of consumer interactions and relationships [21]. Wind et al. [106] suggest that digital channels can create unique and positive experiences for consumers. As a result, real-time mobile workforce collaboration can be achieved to enable effective business transformation.

## 3.2. Innovation co-creation framework

We propose a framework for innovation co-creation using mobile technologies with two levels of abstraction. An innovation co-creation ecosystem is based on three components: frequency, direction, and depth. Based on our literature review, increasing the extent to which all these aspects are achieved is crucial to improving innovation co-creation in virtual environments. Not only must there be a large number of conversations that take place over time, but they must also involve the exchange of rich and relevant information. Furthermore, firms must treat consumers as equal partners in the innovation co-creation process. As we increase the frequency and depth of interactions, a balance of exchanges in the form of two-way dialogues between involved parties must be achieved over time. This process can then be viewed on a more detailed level in terms of how each of the three components can be realized. Increasing the number of participants and the degree of sharing enhances the frequency of interactions, while working outside organizational boundaries and increasing the degree of complexity enhances the depth of interactions (Fig. 1). The greater the extent to which the four aspects are achieved and the more two-way dialogues that take place over time, the higher the level of continuous new product development becomes. In turn, the chances that innovation co-creation becomes successful are improved.

## 3.2.1. Number of participants

Number of participants concerns generating interest and increasing participation in innovation co-creation tasks. Consumers need to be motivated to connect with the co-creation platform adopted and share their ideas with the community.

Prahalad and Ramaswamy [76] suggest that the propensity to engage in co-creation will be higher in societies with higher levels of electronic-based communications and where there are fewer social or political barriers to the use of such technologies. Cocreative consumers tend to be located in Western countries where more discretionary time is available to engage in co-creation [24].

Current technologies for supporting innovation co-creation are largely desktop-based, and as a result, participants must be close to their desks to maintain awareness of each other’s status [55]. Because mobile technologies are ubiquitous and tend to have Internet connectivity, co-creation participants can increase their level of involvement over time and across sessions. Mobile technologies have the potential to allow users to contribute to discussions and make decisions when they have the time [98]. They also have the potential for users to record ideas at the moment of inspiration because they remain close and connected to their owners [41,55]. As a result, these factors motivate users to participate in innovation co-creation tasks.

## 3.2.2. Degree of sharing

One of the key features of innovation co-creation is the continuous process of sharing ideas and results. This process involves creating ways to facilitate alternating inbound and outbound flows among participating firms and consumers [5]. Mobile technologies facilitate the submission of content by allowing users to provide feedback anytime and anywhere. They also provide an effective and efficient way of connecting a large number of users to external sources of knowledge. Because mobility solutions provide flexibility and enable users to work with each other in real time, mobile technologies enable innovation co-creation processes to be executed faster.

## 3.2.3. Working outside organizational boundaries

Firms must be flexible and open to working with other parties to increase the chances of product and market success. This research suggests that it is beneficial to use incremental innovation practices such as co-creation to become immersed in the consumer’s context as much as possible. This immersion can be achieved by spending more time communicating with consumers in their own environments.

Most activities performed on mobile devices occur in social contexts where the participants collaborate to achieve a common task or goal. When designed correctly, technologies that enable collaboration can make many group activities more efficient and effective [55]. Through the development of virtual communities, social networks that include both firms and consumers can be formed. Providing access to these communities creates the potential to search for appropriate co-creators to participate in innovation processes.

## 3.2.4. Degree of complexity

Degree of complexity concerns facilitating the development and selection of ideas that are most effective and relevant in solving problems. Most innovation processes are based on the common steps of idea generation, idea selection, idea management, and realization of plans. Emergent innovation is not primarily dependent on exceptional individuals who can create radical innovations but rather on a larger group of members who have the understanding and skills to re-design and re-structure activities based on varying assumptions and existing solutions [69].

The adoption of mobile technologies for innovation co-creation brings together consumers that not only have a common interest but also those who are already ‘‘tech-savvy’’ and therefore have some familiarity with online co-creation tools [54]. As previously discussed, mobile technologies have been used extensively to carry out internal business processes. Furthermore, consumers are already using mobile technologies extensively to research products and brands for personal purposes and therefore are reasonably aware of new trends and innovations in different markets [91]. By allowing like-minded individuals to be connected, participants can take advantage of mass collaboration to develop and select original ideas.

## 3.3. Innovation co-creation roadmap

We now discuss a generalized roadmap involves four stages for achieving and maintaining the required innovation co-creation framework. To this end, we must establish an environment of processes and technologies that allows for direct and immediate communication between co-creation participants. It is important for firms to go through similar steps because doing so ensures that the required frequency, direction, and depth of interactions will be achieved for innovation co-creation.

Fig. 2 illustrates an abstract roadmap for innovation cocreation. Based on our literature review, four steps are required to implement the required enablers, capabilities, and characteristics for initiating and continuing innovation co-creation. Each iteration of the cycle involves a series of two-way rich dialogues that increase the extent to which innovation co-creation can be achieved.

## 3.3.1. Opportunity

This step is about creating the opportunities to participate in innovation co-creation. Opportunities can be created by producing an open platform where multiple connections can be made to initiate idea generation. By providing an easy and accessible means to communicate, such as through the use of mobile technologies, such a platform would attract repeat visits from users [54].

## 3.3.2. Community

Mobile technologies initiate interactions between potential participants. By allowing users to acknowledge input and prove that they are listening, mobile technologies facilitate the building of relationships that provide the foundation for subsequent engagements.

## 3.3.3. Collaboration

Through dynamically connected communities with the most appropriate users, problems and challenges can be solved collaboratively. Mobile technologies can act as a feedback mechanism during the development and testing phases of innovation cycles when knowledge needs to be shared and validated. With the power of real-time responses, this system facilitates faster and improved decision-making.

## 3.3.4. Culture

The result is a transformed organizational culture of increased sharing and creativity. By opening up the firm to key influencers of the brand or product, employees are prompted to adopt new perspectives that may trigger new ideas. A new culture leads to a modification in the strategic direction of the firm and thus new opportunities to continue new product development.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

Because consumers are no longer passive recipients of goods and services, their role in new product development has dramatically changed. The purpose of innovation co-creation is to allow firms to create simultaneous opportunities for innovation with consumers, rather than simply for them. Consequently, there is a higher chance of market acceptance and thus a lower risk of failed projects.

The Internet and related technologies have provided firms with the ability to implement distributed innovation practices in which consumers become active participants throughout the new product development process. However, the challenges of managing communities in virtual environments without compromising the ability to share resources and create active dialogues have yet to be overcome.

Mobile technologies provide participants with enhanced information and networks for innovation co-creation. They offer the greatest power to contribute ideas and select components that should be incorporated into new product offerings. By providing an open platform that is jointly maintained by the firm and consumers, mobile technologies have the potential to accelerate internal business processes and improve consumer relations.

Innovation co-creation has always been a significant factor in the long-term success of organizations across various industries and sizes and is therefore a popular topic both in research and practice. However, there appears to be a lack of in-depth understanding within existing research on how to best align the latest collaboration technologies with the necessary ecosystem for innovation co-creation.

We explored and synthesized concepts and frameworks from various fields of study related to innovation co-creation. We developed a new framework and roadmap to solve the identified problems, address identified issues, and fulfil the requirements identified in the literature for achieving innovation co-creation.

First, firms may apply different communication strategies depending on the degree of innovativeness of a co-creation task. Therefore, a more detailed study is required on how the enablers, capabilities, and characteristics of innovation co-creation may change depending on the nature of the product and industry.

Next, privacy and security concerns may limit the depth of information that consumers are willing to share with others. Future research may wish to investigate the effects of privacy and security on innovation co-creation in mobile environments and how to overcome the challenges of improving collaborative trust.

Finally, the limitations of mobile technologies in terms of factors such as limited energy supply, low data transfer rates, delayed input functionality, and small displays have not been considered in this study. Future work may wish to explore the specific design requirements of interfaces for innovation cocreation tools in the context of mobile technologies.
