# Opening the black box of system usage: user adaptation to disruptive IT

Christophe M. Elie-Dit-Cosaque<sup>1</sup> and Detmar W. Straub<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

According to Benbasat and Barki (2007), systems usage has remained a black box in spite of the fact that the construct lies at the heart of a host of studies in the field. We know very little about how exactly users cope with information technology (IT), especially disruptive IT. To answer such questions, we grounded our current work in Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s Coping Model of User Adaptation (2005; CMUA), a model that explains user strategies appraising an IT event. These strategies are a response to threats and opportunities embedded in the IT event and are impacted by the level of control users have over the situation. In the current study, following CMUA, we develop and test measures for a deeper understanding of systems usage and user adaptation to IT through a 2 - 2 laboratory experiment. Overall, we found strong support for the CMUA model.

Keywords: coping model of user adaptation; systems usage; disruptive information technologies; threats; opportunities; control

## Introduction

Systems usage is virtually unexplored territory. As Burton-Jones & Straub (2006) point out, most researchers treat it as a black box, with relatively ‘lean’ measures of use frequency and distant antecedents that are not theoretically connected to usage behaviors. This black box needs to be opened (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). A way of doing so is provided by Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005). These researchers have put forward a model that proposes that users engage in activities that can best be described as ‘adaptation’ to information technology (IT), and these adaptive responses mediate antecedents such as perceived usefulness and ease-of-use and systems usage. Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s Coping Model of User Adaptation (CMUA; 2005) is conceptually ground-breaking in that it explicitly models user adaptation as a form of mediation. While formulating an extremely interesting conceptual model, Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s work did not develop scales to measure CMUA variables. A key remaining question, therefore, is whether their results will hold when examined through a quantitative approach and a much larger sample than the six subjects they originally worked with. The basic question is rectitude: Exactly how good is their model? Our primary research question, therefore, is: As applied to the setting of disruptive IT, can we find empirical support for CMUA?

We propose to study CMUA in a disruptive IT setting for the following reasons. First of all, Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005) specifically suggest that disruptive IT is the most amenable setting for studying CMUA. Moreover, non-disruptive IT, systems that are compatible with previous systems or processes with which the users are already familiar, have been the object of many information systems (IS) studies over a long period of time. These kinds of non-disruptive technologies still offer challenges to managers, but the technology itself is not inherently alien.

The same cannot be said about disruptive technologies. A disruptive innovation is ‘a novel idea or behavior that, when introduced in organizational settings, causes dramatic changes in the structure of work processes’ (Sherif et al., 2006, p. 341). When the technology is ‘disruptive’, prior work suggests that managers and users do not respond in as predictable a manner. Disruptive IT innovations involve pervasive and radical changes in the organization and in organizational processes (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Hsiao-Lan et al., 2005).

There are several recognizable classes of disruptive technologies. For example, technologies such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems are disruptive technologies when the organization is overhauled during the process of implementation (Davenport et al., 1989; Hammer & Champy, 1993). Furthermore, those systems wherein usage is most often mandatory in organizations are frequently depicted as non-flexible (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). Therefore, individuals are forced to adapt in different ways, depending on the degree of the disruption caused by the IT. For these reasons, it is important to learn how employees adapt to IT in order to better respond to their needs. Also for these reasons, understanding how users adapt to disruptive IT is the main objective of this study.

Thus, to this date, we have limited knowledge about how users adapt to disruptive IT. According to Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005), prior scholarly models have been either piecemeal or not fully tested. Moreover, while some researchers have dealt with aspects of the issue before Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s CMUA (2005), there has been no fully integrated approach. Therefore, additional integrative studies are needed to pull together prior piecemeal views.

In fact, most frequently, quantitative researchers consider user adaptation to be implicit in system usage. This raises the question of whether a quantitative approach can address user adaptation. This study argues that it can. Because Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s adaptation strategies can be modeled as mediating system attributes and system usage, their model captures social embeddedness. This approach, thus, will allow us to better understand the contingencies surrounding use beliefs and use of IT in organizations.

In order to answer our key research question of whether CMUA is empirically verifiable, the study: (1) develops and validates an instrument for empirically measuring user strategies of adaptation to IT, (2) shows how user strategies of adaptation to IT can inform user interactions through the CMUA (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), and, finally, (3) demonstrates the need to take user strategies of adaptation into consideration in future research related to IT adoption and use.

## Why study user adaptation?

Despite its importance in our understanding of user interactions with IT, few quantitative researchers have attempted to explain how users adapt to IT (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). Previous models of IT acceptance and use treat user responses to working with systems as a black box. Models of IT acceptance and use literature employing TAM as a theory base, for example, neither conceptualizes nor tests user adaptation (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). This black box, therefore, needs to be opened. As shown in Figure 1, the argument is that there is a virtually unstudied ‘black box’ between usage behaviors and their most frequently studied antecedents. Antecedents of intention to use a technology include: (1) information quality variables in the Information System Success Model (ISM) (DeLone & McLean, 2003), (2) perceived usefulness and ease of use in TAM (Davis, 1989), and (3) a set of variables grouped under the concepts of performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence in Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003).

The large body of research following or refining these conceptualizations has analyzed numerous factors that permit us to better understand what motivates individuals to use IT. This body of work also yielded insights into the contingencies influencing IT use. To our knowledge, however, in none of these models does user adaptation to systems explicitly appear. Yet, some researchers have argued that by under-estimating context and the complexity of user behaviors, the models that work well in experimental settings lose predictive power in real-world settings (Gallivan, 2001). It is also important to understand user adaptive process because they can help explain specific positive or negative IS outcomes (Kock et al., 2006). For example, in their research, Kock et al. (2006) suggest that teams have often to face obstacles in the course of mediated communication. The researchers argue that when team members face such obstacles, they often strive to compensate it through changes in their behavior. They then show that this process can lead to greater team communication performance than what is achieved with face-to-face communication. These insights show that the adaptation process in the course of interaction with IS is not neutral and that we still need to gain knowledge regarding its key determinants and outcomes.

Researchers are more and more concerned about these limitations in the IT acceptance and usage stream of research. There are now, indeed, influential advocates for shifting in the way we study how users react to IT. For example, Benbasat & Barki (2007) specifically argue that PU and PEOU are themselves black boxes that are not easily opened nor easily applied to all technologies. They recommend that researchers improve the conceptualization of system usage, an issue also addressed by Burton-Jones & Straub (2006) and Straub & Burton-Jones (2007). They finally suggest that scholars should include user adaptation in conceptualizations. Benbasat & Barki (2007) state:

As a solution we propose that researchers broaden their perspective of system use from one that exclusively focuses on a narrow ‘amount’ view of users’ direct interaction with systems to one that also includes users’ adaptation, learning, and reinvention behaviors around a system. (p. 215)

Benbasat & Barki (2007) unambiguously argue that IS researchers build richer models to take into account a broader range of behaviors instead of focusing only on the direct relationships between use behaviors and their antecedents. User strategies of adaptation mediate the relations between usage behaviors and their antecedents. Such a conceptualization enriches the literature on acceptance, going beyond the overly simplistic relationships often posited by researchers.

Positivist, quantitative research in IS, in particular, has focused on usage behaviors far removed from antecedents – on outcomes such as usage or intention to use. What seems clear is that a complementary view of user adaptation allows a broader range of individual responses to IT (Benbasat & Barki, 2007), one that also implies a fascinating agency perspective (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

## Theoretical framework: CMUA

## The value of synthesizing piecemeal views of user adaptation

Consistent with Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005), we offer an integrative approach grounded in the theory of coping. These researchers define the adaptation process as a one-sided coping process: ‘coping deals with the adaptational acts that an individual performs in response to disruptive events that occur in his/her environment’ (p. 494). In accordance with the Theory of Coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), they go on to assert that IT users employ two processes to cope with a disruptive IT event. The first process is an appraisal whereby individuals evaluate the importance of an event, given their own situation and interests. The second process is the process of coping. In order to cope, users attempt to manage the situation (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) through what the authors called the ‘coping effort’. Applied to IS, the CMUA approach to user adaptation can enrich our view of individual reaction to IT and go beyond the sundry limitations found in the IS acceptance and usage literature. Our general conceptual model with underpinnings from CMUA is depicted as Figure 2.

With respect to the problem of adaptation being a black box, CMUA specifically takes into account user appraisal of threats or opportunities as they are experienced during IT implementation. The agency framing of CMUA pushes the researcher to go beyond a view of users focusing only on features of the technology. Hence it offers insights into more complex user thinking with respect to IT. In short, CMUA opens up the black box of user adaptation strategies.

Appraisal process A key concept in CMUA is appraisal of the situation. Lazarus & Folkman (1984) define cognitive appraisal as ‘the process of categorizing an encounter, and its various facets, with respect to its significance for well-being’ (p. 31). In CMUA, appraisal is a two facetted process: the primary and secondary appraisal. According to Lazarus & Folkman (1984), the two appraisal process interplay and can occur simultaneously:

We want to emphasize that we do not view threat and challenge appraisals as poles of a single continuum. As we stated above, threat and challenge can occur simultaneously, and must be considered as separate, although often related, constructs. (p. 33)

Primary and secondary appraisals are thus ‘continuous rather than dichotomous constructs’ (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005, p. 500). Other researchers offer somewhat similar definitions of appraisal as multiple interacting components and processes. For example, Bagozzi et al. (1992) suggest that three interacting appraisal processes are carried out after intentions of pursuing a goal are formed (Bagozzi et al., 1992, p. 474).

Primary appraisal: threat and opportunity perceptions According to CMUA and the theory of coping, the primary appraisal leads to an interpretation as to whether there are stakes let out in a stressful encounter for the individual (Folkman et al., 1986, p. 994). In other words, it reflects how risky a situation is perceived to be by an individual (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Tomaka et al., 1993). Following threat and opportunity assessment is a secondary appraisal.

Secondary appraisal: control over the situation Secondary appraisal refers to users’ evaluation of the level of control they have ‘over the technology, their work, and themselves’ (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005, p. 507), as well as of the ‘coping options’ available to them (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). This is an evaluation the user makes about his/her personal resources. In this phase, CMUA posits that users evaluate the level of control they have over the situation. In CMUA, the level of user control shapes their perceptions about the disruptive IT event and influences their subsequent strategy of adaptation. Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005) articulate three dimensions of control: (1) control over the work, (2) control over self, and (3) control over the technology. Control over the work includes having sufficient autonomy at work and the capacity to modify tasks in response to the changing environment (Thomas & Velthouse, 1990; Shaw & Barrett-Power, 1997). Control over self refers to how well the individual could adapt to the situation. Finally, control over the technology deals with the degree of mastery individuals have over features and functionalities of the IT, whether they were involved in the development of the system or not.

The process of coping Last, after the primary and secondary appraisal, individuals engage in a coping process in which CMUA distinguishes four generic user adaptive strategies. Lazarus & Folkman (1984) defined coping as ‘constantly changing cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage specific external and/or internal demands that are appraised as taxing or exceeding the resources of the person’ (p. 141). According to these authors, this definition highlights four characteristics of coping:

(1) Coping is a process, rather than a trait. This highlights the dynamic, changing nature of coping. A process-oriented approach considers coping to be ‘a response to the particular psychological and environmental demands of specific stressful encounters’ (Folkman et al., 1986, p. 992), whereas the traitoriented approach considers the environment to be meaningless, that coping is essentially a property of the person.

(2) Since coping involves effort, it differs from ‘automatized adaptive behaviors’ which do not involve effort (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984, p. 141).

(3) Coping is defined as an ‘effort to manage’ a situation and hence differs from its outcome (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984, p. 142).

(4) Coping is different from ‘mastery’ (p. 142).

Coping takes on two basic forms. The first is the ‘emotion[-]focused form’. For Lazarus & Folkman (1984), it ‘consists of cognitive processes directed at lessening emotional distress and includes strategies such as avoidance, minimization, distancing, selective attention, positive comparisons, and wresting positive value from negative events’ (p. 150). The second form of coping, the problem-focused form, is ‘directed at defining the problem, generating alternative solutions, weighting the alternatives in terms of their costs and benefits, choosing among them, and acting’ (p. 152).

Consistent with Lazarus & Folkman (1984), CMUA adopts a process-oriented rather than a trait-oriented approach to coping. Applying the theory of coping to the IS field, Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005) identify four main strategies that individuals adopt in order to cope with changes created by IT. As summarized in Table 2, users adopt a ‘benefits maximizing’ (BM) strategy when the technology is perceived to be providing opportunities and users believe they have a large amount of control. With an appraisal of opportunity embedded in system use and a perception of low control, users adopt a ‘benefits satisficing’ (BS) strategy.

Two other, different strategies are adopted when the technology is thought to be a threat. When users believe themselves to have high control, they adopt a ‘disturbance handling’ (DH) strategy. With low control perceptions, they espouse a ‘self preservation’ (SP) strategy (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). These coping strategie are summarized in Table 1.

## Model and hypothesis

The core principle of CMUA is that depending on: (1) the threats and opportunities in a situation and (2) depending on the level of personal control, individuals develop coping strategies to disruptive IT. We thus formulated four hypotheses in order to test whether CMUA is empirically supported as applied to the setting of disruptive IT.

## User coping strategies of adaptation

Based on a qualitative study of six managers, Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005) identified the four main user strategies of adaptation just discussed. These four strategies are summarized in Table 1 and shown causally in Figure 2. Consistent with the strategic characteristics emphasized by Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005), our aim is to develop and validate measures and to test them using a quantitative approach. Our first hypothesis basically posits the distinctness of these strategies.

## H1: The four user strategies of adaptation in CMUA are empirically distinct from one other.

One way to test hypotheses in CMUA is to conceptualize user strategies of adaptation in terms of an individual’s inclination to adopt one strategy more than the other. We label user coping strategy choice under opportunity—OpChoice, and user strategies under threats—ThChoice. These hypotheses are detailed next.

## Coping strategies in opportunity conditions (OpChoice)

As noted above, the primary user appraisal is of threats and opportunities. Users might find in IT an opportunity to improve job performance (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005) in that IT allows automating routine tasks and promoting tasks with more added value (Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 1993). Together, results from prior studies suggest that when users perceive IT to offer a potential for opportunity, they are likely to adopt strategies focused on technological benefits.

With a secondary appraisal, users are posited to feel they can control the consequences of the system, and when this occurs, they are more likely to have positive intentions toward it (Jackson & Dutton, 1988; Venkatesh et $a l . ,$ 2003; Vaast & Walsham, 2005). If they believe they can perform their tasks better with help of the system and perceive they control the situation, they are likely to more heavily use the IT. In other words, they will be problem-focused in their adaptation. When the system is less restrictive and allows multiple features left up to the discretion of users, those users believe it to be controllable, more profitable, and they will want to maximize its use. Therefore, when the situation is appraised as offering many opportunities and users feel they have a relatively high level of control over the situation, they are likely to adopt a ‘benefits maximizing’ (BM) strategy of adaptation (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

## H2: When users appraise the situation as an opportunity, the more control they have over the situation, the more inclined they will be to adopt a BM coping strategy.

Conversely, if users believe they have low control even though the situation still provides opportunities, Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005) argue that users are likely to make limited efforts to adapt to the technology. Under such conditions users accept benefits but exert few efforts toward that goal, since they have too little control over the situation. They may, for example, feel they have insufficient competencies (Thomas & Velthouse, 1990) to adapt to changes or to master features of the technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003). As a consequence, instead of maximizing the value of their strategy, which can be difficult when they have only a small amount of control (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), they are more likely to choose a passive coping strategy rather than exerting much adaptive effort. According to Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005), users will be neither problem-focused (being unable to take full advantage from the system) nor emotion-focused (having no emotional tension to reduce). Therefore, on a secondary appraisal, low levels of control over the situation (even when the situation offers opportunities) will result in a ‘benefits satisficing’ (BS) strategy of adaptation (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

H3: When users appraise the situation as an opportunity, the less control they have, the more inclined they will be to adopt a BS coping strategy.

## Coping strategies under threat conditions

When users appraise the situation as threatening, their coping strategies differ compared to viewing the IT as an opportunity. Because disruptive technologies overhaul an organization (Davenport et al., 1989; Hammer & Champy, 1993). and involve radical transformation (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Sherif et al., 2006), they can be threatening to individuals. With the implementation of a disruptive technology, several factors can render a situation threatening for an individual. These include for example: potentially negative impacts on work and performance (Martinko et $a l . ,$ , 1996; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) or beliefs about user influence on control and resources (Crozier & Friedberg, 1977; Jackson & Dutton, 1988; Martinko et $a l . ,$ 1996; Chattopadhyay et al., 2001). Any of these can contribute to greater user threat perceptions.

The large body of literature on user resistance to IT change suggests that when a technology is threatening, users may try to avoid using it in order to preserve themselves from potential negative consequences (Martinko et al., 1996; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). Furthermore, research suggests that threat perceptions are linked with control perceptions (Jackson & Dutton, 1988). Therefore, when users perceive the technology to be a threat, CMUA envisages a greater disposition to ‘disturbance handling’ (DH) and ‘self-preservation’ (SP) strategies (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). As with opportunity, choice between these strategies depends on the level of user control.

Threat perceptions occur with a user’s first appraisal of the disruptive IT situation. When users feel they have high control, their strategies of adaptation are mainly problem focused (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). Indeed, even if they perceive the situation is threatening, they are likely still to be very confident that they can manage the situation. Therefore, they are likely to improve their capacity to work with the system or to modify its features. Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005, p. 502) illustrate such behaviors as: (a) looking for training, (b) changing the features of the technology, or (c) adapting work procedures to be behaviors reflecting a disturbance handling strategy. Also, having control, users might be able to minimize the potential negative consequences of the system. This makes their coping strategy emotion-focused also. Consequently, when system users appraise the situation as threatening, but believe they have control, they will adopt a ‘disturbance handling’ (DH) strategy of adaptation (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

H4: When users appraise the situation as a threat, the more control they have, the more inclined they will be to adopt a DH coping strategy.

As just noted, in a threat condition with user high control, users will adopt a DH strategy. When appraising low control over the situation, users are more prudent in their adaptation to the technology. A less flexible system diminishes the discretion users can have over the system and subsequently their control over that system (Nelson & Ghods, 1998). Also, when the situation possesses threat and individuals do not feel they are sufficiently competent or skilled in use of the technology, they are likely to engage in a minimal adaptation effort (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). As a consequence, coping efforts will be emotion-focused since the users will be likely to minimize the negative consequences the system has on them, or else they will simply try to avoid using it wherever possible (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). Therefore, when users appraise the technology as threatening and when they feel they have little control over the situation, they are predicted to adopt a ‘self preservation strategy’ (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

H5: When users appraise the situation as a threat, the less control they have, the more inclined they will be to adopt an SP coping strategy.

## Research design and methods

A 2 - 2 repeated measures experimental design was chosen to test the research model. Since the design assigned treatments randomly to subjects, it was a true experiment conducted in a laboratory setting (Cook & Campbell, 1979). Subjects were business students in a large French university. In all, 71% of subjects had significant professional experience or currently had an apprenticeship in a company.

The IT artifact chosen for the experimental scenario was an ERP IS, SysControl, which invoked radical changes in the way management controllers performed their work. The system, therefore, was disruptive since the expected change is both radical and pervasive (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003). For the purpose of this study, subjects assessed a series of real-world scenarios (e.g., Straub & Karahanna, 1998). Four hypothetical scenarios were posed to subjects and data were collected via experimental questionnaires. The key points in each scenario were based on prior research dealing with the impacts of ERP systems on individuals and organizations. In fact, we included some of the commonly evoked potential (a) threats of ERP systems in the scenarios, such as undesirable ERP effects or quality problems (Bernard et al., 2004) and (b) benefits, such as increased rigor and speed in data processing (Hammer, 1990); we also included feelings that reflect the fact of having different degrees of control over the consequences of the implemented IT according to the descriptions of Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005). This approach appears to be particularly apt, given that the level of analysis of this study is the strategies adopted by users who are confronted with the imminent implementation of a disruptive IT. Consistent with Straub & Karahanna (1998, p. 164), we manipulated independent variables by changing the wording of the scenarios.

## Scenario

The scenario as elaborated deals with ERP systems, disruptive technologies that bring about radical change in the way individuals work together, and reconfiguration of work processes (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003). Subjects were told that their company had implemented a new ERP system and that the system created radical changes in the way they would have to work with their collaborators. Consistent with the process approach of coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), threats and opportunities, as well as control were defined with respect to the psychological and environmental demands placed on the individual. In order to manipulate threat/opportunity as well as high and low control conditions, we created scenarios assessing four different psychological and environmental situations.

## Procedure

The scenarios were administered partly via a paper-based questionnaire at the end of Business or IS courses (102 subjects, 0% mortality rate, but with two withheld from analysis because of low percentage of answers) and partly via a Web experiment (68 subjects, 40% response rate). For research integrity purposes, but also to minimize common method bias, the anonymity of the subjects was guaranteed in both settings. In such circumstances, response anonymity can reduce subject apprehension (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Per Podsakoff et al. (2003), subjects were told that there were no right or wrong questions and that only their honest responses were required.

## Experimental manipulations

Manipulations were embedded in the wording of the scenarios. Subjects were asked to role-play a management controller in BestInsurance, Inc. and told that the ERP, named SysControl, was expected to increase management controller performance. Management controllers are a common entry-level position in French firms and so subjects understood the role required of this position.

Common to both scenarios is a general description of the purpose of SysControl and of its context of implementation in the firm BestInsurance, Inc. A second part is an assessment of the work context (threat/opportunity) and capabilities of the user (high control/low control) with respect to the system and the situation. The manipulations were phrased so that subjects clearly perceived threats or opportunities as well as whether they had a great deal of control or little control in responding to the threat/opportunity.

In the scenario focusing on opportunity (the opportunity condition), SysControl was said to offer many options to management controllers. Consistent with the literature on ERPs (Davenport et al., 1989; Hammer & Champy,

1993), subjects were told that SysControl increased overall performance at work.

On the other hand, in the scenario focusing on threats (the threat condition), it was stressed that the ERP could replace the management controller position. Furthermore, controllers at BestInsurance, Inc. were said in the scenarios to be lower performers than those in rival companies. Thus uncertainty was deliberately incorporated into the scenario about the system being used to fire controllers. In addition, it was emphasized that SysControl would be unlikely to increase the efficiency of controllers, but rather contribute to errors and loss of data. In sum, SysControl was framed as a poor system, threatening and badly implemented, and the replacement for old, but non-threatening legacy systems. Overall, the scenarios focused on threats associated with the implementation of SysControl and described a stressful situation to which individuals would have to cope.

Similarly, control over the situation was manipulated. Aspects of control of CMUA (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005) include control of work (Thomas & Velthouse, 1990; Shaw & Barrett-Power, 1997), self, and technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). In the scenario, subjects were shown that they either had high control on their work, self, and the technology simultaneously, or else low control. Manipulation checks (Boudreau et al., 2001) were included in order to prove that the manipulation worked. Details on the scenarios are given in Appendix A.

## Measures and pretest

Content validity was examined via a literature review that helped to build our constructs and by four expert judges familiar with our domain of investigation (Boudreau et al., 2001). Since item complexity and ambiguity can induce common methods bias, we attempted to create questions to be as simple and neutral as possible (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Questionnaires items are provided in Appendix B. Table 2 provides the detail of the constructs and of their theoretical rationale.

Perceived control tapped into the concepts of control over self, work, and technology consistent with CMUA. This manipulation has been checked against items of perceived behavioral control (Venkatesh et al., 2003) for control over self and over technology. Items for control over work were developed from the work of Shaw & Barrett-Power (1997). Items for user coping strategies of adaptation were based on the work of Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005).

BM is an active coping strategy, which basically means dealing with the event directly. As such, the wording of the items includes phrases for behaviors that take maximum advantage of the new IT. In contrast, the BS coping strategy is emotion-focused. This implies that individuals lower their emotional tensions subsequent to their awareness of the IT implementation. In the meanwhile they benefit from the advantages of the IT.

The DH coping strategy is both problem- and emotionfocused. This means that while individuals try to lessen the negative consequences of the IT, they still try to find ways to effectively manage it or even to benefit from it. Item wording such as ‘I am thinking that with SysControl, after some adjustments, I could expect it to increase my performance’ (DH3), and ‘Although SysControl is somewhat confusing, it will probably allow me to be a better controller’ (DH2) tap into both problem and emotion-focused aspects of the construct. In contrast to all other constructs, SP is solely emotion-focused. This implies that, since the IT has negative consequences on the individual and the individual has low control, individuals are expected to try to change their mind about the situation. With respect to the SP strategy, Beaudry and Pinsonneault argue:

Users will try to change their perception of the IT event by minimizing the perceived negative consequences (e.g., maintaining hope that the expected negative consequences will not materialize), positive comparison (e.g., comparing themselves to other users who are worse off), self-deception and avoidance, selective attention, and distancing. (p. 503)

Accordingly, SP was developed to reflect how individuals attempt to change their perceptions about the IT. Item wordings such as ‘Given that I have a lot of important things to care about, I would not be likely to pay too much attention to SysControl’ (SP1, e.g., distancing), ‘SysControl will not be much of an interesting system for controllers’ (SP2, self-deception), and ‘SysControl will not help me to be a better controller than I am already’ (SP3, positive comparison) are expected to tap into the intended construct. These items all invoke individuals’ intent to change their perception as a response to a threatening, newly implemented IT. As such, all these items depict states of mind that contradict the initial purpose and scenario expectations about why the system was being implemented. In this respect, it reflects the change individuals make in their perceptions when adapting to the new IT.

## Sampling

In all, 168 students in a French public university took part in the study. Of the participants, 82% were undergraduates and 18% graduate students. With respect to respondents characteristics, 49% were male, 51% female and their average age was 21.93 (standard deviation ¼ 1.58 years). They reported using a computer 29.9 hr a week (standard deviation ¼ 17.67 hr) with a reasonable level of computer efficacy (mean on the 7-point scale ¼ 4.86, standard deviation ¼ 1.21). On average, subjects spent 4.16 years studying at a university level (standard deviation ¼ 0.75) and worked 1.39 years (standard deviation ¼ 1.27). ## Data analysis

After assessing that the experimental manipulation worked on the subjects (see Online Supplement), we analyzed measurement properties and hypotheses.

## Measurement

Convergent and discriminant validity were assured via a principal component analysis (PCA) (Appendix D). Analysis indicates that the instrument was valid. It was also reliable. Since all constructs of our model are reflective, Cronbach’s alphas are appropriate for testing reliability (Gefen et al., 2000). Cronbach’s alphas were of 0.87 for BM, 0.72 for BS, 0.87 for DH, and 0.85 for SP, which is within the range of commonly accepted values (Nunnally, 1967). Overall, therefore, our instrument has acceptable measurement properties.

## Scales for measuring user coping strategies

Next, we built continuous scales to measure user preference of one coping strategy of adaptation over another. Since subjects could state their perceptions of two strategies for each scenario, the most useful scale would predict which one of the two strategies that subjects were more likely to choose. Scales thus were built by taking the means of items for each strategy constructs. This procedure is reasonable in that the constructs are reflective, interchangeable, and are expected to be similarly represented in their latent variable (Petter et al., 2007). Opportunity condition If H2 is accurate, we predicted: (1) that OpChoice (thus BM-BS) would be positive and (2) that the relation between Control and OpChoice would be positive and significant. As expected, the results show that control is positively associated with OpChoice (F ¼ 14.16, Po0.000). Also, OpChoice is positive in control condition. ## Discussion, limitations and future research

This study has important implications. Users are likely to be critical change drivers following the implementation of disruptive IT (Hsiao-Lan et al., 2005). It is thus necessary to understand the dynamics of their interactions with such IT. The CMUA is a ground-breaking description of this dynamic and the current study is the first larger scale test of this model.

In order to test CMUA, we (1) developed valid and reliable measures for the CMUA; and (2) applied CMUA to the setting of disruptive technologies; and (3) tested CMUA with a sufficiently large sample in a scenario experiment. The overall model was well supported in threat conditions. In opportunity/control and no-control conditions, users tend to prefer, on average, a BM coping strategy. This shows that in opportunity conditions, the influence of control is less salient. In such a condition, therefore, it is especially important that system designers and managers place their efforts specifically on the benefits users can obtain from the implemented IT. CMUA is thus a valid model to further our scholarly knowledge of system usage. To validate CMUA, we adopted a rigorous method for testing a parsimonious model of user adaptation to IT, in accordance with the CMUA the calls for further research of Benbasat & Barki (2007). Our instrument utilized a scenario design that specifically addressed our research questions.

All studies have limitations and these can lead to future research. Our first limitation is that we assess adaptation to disruptive IT but do not consider non-disruptive IT. Future research could and should simultaneously compare user responses to both disruptive and non-disruptive IT. We posit that changes involved in non-disruptive IT are less important than those involved by the disruptive ones, but this is untested. One might examine how users evaluating the implementation of non-disruptive IT and subsequently adapting to it respond differently (but not less importantly) than how they respond to disruptive IT.

Second, whereas it might be thought that student subjects lack critical realism and threats to external validity (Gordon et al., 1986), most of the subjects who took part to the study did have professional and working experience, or were currently working. Furthermore, the management controller position is a very likely entry position, especially in France. Therefore, while we believe that these student subjects were appropriate surrogates for management controllers, a free simulation experiment in a real-world setting with real-world management controllers would certainly offer additional insights in future research. One merit of the current scenario, however, is that it permits people to answer more freely, without the real-world pressures of their current job and the constraints it imposes. Future research using a free simulation experiment could conceivably test the model by minimizing any common methods bias that might occur. In spite of these limitations, subjects were successfully manipulated to believe that they had control over system features and that there were degrees of freedom in how they reacted to the implementation.

Even though our results are still credible from the viewpoint of rigorous experimental design standards (Fromkin & Streufert, 1976), adaptation outcomes not studied here would probably lead researchers to learn more about coping effort directed at changing personal tasks and self, rather than changing the IT itself. Depending on system designer intentions and expectations, system outcomes may thus be affected.

In addition, future research can test the impacts of system use mode, whether voluntary or mandatory usage, and that of system flexibility. We examined mandatory rather than voluntary settings, in contrast to other studies of IT adoption and acceptance. It could be expected that with greater degrees of free will, individuals would adapt differently to IT disruptions. As emphasized by Wu & Lederer (2009) in their meta analysis, voluntariness moderates the impacts of variables such as ease of use and usefulness on behavioral intention. Moreover, we studied adaptation to ERP systems, which are believed to be IT that is very inflexible (Hammer & Champy, 1993). Such IT allows for fewer adjustments to tasks and existing business processes (Nelson & Ghods, 1998).

Finally, researchers of the IT acceptance stream widely acknowledge the influence of individual differences on IT acceptance. Similarly, we believe the study of individual differences can inform us better about user coping strategies of adaptation. For example, the literature on emotions and coping asserts that cognitive appraisal styles significantly impact how users appraise and finally cope with stressful situations (Folkman et al., 1986; Tomaka et al., 1993; Skinner & Brewer, 2002). Another domain of investigation is the understanding of the influence of national culture on user coping strategies of adaptation. Indeed, we know little about how national culture influences how users adapt specifically to IT. Future research could and should analyze these issues as well.

Further discussion of the results of this study permits us to highlight several contributions for theory and practice. We explore those contributions next.

## Contributions to theory and practice

The study makes several contributions to theory and practice, as summarized in Table 7.

The first contribution is the development of measures for variables in CMUA. We provide a rigorous method and reliable measures for measuring user adaptation to IT, a topic that has been under-investigated to date. Further validation of our instrument can be carried out in a real-world setting in order to test the extent of its applicability.

Second, we show that CMUA offers a way to open the black box of user adaptation, hopefully making the IS scholarly community more cognizant of what happens between use antecedents and use behavior. In particular, we show that CMUA can address major gaps arising from the traditional user acceptance and usage stream of research. Extant research on IT acceptance and use largely ignores user adaptation. Including user coping strategies as mediators might help better explain individual responses to IT change. Coping strategies of adaptation could well be the missing link in adoption research and we suggest they be included in future research on acceptance and use of IT.

Van der Heijden (2004) stressed the importance of taking into account the kind of technology being implemented. Fourth, our study also takes a different tack from other studies in showing that individuals can follow their own strategies of adaptation. It can also help to understand why IT that are designed for specific purposes can be used in both intended and unintended ways (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Also, it suggests that even in mandatory use contexts, workers can still adapt in different ways to the technology, a notion that resonates with other research (e.g., Boudreau & Robey, 2005). In the final analysis, how workers adapt to a system can have an important impact on the overall success of the system.

## Conclusion

Change evoked by disruptive IT can be particularly threatening for users who are also social actors (Lamb &

Kling, 2003). Research in IS has often neglected this and the need to take into account user adaptation to IT and subsequent systems usage. This has created a ‘black box’ which we attempt to partially open in this study. For that, we designed a rigorous scenario-based experiment whereby subjects had to assess their coping strategies when confronted to threats or opportunities of a disruptive IT. We show that CMUA is valid and that it is a step toward enriched IS models of acceptance and use of IT.

## Scenario

General context: background on BestInsurance Inc You are a management controller at BestInsurance, a major insurance company. BestInsurance is concerned that even though it has many management controllers, it still lacks capabilities in its management controlling activities, including financial reporting and forecasting. In comparison with other companies in its class, BestInsurance is late in implementing and using information technologies.

Therefore, the top managers of BestInsurance have decided to overhaul its old information system in order to improve financial processes with a single unified system. Today, you have been told that SysControl, the newly implemented ERP system, has just been launched and that you can begin to use it.

SysControl integrates and automates all major data processes related to financial control at BestInsurance. SysControl is expected to result in more accurate and faster financial reporting. Working with SysControl will be a revolution for employees at BestInsurance, and primarily for management controllers and accountants who are first in line for using it. Indeed, the way business processes have worked previously at BestInsurance has been technologically antiquated and not very effective.

Top managers expect management controllers to fully use SysControl since improving costs and process control is essential for remaining competitive. They are convinced of management controllers’ interest in using SysControl since they believe it will help them achieve greater performance with more added value in their work.

## Manipulation

Scenario/opportunities You believe SysControl can affect you positively, both personally and professionally. It offers a lot of opportunities to improve your performance. Like many of your colleagues, you see in SysControl a way to improve your efficiency (e.g., reducing time and effort), your effectiveness (e.g., improving the quality of work output), and your overall performance. That is, you believe you would be a better controller if you used SysControl and adapted your work to it, than if you continued working with the old

BestInsurance systems and procedures. In order to be able to use SysControl, you realize that you will need to learn new skills and to make adaptations to your work procedures.

Scenario/threats At the present time, you believe Sys-Control can affect you negatively, both personally and professionally. Like many of your colleagues, you are afraid that BestInsurance will use SysControl to cut jobs. In addition, you think the system is unlikely to help you to improve your efficiency (e.g., reducing time and effort), your effectiveness (e.g., improving the quality of work output), and your overall performance. Conversely, you think it can increase errors and lower the overall quality of your work. That is, you believe you would be better off using legacy systems and procedures than working with SysControl. In order to be able to use SysControl, you would need to learn new skills and to adapt your work procedures in a major way.

Control over the situation: control However, even with these reservations (threats), you believe you have a great deal of control over the situation. Indeed, you believe you have autonomy in your job and that you are able to modify your tasks when using a system like SysControl. You also believe that you can adapt yourself to the new environment created by the implementation of SysControl. Finally, you feel that you have a lot of control over the features and functionalities of SysControl since you have been involved in its development and well understand how to utilize it beneficially.

Control over the situation: no-control In addition to these reservations (opportunities)/‘unfortunately’ (threats), you believe you have little control over the situation. Indeed, you believe you do not have sufficient autonomy over your job and that you are not able to modify your tasks in response to SysControl. You also believe that you cannot adapt yourself to the new environment created by the implementation of SysControl. Finally, you feel that you have little control over the features and functionalities of SysControl since you have not been involved in its development and do not understand well how to utilize it beneficially.

## Measurement

We first tested convergent and discriminant validity (Boudreau et al., 2001). For that purpose, we performed a PCA with each pair of coping strategies of adaptation constructs. In order to improve the psychometric properties of the instrument, we excluded BM4, BM5, and BS1 from analysis in the opportunity condition scenario. Although the original instrument showed yet appropriate convergent and discriminant validity, this deletion allowed us to increase variance extracted from 59.83 to 72.38%. In the threat/condition scenario, we excluded SP1 and DH4 from analysis. Improved convergent and discriminant validity as well as a better variance explained resulted from this exclusion. At this point, all items loaded cleanly on their intended construct. One exception is DH3 which loads at 0.41 on the SP construct, which is slightly above the 0.40 level that warrants appropriate discriminant validity (Gefen et al., 2000). Because it was marginally over the threshold, we decided to keep this item. Other statistics were checked, including the level of variance explained and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy. All show appropriate levels. The results of the PCAs are shown in Table D1.

Convergent validity can be assessed when the items load significantly on their intended construct. All of the items in the PCA load at least 0.74 on their intended construct, which shows appropriate convergent validity. Also, since items load more highly on their intended construct than on any other construct (Boudreau et al., 2001), discriminant validity was reasonable. We can therefore conclude that the instrument has appropriate discriminant validity.
