# Dual efects of social support seeking in patient-centric online healthcare communities: A longitudinal study

Na Liu<sup>a</sup>, Yu Tong<sup>b,</sup>\*, Hock Chuan Chan<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Business Information Systems Discipline, University of Sydney Business School, Australia

<sup>b</sup> Department of Date Science and Engineering Management, School of Management, Zhejiang University, 310058, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems and Analytics, National University of Singapore, Singapore

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online healthcare communities Social support seeking Uncertainty in illness Information credibility Social identity Health severity

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates exploratory and purposeful social support-seeking behaviors in the patient-centric online healthcare community (POHC). The antecedents and varying efects of the two behaviors on uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment are examined. Two rounds of field surveys are conducted among users in three POHCs. Findings indicate that exploratory and purposeful social support-seeking behaviors exert varying efects on the change in uncertainty in illness and are influenced diferently by the perceived information credibility of users, their identification with a community, and the perceived severity of their illness.

## 1. Introduction

Online channels are becoming an important information source for people facing health problems. More than half of the internet users in the U.S. have looked for health information online in the year 2012 [1]. A survey on U.S. internet users’ health information seeking preference shows that 24 % of people got information or other forms of support from people having the same health condition [1]. Taking the form of online communities. the patient-centric online healthcare community (POHC) is increasingly becoming an important venue to form supportive and intimate online social networks among patients [2]. It provides informal health services for patients to look for not just information support but also other types of social support from similar others [3]. Social support seeking<sup>1</sup> denotes the degree to which an individual who experiences potentially stressful events contacts others for support [4]. This behavior emphasizes on how individuals can satisfy needs from their social network and the strategies they use to obtain social support [5].

Users of POHCs can exhibit diferent social support-seeking behaviors. Maintaining historical information for users to come back to all discussions at any time they want, POHCs serve as a knowledge repository for users to broaden their knowledge base on a disease. Hence, they can browse the contents or scan broadly to discover social support in an exploratory way. This social support-seeking behavior is referred as exploratory social support seeking. With the communication functions and supportive culture, POHCs also have the potential to become a huge digital brain, which enables users with specific doubts to crowdsource medical suggestions and ask a wider population to get support [6]. This social support seeking behavior through direct sending of postings in the community is referred as purposeful social support seeking.

While these two behaviors are common in POHCs, extant research lacks a consistent understanding on them. Varying findings are found on how users are afected by their social support seeking behaviors. For instance, reseachers have looked at the importance of patients being active consumers of health information [7]. However, some studies found that for those users who do not actively send postings in POHCs still report increased confidence and acceptance of the disease (van Uden-Kraan 2008). Interestingly, a prior study reported that when users browse the contents of POHCs, they may feel overwhelmed with the amount of links posted on the online support group, and feel uncertain about the accuracy of this information [8]. The users may also have negative feelings when they are exposed to the negative aspects of an illness [8]. Yet, another study found that there is no diference between users who only browse the contents of POHCs and those who post in POHCs in terms of feeling better informed and more confident about the treatment [9]. Therefore, how diferent social support seeking behaviors influence health-related outcomes is unclear.

The study aims to scrutinize these two social support seeking behaviors. Specifically, we propose that purposeful and exploratory social support seeking exhibit distinct characteristics, which may explain the various efects and require diferent sets of antecedents to manage. We focus on uncertainty in illness as the important outcome to measure the efect of social support seeking. Uncertainty is a common psychological reaction when a person experiences illness ([10–12]). It refers to the “the inability to determine the meaning of illness-related events” and accurately anticipate or predict health outcomes [11]. Uncertainty may occur at any point of the illness trajectory, but it is more common at diagnosis, making decisions on treatments, as patients are facing unfamiliar, complex, and potentially threatening experiences [13]. POHCs are becoming a channel for patients to obtain support and manage their uncertainty related to illness. By seeking various kinds of social support, users are looking to improve their understanding on an illness, get empathy or get assurance on their decisions [14], which has great potential to cope with illness-related uncertainties.

To investigate the antecedents of social support seeking in POHCs, we draw on the behavioral model of Andersen for health service utili zation [15,16]. Health service refers to a service provided for the benefit of the health of a person [16]. Online social support is a new form of health service enabled by POHCs [17], through which users can utilize the service based on a vast array of web users with similar concerns [3,18,19]. This health service utilization model suggests that the use of a health service can be explained by predisposing characteristics, enabling factors, and the need for help of an individual [15]. In this study, we will also investigate the predictors of social support seeking from these three perspectives.

Two rounds of field surveys are conducted among actual users of POHCs in this study. The results demonstrate that exploratory and purposeful social support seeking in POHCs have varying efects on the changes in uncertainty in illness. In turn, these support seeking beha viors are influenced diferently by the users’ perceived information credibility, users’ identification with the community, and the perceived health severity of users. The findings of this research provide several important contributions to the current literature on online healthcare communities and health psychology. In particular, this study adds to the burgeoning research on POHCs by conceptualizing social support seeking, deconstructing it into two types (i.e., exploratory and purpo seful social support seeking), and evaluating their opposite efects in POHCs on changing the uncertainty in diagnosis and treatment. The study also contributes to the literature on online healthcare communities by investigating the antecedents of the two social support seeking behaviors in POHCs. The study also provides community managers with practical guidelines for improving the efectiveness of POHCs.

## 2. Theoretical background and research model

## 2.1. Exploratory and purposeful social support seeking in POHCs

Exploratory social support seeking refers to users’ behavior to discover social support through unobtrusive observation of community activities [20]. Users engaging in such activities usually don’t have a specific goal in mind for what support to look for. A sizable proportion of online community participants consist of users [21] who prefer to read other users’ discussion but not post anything in the community. Exploratory support seeking behavior is also common in POHCs. Given that health-related concerns are sometimes sensitive, people may feel embarrassed to reveal their identity when asking for information about their diseases [22], and thus, they hesitate to inquire directly in the community. POHCs have information databases that can be browsed by users for information in a noncommunicative manner so that they can avoid embarrassment, and such information can be used as an exemplary experience to evaluate their current state of illness. In the literature of health information seeking, information scanning is also found to be an efective way to obtain health knowledge and empower patients [23]. In addition, users can also obtain courage and support from the self-disclosed feelings and shared experiences of others. When exploring social support in POHCs, users are exploring a wide overview of a topic to broaden their knowledge base on it [23].

Purposeful social support seeking refers to the behavior of seeking social support to satisfy certain goals through direct asking in POHCs [24]. Users seeking social support purposively either look to fill a knowledge gap or they have other specific needs to meet. This behavior usually takes the form of posting in a forum [25], either through starting a new thread or posting under other people’s threads. A content analysis of the posts in an online cancer support group shows that selfdisclosure is an efective means of obtaining social support [26], which can only be accomplished by posting in a community. Users may share their experiences with other community members in return for emotional support and raise questions in the community to obtain informational support.

Exploratory and purposeful social support seeking in POHCs difer in many aspects. First, the main activities of support seekers are different. Support exploration consists of activities without direct interaction with others. Browsing is the main activity in social support exploration. Users can either scan broadly on the profile, posts, and all threads of users in the community with no real goal or browse prespecified topics of interest. Posting is the main activity in purposive social support seeking, including both starting a new thread and asking under others’ thread of discussion. Second, users with these two behaviors expect diferent levels of support relevancy. When users seek social support through posting in a POHC, they lead the attention of the collective on a targeted topic and expect replies with high relevancy to their topic. However, support obtained from POHCs through an exploratory way needs to be accessed for its relevancy to the users’ special conditions. Third, exploratory social support seeking has a self-controlled process because users can scan as much content as they want to explore ways to address their needs. However, the process of purposive support seeking depends heavily on the participation and contribution of others. Fourth, the specificity of knowledge obtained through exploratory social support seeking and purposeful social support seeking is diferent. Support exploration in terms of browsing is a process of serendipitous discovery or just an increase in knowledge about topics of interest [27]. The findings may add on to the broad knowledge of the subject of matters. Purposeful support seeking increases knowledge on areas with narrow boundaries, and increases the specificity in the support expected. With the diferent characteristics of social support exploration and seeking strategies, we expect that they have varied efects on changing POHC user’s uncertainty in illness and that they are driven by diferent antecedents. In the subsequent subsection, we delve into uncertainty in the illness theory to identify the diferent components of uncertainty in illness and to determine how they are afected by social support exploration and seeking, respectively.

## 2.2. Uncertainty in illness theory

Uncertainty in illness theory is a mid-range theory, which suggests that patients require various interventions to adapt and cope with uncertainties [28]. Originally developed to explain the uncertainty of hospitalized patients, the theory indicates that uncertainty is an evolving process [29]. Social support is an important factor that influences the uncertainty of an individual indirectly and directly [28].

Indirectly, social support afects uncertainty in illness by changing the understanding of individuals about their symptom pattern and their knowledge about the illness [28]. Previous studies on cancer patients have asserted that they tend to use their fellow patients for social comparison when they have high extent of uncertainty on their own diseases [30]. The information from other patients with similar illness or undergoing similar treatment provide useful data and reference for patients to understand their own illness and improve their familiarity with the problem [31]. Emotional support also helps reduce anxiety [32] and appraise the patient’s illness rationally, thereby influencing individuals’ knowledge accumulation of their own illness. However, the theory also pointed out that the indirect efect is valid only when the knowledge based on the illness can be actually improved, implying the importance of support relevancy to individuals.

Directly, social support in the form of afirmation can influence uncertainty in illness [12,28]. Patients who look for ways to interpret their internal state may seek social support to afirm their beliefs about their illness [33], such as whether their symptoms formed a certain pattern. Social support enables patients to establish a dependency network. When others within this network actively share the preferred interpretation of uncertainty events, the personal constructed view of patients is confirmed, which helps reduce uncertainty in illness. A prior study suggests that when determining outcomes of individuals’ health information seeking behavior, the congruence between the information individuals want and what they obtain should be considered. Therefore, the outcomes of health information seeking behavior are more positive when individuals seek desired information [34,35]. Information seekers who receive the information they want report less anxiety than those who do not [35,36].

In the context of this study, we posit that social support seeking activities influence a POHC user’s uncertainty in illness both indirectly and directly. If the knowledge of users on a specific topic related to their illness is increased, then their uncertainty in illness will be reduced through an exploratory or purposeful social support seeking behavior. If users cannot obtain any information or reference to improve their understanding of their illness, but can find something that is consistent with their own interpretation of the symptom patterns and own judgment on the treatment then their uncertainty may also be reduced. In relation to the diferent characteristics of exploratory and purposeful social support seeking behaviors, this study will investigate how the indirect and direct efect of social support seeking on un certainty in illness occurs through the two behaviors.

Uncertainty in illness is a multidimensional construct that consists of diferent perspectives of an illness experience [10,37]. For POHC users, diagnosis and treatment are the two most important components of uncertainty in illness. A study found that 40 % of online health seekers use information to diagnose a health problem (diagnosis) and one in three attempted to treat health problems with online information (treatment) [38]. Uncertainty in diagnosis refers to the complexity and ambiguity of the treatment and care system, and in identifying the nature and cause of one’s health problem adequately (Mishel, 1985, [12]). Despite the distinct characteristics of diagnosis and treatment, few studies have separately investigated the change in uncertainty across diferent perspectives of an illness experience. In the following section, we diferentiate uncertainty in diagnosis and treatment, and explain the varying efects of exploratory and purposeful social support seeking behavior on uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment.

## 2.2.1. Social support seeking and change in uncertainty in diagnosis

Diagnosis, as a short form of medical diagnosis, refers to the determination of which disease or condition that explains individual symptoms and signs [39]. Diagnosis aims to understand the causes and natures of an illness and to classify the signs and symptoms of an individual into separate and distinct categories. Many people can describe the symptoms they experience, but may find it dificult to accurately diagnose their illness without suficient knowledge. Therefore, people have uncertainty in diagnosis when they are unsure about the causes of their illness or how to classify their symptoms with a disease category. Patients, including POHC users, would like to appraise the meaning of the somatic change they experience [40], and establish their own understanding of the origins and natures of their medical problems through self-diagnosis [41] because many illnesses reveal themselves symptomatically in their early stages. POHC is regarded as an important channel for patients to obtain support for self-diagnosis [42].

Purposeful social support seeking in POHCs is a process, which enables users to have a focused discussion about their concerns with other community members directly. Through starting a new thread of discussion or posting under other people’s threads, users engaging in purposeful social support seeking are leveraging the “digital brain” feature of POHCs to address their specific concern [6]. For users with uncertainty in diagnosis, seeking support directly from POHC members will attract more people with similar symptoms to provide relevant replies as references for self-diagnosis. Under such circumstances, users can improve their understanding on the causes and natures of their illness. In addition, according to uncertainty in illness theory, when the social network of an individual provides the preferred interpretation of uncertain events, individual uncertainty will be reduced [12]. When users look for support intentionally in a POHC, replies will also address their concerns directly and thus provide emotional support in terms of encouragement and assurance of the users’ personal interpretation of the illness [43]. For example, if a patient asks whether his symptoms are related to renal disease, and he receives the confirmation from other renal patients, he may be confident about the self-diagnosis outcome. Therefore, we hypothesize that the user’s perceived uncertainty in di agnosis can be reduced through purposeful social support seeking.

H1a. Purposeful social support seeking behavior will reduce uncertainty in diagnosis.

Users engaging in exploratory support seeking strategy in POHCs tend to browse contents broadly, hoping to find something useful. Browsing is casually looking through material generally with nothing specific in mind, and it only leads to the serendipitous discovery of social support [27]. Although information scanning is a behavior that can potentially enhance disease-related knowledge, the efectiveness of such behavior also depends on whether there is suficient knowledge from diferent information sources [44]. Even though the users knowledge base on a particular illness increases, they also have to assess the relevancy of knowledge for self-diagnosis. Research also points out that patients may get confused with more health-related information especially when they are contradictory [45]. In addition, because of the noninteractive nature of the support exploration process, the opportunity to receive support that confirms the users’ interpretation or reduces their anxiety is low [46]. For example, when users browse in POHCs to determine their problem, they may obtain various possible answers (e.g., a continuous headache can be caused by either insomnia or migraine) but with relatively low relevancy with their own situation. Information and experience shared by other POHC members are based on individual illness and experience, which are insuficient to form the rules to associate the symptoms of a reader with a certain disease type. Therefore, users may get more confused to understand a variety of possible illness categories to determine the best fit to their problem. In addition, users hardly receive any emotional support or assurance due to the low relevance of others’ posts. Therefore, we hypothesize that social support exploration may increase the possibility of having irrelevant or contradictory health information thus increase the dificulty to categorize an illness into a certain disease type; it also has a low chance of providing encouragement or an afirmative view to the interpretation of users.

H1b. Exploratory social support seeking will increase uncertainty in diagnosis.

## 2.2.2. Social support seeking and change in uncertainty in treatment

Treatment is the process of administering appropriate medical care to patients; and it is the attempted remediation of a health problem, following some formal or informal algorithmic guidelines [47]. The inputs to the algorithm and guidelines are highly individualized, and the regimens can be very complicated. Depending on diferent health conditions, treatments can be provided through diferent means, such as by drugs, by medical devices, or by counseling services. Even for the same type of medicine, the dosage needs to be adjusted for a patient over time. Therefore, an efective treatment involves continuous monitoring and adjustment [48], and the same treatment plan may work fine for some people but not for others.

People with uncertainty in treatment are concerned about the treatment options, the efectiveness of treatment in their own illness, and whether the treatment efect can be achieved by a predetermined time [11]. Prior research finds that patients’ decisions are more likely to be influenced by information received through purposeful seeking than through browsing [49]. In the context of this study, we posit that purposeful social support seeking can efectively reduce patients’ uncertainties in treatment through two ways. First, when users ask others to comment on their concern in treatment by posting in POHCs, they are likely to get replies from others undergoing the same treatment. Such replies can be with high relevancy to their own conditions. Second, users may also receive encouraging words to address their concerns or confirm their doubts in the treatment efects [3] through purposeful social support seeking. It also reduces users’ uncertainty in treatment. Thus, we hypothesize that users are likely to receive a direct reply and confirmation from others by purposeful social support seeking, which helps reduce their uncertainty in treatment.

H2a. Purposeful social support seeking will reduce uncertainty in treatment.

Through browsing broadly the contents of POCHs, patients with uncertainty in treatment may discover others’ experience of treating the same illness. As most POHC users do not have strong medical expertise to evaluate and apply the information received, the information shared by some users may not be appropriate to satisfy the needs of others [50]. For example, the treatment of obesity due to the overconsumption of food is diferent from treatment due to organic pathological changes. Given the complexity involved in health-related concerns, personal experience and description may only be applicable to one aspect of the illness. Contradictory feedback may also be found on the same treatment [51]. For example, a patient looking for a solution to a high cholesterol problem may find comments on the ineficiency of diet and exercise as a solution, but also see someone sharing their terrible experience from the side efects of cholesterol-lowering medication. The diversified and contradictory information obtained will increase their confusion and uncertainty about treatment options, because the interpretation of users on the treatment will not be confirmed by pure browsing in POHCs. We hypothesize that social support exploration will confuse the understanding of users on treatment options and does not ofer any direct afirmation to the judgment of users, and thus increase the uncertainty in treatment.

H2b. Exploratory social support seeking will increase uncertainty in treatment.

## 2.3. Antecedents of social support seeking in POHCs

Prior studies have investigated primarily the antecedents of different strategies to obtain social support in the ofline context. Individual characteristics, such as gender [52], personality [5], and rejection sensitivity [5], have been shown to influence the social support seeking strategy and frequency. Moreover, previous studies have identified that the belief of an individual toward support seeking, including the attitudes [53], perceived benefits, and costs [54] of support seeking, predicts the social support seeking behavior of an individual. Knowledge seeking literature has recognized personal factors (e.g., selfeficacy [55]) as important antecedents of the knowledge seeking intention of an individual. Factors related to online communities (e.g., collaborative norms [56], social network size [5], and resource-facilitating conditions [55]) were found to influence the knowledge-seeking behavior of an individual in such communities too. The value of in formation (e.g., perceived usability of information) is also considered an important predictor of knowledge-seeking behavior [57].

The above literature review suggests that the antecedents identified in prior literature could generally be grouped into three categories, namely, the support expected (personal belief toward support), community (factors related to online community), and users (personal characteristics). However, these antecedents do not adequately capture the unique traits of social support seeking in POHCs, or diferentiating support-seeking strategies. Hence, a framework that encompasses the important categories of social support/knowledge seeking and characteristics of online healthcare services are required to guide the antecedent selection of social support seeking in POHCs.

The behavioral model of Andersen for health service utilization [16] is an appropriate overarching lens to understand social support seeking in this context. This model was developed initially to understand the reasons why families use health services [58]; however, the unit of analysis was later shifted to an individual level [15]. This model suggests that the use of a health service includes three types of factors, namely, predisposing characteristics (characteristics and attitudes of an individual formed prior to health service utilization), enabling factors (individual and contextual conditions that foster or prohibit access to healthcare service), and illness level interpreted as the need for help of an individual [15]<sup>2</sup> . These factors are generally in line with the categories we identified for antecedents of social support seeking (i.e., personal characteristics, online community-related factor, and personal belief toward support seeking).

The behavioral model of health service utilization stipulates that predisposing characteristics consist of individual attitudes or characteristics that exist prior to the onset of specific episodes of illness or utilization of a health service [16]. The perceived information credibility of POHCs represents the extent to which one perceives the information to be believable [59,60] and predisposes the use of cyber healthcare service for social support seeking. Accuracy and perceived credibility of online health communities are pivotal in facilitating social relationships [61].

Given the asynchronous and ephemeral nature of online healthcare communities, the inaccurate information posted by an original member may not be corrected in a timely manner or may not even be amended by other users [19]. A study on an online eating disorder group revealed that more than 10 % of the information in that group was inaccurate and failed to adhere to medical or psychological standards; such inaccurate information was usually corrected one to two weeks after the original posts were made [62]. Online health community users are aware of such limitations and have shown concerns about information credibility [19]. In the e-commerce context, the perceived credibility of online reviews influences the purchase intention of users [63]. In the context of this research, the quality of information afects the attitude of health information users, as well as their intention toward health-related behaviors [64].

Users engaging in purposeful support seeking ask questions to other members in the POHCs directly with reference to their specific concerns. It happens when the users cannot find relevant information or meet their specific needs in the POHCs. Even the existing information are of high credibility, they may still have a low relevancy to users needs. Thus, purposeful support seeking behavior is not directly determined by the credibility of existing and ongoing discussion. The supportive atmosphere and interaction among users may set the expectations of users on the replies before they engage in purposeful so cial support seeking. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H3a. High perceived information credibility is not associated with frequent purposeful social support seeking.

When an individual browses posts and other contents in the POHCs, such as the personal experience or medical history of other users in POHCs, the credibility of this information is vital to the social support exploration activities of that individual. When individuals think that the content in a POHC is incomplete, wrong, or spurious, they will be less likely to read anything shared in that community [65]. The perceived information credibility predisposes the subsequent usage of the POHC through social support exploration. Correspondingly, if users believe that the information provided by a POHC is credible, they would browse and read information constantly from the community. Therefore, we predict that:

H3b. High perceived information credibility is associated with frequent exploratory social support seeking.

The enabling factors in Andersen’s behavioral model of health service utilization are individual or contextual conditions that either en: able or impede health service use [16]. The enabling factors allow an individual to act on a value or norm related to health service utilization [16]. However, some of the factors identified in prior literature (e.g., access to health insurance and resources) [15] are not significantly relevant to the use of POHCs. Family and community support are considered as enabling factors for health service utilization in the of line context [66].

Community support is particularly relevant for online communities. Researchers have specified that people’s behaviors are influenced by the social groups to which they belong to and from which they derive their social identity [67]. In the context of this study, most users have no prior relationship with other users, and their online behavior is likely to be influenced by other online users. By indicating how well one is identified with online communities [68], social identity promotes a norm of social support that can either enable or impede the unitization of online health services. Considering that most online discussions occur between people with similar health problems, a strong sense of community can help users ensure community support and is therefore important to its members [69].

Social identity is the “part of the individuals’ self-concept, which derives from their knowledge of their membership of a social group (or groups) together with the value and emotional significance of that membership” [70], p255. When individuals perceive themselves to be members of a social category, they will be emotionally involved in this common definition of themselves and reach a certain degree of consensus on how to evaluate the group and the associated membership [71]. Social identity explains the role of the individual-community relationship in the participation process and emphasizes the importance of group membership to one’s self-concept and subsequent behavioral intention [72]. In the context of online communities, social identity reflects the identification of members and their sense of belonging to a community. Such identity enables them to participate continuously in online community activities [67,68]. Social identity is also found to influence intentions of membership continuance in online health communities [73].

High social identity with a community implies that an individual is aware of the similarities among members [74,75], and understands that the POHC is a proper channel for acquiring relevant support. Researchers have also suggested that seeking social support can be influenced by the relational characteristics of potential support providers (e.g., knowledge on the expertise of information providers and feelings on the potential value of the information received) [76]. If individuals want to turn to their social network for social support [77], they will also evaluate whether other people in the network are warm and empathetic [78]. High social identity encourages individuals to associate positive feelings (e.g., trustworthiness) toward others from the same group [79]. Therefore, an individual associates positive feelings with a POHC and expects interaction from the members if they highly identify themselves with such a community [80]. High social identity also implies frequent cognitive access of community membership [81] (i.e., high social identity with a POHC implies that the community membership can be thought of often by users whenever they need support). Thus, users are likely to look for social support in a POHC frequently if they highly identify with the community. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H4a. High level social identity is associated with frequent purposeful social support seeking.

H4b. High level social identity is associated with frequent exploratory social support seeking.

The need to utilize health services [81] usually refers to the general health, functional state, and illness symptoms of people [15]. Considering that certain POHC users are concerned with subhealth pro blems and do not have specific diagnosis on their illness, we use perceived health severity, which denotes one’s conviction about the seriousness of a given health problem [82], to capture the need factor.

Perceived severity is a major concept in several health behavior theories, including the Health Belief Model (HBM) and Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) [83]. HBM asserts that perceived disease severity forms a perceived threat and influences how people process health information, as well as their decision on whether to perform a health-related behavior [84,85]. Similarly, PMT predicts that people with high perceived disease severity will engage in activities to reduce their fears [83]. The perceived health severity level may also play an important role in influencing the behavior of people to address a health problem. A POHC gathers people with similar concerns and contains more relevant information than the results obtained from a search engine. Users with high severity may like to read the discussion from similar others, seek opinions from them, and leverage their collective intelligence for decision-making. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H5a. High level perceived health severity is associated with purposeful social support seeking.

H5b. High level perceived health severity is associated with exploratory social support seeking.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Research background and sampling strategy

The proposed model is validated through a two-stage survey conducted among POHC users in China, where three POHCs satisfied the sampling criteria of this research and were selected for analysis. These criteria are specified in the subsequent paragraph. First, selected com munities should cover major types of POHCs. In this study, we selected two established online sources in which communities often reside in: online patient support group with sections dedicated to diferent health concerns and general online community with the health section as one of its subsections. The selection should have the common features of online communities. For example, the user profile is visible to all registered members, who can initiate threads or leave comments in all forums, and popular posts are highlighted by moderators. Second, the topics and discussions in the communities should be patient-centric, and no medical professional should serve as moderator or participant. Third, POHCs should not have any feature to help users assess content credibility. A few POHCs satisfied the abovementioned criteria. Accordingly, we approached the managers of these communities to seek permission to send private messages to their users in a massive approach. However, only three communities one online patient support group with six subsections dedicated to diferent types of health concerns and two health forums in general online communities with at least 2000 registered members acceded to our request.

## 3.2. Scale development and conceptual validation

Existing validated scales for survey instrument development were adopted wherever possible. The survey questions were also adjusted to reflect the specific context of POHCs. Consistent with previous literature [68], social identity was modeled as a second-order construct with in-group identification, group self-esteem, and afective commitment as first-order constructs. Items for social identity were adapted from Tsai and Bagozzi [68]. The scales for uncertainties in diagnosis and in treatment were adapted from Mishel [10]. The original scales were developed for hospitalized patients. We rephrased certain items and removed several items to make them relevant to POHC users who may have diferent types of health concerns and who may not have consulted doctors. For example, “It is vague to me how I will manage after I leave the hospital” is removed because many POHC users do not have any hospitalization experience. Changes in uncertainties in diagnosis and in treatment were calculated based on the uncertainty score collected at Time 2 (T2) minus the uncertainty score collected at Time 1 (T1). All these constructs were operationalized as reflective constructs using a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree).

Items for exploratory and purposeful social support seeking captured members’ frequency of browsing and posting activities to obtain social support within the time frame of the two surveys. Basic types of support related to POHC users were identified, including informational, emotional, coping illness, and medical knowledge support. Frequency for each activity was reported by a seven-point Likert scale, with 1 representing no such activity at all and 7 representing very frequent activities.

For control variables, we measured gender and age using diferent categories. Privacy concern was captured by the extent to which people are concerned for their privacy when using technologies [86]. Health concern was reported by respondents and further coded as a type of health concern. We employed two research assistants to code health concern into two types: subhealth problems versus disease management. Subhealth is a state between health and disease. For instance, all medical tests show negative results, but the person experiences discomfort and even pain [87]. Some concerns include fatigue, insomnia, and headache. Members with symptoms that interrupt their daily life were coded as disease management. The type of community (i.e., online patient support group and health forum) was operationalized as a binary variable. The summary of the construct operationalization can be found in Table 2.

The two sets of questionnaires for T1 and T2 were first created in English and reviewed for content validity by a group of POHC users and IS academics. The English questionnaires were translated into Chinese and then back to English to ensure translation equivalence [91]. The questionnaires were given in Chinese. Two bilingual researchers, who are native speakers of both English and Chinese, independently translated the original items from English into Chinese. They analyzed the independently translated Chinese versions of the items and agreed on the final version of the questionnaires. The questionnaires were validated further by two native Chinese speakers with no knowledge of English, and they were then translated back into English by another bilingual researcher to confirm translation equivalence. The questionnaires were pilot tested among a group of 20 POHC users, who were not included in the main survey.

## 4. Data analysis and results

## 4.1. Instrument validation

For instrument validation, we assessed the reflective constructs for their convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity is established by examining the composite reliability (CR), Cronbach’s α, and average variance extracted (AVE). CR and Cronbach’s α for all constructs in Table 4 exceeded 0.7. Thus, each test result met its threshold criterion, thereby supporting the convergent validity for the constructs. The AVE for each construct was greater than 0.5 (Table 5).

Table 4 also shows all items loaded on their own constructs and loaded less on other constructs. Another method of assessing discriminant validity of the reflective construct is by comparing the square root of AVE for each reflective construct with correlations between the reflective and other constructs. A square root of AVE greater than the correlations between the reflective and other constructs indicates discriminant validity [92].

As shown in Table 5, the square root of AVE for each construct exceeded the correlations between the reflective and other constructs, meeting the requirement for discriminant validity. The test for the validity of social identity as a second-order construct showed satisfactory results (Appendix A).

## 4.2. Hypotheses testing

Hypotheses testing using seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) was conducted after establishing the validity of the survey instrument to account for the theoretically correlated nature of two mediators (ex ploratory and purposeful social support seeking) and two dependent constructs (change in uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment). SUR employs feasible generalized least squares techniques to estimate coeficients of multiple linear equations while accounting for correlated errors [93]. Thus, SUR is considered to be more appropriate than Partial Least Squares (PLS) in testing the proposed hypotheses in this study. Path coeficients and test significance are reported in Table 6.

All hypotheses tests were assessed at 5 % level of significance. Table 6 presents the results of the SUR analyses for the two models: control and full models (i.e., control and theoretical variables). The Breusch-Pagan test of independence was used to assess the extent of correlation between the residuals of the two equations [94]. The test results for all four models indicate that residuals are not independent, thereby supporting the necessity of using SUR. For the model testing of the antecedents of exploratory social support seeking, an R<sup>2</sup> value of 6.2 % was obtained for the control model and 22.2 % for the full model. For the model testing of the antecedents of purposeful social support seeking, 14.7 % was obtained for the control model and 24.9 % for the full model. For the change in uncertainty in diagnosis model, an $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ value of 9.1 % was obtained for the control model and 15.7 % for the full model. For the change in uncertainty in treatment model, 6.0 % was obtained for the control model and 11.3 % for the full model.

Social identity predicts purposeful (path coeficient = 0.282) and exploratory social support seeking behavior (path coeficient = 0.195) significantly. Perceived information credibility also has a significant efect on exploratory social support seeking as predicted (path coeficient = 0.259). Perceived health severity only influences purposeful social support seeking behavior (path coeficient = 0.140), but not exploratory social support seeking behavior (path coeficient = 0.045). As predicted, purposeful social support seeking behavior leads to a negative change in uncertainty in diagnosis (path coeficient = −0.218). By contrast, exploratory social support seeking positively afects the change in uncertainty in diagnosis (path coeficient = 0.302) and change in uncertainty in treatment (path coeficient = 0.274).

## 4.3. Common method bias

Although this study uses self-reported data, data were collected through two rounds of surveys conducted at diferent times to alleviate concerns on common method bias. We also applied Harmon one-factor test to further assess the severity of common method bias [95]. The result shows that the model explains 21.3 % variance, which is less than 50 %. Thus, common method bias is less of a concern for the study.

## 5. Discussion of findings

This research examines social support seeking in POHCs by 1) dif ferentiating the various efects of purposeful and exploratory social support seeking on the changes in uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment and 2) investigating the antecedents of social support seeking behavior in POHCs. Eight out of the ten hypotheses are supported with the results from a longitudinal survey.

With empirical findings supporting H1a but not 2a, the study reveals the efect of purposeful social support seeking on the change in uncertainty in diagnosis only. The POHCs are efective in helping users with self-diagnosis through seeking support from other community members. As uncertainty in diagnosis is usually related to the dificulty in understanding the causes and natures of an illness, posting in POHC is a way to ask for direct support that can be used as reference of users to improve their understanding on the cause of the illness, or reducing the uncertainty through confirmation of users’ own interpretation of the illness. However, the support related to treatment may require professional knowledge to comprehend or apply, thus purposeful social sup port seeking may not have direct impact on uncertainty in treatment.

Exploratory social support seeking behavior increases users’ uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment, which support H1b and H2b. Although information browsing, in general, has been found to improve the patients’ general knowledge base of a certain illness [23], users’ benefit mostly from improved understanding on what screening test to do [49]. Our study further found that social support exploration in POHCs do not reduce users’ uncertainty in diagnosis or treatment. Seeking support through browsing may increase the diversified information consumption and apprehension, making it dificult for users to judge the relevancy of the information obtained. A study on POHC revealed that “people tend to have a dificult time filtering out in formation and being objective about them” [47]. Previous research has stated that information seekers experience negative outcomes, such as feeling overwhelmed or worried [94,96]. In addition, users can hardly obtain emotional support due to the noninteractive nature of social support exploration activities. Thus, when users just browse contents on POHCs, they will experience increased uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment.

As hypothesized, perceived information credibility of a POHC motivates the subsequent social support exploration behavior of users (H3b) but not purposeful social support-seeking behavior (H3a). The former is more efective in obtaining informational support due to the noninteractive nature of social support seeking. Thus, information credibility is particularly important for support exploration when users don’t have any specific goals to achieve. Purposeful social supportseeking behavior depends on the supportive culture of the community, as users are expecting replies and answers from other members.

Social identity positively afects exploratory and purposeful social support-seeking behaviors, which support H4a and H4b. Previous research has shown that social identity explains how well a person is involved with a group and that high social identification with a group leads a person to become active in group activities [72]. Thus, our finding further extends the findings in the POHC context. In addition, the study finds that, reflecting the mutual support norm of the community, social identity with the POHC drives both types of supportseeking behavior of users.

The perceived health severity of users influences purposeful social support-seeking behavior (H5a) but not exploratory social supportseeking behavior (H5b). Previous studies have suggested that disease severity influences the need for disease-related information [97]. Users with a severity health problem prefer a prompt feedback from the collective brain of community members. Having a specific goal or need in mind, people with severe diseases do not have the patience of browsing posts and relying on the serendipitous discovery of knowledge to cope with their uncertainty.

In summary, the findings demonstrate that the social supportseeking behaviors of POHC members are afected by various factors, including social identity, perceived information credibility, and severity of health problems. However, exploratory and purposeful seeking behaviors afect the changes in uncertainties in diagnosis and in treatment in diferent ways. The theoretical and practical implications of the findings are discussed in the next sections.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

The study conceptually distinguishes exploratory and purposefu social support seeking in POHCs and develops a research model to explore the distinct antecedents and efects of these behaviors using a two-stage survey design. The study makes several contributions to the POHCs literature, uncertainty in illness theory, and social supportseeking literature.

This study contributes to the emerging POHCs research [2,98–101] by theoretically distinguishing exploratory and purposeful social support-seeking behavior. The study also theoretically proposed and empirically tested the diferent antecedents and consequences of the two behaviors to further highlight the diference between the two social support seeking behaviors. The study also improves the understanding of the mixed findings on the efectiveness of social support seeking in POHCs by identifying two outcome variables. The study adds on to the literature by revealing that purposeful and exploratory support-seeking behavior have an opposite efect in changing patients’ uncertainty in diagnosis and treatment.

This study also adds to the uncertainty in illness theory [10,11] by applying it to an online healthcare community setting, reflecting the rapid growth of a new form of patient-centric healthcare in the digital era [102]. This study synthesizes the literature on the antecedents of social support and knowledge seeking by summarizing the previously identified antecedents into three categories: seeking content, community, and users. Drawing on insights from previous literature, Andersen’s behavioral model for health service utilization is applied as the overarching lens to select appropriate antecedents for social support seeking in POHCs. The findings of this study verify the distinct importance of social identity, information credibility, and health severity in promoting exploratory and purposeful social support-seeking behavior in POHCs.

## 5.2. Practical contributions

The emergence of various forms of POHCs has attracted many individuals with health concerns to look for online social support and transformed the traditional physician-dominated healthcare to a patient-centric healthcare. It is usually assumed that users can benefit from participating in POHCs, and thus prior research focuses more on the types of social support exchanged. However, a clear understanding of how POHCs are used and how users can benefit from them remain lacking. Although research has suggested that users can benefit from seeking social support from POHCs [2,3], others argue that asking people outside the medical community for health support carries its own sets of risks, and collective problem solving from users of POHCs may not lead to sound medical advice [103]. Thus, strategic guidelines on how POHCs should be operated efectively are likewise lacking. Our study provides important implications on the practice of POHCs.

First, exploratory and purposeful social support seeking behaviors can induce distinct consequences on the uncertainty reduction of users in diagnosis and in treatment. The finding of the study demonstrates that pure browsing in POHCs is not able to reduce users’ uncertainty in illness, and purposeful social support seeking only reduces users’ uncertainty in diagnosis. Practitioners must be mindful of such diference and encourage direct social support seeking in POHCs so that users can obtain prompt and relevant replies.

Second, community managers should be aware that purposeful social support seeking reduces uncertainty in diagnosis but not in treatment. They are suggested to remind users constantly that treatment is a highly individualized medical plan, and they should interpret the replies of others carefully. Moreover, they can include more features to remind users that generalizing experiences from a small sample size is risky. Clinicians warned that the messages recommending drugs or mentioning dosages in an online community are “red flag” messages [48], and they should be monitored by the community managers.

Third, perceived health severity enhances purposeful social support seeking. Thus, special attention and care can be given to users with severe health concerns. Mechanisms can be deployed to detect health severity. For example, data mining techniques can be applied to posts or messages to detect users who are quite concerned with their illnesses. Posts or messages from those users can be highlighted in a certain manner to attract the attention of other users in the community.

Also, the finding that high social identity encourages both social support seeking activities suggest that community managers should focus on increasing user identification with POHCs and enabling users to share their feelings and experiences freely in the community. For example, community managers can promote the culture of caring and sharing among POHC users, encourage users to reply to the posts of others and help one another. They can also manage a good community brand image to enhance the evaluative social identity of users in the community.

Lastly, with the increasing popularity of online patient support groups, many patients treat the group as a collective digital brain for decision-making. However, it has been reported that there are patients who followed the wrong information from health forums and made wrong treatment decisions, which caused the death of patients [104]. Our study also found that patients’ uncertainty in treatments are increased even as they ask for social support directly from POHCs. Thus, public health agencies should educate citizens to seek professional opinions regarding medical problems especially relating to treatment decisions. They should interpret the information obtained from online sources carefully.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This study has a few limitations, which open opportunities for future research. First, members from only a few active communities were surveyed because of permission issues. Hence, other findings reported here may not be extended to all community settings. Future research can examine the influence of diferent community settings, such as whether the results of this study can be applied for disease-specific POHCs $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ for cancer support and chronic disease management). Second, our measurement of social support-seeking behavior is selfreported frequency during the surveyed period. Future research can support our findings with archival data, such as logged activities of members. Content analysis can also be adopted to assess diferent be havior types. Third, searching is also a common social support-seeking behavior in POHCs. Future studies can investigate the role of social support seeking through searching.

## 6. Conclusion

The study investigated the varying efects of exploratory and purposeful social support seeking in POHCs on users’ change in uncertainty in illness. Antecedents of social support-seeking behavior in POHCs were also examined. Our study synthesized and extended the literature on online healthcare communities, uncertainty in illness theory, and

We theoretically conceptualized and empirically tested the efects of exploratory and purposeful social support seeking in POHCs on the users’ uncertainty in illness.
