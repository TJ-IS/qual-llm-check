# PEER INFLUENCE IN THE WORKPLACE: EVIDENCE FROM AN ENTERPRISE DIGITAL PLATFORM<sup>1</sup>

Haoyuan Liu Division of Information Technology & Operations Management, Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, SINGAPORE {haoyuan.liu@ntu.edu.sg}

Wen Wen and Andrew B. Whinston

Department of Information, Risk & Operations Management, McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin Austin, TX, U.S.A. {wen.wen@mccombs.utexas.edu} {abwhins@gmail.com}

Stephen He Department of Marketing, Alvarez College of Business, University of Texas at San Antonio San Antonio, TX, U.S.A {stephen.he@utsa.edu}

We investigate how the sharing of peer success on an enterprise digital platform influences other workers’ work effort. We focus on one important advantage that organizations gain from using digital platforms— the ability to regulate the content of the messages by incorporating various elements about peers. Specifically, we examined two types of peer success messages: messages that highlight a peer’s effort (i.e., effort-focused success messages) and messages that highlight a peer’s ability (i.e., ability-focused success messages). Using data from a group of sales workers in an information technology service company, as well as an experiment with online participants, we found that both ability-focused success messages and effort-focused success messages can motivate workers to work harder, although we observed important heterogeneity in the responses. In particular, workers’ responses to effort-focused messages remained strong regardless of social distance between the worker and the peer. In contrast, workers’ response to ability-focused messages was stronger when the messages praised the ability of peers who were socially close but not that of peers who were socially distant.

Keywords: Work effort, peer success, peer influence, peer ability, peer effort, online organizational communication, social distance

## Introduction

An important theme in information systems (IS) research involves examining the interplay between organizational communication, facilitated by digital platforms, and worker behavior (e.g., Sproull & Kiesler, 1986). Recent studies have explored this area extensively from the perspective of employees and their social networks on digital platforms. These studies have examined the effects of social interactions among workers on the use of enterprise systems (e.g., Wattal et al., 2010; Sasidharan et al., 2012), on collective task completion (e.g., Subramaniam et al. 2013), and on worker performance (e.g., Wu, 2013; Zhang & Venkatesh, 2013; Sykes et al., 2014). However, they have not yet examined how peer information shared via digital platforms affects workers. As more organizations support remote work, understanding and leveraging enterprise digital platforms to promote productivity among a distributed workforce becomes crucial.

Extant research has demonstrated that the presence of productive coworkers can affect workers’ productivity in physical environments, where workers can observe each other’s behavior (e.g., Bandiera et al., 2005; Bandiera et al., 2010; Mas & Moretti, 2009; Cornelissen et al., 2017).

However, a separate body of literature has demonstrated differences in the efficacy of offline versus online communication (e.g., Burke et al., 1999), which raises questions about how the presence of productive coworkers online affects other coworkers’ offline productivity.

Building on these ideas, we explore how sharing information about highly productive peers (i.e., “peer success” information) on an enterprise digital platform can influence the work effort of other workers. We focus on one important advantage of using digital platforms: Information sharing on digital platforms is deliberate and documented, enabling organizations to manage communication content. To illustrate, instead of noting workers’ successes on leaderboards, organizations could incorporate various elements into success-sharing messages, such as praising workers’ effort or ability. Our study specifically examines two issues: whether success messages that praise peers for their effort (labeled as effort-focused success messages) and success messages that praise peers for their ability (labeled as ability-focused success messages) both motivate workers to work harder and, if so, whether they motivate all workers or specific individuals.

We propose that success messages highlighting either peers’ effort or peers’ ability can make workers perceive success as more attainable if they perceive the messages to be relevant to their own success; such greater perceived attainability of success could motivate workers to work harder (compared to a scenario without such messages). Because most people perceive effort as a controllable and actionable factor contributing to success, workers may find effort-focused messages relevant to themselves, regardless of their social distance from the praised peer, and thus be motivated by them. In contrast, because most people perceive ability as uncontrollable and unchangeable (at least in the short term), workers may find ability-focused messages relevant and thus be motivated by them only when they are socially close to the praised peer and thus perceive that they have similar abilities as the peer.

We tested these predictions using a combination of both observational data and experimental data. Our observational dataset consisted of detailed information on a sample of sales workers in a software-as-a-service (SaaS) company based in China. All sales workers were required to install an app that included a branch-level chat group for workers in each branch and one human resources representative (HR rep) from the company’s headquarters. Whenever a sales worker closed a new deal, the HR rep shared the news with all sales workers in their designated branch via the branch-level chat group, regardless of the branch with which the successful worker was affiliated.

To investigate the effect of peer success messages on the effort of sales workers in closing deals, we used the sudden resignation of existing HR reps and the arrival of new ones as exogenous shocks to the sharing of peer success (because only the HR reps shared such messages). We found that the effect of effort-focused success messages was consistent across varying social distances; an increase of 10 percentage points in intensity (i.e. percent of success messages that focused on peer effort) led to workers’ making an average of 1.1 more phone calls per day for socially close peers’ success messages and 0.8 more phone calls per day for socially distant peers’ success messages. The difference between the effects of messages on socially close vs. socially distant peers was statistically insignificant. However, an increase of 10 percentage points in the intensity of ability-focused success messages on socially close peers led to an average of 1.3 more phone calls per worker per day, but ability-focused success messages regarding socially distant peers had little impact on workers’ effort. The difference between the two was statistically significant. We conducted various robustness checks to rule out alternative explanations and to test the robustness of these results.

We also conducted an experiment with online participants who were employed when the study took place. Participants were randomly assigned to one of five between-subjects conditions: no-message-control, effort-message-close-peer, effort-messagedistant-peer, ability-message-close-peer, ability-message-distantpeer. Consistent with our findings from the observational data, experimental results show that participants in both the effortmessage conditions and the ability-message conditions planned to work harder than participants in the no-message-control condition. Moreover, results show no significant difference between the effort-message-close-peer condition and the effortmessage-distant-peer condition, but participants in the abilitymessage-close-peer condition planned to work significantly harder than those in the ability-message-distant-peer condition. We also found evidence consistent with the argument that peer success messages, when perceived as relevant to workers’ own success, can increase the perceived likelihood of their own success and ultimately result in greater work effort.

This study contributes to several streams of literature. First, it relates to several IS studies that have investigated how social networks among workers shape job performance (e.g., Wu, 2013; Zhang & Venkatesh, 2013; Sykes et al., 2014). Our study differs from these prior works in important ways. In contrast to their focus on network structures among coworkers, our study seeks to explore peer influence resulting from the virtual presence of productive peers. We demonstrate that embedding various types of information about peers into the information-sharing process generates heterogeneous peer effects.

Second, our paper contributes to the existing literature on social influence in the IS field (e.g., Aral & Walker, 2011; Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Rishika & Ramaprasad, 2019), particularly regarding how the content of broadcasted messages can influence behavior (e.g., Jung et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020). Although previous studies have explored how peers influence each other’s decision-making processes, evidence of how peer information on enterprise digital platforms can shape worker behavior is lacking. This area of research poses several challenges, including difficulties in obtaining and documenting peer interactions at the worker level and in overcoming endogeneity issues. To address these challenges, we drew on a dataset that provides a granular view of communication patterns and worker behavior in an organization; we also used exogenous shocks to the sharing of peer information to identify influences on workers’ behavior.

Third, our study adds to a limited body of literature that examines the effects of public relative performance feedback (RPF) and leaderboards on worker productivity (e.g., Hannan et al., 2008; Azmat & Iriberri, 2016; Song et al., 2017). Past research has focused on comparing public and private RPF (e.g., Hannan et al., 2008; Tafkov, 2012) or the influence of public RPF via knowledge transfer (e.g., Song et al., 2017). In contrast, we investigated a novel setting where successful (but not unsuccessful) workers’ performance was revealed to their peers. Unlike most RPF and leaderboard studies, which provide only simple rankings, we analyzed the specific content of peer success messages. This approach allowed us to pinpoint ways in which firms can harness enterprise digital platforms to amplify their messaging and achieve desired outcomes.

Fourth, our research is part of a broader body of labor economics studies exploring the link between peer effects and productivity, including notable works by Bandiera et al. (2005, 2010), Mas and Moretti (2009), and Cornelissen et al. (2017). As noted earlier, these studies have largely focused on peer influence in offline settings, while we investigate how online peer influence affects offline activities. More importantly, we demonstrate that success messages that praise the effort of socially distant peers can still have an effect even on distributed workers, in contrast to the highly localized effect observed in most offline settings.

## Theoretical Motivation

Messages that praise successful individuals can vary widely in content. In this paper, we are particularly interested in two types of success messages identified by research in social psychology: messages praising an individual’s effort and messages praising an individual’s ability (Dugan, 1989; Dweck, 2000; Weiner et al., 1972). Previous research has delved into how praising an individual using distinct message types influences their behavior, showcasing different psychological and behavioral consequences (Weiner, 1985). Building on this literature, we investigate how praising peer workers using certain types of messages influences other workers’ behavior in the workplace. From the perspective of a worker, when a peer worker achieves success and receives praise (e.g., for the worker’s effort or ability), does the worker receiving the peer success message work harder compared to a scenario without such messages?

Because our research examines workers whose peers receive praise, it adds a social aspect to the original paradigm. Consequently, the effects of success messages on workers likely stem from both the message content and how the worker perceives their relationship with the peer. In this paper, we explore one element of such relationships: social distance. Social distance represents the subjective feeling of distance between individuals (Liberman et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2023). Greater relationship ties or attribute similarities between two individuals tend to reduce perceived social distance. Friends are closer than strangers, persons with more characteristics in common are closer than persons with fewer characteristics in common (Liviatan et al., 2008; Tesser, 1988). More importantly, if two individuals perceive themselves as socially close, they tend to infer even more shared attributes. For instance, people often perceive others with whom they frequently interact or those who share certain characteristics as socially closer peers (Cialdini & De Nicholas, 1989; Finch & Cialdini, 1989). This social closeness can subsequently result in inferring greater similarity in other aspects (e.g., task performance) between the two individuals (Brown et al. 1992; Mussweiler et al., 2004).

In the subsequent sections, we explore the two types of messages that are used to share peer success (effort-focused and ability-focused messages), their effects on the worker, and the potential role of social distance in shaping the worker’s response to the messages.

## Effort-Focused Peer Success Messages

The messages used to praise a successful individual wield significant influence over how success is attributed (Mueller & Dweck 1998; Leung et al. 2020). When a peer’s success is praised in a way that emphasizes effort in the message, the message encourages workers to attribute the peer’s success to effort.

Effort is widely believed to be a controllable and actionable factor contributing to success (e.g., Haimovitz & Corpus, 2011; Klein & O’Brien, 2017). Thus, workers would perceive a message that highlights the role of effort in achieving success as relevant and personally relatable. In other words, regardless of individual characteristics, workers generally believe that exerting effort is a pathway to attaining success. This perception of the message’s relevance would hold, regardless of the social distance between the worker and the praised peer. For example, students in one study identified renowned scientists (e.g., Albert Einstein) as more inspiring when the scientists had achieved eminence through their concerted effort (Lin-Siegler et al., 2016). Notably, despite perceiving renowned scientists like Einstein as extremely socially distant, the students still considered the scientists’ effort-induced success to be relevant to their own pursuit of success and believed that exerting effort would similarly enhance their own likelihood of success. Thus, when workers receive messages emphasizing success through effort, they will tend to find these messages relevant, which in turn will lead them to perceive success as more attainable, compared to a scenario without such messages.

Extensive literature has indicated that perceiving success as attainable serves as a powerful motivator for hard work. Individuals often exhibit heightened dedication when they perceive that the goals, they pursue are more achievable (Carver et al., 1979; Huang et al., 2017). As a result, we expect such a strengthened belief in attaining success to lead to increased work effort, aligning with findings in the existing literature (Chowdhury, 1993; Chung et al., 2021).

## Ability-Focused Peer Success Messages

While success messages praising a peer’s effort cause workers to attribute the success to the peer’s effort, ability-focused messages often lead workers to attribute success to the peer’s ability (Schunk, 1983). In contrast to effort, people often perceive ability to be uncontrollable and unchangeable, at least in the short run. Existing literature suggests that workers’ lack of a specific ability cannot be easily addressed by acquiring it quickly or substituting it with another ability (Miller et al., 1975; Schunk, 1983; Weiner et al., 1971). Because workers may have abilities that are distinct from those of a praised peer, they may not always perceive messages emphasizing the role of ability in achieving success as relevant and personally relatable.

Still, ability-focused messages may resonate with workers under certain circumstances, such as when a worker is socially close to a praised peer. As noted, socially close individuals tend to perceive each other as similar in various traits and abilities (Brown et al., 1992; Mussweiler et al., 2004), regardless of the accuracy of these perceptions (Tesser & Campbell, 1982). Following this line of reasoning, workers who are socially close to a praised peer will tend to view themselves as possessing similar abilities. Therefore, workers may find messages emphasizing ability-induced success relevant to their own pursuit of success. That is, if they believe they share similar abilities, workers who are socially close to a praised peer may perceive success as more attainable when exposed to ability-focused messages, compared to a scenario without such messages.

A strengthened belief in their capacity to attain success may, as previously mentioned, lead to workers’ increased work effort (Chowdhury, 1993; Chung et al., 2021). Hence, we posit that after receiving messages that highlight abilityinduced success about a socially close peer, workers will be more likely to perceive success as more attainable and thus exert more effort.

In contrast, when workers are socially distant from the peer receiving praise for ability, they are less likely to perceive themselves as having similar abilities as the peer being praised (Brewer & Weber, 1994; Cialdini et al., 1976). Because similar abilities are difficult to acquire in the short term, workers may not find such messages relevant to their own success. Thus, success messages praising the ability of a socially distant peer may not significantly affect workers perception of the attainability of their own success. Consequently, they may not be incentivized to exert more work effort.

## Empirical Investigation Based on Observational Data

## Empirical Setting

Our observational data were collected from a software company that provides SaaS solutions to the retail industry in China. It has 28 branches across different regions in China and about 340 active, full-time sales workers. Because these sales workers spend most of their time in the field and have limited offline interactions, they are likely to respond to online information sharing about productive peers. (See the next paragraph for details.) Since they work in their own geographic territories and are paid based on their individual sales performance, competition has minimal influence on how peer success affects individual workers. A common approach for sales workers to sell products is to contact potential customers through phone calls and, if a potential customer is interested, to visit the customer. The sales job is very challenging and requires both effort and luck to close a deal. Each sales worker in this company closes, on average, 0.56 deals per month.

The company requires all sales workers to use a mobile app for customer information management, task management, and communication. This app not only supports one-to-one realtime communication but also has a group chat feature that allows many-to-many real-time communication. The chat groups are created based on the company’s organizational structure. Each branch, led by the branch manager, has a separate chat group. Within each branch are several teams, and each team is led by a team manager. All sales workers and their team managers in a branch are included in their respective branch-level chat groups. In the branch-level chat group, sales workers exchange information with each other about customers, progress reports, and meeting schedules.

The company’s headquarters (in Beijing) has a group of HR reps. Their major responsibilities include maintaining the company-wide work structure (e.g., updating job descriptions for all positions), hosting various events (e.g., training, retreats, and contests), and documenting employee information (e.g., transfers and layoffs). Because some of these duties involve interactions with local sales workers, each HR rep is assigned to one specific branch. Since HR reps are physically located in Beijing and rarely travel to their designated branch, their communication with local salespeople happens primarily through the app within the branch-level chat group. For instance, when a new sales training opportunity is available, they announce this event in the chat group; if someone is interested, HR reps may help set up the training.

A distinctive feature of our research setting is that, in this company, HR reps share almost all of the news about recently closed deals. Section A in our transparency materials (TM)<sup>2</sup> illustrates the exact process through which HR reps share peer success messages and provides a few examples of success messages normally seen in the branch-level chat groups. One important question is whether an HR rep might share success messages based only on specific branch performance measures. We believe such selective sharing is unlikely for the following reasons. HR reps are involved in many company-wide and headquarter-specific tasks, and sharing peer success represents only a small fraction of their workload. They earn a fixed salary, which is not related to their interactions with local branches. The schedules and availability of HR reps are idiosyncratic, and they may notice only some of the success messages shared in the HR chat group, resulting in variations in the messages that each branch receives. We further examined the correlation between sales workers’ daily activities in the preceding week and the various messages sent by HR reps in the following week using data from all branches during the pre-HR-rep-resignation period. We found that, despite a strong correlation among different worker activities, such activities had little correlation with HR reps’ sharing activities.

## Baseline Empirical Models

In the baseline models, we leveraged the sudden resignation of existing HR reps and the arrival of new HR reps during our sample period as exogenous shocks to the sharing of peer success. More specifically, our sample covered nine branches from April 5, 2017, to June 14, 2017. During this period, four branches experienced the unexpected resignation of their HR reps. They also experienced different arrival times for new HR reps. During the period following the resignation of old HR reps and before the arrival of the new ones, workers in the four branches stopped receiving messages from HR reps, including messages about their peers’ success, although they could still communicate with each other in the branch-level chat groups. After the arrival of new HR reps, the sharing of peer successes then resumed. Table 1 shows the regions and cities where the four branches affected by the HR rep changes were located, as well as the timing of both the departure of the old HR rep and the arrival of the new HR rep.

In the baseline model, the treatment is the sharing of peer success messages by the new HR rep; thus, the treatment group included branches that first did not receive any peer success messages (because of the absence of the HR rep) and then began receiving them after the arrival of the new HR rep. An ideal control group would include workers in branches that shared similar characteristics with workers in the treatment group but did not receive any peer success messages for both pre- and post-treatment periods. To identify the control group, we leveraged the fact that the four branches faced different timing for the departure and arrival of the HR reps. In particular, as shown in Table 1, the period during which Chongqing did not receive any peer success messages was from May 3, 2017, to May 8, 2017, and for Nanjing, the period was from May 3, 2017, to May 14, 2017. After May 8 and May 14, respectively, they started to receive messages again. The period during which Shenyang did not receive any peer success messages was from April 28, 2017, to June 7, 2017. As a result, for the period from May 3, 2017, to June 7, 2017, we were able to use Chongqing and Nanjing as the treatment sample and Shenyang as the control sample. One advantage of this identification strategy is that both the treatment sample and the control sample faced a similar likelihood of “treatment,” except for the difference in the timing of treatment.

However, one important issue is whether the timing of the new HR rep’s arrival correlated with the activities of the local branch. To investigate this possibility, we compared the treatment sample with the control sample in terms of various worker activities during the period of the HR rep’s absence. As Table 2 shows, workers’ activities remained largely similar. Another related question is whether an old HR rep’s resignation decision correlated with the performance of a local branch. An affirmative answer seems unlikely due to the nature of an HR rep’s duties and compensation structure, as discussed earlier. Still, we compare the four affected branches during the pre-HRrep-resignation period with other branches in the same region that did not experience HR rep changes (i.e., the branches shown in the last two columns of Table 1). As shown in TM

Section B, sales workers in both the affected and unaffected branches exhibited similar levels of daily activities and received a similar intensity of peer success messages.

(2016) and implemented a synthetic control method. That is, we used workers in Shenyang as donors to construct a synthetic control sample for workers in the Chongqing branch and the Nanjing branch. We next used the following specification to understand whether workers responded differently to ability-focused success messages about peers at different social distances:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Effort} _ {i j t} = \beta_ {0} * \text {After} _ {i j t} + \beta_ {1} * \text {After} _ {i j t} * \text {Close - Peer} \\ \text {Ability - Focused Message Increase} _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} * \text {After} _ {i j t} * \\ \text {Distant - Peer Ability - Focused Message Increase} _ {i j} \\ + \text {Control} _ {i j t} + v _ {i j} + \eta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

In this model, Close-Peer Ability-Focused Message Increase captures the extent to which a worker experienced an increase in the intensity of ability-focused success messages about socially close peers. Similarly, Distant-Peer Ability-Focused Message Increase is the extent to which a worker experienced an increase in the intensity of ability-focused success messages about socially distant peers. If a worker responded to ability-focused success messages about socially close peers only, we would expect $\beta _ { I }$ to be significantly positive, but not $\beta _ { 2 } .$

We measured whether a peer was socially close to or distant from the worker based on whether the worker knew the peer personally. We relied on the following two indicators. The first indicator is whether the worker and the peer physically work in the same branch. Because sales workers in a branch occasionally have meetings together, we can reasonably assume that workers in the same branch know each other. The second indicator is whether the worker has ever sent one-toone private messages to the peer. On this app, sending private messages requires that the two people are friends with each other. Thus, as long as the worker is in the same branch as the peer or has ever sent private messages to the peer, we considered the two to be socially close; otherwise, they were considered socially distant. Next, for each worker-message pair, we classified whether the message emphasized ability or not and whether the message involved a socially close or socially distant peer. Then, Close-Peer Ability-Focused Message Increase was measured by the percentage of success messages shared by the new HR rep that emphasized the ability of socially close peers; Distant-Peer Ability-Focused Message Increase was measured by the percentage of success messages shared by the new HR rep that highlighted the ability of socially distant peers. These two variables are greater than zero for workers in the treatment sample and are zero for the synthetic control sample.

## Baseline Results

The estimated coefficient in Column 1 of Table 3 shows that an increase of 10 percentage points in the intensity of effortfocused success messages motivated a sales worker to make 0.9 more phone calls per day. Given that the chance of converting a call to a deal is 0.1% and that the average sales amount for a deal is about \$3,000, such an increase could lead to an increase of 0.027 deals per worker per month, which is equal to sales revenue of \$81.

As discussed above, the relevance of effort-focused messages regarding both socially close and socially distant peers means that we would expect to find similar effects on workers’ effort from such messages. To test this prediction, we adopted a similar specification as Equation (3), replacing Close/Distant-Peer Ability-Focused Message Increase with Close/Distant-Peer Effort-Focused Message Increase. To measure these two variables, for each worker-message pair, we classify whether or not the message emphasizes effort, as well as whether the message involves a socially close or socially distant peer. Then, we measure Close-Peer Effort-Focused Message Increase (and Distant-Peer Effort-Focused Message Increase) by the percentage of success messages shared by the new HR rep that emphasize the effort of socially close (and distant) peers; both variables are greater than zero for workers in the treatment sample. As shown in Column 2 of Table 3, an increase of 10 percentage points in the intensity of effort-focused success messages about socially close peers motivated a sales worker to make 1.1 more phone calls per day; an increase of 10 percentage points in the intensity of effort-focused success messages regarding socially distant peers motivated a sales worker to make 0.8 more phone calls per day. As shown in the last row of Table 3, the difference between the two coefficients is statistically insignificant (p-value = 0.485), suggesting that both types of effort-focused messages have similar effects on workers’ effort.

We next examine how workers responded to ability-focused success messages using Equation (2). Column 3 of Table 3 shows that an increase of 10 percentage points in the intensity of ability-focused messages motivated workers to make an average of 1.2 more calls per day. This shift represents an increase of about 0.036 deals (i.e., sales revenue of \$108) per worker per month.

Based on Equation (3), Column 4 of Table 3 presents results on how ability-focused success messages about close peers vs. distant peers influenced workers differently. The results show that when the intensity of ability-focused success messages about close peers increased by 10 percentage points, workers made an average of 1.3 more phone calls per day. However, when the percentage of ability-focused success messages about distant peers increased, we saw no significant change in workers’ effort. As shown in the last row, the difference between the effect of the two types of ability-focused messages (close peers vs. distant peers) is statistically significant (p-value = 0.025). Column 5 includes all key variables in one model and shows similar results.

We implemented a range of additional analyses to check the robustness of these results. First, we investigated whether workers simply responded to peer success in general, instead of responding to the specific content of peer success messages. Second, we excluded messages that emphasized both peer effort and peer ability and reran the analyses. Third, we implemented a set of analyses to rule out alternative explanations, such as whether the observed effects were driven by workers’ managers, by workers’ reputation concerns, or by an increase in organizational support caused by the arrival of a new HR rep. Overall, our empirical results are largely consistent with our baseline results. We provide detailed discussions on these analyses and results in TM Section D.

## Experimental Study

## Experimental Method

Our scenario-based experiment required participants to imagine themselves working for an organization, and we limited participation to people who were employed by an organization when the study took place. We recruited 228 U.S. residents from Amazon Mechanical Turk. The sample size was based on recent studies on workplace motivation, as well as on our own pretest results (Boichuk et al., 2014). Participants were randomly assigned to one of five betweensubjects conditions (no-message-control, effort-messageclose-peer, effort-message-distant-peer, ability-messageclose-peer, ability-message-distant-peer).

We presented all participants with hypothetical scenarios aligned with their respective experimental conditions prior to their engagement with our primary dependent variable, the measure of work effort. The only exception was the nomessage-control condition, in which participants were directly shown our work effort measure. To manipulate peer success messages, we told participants to imagine a colleague of their organization who had recently been honored with an award. In the ability-message conditions, participants were told that this colleague had the ability and skills required to be successful. In the effort-message conditions, participants were told that this colleague had worked very hard and their achievement resulted from their effort. To manipulate social distance, we asked participants to name a colleague of their same rank (Liviatan et al., 2008). In close-peer conditions, participants were asked to imagine that this target colleague was the one recently honored with the award. In distant-peer conditions, participants were asked to imagine a worker who happened to have the same first name as the colleague but who worked for a different branch and whom they had never met. They were then asked to imagine that this unfamiliar colleague was recently honored with the award.

Both our message and social distance manipulations were pretested using participants who then were not involved in the actual study $( N ~ = ~ 1 7 1$ , mean age = 39, 45% female). Participants reported the extent to which they thought their hypothetical, award-winning colleague’s effort and ability contributed to their outstanding performance, based on a 7- point scale (Lockwood & Kunda, 1997). The difference between these two scales (i.e., ability and effort) formed our relative ability-effort attribution measure. Participants also rated how close and how similar they were to the colleague using two 100-point scales, adapted from Aron et al. (1992). Participants’ ratings of these two scales were averaged to form our social distance measure $( \mathfrak { a } = 0 . 7 4 )$ . Pretest results show that participants in the ability-message conditions attributed their colleague’s success more to ability than to effort, compared to participants in the effort-message conditions $( M _ { a b i l i t y } = - 0 . 1 3 ~ \mathrm { v s . } ~ M _ { e f f o r t } = - 0 . 7 2 ; ~ F ( 1 , ~ 1 6 7 ) = 1 3 . 3 7 , ~ p ~ <$ 0.001). Meanwhile, participants in the close-peer conditions perceived themselves to be socially closer to the colleague than participants in the distant-peer conditions $( M _ { c l o s e } = 5 6 . 4 1 $ vs. $M _ { d i s t a n t } = 5 1 . 0 8 ; F ( 1 , 1 6 7 ) = 3 . 8 9 , p = 0 . 0 5 )$ . Therefore, the results indicate that both our peer success message and social distance manipulations were successful.

After being exposed to our peer success message and social distance manipulations, participants answered a few questions adapted from previous research on workplace motivation (Boichuk et al., 2014). Their responses to these questions were averaged to form our main dependent measure of future work effort. They also responded to questions about how relevant the praise message would be to planning their own work and the likelihood of reaching a similar level of success in the future. Please refer to TM Sections F and G for more details about our pretest, procedural details of the experiment, and the actual stimuli.

Eight participants either had suspicious IP addresses or experienced technical difficulties during the study. Their exclusion left a sample of 220 participants (mean age = 39, 46% female). The participants came from various industries, but most of them indicated the categories of sales and related (22%), business and financial operations (21%), and computers and mathematics (19%). On average, participants had been working for their organization for 4.32 years (SD = 1.85), and nearly half of these organizations (48%) employed more than 500 people. ## Conclusion

Many organizations have adopted enterprise platforms to facilitate communication and collaboration among workers. In this study, we focus on the specific content shared on these platforms regarding peer success—effort-focused success messages and ability-focused success messages— and explore their influences on workers’ effort. Based on a combination of both observational data and an experimental study, we demonstrate that workers exert more effort as the intensity of either effort- or ability-focused success messages increases. Moreover, we found that workers react similarly to effort-focused success messages, regardless of the social distance from their peers. We also found that workers respond strongly to success messages that highlight the ability of socially close peers but not to messages regarding the ability of socially distant peers.

This research has important implications for organizations. Our finding that sharing either effort-focused or abilityfocused success messages has a positive effect on work effort differs from the commonly held belief that organizational communication via mobile apps kills productivity (e.g., Salihefendic, 2017). We show that sharing at least one type of peer information—peer success that conveys rich content about peers—appears to be beneficial. Most companies have implemented some form of real-time communication platform (e.g., Slack), allowing easy sharing of peer performance. Thus, companies could use this practice to realize a significant productivity gain. The benefit of having a highly productive worker could be greater than their individual contribution because their success can motivate other workers to increase their effort as well.

For organizations that choose to share peer success, our research offers practical advice on how to construct the right message on enterprise digital platforms. For example, when a success message highlights a peer’s ability, workers who are socially close to this peer are more susceptible to being influenced by such information. However, in organizations with a distributed workforce, workers may not know each other. Our findings suggest that such organizations should consider crafting effort-focused (instead of ability-focused) messages when sharing peer success.

Our research has several important limitations. First, workers responses to peer success messages may be nonlinear and may reach a point of fatigue. In other words, workers may positively respond to earlier peer success messages, but as the number of such messages increases past a certain point, they may either become insensitive to such messages or respond in a negative way. Further research might consider whether certain types of peer success messages are particularly susceptible to a nonlinear effect.

Second, peer success sharing can not only inspire effort but can also encourage workers to reach out to successful peers to learn best practices. As a result, workers can learn to work both harder and smarter. We looked into our empirical setting for such a knowledge transfer mechanism but found only a few instances. Still, knowledge transfer and the resulting effect on workers’ long-term performance would be interesting to investigate (e.g., Azoulay et al., 2010; Chan et al., 2014). Because most studies look at how peers share knowledge and influence each other in a physical setting, future studies should examine how enterprise digital platforms enable knowledge transfer among workers in ways that are different from the offline environment. Given the short time window used in our empirical study, we did not examine how workers’ ability improves as a result of these messages. Future research with a longer time span could potentially shed light on this topic.

Third, our study focused on assessing the effect of effortfocused and ability-focused messages on sales workers’ effort, but we did not directly measure variations in the perceived similarity in effort or ability resulting from social distance. In observing differences in the perceived relevance of abilityfocused messages between socially distant and close peers, we inferred that these differences arise from variances in perceived similarity in ability. A potential avenue for future research would be to delve deeper into the psychological mechanisms driving perceived relevance and devise more precise measures to assess perceived similarity in effort and ability.
