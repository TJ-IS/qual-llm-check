# Creating value through governing IT deployment in a public/private-sector inter-organisational context: a human agency perspective

## Abstract

Harnessing value from Information Technology (IT) has long been a focus of research, but evidence is lacking about how effective practice of Information Technology Governance (ITG) contributes to creating value for stakeholders in inter-organisational contexts. This is especially so for public/private sector partnerships. In this study we used ISO/IEC 38500:2008, the corporate governance of IT standard, to direct analysis of how ITG was practised in deployment of a large IT project in an inter-organisational public/private sector context. The findings demonstrate that ITG strategies related to human agency contribute to the realisation of value for participating stakeholders, particularly through pre-emptive stakeholder participation in evaluating IT functionality of the old system and iteratively in deployment of the new system. Further, our investigation shows that ISO/IEC 38500:2008 has merit as an analytical framework to objectively evaluate corporate governance of IT, although there is need for some enhancement.

## Introduction

Information Technology (IT) pervades social, communication and commercial systems (Bruque et al, 2008). While there are many motivators for adopting IT, a primary concern is maximising the potential value of such investments (Kohli & Grover, 2008). IT governance (ITG), as a component of corporate governance, seeks to ensure that IT investments are directed and implemented in a manner that achieves business value. As negative outcomes from poor IT investments are attributable to an ‘emphasis on the technical, financial and scheduling aspects of IT activities rather than emphasis on the whole business context of IT use’ (ISO/IEC 38500, 2008, p. v), there is merit in taking a strategic perspective to consider ITG’s role in evaluating, directing and monitoring IT investment and deployment.

Frameworks and methodologies that direct and evaluate ITG include COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and related Technologies), Risk IT, ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library), Val IT and Prince 2. In an inter-organisational context, where accountability and authority are shared, the strategic perspectives offered by the international standard

ISO/IEC 38500:2008 Corporate Governance of Information Technology (CGIT) should have more applicability. Through its six principles (ISO/IEC 38500, 2008, p. 6) the standard distinguishes governance from management. It conveys that ultimate responsibility for ITG and realisation of value rests with the entire leadership team. With its sector-agnostic focus on the strengths and weaknesses of ITG’s delivered outcomes, this newly adapted international standard ISO/IEC 38500:2008 (based on AS8015, 2005) is claimed to offer significant opportunities to explore ITG effectiveness in interorganisational scenarios (Calder, 2008). Thus, the primary aim of this research was to use ISO/IEC 38500:2008 to direct analysis of the execution of ITG in deployment of a large IT project in an inter-organisational public/ private sector context. Our study reveals the merit of the standard as an analytical framework to objectively evaluate ITG, but highlights a need to enhance prescriptive aspects like performance management.

Our case study involved redevelopment and deployment of the complex Employment Services System (ESS) within a major Australian public-sector organisation, namely the Department of Education, Employment and Workplace Relations (DEEWR). Although funded and developed by DEEWR, the ESS was designed to support external public/private stakeholders and their organisations who are contracted to deliver employment service programmes on behalf of the Australian Government, including business and operational needs. Deployment was widely regarded as successful, delivering value to public and private sector stakeholders (MMC, 2010).

In investigating this case study, our research questions were:

RQ1: In an inter-organisational public/private context, what ITG strategies facilitate delivering business value to the myriad of stakeholders involved?

RQ2: How can authority and accountability be managed to deliver successful outcomes to stakeholders?

The importance of investigating best practice for ITG can be gauged from prior studies, which have shown that IT-enabled change is often wasted, challenged or fails to bring a return to the enterprise (ITGI, 2009). Examples include Queensland Health Australia’s debacle where management transferred ‘75,000 workers onto an untested system and [hoped] for the best’ (Caldwell, 2010, p. 4), resulting in workers being either significantly overpaid/underpaid, with flow-on effects to taxation and superannuation. Similarly, Oroton Australia experienced a \$1 million reduction in earnings from IT supply chain problems (Australian Business Intelligence, 2005).

In the public sector, the Victorian Auditor-General’s Office (2008, p. 2) found that ‘robust governance and skilful management can play a significant role in achieving success with an y [IT] investment y [with] y authority and accountability for the delivery and realisation of benefits y [being] especially critical in [situations involving] multi-agency collaborative investments where authority and accountability can become diluted or confused’. Similarly, audit findings generated by the German SAI about IT structural problems in German federal departments highlighted problems with integration of IT applications and structures due to a lack of ITG (German SAI, 2008). Gershon (2008) identified similar problems with the Australian Government’s IT deployments. Consequently, DEEWR consciously ascribed importance to ITG in producing value from its IT deployment. The focus of our case study concerns realising and demonstrating how ITG delivers IT/organisational value for public and private stakeholders despite different backgrounds, requirements and understandings. Herein our findings contribute new knowledge about the value of stakeholder participation in all stages of ITG, including pre-emptive evaluation of problems with existing IT.

The paper is organised as follows. After reviewing literature about ITG and the theoretical underpinnings for our case study, we outline our research method and context. We then present case study findings that illustrate ISO/IEC 38500:2008’s value in framing strategic understanding of the ITG exercise. Finally we discuss our findings and related implications, outline limitations and suggest opportunities for future research.

## Literature review

Organisations have placed emphasis on strategically using ITG to build capability and improve business value by expending effort in creating agile structures, accompanying processes and relational mechanisms. Using the ISO/IEC 38500:2008 standard to direct analysis, this research explored the link between the role of the principal in the practice of ITG and the delivery of value to a broad cohort of stakeholders in a large, complex, notfor-profit inter-organisational setting. Given our study involved participation of a myriad of stakeholders, we used Agency Theory to frame this understanding. Our findings show how strategic and timely appreciation of desired outcomes, including active stakeholder participation, can co-create value for both principal and agent stakeholders.

## IT governance

Corporate governance is the system which directs and controls organisations to manage their operations effectively in order to meet shareholder/stakeholder expectations for financial and environmental prudence, reputation and risk management (Cadbury, 1992; OECD, 1999). Recent corporate collapses, the global financial crisis and compliance requirements like Sarbanes Oxley and the Basel Accords, have renewed interest in corporate governance to deliver integrity of business transactions and business value

Recognised by top management as an essential part of corporate governance (Hardy, 2009), ITG is concerned with ‘evaluating and directing the use of IT to support the organisation and monitoring this use to achieve plans’ (ISO/IEC 38500, 2008, p. 3). ITG’s historical antecedents can be found in research into: organisational issues regarding improved return on IT investment (Garrity, 1963); Strategic Information Systems Planning (Lederer & Sethi, 1988); the view that IT is as much concerned with human activity and organisation as technology (Galliers, 1991); and strategic IT alignment wherein IT has arguably evolved from providing administrative support to a strategic function requiring mechanisms and processes to deliver its full potential (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1999). Consequently, ITG places the strategic responsibility for IT with the entire leadership team (Wilkin & Chenhall, 2010).

ITG is customarily deployed using a combination of processes, structures and relational mechanisms (Van Grembergen, 2004; Campbell et al, 2010). Processes include monitoring, decision-making and balanced scorecards; structures include IT councils and steering committees; and relational mechanisms include business partnerships, shared learning, stakeholder participation and collaboration between functional areas or other organisations (Van Grembergen, 2004).

## Standards, frameworks and methodologies that assist ITG

Several standards, frameworks and methodologies have evolved that assist in maximising potential from IT investments including Val IT, COBIT, Risk IT and ITIL. While these support practical application of ITG, they lack strategic perspectives for analysing accountable involvement of the leadership team. ISO/IEC 38500:2008 has emerged to address this. Its high-level principles-based approach aims to: (1) assure stakeholders about an organisation’s effective CGIT; (2) guide key stakeholders about effective IT use; and (3) provide a basis for objective evaluation of CGIT (ISO/IEC 38500:2008, p. 1). This last purpose addressed our need for a framework to analyse the execution of ITG in an inter-organisational public/ private-sector context (RQ1). While prior research has largely focused on the co-creation of value in singlefirm private sector contexts (Wilkin & Chenhall, 2010), there are systemic differences between the two sectors (see Table 1). Consequently, our investigation of ITG in a public/private-sector inter-organisational context offers new insight into how authority and accountability can be managed across the myriad of stakeholders to co-create successful outcomes (RQ2).

ISO/IEC 38500:2008’s six principles delineate requirements related to responsibility, strategic considerations, accountability regarding the acquisition of IT, appraisal of performance and conformance as well as appreciation of the human element of the activity in ITG (see Table 2).

In examining the application of ITG, these six principles are reviewed through three main tasks (see Table 5 which details their application in our case study):

\- Evaluate – the current and future use of IT.

\- Direct – by preparing and implementing plans and policies to ensure IT is used in a manner that meets organisational objectives.

\- Monitor – how well IT conforms to policies and performs compared to organisational plans.

As ITG is impacted by a number of factors including stakeholder conflicts of interest, we briefly reflect on stakeholder participation and a human agency perspective before detailing the case study.

## ITG and user/stakeholder participation

User/stakeholder participation is defined as the ‘behaviours and activities that users or their representatives perform in the systems development process’ (He & King, 2008, p. 302). The literature has long recognised a likely link between user/stakeholder participation and satisfaction with IT outcomes (McKeen et al, 1994; Subramanyam et al, 2010). Yet research has delivered equivocal results. Hunton & Beeler (1997) found that user participation was positively related to satisfaction with IT maintenance projects, but participation in new IT delivered more satisfaction to system developers than users. This uncertainty accords with earlier conflicting findings (i.e., Ives & Olson, 1984; Cavaye, 1995). In response, research has attempted to link the lack of success from user/stakeholder participation to poor communication and poor relationship building, and investigated the value of strategies like participative design and top management support (He & King, 2008).

Yet these findings may relate to stakeholder attitudes being constrained by involvement that effectively occurs after the key decision has been made (i.e., what constitutes the new IT) and is attributable to these users/ stakeholders feeling disenfranchised at the outset. This may equally be true for ITG, where both research and practice focus on strategies for building relational mechanisms and effective structures through active engagement with users/stakeholders via approaches like IT steering committees and top management leadership using formal policies and planning (PriceWaterhouseCoopers, 2006: Wilkin & Chenhall. 2010). The ITG literature indicates the benefit of fostering relationships through communication (Bierberstein et al, 2005; Pan et al, 2008), and building trust (Li et al, 2008) and cooperation (Bagranoff & Brewer, 2003) through team building (Jun et al, 2007) and communication (Huang et al, 2010). However, research that investigates the timing and consistency of engagement with the broad stakeholder cohort is lacking. Consequently, in this study we seek to contribute new knowledge by exploring the effects of pre-emptive user/stakeholder participation upon engagement with new IT when this begins with analysis of the old system and is accountably and consistently carried forward through the life of the ITG exercise.

## ITG: a human agency perspective

A primary objective of ITG is to maximise business value from IT investment. This is more complex in public/ private inter-organisational contexts, where the myriad of stakeholders have differing goals and needs that affect co-creation of business value. Marketing, a related discipline, views value co-creation as including: ‘where the supplier provides an experience and the customer is part of this context’; and when ‘the customer and supplier engage in the especially important activity of co-design of products’ (Payne et al, 2008, p. 84). In these scenarios customers may transform from ‘passive audiences’ to ‘active players’ (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2000). This is equally relevant in an IT inter-organisational context wherein a principal firm consults and delegates work involving system use to its agent counterparts. Herein agency, as depicted by Agency Theory, may affect the experiences of participating stakeholders, thereby creating problems (RQ2). Firstly, conflict may arise between the desires or goals of the principal and the agent. Secondly, there is difficulty/expense in verifying what the agent is actually doing (Eisenhardt, 1989a). While various mechanisms like profit sharing may be used to align stakeholder interests, in a private/public sector context these are less feasible.

In seeking perspectives on human agency, a number of understandings apply. The most relevant to this study is the temporal theory of human agency, which situates agency as a process in which actors engage with the past, the present and the future understandings of their potential actions through habit, imagination and judgement (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). It explains why people both seek and resist change. As expounded by Giddens (1984), Structuration Theory explains outcomes of inertia and habitual behaviour related to IT adoption, where past familiarities may foster inertia.

Alternatively, Orlikowski (2000) argues that human actors are subject to individual understandings and social influence, with the ability to exercise discretion regarding IT use and improvisation. Herein agency is less predictable. In capturing both perspectives the temporal conception of agency argues that ‘agency involves working through emerging choice situations with eyes on the past and the future, while simultaneously responding to the practical contingencies of the present’ (Chu & Robey, 2008, p. 86). This interplay is what we seek to explore.

Moreover, agent and principal responses to differing pulls and pressures (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999) are also the result of power, legitimacy and urgency, which create opportunities for stakeholder influence (Mitchell et al, 1997). ‘The relationships of power are a total structure of actions brought to bear upon possible actions’ (Foucault, 1982, p. 208). This may be reinforced by legitimacy, which defines the extent to which stakeholders are recognised as possessing socially desirable attributes (Mitchell et al, 1997). Herein, subject to the level of power possessed, they impose their authority upon others. Finally, urgency concerns the degree of immediate attention that a stakeholder can command. Since stakeholders with all three attributes become very influential, transparency of accountability and authority become important. Given our focus on ITG in a public/ private setting, aspects of power, legitimacy and urgency are predictable and identification of the enabling human agency perspective in successful deployment of IT should contribute knowledge (Saraf et al, 2007).

Jasperson et al’s (2002, p. 402) review of research into power and agency in IT applications developed metaconjectures that direct IT researchers to ‘view the complexity of power relationships in IT’. Given the Australian Government’s investment in DEEWR’s ESS, we expected to find (through RQ2) evidence of their meta-conjectured organisational approach with evidence of: resource dependency by the service provider stakeholders; institutional structures wherein government used its power to determine the service providers; and political imperatives that historically run counter to business objectives (Markus & Robey, 1988).

Further, as the principal organisation delegated use of the core system to its agent counterparts (its public/ private-sector service providers), the temporal theory of human agency should contribute new understanding about management of authority and accountability to deliver successful outcomes to stakeholders through exploration of relevant attitudes to past, present and future IT performance.

## Research method and context

## Method

Case study research, as the adopted methodology, permitted deeper understanding of the uniqueness and complexity of our public/private sector inter-organisational context (Lee, 1991; Gable, 1994). Being widely used in IS research (Eisenhardt, 1989b) in situations of power and politics (Markus, 1983), and negotiations involving IT initiatives (Feller et al, 2011), it permitted us to appreciate the complex social interactions that enabled successful outcomes and to derive theoretical understanding. In so doing, our approach had positivist and interpretive components. Yin (2005, p. xiv) espoused such use of case studies where there is need for ‘descriptive richness and analytic insight into people, events, and passions as played out in real-life environments’, including the ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions related to understanding the role of power and politics in determining IT value (Yin, 2003).

In our case study the primary focus was to investigate what ITG strategies facilitate delivering business value to the myriad of stakeholders involved (RQ1) and the related focus on authority and accountability (RQ2). Given evidence that ITG research lacks some currency with practice (Wilkin & Chenhall, 2010), this addresses an identified gap. We initially adopted a positivist approach (Stage 1) as we were seeking to objectively identify the structures, processes and relational mechanisms in DEEWR’s ITG exercise. Herein we structured our analysis of the project information (see Table 3) using the ISO/IEC 38500:2008 framework. This facilitated determination of how ITG was practised in the DEEWR case study and informed our ontological position. Our analysis (see Tables 5 and 6) did allude to co-creating subtle interactions in the relational mechanisms between DEEWR and subordinate stakeholders in the ESS deployment (the unit of analysis) and to new approaches in managing stakeholder engagement.

To explore this interaction between DEEWR’s strategic imperatives and how the relational mechanisms, accountability and authority were played out to create value for the dispersed and diverse stakeholder cohort, we adopted an interpretivist approach in Stage 2 (Walsham, 2006). Herein Agency Theory offered the potential for insights by which to frame our understanding of how a principal’s authority and accountability can be managed to deliver successful outcomes and what strategies relate to engagement of agent stakeholders in an ITG exercise (see Table 7). It is interesting to note our unexpected conclusions here (Walsham. 1995).

Analysis was derived from data collected over an 18-month period, including meeting agendas, minutes, web releases and reports (see Table 3). Documents play a critical role in communicating and reproducing culture and action and, in so doing, provide the basis for organisational activity (Hasselbladh & Kallinikos, 2000). As outside observers we made extensive use of this documentary data as a source of commentary and in interpreting the social reality inferred by their existence and use in practice. This provided insight into individual and collective actions and meanings, including the ITG structures and dynamics associated with the ESS. In line with Wright (2011), DEEWR’s documents and records constructed social and organisational reality. Importantly, the documentary data made organisational behaviour visible, traceable and interpretable, with critical detail about the focus activities and chronology of events (Prior, 2003).

Firstly, we identified all available documentary data associated with the IT deployment that could provide insight into DEEWR’s ITG structures and practices. This yielded 192 documents for analysis. We then classified this material into six project information categories and supporting research resources (see Table 3). Use of multiple sources of evidence to define constructs is an acknowledged mechanism for establishing content validity (Eisenhardt, 1989b). Since the majority of ESS documents were publicly available through DEEWR’s IT Consultation web pages, document authenticity was well-established. Moreover we verified the truth and accuracy of the events and processes portrayed in the documents with the principal stakeholder. Consequently, source criticism was not an issue.

In both stages we were conscious of the potential for bias (Yin, 2003). In the positivist component, bias was minimized as multiple sources of data were independently mapped to a theoretical pattern, the ISO/IEC

38500 framework. In effect this process was akin to pattern matching (Pare, 2004; Trochim, 2006). Then, in the interpretive component, acknowledging that ‘valuefree data cannot be obtained’ (Walsham, 2006, p. 376), we continued to independently document our understandings. Our use of underlying theoretical concept also supported the existence of structures in the data, which contributed to internal validity in the findings (Eisenhardt, 1989b). We then compared results, discussing the inferences drawn and the extent to which our conclusions were substantiated. Finally we triangulated our findings by consulting with the key stakeholder about our derived conclusions and discussing their theoretical implications.

## Research context

As already mentioned, our case study involved DEEWR, who contracts to external public/private providers of employment services, through the ESS that ‘lets providers enter vacancy and jobseeker information, particulars of events in the course of assisting a jobseeker [and] financial records of payments y that [DEEWR] reimburses y for employment services delivered’ (DEEWR, 2009a). DEEWR features in the Education, Employment, and Workplace Relations Portfolio of the Australian Government. During the 2009–2010 financial year, this portfolio had total resources of AU\$45,199,881,000, with DEEWR responsible for AU\$44,578,501,000 (DEEWR, 2009b).

DEEWR’s employment services have existed in some form for more than 10 years. However, following extensive consultation with service providers, the Australian Government undertook deployment of a new model, Job Services Australia (JSA), in 2009 for AU\$4.9 billion worth of services over three years. JSA’s innovative approach to employment and recruitment focuses on the individual needs of job seekers and employers instead of a ‘one size fits all approach’ (see Table 4). For job seekers, JSA delivers more tailored assistance to facilitate securing employment, while for employers there is greater emphasis on finding work-ready and appropriately skilled job seekers through initiatives including employer brokers.

## Analysis

Using ISO/IEC 38500:2008’s principles and tasks (see Tables 2 and 5) we mapped DEEWR’s ITG practices in deployment of the ESS (see Table 5). Our results demonstrate ISO/IEC 38500:2008’s practical value in analysing real-world applications.

Acknowledging the previously reported weak levels of ITG and consequential sub-optimal outcomes (Gershon, 2008), DEEWR introduced control in deploying the ESS through a transparent ITG model. ISA. as a government mandate, necessitated strategic fit including to the government’s Social Inclusion Agenda. Despite the complexity and diversity of operations across Australia, DEEWR initiated strategies that fostered comprehensive stakeholder participation. Resultant examples of DEEWR’s ITG structures, processes and relational mechanisms are outlined in Table 6.

During ESS development and deployment, DEEWR targeted inclusive formal relational mechanisms, which enhanced positive human agency and system success (see Table 7). In particular, accountable use of inclusive stakeholder consultation (including pre-emptively about the old ES) engaged participation through reflection on past deficiencies and desirable future options (see Probity in Table 7).

The principal (DEEWR) consistently adopted a pattern of inclusivity and consultation as part of ITG through actively consulting with stakeholders about strategies for engaging participation and instituting ITG; publishing its draft communication plan; and pilot-testing surveys. By pre-emptively requiring stakeholders to identify deficiencies with the old ES, DEEWR enhanced agency and began the process of transition from the past to the future, thereby reducing inertia and engaging stakeholders with the future (Chu & Robey, 2008).

Drawing on the material contained in Tables 6 and 7, it is apparent that ITG co-created value in the new ESS.

\- For DEEWR, the system was delivered on-time and on budget, with functionality that facilitated sharing jobseekers’ data between service providers with seamless efficiencies.

\- For service providers, jobseekers’ information and DEEWR’s service payments were more accessible and timely.

\- For jobseekers, employment services were better tailored, with more accurate and prompt delivery.

\- For employers, identified job seekers were work-ready and appropriately skilled for advertised vacancies.

What strategies contributed to this success? DEEWR preemptively and strategically determined that success depended on commitment to the new ESS. Consequently they used a timely, inclusive, informative and consultative approach (see Tables 5–7) that respected the needs and desires of the myriad of stakeholders involved. Sharing knowledge of past inefficiencies established a common base-line from which new goals were derived. These strategies fostered the projective element of human agency (Chu & Robey, 2008).

With respect to managing authority and accountability to deliver successful outcomes, conflicts were apparent between the desires and goals of the two primary stakeholders (DEEWR and the service providers). Given the ESS was driven and funded by the principal (DEEWR) who had overwhelming power in the relationship, conscious efforts to enhance transparency and minimise the impact of its power through the use of structures and relational mechanisms is evidence of sound practice. From the outset accountability and knowledge transfer were facilitated by the principal’s engagement in dialogue with agent stakeholders and their identified IT contact person. DEEWR’s use of LiveMeet and programme evaluations also conveyed to agent stakeholders that accountability was genuine and transparent. These actions suggest that the principal purposely subordinated its power in order to generate stakeholder/agent commitment and effectively manage change to the ESS, thereby creating value. However, a weakness concerns the apparent lack of a publicly available performance management framework for reviewing the deployed ESS against business strategy and desired outcomes. This is a common public sector issue where the decision to change a public programme often itself becomes the business case.

Based on retrospective application of ISO/IEC 38500: 2008, we found that the standard identified weaknesses in DEEWR’s ITG practices predominately in the monitoring task and to some extent in the evaluation and direction tasks. For example, ISO/IEC 38500:2008 showed that: (1) CIO reporting was not obvious; (2) alignment with objectives seemed one-sided; (3) no obvious comparisons were made against the business strategy/ investment mix: (4) no external assessment of business value was apparent; (5) little evidence of a budget based on full economic life-cycle costs; (6) there was need for refinement and sign-off of requirements; and (7) consideration of resource interdependencies was not apparent.

## Contribution from the perspective of Agency Theory

As detailed earlier, we expected our scenario to exemplify an organisational approach (Jasperson et al, 2002). Yet findings show that due to conscious choices by the principal, the outcomes equate best to an emergent approach because the ESS deployment was treated as much as an exercise in change management through complex social interactions as a technological/organisational imperative. In fact, the successful outcomes can largely be attributed to the principal’s choices regarding inclusive stakeholder participation (see Tables 5–7). Given the complexity involved, we expected that DEEWR may take time to regain equilibrium with new power structures (Jasperson et al, 2002). Yet we found that equilibrium was quickly achieved. Despite its self-evident power, DEEWR’s benign and sustained use of authority demonstrated to its stakeholders that ITG would be exercised accountably (see Tables 7).

## Reflections on the use of ISO/IEC 38500:2008

It is difficult to use a standard to evaluate ITG performance and at the same time identify weaknesses in the standard’s capacity to provide a basis for objective evaluation (ISO/IEC 38500:2008). Yet some results cannot go unremarked. While the standard did effectively map analysis about ITG in DEEWR’s ESS deployment, we found problems with:

\- the choice of labels for the three main tasks (evaluate, direct and monitor), as the term evaluate was used to refer to an initial scan of practice, not a final assessment; and

\- ordering of the principles. The first principle, namely responsibility, related to the supply and demand for IT; while the second, strategy, was where consideration was given to what is actually needed. Further, performance preceded conformance.

Moreover the standard, particularly in the monitoring task, did not address the specific governance issues of our context, with little direction regarding how the six IT governance principles should be accountably operationalised in situations having inter-organisational stakeholders. Given such scenarios are increasingly the norm, further guidance is required either within the existing standard or through the development of ancillary documentation. Finally, despite our findings that highlight the importance of robust and transparent stakeholder consultation mechanisms for effective ITG, ISO/IEC 38500:2008 provided little direction into possible agency effects, related governance, and strategies to negotiate diverse, even conflicting stakeholder views.

## Contribution to the understanding of ITG

Firstly, our study extends theoretical understandings recently derived by other researchers. For example, in their study of SMEs, Huang et al (2010, p. 295) demonstrated the ‘desirability of explicitly facilitating communication exchanges’ across an organisation and called for an extension of this research into larger organisational contexts. Beyond reinforcing their findings, our study contributes understanding about effective communication mechanisms (i.e., LiveMeet) in complex, dispersed settings. Further, despite its disproportionate financial power and required adherence to government agendas, by demonstrating how DEEWR achieved inclusive stakeholder participation, we extend Spears & Barki’s (2010) work that demonstrated the value of user participation for improving IS security risk management, although theirs had a proactive rather than pre-emptive focus.

Secondly, our study contributes to knowledge about identified barriers to ITG performance – namely adroitly managing resistance to change in order to achieve improved business performance (PriceWaterhouseCoopers, 2006; Wilkin & Chenhall, 2010). While success has long been linked to top management commitment, leadership and communication (Sarker & Lee, 1999), studies that contextually explore application of this understanding are lacking and suggested strategies less defined. Our study, with its complexities of dispersed location and private/public sector competing agendas, demonstrates how a clear focus on these imperatives is achievable and productive.

Similarly, our study addresses an identified gap in the literature. Specifically, in their review of 12 major journals in the period 1999–2008, Wilkin & Chenhall (2010) found that only 2.2% of papers provided a holistic perspective about ITG, with these papers tending to report on achievements in single organisations. The remaining 485 papers dealt with one or more focus areas (risk and resource management, strategic business/IT alignment, achieving value and performance measurement) as identified by ITGI (2009). While the review identified strategic alignment and strategic direction as key research foci, the disparate nature of the literature demonstrated the need for new research that provides a holistic perspective to ITG. This may explain findings like those reported by Marnewick & Labuschagne (2011) who found that South African IT projects are being initiated without adopting ITG and while organisations practise corporate governance, its IT equivalent is ignored.

Finally, our study extends theoretical understanding of ITG by identifying the concept of pre-emptive stakeholder participation and its role in achieving successful outcomes. In our case study there was widespread consultation with stakeholders about deficiencies with the old ES and approaches and strategies adopted in deployment of the new ESS. This permitted stakeholders to derive and contribute understanding about deficiencies with the old system and the need for a new system. As their input was so valued, this produced mutually beneficial results. Indeed DEEWR accessed a range of ideas upon which they built and generated a sense of cooperative ownership. Conceptually, by consciously managing accountability and authority through a human agency perspective to achieve stakeholder engagement with change management, such pre-emptive stakeholder participation contributed to the co-creation of value. Indeed this concept of stakeholder engagement through lateral strategies and related interactive relational mechanisms (both before and during an IT project) in order to generate shared understandings and commitment, not only contributes new theoretical understanding of successful strategies for ITG, but its benefits are likely to flow on to other aspects of organisational performance.

## Conclusion

In this study we used ISO/IEC 38500:2008, the corporate governance of IT standard, to direct analysis of how ITG was practised in deployment of a large IT project in an inter-organisational public/private sector context. Through our conceptualisation of the role of human agency two key conclusions can be drawn from our study. Firstly, through benign and sustained use of authority, a principal can achieve inclusivity from its agent counterparts whereupon active participation in ITG processes and decision making can contribute to the ongoing success of the project. Secondly, pre-emptive stakeholder participation in evaluation of problems with existing IT can foster a reflective process that contributes to such stakeholders engagement with an ITG exercise. These concepts concerning pre-emptive stakeholder engagement and related interactive communication mechanisms not only contribute new theoretical understanding of successful strategies for ITG, but equally are likely to enhance organisational performance in other change management exercises. Further research into the timing, nature and consistency of engagement with the broad stakeholder cohort may be of interest to scholars and practitioners.

Additionally, our investigation shows the merit of ISO/IEC 38500:2008 as an analytical framework, but highlights possible need to enhance aspects like performance management. This requires further investigation. Moreover few studies have examined the environmental relevance of a formal standard from a Design Science Research perspective (Campbell, 2007), and reflecting upon our ex-post application of the standard in a naturalistic setting, we suggest that there are opportunities here too for future research.

While our theoretical findings are analytically generalizable (Walsham, 1995), there are some limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, our reliance upon publicly available data from a single case study (albeit a complex one) limits our resultant conclusions. This material was transparent and relatively comprehensive, but follow-up interviews with other key stakeholders may possibly enrich findings. Secondly, although the insights gained outweigh the disadvantages, retrospective application of any framework presents its own restrictions.

Despite these limitations we believe our study contributes practical and theoretical understanding of ITG. Through Agency Theory we have contributed to theoretical understanding of ITG, namely that inclusive pre-emptive participatory stakeholder involvement can contribute to success in such significant change management exercises.

Given the importance of the human element and interactive relational mechanisms to exercises involving technology (such as ITG), we hope that our rich insights prove to be of interest to practitioners and scholars alike as organisations engage with new IT initiatives.
