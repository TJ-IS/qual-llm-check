## Abstract

User-led development is gaining popularity with organizations wishing to increase user involvement and control. Typically in this approach a small group of users is given the responsibility for managing the project and representing the user community in determining requirements, testing, training, and system implementation. This paper explores the end user's perception of the quality of his or her representation and satisfaction with the application system within an organization employing user-led development. The end-user communities for two systems developed in a large Australian government corporation were surveyed. The results indicate that the user perception of representation is the most significant influence on user satisfaction—the correlation scores for the two systems studied were in excess of 0.6. Also of importance is the user's perception of management support. Both systems recorded a low average score for user representativeness (2.5 and 2.7), which is attributed in part to the large number and geographic spread of the users and to the approach adopted by the user representatives in the user-led development team.

Keywords: User involvement, user-led development, user representation, user satisfaction

ISRL Categories: AI0108, FA05, FA09, EI0207

## Introduction

While many argue that user involvement in systems development is primarily a social justice issue, they also believe it to be associated with positive organizational and economic benefits (Bjørn-Andersen, 1988; Land and Hirschheim, 1983; Lodge, 1989). Others argue, that, from an economic standpoint, participation in the systems development process is manipulative, will impair labor shedding, will entrench poor practice, can lead to poor design, is not cost-effective, and can be dysfunctional because it can lead to political problems (Hirschheim, 1989; Kraft, 1979).

Despite these criticisms, an increasing number of businesses have changed their organization of systems development to increase user involvement and user control. Three levels or types of user involvement or participation in systems development have been defined: consultative, representative, and consensus (Mumford, 1981; 1983; Mumford and Henshall, 1979). These levels are distinguished largely by the degree of influence and control vested in the user. In consultative participation the main decisions on the design of the new system are made by the information systems personnel, although the user is consulted, perhaps extensively, by the systems developers. In representative participation a team is formed with representatives of the users and systems analysts and is entrusted with the design of the system and management of the project. Consensus participation uses the democratic approach by attempting to involve all users continuously throughout the systems design process. This type is usually considered feasible only for developing systems involving few users.

One popular form of representative user involvement is called user-led development (Lodge, 1989). In this case, user representatives define the system requirements, oversee system testing, manage the training and systems development, and manage the overall project. While control is shared by the user and systems personnel in another form of representative user involvement (Mumford and Henshall, 1979), in user-led development the user representatives have clear control over the project.

Both information systems and user managers are becoming more concerned with user satisfaction. Users today often have complete control of their IS budget and are able to outsource systems development if not satisfied with the in-house group. Although one of the motivations of user-led development is to improve user satisfaction with the system, we know of no study carried out to investigate this issue. However, comments of researchers (Mumford and Henshall, 1979) and studies in closely related environments (e.g., Olson and Ives, 1981; Tait and Vessey, 1988) would lead us to anticipate that a user's perception of the quality of his or her representation would be an important factor in explaining user satisfaction.

A number of factors that may negatively affect the quality of user representation have been proposed (Land and Hirschheim, 1983):

1. The user representatives may be viewed by colleagues as hostages to the “experts” or having “joined the other side.”

2. The proposed system may involve large numbers of users scattered over many locations.

3. The users may not be sufficiently motivated to take an interest in their representation.

4. The user representatives may not see themselves as responsible to or representing the users in the development activity.

This paper investigates the end user's perception of representation (i.e., user representativeness) and his or her satisfaction with the system within an organization employing user-led development. Other factors, such as top management support (Franz and Robey, 1986; Lucas, 1975; Maish, 1979), user expectations concerning the system's benefit (Hirschheim and Newman, 1988), and previous experience with computer systems (Senn, 1987) have also been suggested as influencing user satisfaction. These other factors are briefly described to ensure that alternative explanations for user satisfaction are considered (Lee, 1989).

As part of the investigation, this paper reports the results of a study of the correlates of user satisfaction associated with the implementation of two large systems by an organization employing user-led development. First, user involvement and representation are discussed. Then the research model and hypotheses are described. Then the study is outlined and the results are tested. Finally, the discussion and the conclusions are presented.

## The Effects of User Involvement

Studies on the effects of user involvement in information systems development have generally taken either a descriptive, case-study-oriented approach (e.g., Hirschheim, 1989; Mumford and Henshall, 1979) or a quantitative, cross-sectional survey approach attempting to show a correlation between user involvement and system success or satisfaction (e.g., Baroudi, et al., 1986; Nicholas and O'Connor, 1990; Tait and Vessey, 1988). While the former type of study generally supports user involvement as beneficial to the organization, the latter has not provided consistent evidence of its benefit. Despite great care, the latter quantitatively based studies suffer from a number of methodological problems that have prompted others to call for alternative ways of studying the issue (Newman and Noble, 1990). $^{1}$

The mixed results on the influence of user involvement on user satisfaction are all the more surprising given the generally positive impact of participation on job satisfaction: Locke and Schweiger (1979) reviewed 89 studies on the impact of participative decision making on job satisfaction and found evidence of a positive relationship. Since user involvement is a form of participation and user satisfaction with a system is a component of job satisfaction, one would anticipate a positive relationship between user involvement and user satisfaction.

Some aspects of these problems with user involvement have been addressed by Barki and Hartwick (1989). They advocate distinguishing between involvement and participation as a means to provide a richer picture and to avoid the limitations of using these terms synonymously to denote purely an activity (i.e., time-allocated-to-task). They propose the term participation to define behavior or activity and involvement to define a subjective psychologic state reflecting an attitude to the system. While a user may not have participated personally in the development of a system, the involvement of a colleague (either one consulted in consultative participation or a representative in representative participation) in whom the user has confidence may give the user the perception of a satisfactory level of involvement.

Thus, it is important in studying user involvement to (a) distinguish the level of involvement along the lines proposed by Mumford and Henshall (1979) and (b) to adopt a measure that embraces the user's perceptions of the extent to which his or her interests are being represented in the design of the system. This form of measure is particularly appropriate for user-led development. While user representatives are in control of defining requirements and managing system deployment, most users have little direct input to the actual systems development. Hence, their feeling of involvement is based on how they perceive their interests are represented by the small user group leading the project. We call this perceived level of involvement by the end users “user representativeness” to make it quite clear we are dealing with perceptions of representation.

Another potential problem with the cross-sectional studies is the variation across the sample in the elapsed time between the implementation date of the project and when the data is collected. Over time, factors other than their involvement during the development of the system are likely to become more salient in influencing the users' satisfaction (Tversky and Kahneman, 1986). Variations in timing may be partly responsible for the weak association observed between involvement and satisfaction in the published studies.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

The model for this study tests certain influences that apply within a user community $^{2}$ (see Figure 1).

The within-user-community environment selected for the focus of this study eliminates a number of problems that have attended previous quantitatively based cross-sectional studies. Among them are the difficulties of identifying and measuring the contingent organizational and project-related variables (Tait and Vessey, 1988) and the timing of data collection with regard to the implementation date of the project.

In user-led development an individual end user's perception of representation may depend on a number of factors, such as the extent to which he/she was consulted by his/her representatives, his/her confidence in them and their knowledge of his/her requirements. Therefore, regarding systems planned through a user-led development approach we hypothesize that:

## H1. End-user satisfaction will be positively correlated with end-user perception of representation.

Planned organizational change theory recognizes resistance to change and attempts to create a suitable environment for change (Lewin, 1951; Schein, 1964). Management support for the system should help create a suitable environment. For this research, the perceived attitude of management to the system is designated as top management support. We hypothesize that the perceived level of top management support will be a factor motivating end-user satisfaction (Franz and Robey, 1986):

## H2. End-user satisfaction will be positively correlated with the end-user perception of top management support.

Organizational change theory also suggests that user system variables, such as user attitudes, may affect information satisfaction (Tait and Vessey, 1988). Two factors that may affect user attitudes are the perceived advantages of the system being developed and the user's past experience with computers and information systems. We hypothesize that an end-user attitude of high expectations for the system will be associated with high satisfaction (Hirschheim and Newman, 1988). One sometimes hears the opposite from practitioners who believe high expectations are frequently unrealistic and lead to low satisfaction. We address this possibility by eliciting both initial and current expectations.

## Methodology

## Study background

Because of the model tested in this study, a case study methodology was adopted to investigate the hypotheses. The within-user-community environment of a case study enables us to “partial out” many contingent factors and overcome the two main criticisms of previous cross-sectional studies discussed earlier, but at the loss of external validity. In addition, it enables us to focus on the issue of representation in user-led development.

Data for the study was obtained at a large government corporation with a number of years' experience in user-led development for new MIS software. The corporation, involved in all aspects of telecommunications, has an annual profit of approximately one billion Australian dollars and employs more than 90,000 people throughout Australia.

Two systems, S and P, were selected for individual detailed study. These systems were chosen because they were being developed at approximately the same time, but the development hardware environment, development location, and language was different. Also, both systems had a large enough user base to permit reliable statistical analysis. Naturally, each system was under the direction of different representative user groups. A common factor for both systems was that they were online systems replacing old batch-oriented systems. The S system was developed on a Honeywell computer using Cobol, while the P system was developed on a FUJITSU computer using ADABAS Natural. Originally it was intended that both systems be developed in a 4GL. However, the project team for system S refused to develop in any language other than Cobol because they had no prior experience with 4GLs and decided there was not sufficient time in the development schedule to learn a new language. Both systems were large applications, with over 40,000 Cobol procedural lines of code for system S and 36,000 ADABAS Natural lines of code for system P.

Both systems were used by three levels of management (district, state, and national), who were concerned with the establishment of corporate targets and the measurement of progress toward meeting those targets. The P system was a product sales performance system, while the S system was a service-performance monitoring and reporting system.

Three groups were principally involved in the systems development, implementation, and use—the end users, the information systems (IS) project group, and the user representative group. Each is discussed in turn.

The end users were district managers. Other end users included marketing managers and state managers. It was initially estimated that there were 100 to 200 end users of both systems located throughout Australia.

The IS project group was responsible for the software design and coding. The group was drawn from the Information Systems Department in the state where the system was being developed.

The user representative group was responsible for managing the project, determining the system requirements, communicating with the IS project group, training end users, and implementing the computer system. The user representative group represented the end users throughout the systems development life cycle. Its members, selected by management for their knowledge of the system's requirements, were drawn from the end-user population and assigned to the user representative group on a full-time basis. The user representative group consisted of six members for the S project and a similar number for the P project. Each group was located in the same building as the IS project group responsible for its system. The members of these groups were not dispersed back to the user community after project implementation and during this study because work was to continue on planned enhancements to the system. Therefore, the members of these groups were not surveyed as end users.

The role played by the user representative group was instrumental in eliminating the need and the opportunity for the IS project group to confer with the actual end users.

Of the four factors mentioned in the Introduction that may constitute a barrier to effective user representation (Land and Hirschheim, 1983), only the second one relating to many users over many locations would, a priori, appear to be relevant. The large number of users and their geographic dispersion does suggest that there may be particular difficulties facing the user representative group in achieving effective user representation.

## Measurement of the variables

## User Satisfaction

The instrument used to measure user satisfaction was the well-known Bailey and Pearson (1983) user information satisfaction (UIS) instrument. The questionnaire was modified to reflect Australian English usage and was verified with a sample of users who did not complete questionnaires in the actual data collection. Three of the dichotomous questions (six individual response scales) were removed from the calculation of the UIS score because they pertained to user representativeness and management support.

System usage was also used as an alternate measure of system satisfaction (Baroudi, et al., 1986), although the compulsory nature of much of the information provided by the system made its inclusion of doubtful value. The instrument used to measure system usage focused on offline rather than online usage (see Table 1).

While poor output design can lengthen system usage, it is not unreasonable to expect for any particular system that the amount of time a user spends examining reports may give some indication of the users' level of satisfaction with the system. The final question pertained to voluntary system usage, which Olson and Ives (1981) and Ives, et al. (1983) claim is the only meaningful indicator of system success.

## User Representativeness

The previously published instruments on user involvement were reviewed (Baroudi, et al., 1986; Koh and Lawrence, 1988; Maish, 1979; Olson and Ives, 1981). These focused on a number of activities to which the end users could respond objectively rather than subjectively to the representativeness perceived by the user. A new instrument was developed comprised of five questions answered on a 7-point Likert scale that measured representativeness by the extent to which users perceived they had been consulted and their views incorporated into the system (Table 2). This conforms broadly to the idea of representativeness as a psychological state (Barki and Hartwick, 1989).

The responses to the two dichotomous questions in the UIS instrument relating to user representativeness were added to the questions in Table 2, giving seven questions in total.

## Management Support

Two questions were included to ascertain the end users' perception of management support (see Table 3). To these was added the UIS instrument question on management support, giving three questions in total.

## System Expectations

Expectations in this study were intended to capture the user's assessment of the worth of the system once it is fully operational. Many systems are gradually phased in—the core system is initially installed and additional reports, functions, and performance improvements are implemented in subsequent releases. In answering the questions on expectations, the users were asked to cast their mind forward to the time when the system is fully operational. In this respect, expectations differ with user satisfaction since the latter measure gives an insight into the current satisfaction of the user.

Five questions based on the Schultz and Slevin (1975) instrument were used (see Table 4) to measure expectations regarding such aspects as accuracy, usefulness, success, and satisfying requirements. Both expectations prior to implementtation and current expectations post-implementation were captured. Because the subjects were asked to recall, after implementation, their prior-to-implementation expectations, these data may be subject to some biases.

## Previous Experiences With Computer Systems

Three questions using a 7-point Likert scale were developed to elicit this information (see Table 5). The first two questions have scales ranging from very low to very high, while the third question has a scale ranging from from very unsatisfactory to very satisfactory.

All the research instruments were designed in close consultation with a sample of users. The final forms were tested under pilot-run conditions and were judged to be valid and complete. The reliability scores, as measured by Cronbach's alpha coefficient, for the relevant questionnaire sections were 0.97 for user satisfaction, 0.89 for representativeness, 0.63 for management support, 0.75 for system expectations, and 0.77 for previous experience with computers. The high reliability score obtained for the user information satisfaction instrument lends further support to the reliability of the instrument developed by Bailey and Pearson (1983) and modified by Ives, et al. (1983). All the reliability scores are above the 0.6 level recommended by Nunnally (1967).

## Data collection

For the S system, 229 questionnaires were distributed to every possible end user. A similar number was distributed for the P system. Most of the questionnaires were dispatched in January 1989, approximately four months after the installation of the system. The respondents were requested to mail the completed questionnaires to the University. The respondents were assured the confidentiality of their response would be respected.

Because the respondents were identified on their questionnaire prior to mailing, it was possible to follow up on all those who did not respond. The goal was to secure a 100 percent response rate. A total of 96 end-user questionnaires were completed and returned for the S system. This represented 42 percent of those mailed out. We discovered later that there were a number of inaccuracies in the mailing list supplied by the organization. (Many of those on the list could not complete the form because they were only occasional or non-users.)

In the case of the P system, 59 user questionnaires were returned. Again, all recipients were contacted by telephone and it is believed that responses were obtained from all those who could validly complete the forms.

## Results

To test the results predicted by hypotheses H1 to H4, the correlations of each of the variables with the user information satisfaction score were calculated independently for each of the systems. These correlations are presented in the first column of Tables 6 and 7. Attention is restricted to pairs of variables that are significantly correlated in both the systems, thereby demonstrating a degree of durability to their relationship. The results provide support for H1 when measured by UIS (but not when measured by usage) $^{3}$ and demonstrate that user representativeness is, for both systems, the most highly correlated variable with user satisfaction. The perception of top management support (H2) is also correlated at the 0.001 level of significance, although the correlation is slightly lower than the correlation for user representativeness. Current expectations (H3b) of the system's value is correlated with user information satisfaction (0.01 level). However, expectations before system implementation (H3a) is not significantly correlated with user information satisfaction, possibly because the subjects were asked to recall these expectations after the system had actually been implemented. Also not significantly correlated is previous experience with computers (H4).

Tables 6 and 7 also show the inter-correlations of the various variables for each system. These two tables show strong agreement across the two systems concerning which pairs of variables are most highly correlated. There are differences between variables less significantly correlated, but we would anticipate some variation across different systems and users. Most notable of the inter-correlations is the relationship between user representativeness and top management support. It is interesting to speculate which is the dependent variable here: does the user feel better represented if the attitude of top management is seen to be supportive of the systems development, or does the user perceive that management is supporting the system if they feel well represented in the systems development? The relationship between expectations before implementation and current expectations is to be expected.

In view of the high inter-correlation between user representativeness and management support, we decided to test that user representativeness is the main factor affecting user information satisfaction: that is, the marginal influence of management support after user representativeness has been accounted for is small. This analysis was performed using a stepwise regression, where user information satisfaction was the dependent variable. As can be anticipated from Tables 6 and 7, the first variable to enter the equation was user representativeness ( $R^{2}=0.44$ , 0.56 for system S, P respectively), and management support was the second variable for both the S and the P systems before the limits were reached. The $R^{2}$ with the two independent variables in each equation was 0.47 (for S) and 0.56 (for P). Thus, management support increased the $R^{2}$ by less than 0.04 for both the S and the P systems. $^{4}$ Clearly, of all the variables, user representativeness best explains user information satisfaction. The regression coefficients obtained when user representativeness and top management support were included in the equation are presented in Table 8, as is the t-test showing that each of these variables is significant.

Another test of the independent contribution of user representativeness and management support to user satisfaction was carried out through a MANOVA analysis. This showed user representativeness to be significant (F = 4.98, significance <0.001), while management support and the interaction term were insignificant (F = 0.70, significance = 0.6 and F = 0.21, significance = 0.21 respectively). This test and the t-test on the regression coefficients indicates that user representativeness is the most important variable in explaining user satisfaction.

For both systems, the mean scores by category are given in Table 9. Also given are the responses to two questions asking the user's opinions on the ideal level of representativeness and ideal level of management support. The scores in this table (apart from system usage) present the average Likert score, where a score of 1 is very bad, 7 is very good, and 4 is the mid-point score. The table shows that the user's ideal level of representativeness and management support are substantially higher than the actual level; both these pairwise differences are statistically significant (t-test -20.11, significance <0.001 and 11.59, significance <0.001 respectively). At first glance it may seem confusing that the users did not rate these ideal levels as 7. The scale included descriptive items (e.g., 7 - extremely high, 6 - quite high, 5 - slightly high, etc.), and the users rated what they perceived to be the ideal level in their environment.

The results may be interpreted as displaying a level of realism on the part of users as to what is ideal. Or alternatively, they may be interpreted as an application of the anchor and adjustment heuristic (Tversky and Kahneman, 1986), displaying the typical bias associated with such an estimate. The actual scores for user representativeness indicate a low level for this variable for both systems. Because both the S and P systems employed the same user-led development approach, one would have expected similar scores for the two systems but at a higher level. The low representativeness scores suggest that some weaknesses may have been present in the implementation of the user-led development. This issue is discussed further in the discussion section.

The current expectations are better than the UIS score but not much different from the initial expectations. Given that both S and P were online systems, replacing earlier batch systems at the request of the users, the satisfaction scores appear quite low, although they are similar to Bailey and Pearson (1983). Bailey and Pearson reported a median score of satisfaction that, when re-scaled to a 7-point Likert scale, gives a score of 3.8.

## Discussion of Results

The research model (Figure 1) proposes that user representativeness and other attitudinal factors, such as the perception of top management support, expectations, and previous experience with computers affect user satisfaction. Of these factors only user representativeness (H1) and top management support (H2) were positively correlated with user satisfaction at the 0.001 level.

An interesting result of this study is the high level of the correlation scores for perceived user representativeness with user information satisfaction, which at 0.66 and 0.72 are higher than in any of the recently published studies on user involvement. Baroudi, et al. (1986) surveyed over 200 users in the same function from many organizations in a carefully controlled study and observed a correlation score of 0.18 between user satisfaction and user involvement. Franz and Robey (1986) obtained correlation scores of 0.33 and 0.24 between perceived system usefulness and user involvement in the design and implementation stages respectively.

The high scores reported in this paper are probably due to the methodology employed: (a) the within-user-community (i.e., within-project and within-organization) nature of the study, which eliminates the impact of many contingent variables of a project or organizational nature, (b) the manner in which user representativeness was measured, and (c) the uniform timing of data collection within a short time of project installation.

In addition the users of both systems were at the same middle management level and performed a similar type of function. In contrast, Baroudi, et al. (1986) conducted a survey of 200 users in the same function but from many organizations, where the data collection occurred at varying times after project installation. This possibly explains their low correlation of 0.18 between user satisfaction and user involvement.

Another possible reason for the high scores reported in this study relates to the fundamental characteristics of the phenomenon under investigation. No one else has studied user representativeness and its relation to satisfaction. Conceivably, it may have a stronger relationship to satisfaction than involvement.

There was a non-significant relationship between user information satisfaction and system usage, which failed to support the results of Baroudi, et al. (1986). The correlations were also calculated between user representativeness and system usage: this showed an insignificant negative correlation (-0.10 for S and -0.22 for P) between the two variables, which again failed to support

Baroudi, et al. The relationships with usage in our study are attributed to the compulsory nature of use of the system.

Mumford and Henshall (1979) suggest that to be effective a representative group should have a constituency, maintain an excellent relationship with them, and be viewed as well-able to represent their interests. This study strongly supports the importance of this relationship between user and representative because user representativeness is strongly correlated with user satisfaction. The low scores for user representativeness, together with the difference in the user's perceived actual and ideal user representativeness, suggests that the user representative group appears to have failed to provide many users with adequate representation. The positive and high correlation between representativeness and satisfaction suggests that had this been provided, it is likely that the overall user satisfaction would have been greater. However, it is possible that the relationship between representativeness and satisfaction is concave, with a peak in representativeness that is not at the users' stated ideal. If this is the case, it would not be advantageous to increase representativeness up to the users stated ideal because this would reduce satisfaction.

A possible weakness of this study is that user perceptions of representation may be colored by their satisfaction with the system. If they do not like the system they may, even unconsciously, downgrade their perception of their representation, particularly when measured by the subjective scale used in this study. The best way around this difficulty is to measure perceptions of representation during development and then relate each subject's later satisfaction to the earlier measure of representation to calculate the association. Tait and Vessey (1988) made a similar observation on their attitude variable.

However, as an alternative for this study, a post-sample telephone questionnaire was carried out to investigate this possibility. The population of users was stratified into three groups based on the user representativeness score, and 12 users were randomly sampled from the highest and lowest groups. These users were telephoned and asked to answer a structured questionnaire that pursued in some depth the type and degree of representation they had with the system development. There was a very clear pattern to the responses between the highest and the lowest groups. Most of the lowest group had no contact at all with their representative; indeed some were not even aware they had a representative. On the other hand, the users in the highest group were mostly from the largest cities, knew their representatives, and had considerable contact with them concerning the system. Thus, we felt that the user representativeness scores obtained appeared to be reliable.

Another weakness this study has in common with other case studies is that the results may be particular to the projects and organization studied. However, the fact that two projects were studied to some extent reduces the project dependence, particularly since the project teams were located in different states and used different development languages. The end-user community overlapped to some extent and naturally were within the same organizational culture. Another possible criticism is that the study may have measured a transitory satisfaction (caused by representativeness) influenced by a recency bias. That is, the satisfaction measured may only remain at that level for a short term before changing over time as system quality dominates. However, securing early acceptance of a new system is an important goal.

A further possible weakness of this study is the low level of user representativeness, which may suggest there have been some failings or special problems in the implementation of the user-led development approach: thus the result may be particular to this situaiton. Some insight can be gained into possible reasons for the low user representativeness score if the barriers to effective representation (Land and Hirshheim, 1983) mentioned in the Introduction are considered. In this case, a number of them could have played a role in reducing the users' perception of representation:

1. The user representative group was, to many users, unrecognizable from the MIS department. Many did not even know of its existence.

2. The large number of users scattered over a country the size of Australia would make communications and coordination difficult and tend to isolate the user representative group.

3. The lack of motivation as a potential barrier does not appear to have been an influence: it would appear from the actual and ideal scores that users were motivated to take a greater interest in their representation than they were offered.

4. Because no attempt was made to formally define a constituency for each user representative or to formally establish and maintain contacts with each potential user, we conclude that the user representative group did not evidence an understanding of the concept of responsibility and the need to develop and manage its relationships with the users.

It is possible that within the concept of user-led development lies some of the roots of the difficulties of user representation evidenced in this study. If the systems analysts form the group responsible for developing the user requirements, they are forced by their lack of knowledge of the users' requirements to consult the users and develop these relationships. However, if the most knowledgeable users are selected to form this group, they, conscious of the basis of their selection, may feel it unnecessary or even inadvisable to consult other users. In time they could be viewed as isolated and not representing the needs of the users at large.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrates the importance of the users' perceptions of representation in explaining user satisfaction and encourages us to assert that careful attention needs to be given to the management of user representation as a key means of increasing user satisfaction. Achieving effective user representation would appear to involve much more than organizationally establishing a user group to lead and manage the development. This is particularly true where the system's users are many and geographically dispersed.

Good management of relationships with the end user would appear to be just as essential in user-led development as in traditional development. This involves ensuring that the users understand the working of the user representative group and have faith in its ability to represent their interests in the systems development process. The user representative group must ensure that the users feel involved and adequately represented in the systems development process, understand the systems objectives, receive adequate training, and are provided with adequate system documentation. If the user representative group in the projects studied had been established, and each member had a constituency and an understanding of all this entails, much of the low perception of user representativeness may have been avoided with a corresponding increase in user satisfaction.
