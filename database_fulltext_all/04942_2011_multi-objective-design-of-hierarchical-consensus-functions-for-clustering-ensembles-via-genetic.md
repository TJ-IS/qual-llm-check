# Multi-objective design of hierarchical consensus functions for clustering ensembles via genetic programming

Keywords: Cluster analysis Clustering ensembles Multi-objective clustering Hierarchical fusion Partition selection Genetic programming

## a b s t r a c t

This paper investigates a genetic programming (GP) approach aimed at the multi-objective design of hierarchical consensus functions for clustering ensembles. By this means, data partitions obtained via different clustering techniques can be continuously re<sup>fi</sup>ned (via selection and merging) by a population of fusion hierarchies having complementary validation indices as objective functions. To assess the potential of the novel framework in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness, a series of systematic experiments, involving eleven variants of the proposed GP-based algorithm and a comparison with basic as well as advanced clustering methods (of which some are clustering ensembles and/or multi-objective in nature), have been conducted on a number of arti<sup>fi</sup>cial, benchmark and bioinformatics datasets. Overall, the results corroborate the perspective that having fusion hierarchies operating on well-chosen subsets of data partitions is a <sup>fi</sup>ne strategy that may yield signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gains in terms of clustering robustness.

## 1. Introduction

Among the several data mining tasks that have been heavily investigated lie those involving the partitioning of data into groups via a process named clustering [29,41]. In a nutshell, the main goal of clustering is to <sup>fi</sup>nd natural groupings of multidimensional data, called clusters, so that both the homogeneity within each cluster and the heterogeneity among different clusters are maximized. That is, instances (i.e., patterns, objects) that belong to the same cluster should be more similar to each other than the instances appearing in different clusters. Albeit simple to state, this is an ill-posed problem to pursue due mainly to its unsupervised nature and to the lack of a precise de<sup>fi</sup>nition of what a cluster really is [5,34]. Actually, data can reveal clusters with different shapes and sizes, and the number of clusters depends very much on the resolution with which the data are effectively analyzed. As a consequence, different clustering algorithms have been proposed and new algorithms continue to emerge, each associated with different assumptions on data and different clustering validation criteria [12,28].

Even though conventional clustering algorithms have been successfully applied in a range of scenarios [41], the choice of the algorithm (and thus the clustering criterion) best suited to a given dataset is still a non-trivial task to realize. This is even more serious when dealing with the discovery of more than one underlying structure that can be present in the data. In fact, as Law et al. [34] have pointed out, “inability to detect clusters with diverse shapes and sizes is a fundamental limitation of every clustering algorithm irrespective of the clustering criterion (objective function) used.” In order to overcome these problems, more advanced strategies for cluster analysis have been recently proposed and assessed in the literature.

Roughly speaking, these advanced strategies work by combining several clustering criteria in order to avoid the choice of one single criterion when nothing is known about the underlying structure present in the data and to guarantee the simultaneous retrieval of all the structures when more than one is available. These strategies encompass clustering ensemble algorithms [15,20], like those proposed by Strehl and Ghosh [38], Fern and Brodley [17], Topchy et al. [40], and Frossyniotis et al. [19]; multi-objective clustering methods [15,34], like MOCK (Multi-Objective Clustering with automatic K-determination) [24]; and multi-objective clustering ensemble models, like MOCLE (Multi-Objective CLustering Ensemble) [13,14].

It is worth noticing that all these alternatives still rely on the use of basic clustering algorithms. In fact, the traditional algorithms are very effective in uncovering the type of structure they were designed to <sup>fi</sup>nd, if this structure is indeed present in the data. The main problem is that usually the real data are not “well-behaved” and nothing is known a-priori about the structures they hide to guide the choice of the best algorithm to adopt.

Typical clustering ensemble algorithms deal with the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in the choice of the most suitable clustering algorithm while still focusing on the discovery of one single structure. The multi-objective approaches, in contrast, try to solve the two issues at once. As a matter of fact, all the aforementioned alternatives were constructed by forming various combinations of partitions produced via traditional algorithms. However, none of them makes explicit which partitions to select and how these partitions are combined. Moreover, a single strategy is used to merge all the partitions, even in the multi-objective case, where the combinations are made progressively.

In this paper, we propose a cluster analysis framework that, like MOCLE, hybridizes the clustering ensemble and multi-objective clustering strategies. However, differently from the previously discussed alternatives, our aim is to synthesize the ensembles that will ultimately produce the partitions. In this framework, henceforth referred to as MCHPF (for “Multi-objective Clustering with Hierarchical Partition Fusions”), a Pareto-based version of genetic programming (GP) [33,36,43] is used to evolve a population of ensembles, taking into account complementary clustering validation measures. Each ensemble, in this case, is a hierarchy of consensus functions applied to a subset of base partitions, also produced with traditional clustering algorithms. That is, MCHPF automatically designs hierarchical consensus functions possibly considering different consensus functions already available in the literature.

By evolving the population in a multi-objective way, the proposed approach can <sup>fi</sup>nd several structures present in the data, in accordance with more than one clustering criterion. Moreover, since the resulting ensembles are sets of individuals, we can easily observe which partitions were fused and how they were fused. Finally, several different strategies for combination can be used in one single hierarchical consensus function.

To evaluate our proposal, systematic experiments on arti<sup>fi</sup>cial, benchmark and bioinformatics datasets with varying structures have been performed, comparing the quality of the partitions obtained by MCHPF with those delivered by traditional as well as other ensemble and multi-objective algorithms. Moreover, we assess the potentials of several distinct con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of the conceptual components of the proposed framework so as to understand how the choice of these components may impact the levels of performance achieved.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y revisit two of the fundamental limitations exhibited by traditional clustering algorithms and then comment upon some important clustering ensemble and multi-objective clustering approaches. Also in this section, the basic concepts and steps of GP are introduced. Section 3 is devoted to the detailed characterization of the proposed framework, providing the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of its standard con<sup>fi</sup>guration as well as other ten variants. In this context, a contrast between MCHPF and MOCLE is provided, highlighting their distinctive conceptual aspects. In Section 4, we report on several simulation experiments we have conducted to evaluate the potential of the novel framework. After explaining how the experiments have been con<sup>fi</sup>gured in terms of the datasets and clustering methods used, the control parameter settings, and the criterion adopted for measuring the quality of the resulting partitions, we present and discuss in a step-by-step manner the results obtained. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and provides remarks on future work.

## 2. Related work

The following subsections outline important aspects related, respectively, to basic clustering algorithms, clustering ensembles, multi-objective clustering, and genetic programming.

## 2.1. Limitations of traditional clustering algorithms

Traditional cluster analysis operates by seeking the structure present in the data that agrees most closely with some speci<sup>fi</sup>ed clustering criterion (that is, is in line with one of the possible cluster de<sup>fi</sup>nitions). However, the practical application of such analysis suffers from two main drawbacks [19,28,34]. First, as there is no single cluster de<sup>fi</sup>nition, it is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to choose the clustering algorithm (optimizing only one clustering criterion) that is more appropriate to discover the underlying structure of the data at hand. Second, in several practical cases, the data themselves can present several underlying structures, each characterized by a different cluster de<sup>fi</sup>nition or at a different re<sup>fi</sup>nement level.

To cope with the <sup>fi</sup>rst problem, one alternative is to apply several clustering algorithms to the data and select the best partition generated according to a single validation measure. However, the validation measures employed to choose the best result(s) are usually biased towards one of the cluster de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, since many of these measures are just more elaborated formulations of the available clustering criteria [12,25]. For instance, the intra-cluster variance, which is a measure that evaluates the compactness of clusters in a partition, has the same properties of the clustering criterion adopted by the k-means algorithm [41]. Another alternative to cope with the problem of clustering algorithm selection is to combine several partitions induced in consonance with different clustering criteria into a <sup>fi</sup>nal solution with, hopefully, improved quality. Clustering ensembles [15,20], discussed in the following, are the simplest way to accomplish this.

## 2.2. Clustering ensembles

Instead of choosing the single best partition, clustering ensemble algorithms aim at <sup>fi</sup>nding a partition that represents the consensus among previously-obtained partitions. These algorithms usually consist of two steps: 1) the generation of a diverse set of base partitions; and 2) the application of a consensus function (fusion operator) to fuse such partitions into a consensus one [22,40]. The set of base partitions can be homogeneous (when generated by the same clustering algorithm) or heterogeneous (when different clustering algorithms are employed). Heterogeneous ensembles are the best suited for dealing with the problem of different cluster de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and, therefore, were adopted in this work. With respect to the consensus function, the main existing methods are based on coassociation, graph/hypergraph partitioning, mutual information or relabeling [40].

One of the <sup>fi</sup>rst and most popular approaches for clustering ensembles is that proposed by Strehl and Ghosh [38]. In their work, the authors formalize the clustering ensemble problem as a combinatorial optimization problem in terms of shared mutual information. In order to tackle the combinatorial complexity of the problem, they propose three algorithms (consensus functions): CSPA (Cluster-based Similarity Partitioning Algorithm); HGPA (Hyper-Graph Partitioning Algorithm); and MCLA (Meta-Clustering Algorithm). A supra-consensus (SC) function was also devised to select the best partition among the results of the earlier three functions.

The CSPA algorithm starts with the construction of a new similarity matrix over the objects according to the base partitions. Each entry of this matrix denotes the fraction of partitions in which two objects are assigned to the same cluster. This matrix is then employed to cluster the objects using any similarity-based clustering algorithm, delivering the consensus partition. In HGPA, the fusion is treated as a problem of partitioning a hypergraph whose hyperedges represent the clusters of the base partitions. The hypergraph is partitioned by cutting a minimal number of hyperedges. Finally, MCLA considers the fusion of partitions as a problem of <sup>fi</sup>nding the correspondence among the clusters of the base partitions.

By other means, Fern and Brodley devised the Hybrid Bipartite Graph Formulation (HBGF) clustering ensemble method, which is also based on graph partitioning [17]. First, HBGF constructs a bipartite graph from the set of base partitions, modeling their objects and clusters as vertices. Then, it partitions the graph using a familiar graph partitioning technique. The resulting division of the objects is deemed as the consensus partition.

One weakness of these clustering ensemble methods is that they may not be able to capture the heterogeneous structures present in the data, in spite of the individual partitions being induced according to diverse criteria. That is, the ensembles do not consider different criteria for different regions of the feature space, similar to what happens with the traditional clustering methods [34]. Moreover, a large number of base partitions of poor quality in the ensemble can also result in a poor consensus partition, even if among the base partitions there are few with excellent quality. This issue of selecting good sets of partitions has been termed as the clustering ensemble selection problem and three approaches to cope with it have been recently investigated [18].

## 2.3. Multi-objective clustering

As mentioned before, the multi-objective clustering focuses on the simultaneous optimization of a number of clustering criteria to generate a set of alternative structures possibly representing diverse interpretations over the data [15]. One seminal approach is that of Law et al. [34], which is composed of two steps. While the <sup>fi</sup>rst involves the discovery of several clusters by different basic clustering algorithms, the second produces an optimal partition from these clusters by solving a speci<sup>fi</sup>c discrete optimization problem.

Evolutionary algorithms (EA) have been widely used to handle multi-objective optimization problems in general [6,7]. Indeed, since they operate on a population of possible solutions, several members of the Pareto optimal set can be retrieved in a single run. Moreover, these algorithms are less susceptible to the shape and (dis)continuity of the Pareto front and can have their components customized in order to yield high-quality and diverse non-dominated solutions. Due to these good properties, EA-based methods like MOCK [24] and MOCLE [14,16] have been devised for the purpose of multi-objective clustering. MOCK and most of the investigations made with MOCLE have considered two validation indices as the objective functions to be minimized: 1) overall deviation (which measures the overall summed distances between data instances and their corresponding cluster center); and 2) connectivity (which evaluates the degree to which neighboring data instances have been placed in the same cluster). In short, these two objectives balance each other's tendency to increase or decrease the number of clusters, avoiding convergence to trivial solutions.

MOCK uses the PESA-II (Pareto Envelope-based Selection Algorithm version 2) elitist multi-objective genetic algorithm (GA) [6] as optimization engine and constructs its initial population using minimum spanning trees and the k-means algorithm. By this means, partitions are initially generated in different regions of the Pareto front. Moreover, MOCK adopts the uniform crossover and neighborhoodbiased mutation operator as genetic operators, whereas a locus-based adjacency representation is used for individual encoding. According to this representation, individuals are vectors of n integer elements, with n being the number of instances to be clustered. The i-th gene with value j means that nodes (i.e., instances) i and j are connected by a link in the graph. Then, by decoding (i.e., applying a graph partitioning algorithm on) an individual, several subgraphs may be produced, each denoting a different cluster. MOCK has received great attention recently and a semi-supervised version of it has also been investigated [23].

While similar to MOCK in several aspects, MOCLE aggregates the strategies of clustering ensemble and multi-objective clustering. Like the traditional ensembles, MOCLE encompasses the steps of generation of a diverse set of base partitions and determination of the consensus partition. The main differences from other clustering ensemble methods is that MOCLE looks for a set of consensus partitions instead of only one and combines the pairs of partitions, iteratively, during the optimization process, instead of the usual combination of all partitions at the same time [16].

For optimization, MOCLE uses the algorithm NSGA-II (Nondominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm version 2) [9] and can use several conceptually different clustering algorithms for the construction of the base partitions (initial population of the GA). Each individual is represented by an array of sets, and each set represents a cluster and contains the labels of its objects. The only genetic operator that MOCLE uses is a special crossover operator designed to <sup>fi</sup>nd the consensus between two parent partitions. In Ref. [16], the MCLA operator was used for this purpose. Despite performing several levels of partition fusion, during the optimization process, to produce the consensus partitions, a weakness of MOCLE is that it does not make explicit which partitions were effectively used to induce the <sup>fi</sup>nal solutions.

## 2.4. Genetic programming

Genetic programming is the machine learning branch of evolutionary computation oriented towards the induction of computer programs to optimally perform user-de<sup>fi</sup>ned tasks [32]. It is a domainindependent methodology viewed as an extension of genetic algorithms in which the evolving structures are not <sup>fi</sup>xed-length strings that encode candidate solutions to a problem, but rather programs that, when executed, solve the problem directly. These programs (aka individuals) usually assume the form of syntax trees, that is, hierarchies of nodes and arcs. While internal nodes (referred to as functions) indicate the operations to be executed and the trees' leaves (labeled as terminals) stand for input variables, numerical constants or zero-argument functions, the arcs denote the arguments of each function.

Genetic programming starts from a high-level statement of the requirements of a problem. This is usually accomplished by specifying [33]: 1) the set of terminals; 2) the set of functions; 3) the <sup>fi</sup>tness function; 4) the set of control parameters; and 5) the termination criteria. In particular, the sets of terminals and functions de<sup>fi</sup>ne the GP grammar and must satisfy two important requirements, namely, the closure and suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency properties [36]. While the latter requires that the functions and terminals chosen should be convenient for the problem domain in sight and that the search space they de<sup>fi</sup>ne is large enough to encompass the optimal solution to the problem, the former states that each function of the function set must be able to process all possible argument values generated by the terminal set or the other functions.

After this preparatory phase, a GP instance can be launched. Once running, no human intervention is needed and a series of problemindependent steps is executed, including: 1) population initialization; 2) program execution and <sup>fi</sup>tness assignment; 3) <sup>fi</sup>tness-proportionate parent selection; 4) application of genetic operators; and 5) offspring creation. These steps are executed iteratively until at least one of the termination criteria holds true.

Concerning population initialization, three techniques are usually employed in GP [33]. With the “full” method, each branch of a novel tree (individual) has the same height imposed by a “maximum initial height” parameter. The contents of intermediate nodes of the tree are randomly selected exclusively from the function set, whereas leaf nodes are taken only from the set of terminals. This method thus grants the generation of well-balanced trees. Conversely, the “grow” method allows the selection of intermediate nodes from the whole set of functions and terminals until the height limit is reached, thereafter proceeding like “full.” Trees created with this method may be very unbalanced, with some branches much longer than others. The “ramped half-and-half” technique, in turn, makes use of the other two for creating the population. An equal number of individuals are initialized for each level between two and the initial tree maximum height. For each level considered, half of the individuals are initialized using “full,” and the other half using “grow.” This method is diversity-oriented, since it creates trees with heights varying from one to the initial maximum limit allowed.

When it comes to the subject of population variation operators, these typically include analogs of naturally occurring phenomena, such as crossover (sexual recombination), mutation and reproduction, albeit some advanced architecture-altering methods, such as permutation, edition and encapsulation, may also be employed [36]. Given copies of two parent trees, crossover involves randomly selecting a crossover point in each parent tree and swapping the sub-trees rooted at the given crossover points. On the other hand, reproduction involves simply cloning certain individuals into the new population, whereas mutation can be implemented in two different ways: 1) a random terminal or function is selected and replaced by another (randomly selected) terminal or function; or 2) a randomly selected sub-tree is replaced by a randomly generated sub-tree.

Finally, in what regards <sup>fi</sup>tness assignment, this is usually accomplished by interpreting, executing and then assessing each individual program in terms of how well it undertakes the task at hand. In most of the circumstances, this evaluation yields a unique numerical value produced by the <sup>fi</sup>tness function speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the preparatory stage. However, for many practical problems, a multiobjective assessment is required, combining two or more criteria that may compete with one another to some degree. Since GP can be considered as an extended version of GA, many of the mechanisms created for the latter to cope with multi-objective problems can be readily incorporated into the former [43]. In this work, in particular, some of the mechanisms adopted in MCHPF have been borrowed from NSGA-II [9], as discussed in the sequel.

## 3. Hierarchical partition fusion via multi-objective genetic programming

As mentioned earlier, MCHPF can be considered as a novel hybrid approach combining the strategies of clustering ensembles and multiobjective clustering into a unique GP-based framework. By making use of individuals arranged in the form of trees, MCHPF allows that novel hierarchical consensus functions (thus, nested ensembles) are automatically designed taking as basis consensus functions already available in the literature. Moreover, by structuring and evolving the population through a multi-objective stance, the approach is capable of <sup>fi</sup>nding a representative set of high-quality partitions for the different structures that may be available for a given dataset.

Fig. 1 summarizes the main steps behind MCHPF, whereas Table 1 describes the mathematical notation used hereafter in this paper. Initially, several data partitions (called base partitions) are generated by applying distinct clustering algorithms on the dataset. Then, a population of fusion hierarchies is randomly generated by resorting to one or more consensus functions that act as primitive functions of the GP grammar. By this means, each individual represents a new way of merging subsets of the base partitions. Next, the individuals are assessed by calculating complementary cluster validation indices over the partitions they yield. As a result, the individuals are organized into fronts and have their <sup>fi</sup>tness and crowding distance values calculated. In a word, the crowding distance value of an individual (partition solution) provides an estimate of the density of solutions surrounding it [9]. Afterwards, novel hierarchies (offspring) are produced through the application of selection and genetic operators, and the quality of the partitions they deliver is also measured. ## 3.1. GP grammar specification, population initialization, and individual interpretation

Even though there are other population-based metaheuristics that could display better performance in terms of the elicitation of a more representative set of Pareto-optimal solutions [7], since the main goal of this work is to investigate the gains that fusion hierarchies can have over simple clustering ensemble models, a multi-objective version of the standard GP technique was adopted as MCHPF's search engine. This choice also allows a more fair comparison with the multi-objective clustering ensemble approaches considered here, viz. MOCK and MOCLE, which are evolutionary in nature as well (see Subsection 2.3).

For the purpose of inducing GP programs in the form of hierarchical clustering ensembles, a set of data partitions of varying quality, with distinct levels of re<sup>fi</sup>nement, and possibly involving a large assortment of cluster types and densities, should be generated at the outset and then incorporated as terminals $X = \{ X _ { 1 } , X _ { 2 } , . . . , X _ { N _ { \tau } } \}$ of the GP grammar. These base partitions should be highly diverse (hopefully, capturing complementary structural “views” from the dataset) and may be produced by resorting to clustering methods associated with different criteria, con<sup>fi</sup>gured with different initial seeds or other control parameter values, and/or operating on different subsamplings of the original dataset [16,40,41].

In particular, for the experiments reported in this work, the base partitions were generated with the following algorithms, which were con<sup>fi</sup>gured with different values for their control parameters [11,41]: 1) k-means (KM); 2) hierarchical single linkage (HSL); 3) hierarchical average linkage (HAL); and 4) shared nearest neighbors (SNN). These algorithms have also been used in the context of MOCLE [13,16] and the main rationale for choosing them is because they are associated with complementary clustering criteria (see next subsection) and thus can <sup>fi</sup>nd groups with varying shapes and separation levels. While HSL and SNN do not pose limitations on the cluster shapes, KM and HAL are biased toward the discovery of spherical groups. Conversely, while the former do not usually work well when the groups are too close to each other, the latter may identify clusters in such conditions, mainly when they are spherically shaped and compact.

When it comes to the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the function set of the GP grammar, in theory, it is possible to take advantage of any consensus function already available or yet to be devised. This is because the outcome of any consensus function is a partition and the application of several functions in a hierarchy will always end up with a partition as a result. This is important to grant the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of the closure property, as required by GP.

In the standard version of MCHPF, however, only the HBGF operator is employed. So, the cardinality of the set of functions, N , is one. HBGF has been chosen because, according to Topchy et al. [40], consensus functions based on graphs/hypergraphs tend to be more robust than those based on co-association matrices. In addition, HBGF does not operate on the original data values, only on the cluster labels associated with the objects, which helps to reduce the complexity of MCHPF.

In the context of the standard version of MCHPF (and the other variants discussed later), any function in a hierarchy has only two or three arguments. That is, each time HBGF is invoked, it receives at least two and at most three partitions to fuse into a consensus partition. The partitions to be merged may be among the base partitions or those produced by previous fusions in the same hierarchy. This means that there is no imposition that all base partitions be merged in all individuals. It also implies that good teams of base partitions can be automatically searched for while assembling the hierarchies whereas the base partitions that do not contribute to improving the ensemble diversity [22] can be progressively avoided, discarded, or replaced along the evolutionary process. As a direct consequence of these properties, MCHPF tends to be more successful in preserving the diversity of cluster types in the resulting consensus partitions than the HBGF (or other consensus functions, such as MCLA), when this operator is applied alone on the whole set of the base partitions.

Moreover, for each application of HBGF in a tree, the number of clusters of the resulting partition is set as the simple average of the numbers of clusters of the partitions taken as arguments. For instance, applying HBGF on two partitions, one with <sup>fi</sup>ve and the other with seven groups, would produce a consensus partition with six groups in total. The average was chosen due to its simplicity in providing a consensus among the different numbers of clusters that might be associated with different partitions. A more diversity-oriented option would be to choose a random number of clusters from those associated with the partitions to be fused.

Having speci<sup>fi</sup>ed the GP grammar, the next step is to generate the population P with $N _ { P }$ individuals. This initial population might contain hierarchies with different shapes and complexities (i.e., with varying numbers of terminals, functions, and nested fusions). In the standard version of MCHPF, however, we adopt the “full” method as described in Subsection 2.4. Although this method gives birth to full trees of pre<sup>fi</sup>xed heights, along the generations of the evolutionary process, other non-balanced structural forms may be generated through the application of the genetic operators. This implies that, in a second moment, a <sup>fi</sup>rst-class single base partition may enter the population as a special kind of tree with only one node.

Fig. 2 provides a sample of hierarchies that might be available in the population. Assuming that $X _ { 1 } = ( 1 , 1 , 1 , 2 , 2 , 3 , 3 )$ $X _ { 3 } =$ (1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3), $X _ { 6 } = ( 2 , 2 , 2 , 3 , 3 , 1 , 1 )$ , and $X _ { 8 } = ( 3 , 3 , 2 , 2 , 4 , 4 , 1 )$ , the interpretation and “execution” of the third individual (Fig. $2 ( \mathsf { c } ) )$ would <sup>fi</sup>rst yield an intermediate partition $X ^ { * } = ( 3 , 3 , 1 , 1 , 2 , 2 , 2 )$ as the result of the application of HBGF on the eighth, third, and sixth base partitions. Next, $X ^ { * }$ would be merged with $X _ { 1 } ,$ bringing about the <sup>fi</sup>nal partition associated with the whole hierarchy (that is, the one that would be assessed by the <sup>fi</sup>tness criteria described in the next subsection). It is interesting to note that, since the consensus functions are nonlinear in nature, swapping the positions of $X _ { 1 }$ and $X _ { 3 }$ in the hierarchy of the third individual might have a substantial impact on the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>nal partition delivered by it.

## 3.2. Fitness assessment and population stratification

For the multi-objective assessment of the quality of the partitions returned by the GP individuals, distinct validation measures should be employed. So far, due to their complementary roles, we have resorted to overall deviation and connectivity, also adopted in MOCK and MOCLE. While the former primarily rates the levels of cluster density and thus encourages the induction of spherical groups, the latter gauges the levels of linkage between samples within a group, allowing the elicitation of clusters with arbitrary shapes. It is interesting to mention that while KM and HSL comply with the overall deviation measure, HAL and SNN are rooted in connectivity.

In a formal basis, the overall deviation of a partition π is measured by summing up the intra-cluster variation related to all its groups. Mathematically, this can be stated as: $\begin{array} { r } { d e v ( \pi ) = \sum _ { c _ { k } \in \pi } \sum _ { x _ { i } \in c _ { k } } d ( x _ { i } , \mu _ { k } ) } \end{array}$ where $x _ { i }$ is a given data sample belonging to the kth group, $c _ { k } ,$ whose centroid is $\mu _ { k } ,$ and $d ( . , . )$ denotes a distance metric to quantify how close $x _ { i }$ is to $\mu _ { k }$ (usually, the Euclidean distance) [24]. On the other hand, since connectivity evaluates whether neighbor points in the data space belong to the same cluster or not, it can be formulated as: $\begin{array} { r } { c o n ( \pi ) = \sum _ { i } \sum _ { j } a ( x _ { i } , n n _ { i j } ) , i = 1 , . . . , n , j = 1 , . . . , \nu , } \end{array}$ where υ is an input parameter that stands for the number of neighbors considered for each data point, while nn denotes the jth nearest neighbor of $x _ { i \cdot }$ The inner function $a ( x _ { i } , n n _ { i j } )$ equals to 1/j, if $\nexists c _ { k } | x _ { i } , n n _ { i j } \in c _ { k } ,$ or zero, otherwise [24].

These are internal indices [29], meaning that they assess the quality of a partition based only on the original data themselves. Besides, in their calculus, no prior knowledge is assumed about the structure underlying the data, which grants the unsupervised nature of MCHPF. Moreover, by employing these indices together, it is possible to strike a balance in the number of groups of a partition: While overall deviation usually favors an increase in the number of groups, the partitions with fewer clusters usually entail an improvement in connectivity.

As already mentioned, once the quality of the consensus partitions delivered by the GP individuals has been measured, the population is strati<sup>fi</sup>ed into groups, called fronts, following a strategy compliant to NSGA-II [9]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst front contains those individuals that are not dominated by any other member of the current population. In particular, since the two objectives of overall deviation and connectivity should be concurrently optimized, an individual P is said to be non-dominated (i.e. Pareto optimal) if there is no other individual P<sub>j</sub> yielding gains in some objective without incurring losses in the other in relation to P . So, the <sup>fi</sup>rst front is composed of partitions representing the best compromises to overall deviation and connectivity. Conversely, the second front contains the remaining individuals that are dominated only by the members of the <sup>fi</sup>rst front, with this process of front formation going on until all individuals of the current population are assigned to a front. The <sup>fi</sup>tness of an individual of a front equals the rank of the front it is assigned to, implying that a minimization process is in course.

## 3.3. Genetic and selection operators

After <sup>fi</sup>tness assignment and population strati<sup>fi</sup>cation, some individuals are selected to undergo the application of genetic operators in such a way as to breed novel individuals (offspring). Like <sup>fi</sup>tness assignment, parental selection for reproduction in MCHPF also agrees with NSGA-II [9]. Thus, it is based on random tournaments of two individuals and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their values of <sup>fi</sup>tness and crowding distance. Since minimization is in course, the individual with lower <sup>fi</sup>tness value wins the tournament; in case of a tie, the one with lower crowding distance value prevails.

As mentioned earlier, the crowding distance estimates the density of the region of the search space where each individual resides: The higher the density, the higher the value of this distance [9]. It is <sup>fi</sup>rst calculated for each normalized objective function by sorting the individuals in ascending values of this function. The solutions with the lowest and highest objective function values are given in<sup>fi</sup>nite crowding distance values so that they are always selected. The crowding distance value for the other solutions is set as the average distance (calculated over the objective function values) to their two neighboring solutions. The <sup>fi</sup>nal crowding distance value of an individual is computed by adding up the values calculated for each objective function separately.

In the standard version of MCHPF, the offspring is effectively generated through the application of the standard genetic operators of crossover and mutation (the reproduction operator has been eschewed since an elitist selection of individuals for the next generation is already used.) The choice of the genetic operator to be used is also done probabilistically (75% for crossover and 25% for mutation), and, in the end of this stage, N newborn individuals are created and have the quality of their partitions measured as discussed previously.

## 3.4. Alternative configurations of MCHPF

Besides the standard version, hereafter referred to as MCHPF1, we have conceived and empirically assessed other ten MCHPF variants, which are described in Table 2. They differ in <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions: the method used to create the initial population; the maximum heights of the initial trees; the size of the population; the consensus function(s) used as primitive function(s); and the solutions delivered as the output of the GP process.

With respect to population initialization, all variants but one of MCHPF either use the “full” or the “ramped half-and-half” methods, as described in Subsection 2.4. (We have also experimented with the “grow” method, but the results achieved with it were, in general, similar to those achieved with “ramped half-and-half”.) An alternative method, developed for MCHPF3 and based upon the “full” method, has also been used. In this case, 90% of the initial population is generated via the “full” method, while the other 10% are formed solely by single-node trees — more precisely, by those base partitions showing the best tradeoff in terms of overall deviation and connectivity. Besides, aiming at generating parsimonious hierarchies, we have <sup>fi</sup>xed the maximum heights of the initial trees at a low value, either two (for the “full” method) or <sup>fi</sup>ve (for “ramped half-and-half”). Actually, preliminary experiments showed that higher values for this parameter usually do not incur gains in terms of effectiveness (i.e., better consensus partitions) but may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the temporal ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the MCHPF approach (due to the manipulation of large trees through the genetic operators).

Moreover, all variants but MCHPF9 generate the initial population of fusion hierarchies from the set of base partitions produced by KM, LM, LS, and SNN, as previously stated. In the case of MCHPF9, we make use of the resampling technique as investigated by Fern and Brodley [17]. More precisely, the base partitions are created by applying KM on different datasets derived by randomly resampling from the original dataset.

Regarding the population size, N , 50 or 100 individuals have been adopted in the different MCHPF con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. From preliminary experiments, we could observe that employing populations with more than 100 individuals usually increases the computational time of the algorithm substantially, without providing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gains in terms of clustering performance. Besides, these experiments revealed that, for a small population size (50), having high mutation rates jointly with the “full” initialization method could lead to better performance than having low/high mutation rates jointly with the “ramped half-and-half” method. This is the main reason why we have adopted a high mutation rate (25%) for MCHPF1 and all other variants con<sup>fi</sup>gured with the “full” initialization method. For the MCHPF variants con<sup>fi</sup>gured with the “ramped half-and-half”, a low mutation rate (5%) jointly with a larger population size (100) were more useful.

The MCHPF variants also differ in terms of the fusion operators used as primitive functions in the GP grammar. Besides HBGF, MCLA has also been adopted, and the variants may use either or both of these consensus functions. In those circumstances where both MCLA and HBGF are used as functions of the GP grammar, heterogeneous ensemble hierarchies of varying complexities are allowed to be induced.

The last dimension of variation alludes to the number and type of the partitions returned as <sup>fi</sup>nal result of the algorithm. In particular, the MCHPF variants may differ in returning only the individuals (partitions) pertaining to the <sup>fi</sup>rst (Pareto optimal) front or all individuals of the last population. The main rationale for the latter option has to do with the fact that clustering criteria other than the two adopted here as objective functions (namely, overall deviation and connectivity) may be actually associated with the data structures (partitions) to be uncovered. In fact, preliminary experiments on some datasets revealed that good clustering solutions (in terms of the validation index used for the <sup>fi</sup>nal partition assessment — see Subsection 4.1) could also be retrieved from the Pareto fronts of the <sup>fi</sup>nal population other than the <sup>fi</sup>rst.

## 3.5. MCHPF versus MOCLE

The conceptualization of MCHPF has been inspired by several related approaches [15]. In particular, MOCLE has been mostly in<sup>fl</sup>uential, since it was the <sup>fi</sup>rst method combining concepts from the clustering ensembles and multi-objective clustering into the same framework [13,14,16]. Actually, MCHPF can be considered as an extension of MOCLE, inheriting a range of functionalities from it. One remarkable property that both methods share is that they were designed to deal well with heterogeneous structures composed of different types of clusters and occurring at different re<sup>fi</sup>nement levels. In fact, the sets of solutions returned by MOCLE and MCHPF stand for different trade-offs between two complementary validation measures related to distinct clustering criteria and are, at the same time, diverse, revealing the different structures present in the data, and concise enough to be analyzed by the domain experts.

However, there are also some salient aspects that make MCHPF distinctive from MOCLE. First of all, MOCLE is based on a genetic algorithm engine, with the individuals being encoded as linear strings characterizing the resulting partitions (i.e., assignments of data samples to the clusters) themselves. In contrast, in the context of MCHPF, hierarchical fusion structures are the key elements of representation and evolution as GP programs, with the resulting partitions being realized only via the “execution” of these programs. This is an important functionality, since by this means MCHPF can automatically design, from an available repertory of consensus functions, novel fusion operators (nested ensembles) more customized to the particularities of the clustering problem in hand.

Secondly, MOCLE relies only on the application of a simple consensus function (viz. MCLA) acting as crossover operator to assemble novel partitions. That is, at each generation of its evolutionary process, the offspring partitions are created simply by merging (crossing over) two parental partitions selected from the current population. This means that no more than two partitions can be concurrently merged into a novel partition, a constraint that may bring about inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency issues and restrict too much the types of the clustering ensembles that can be produced along the search. Conversely, in MCHPF, such constraint does not exist and fusions with higher cardinality (i.e., higher number of arguments) or even embedded within each other can give birth to elaborated clustering ensembles in the course of a few generations.

It is also worth mentioning that, since there is no mutation in MOCLE, practically no new genetic material is allowed to be inserted into the population across the evolutionary process. So, the levels of population diversity may drop substantially in the last generations, a drawback that may jeopardize the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>nal solutions returned by MOCLE. MCHPF, in turn, is not plagued by this drawback.

In MOCLE, the synthesis of the new partitions through the merging of others is an irreversible process: Once realized, it cannot be undone. So, the quality of the partitions produced in the last generations of the search process will depend very much on the quality of the partitions synthesized earlier. Moreover, since “fusions over fusions” can only happen in MOCLE as generations go by (and not at once), it is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess how each ancestral partition really contributes to the synthesis of a new partition.

MCHPF, in turn, does not present such shortcomings. This is because it works with encapsulated compositions of base partitions and fusion operators, each composition behaving like a team. The insides (and thus the complexity) of each composition are readily modi<sup>fi</sup>able through the insertion, deletion or swapping of its building blocks (i.e., terminals, functions, branches, and/or nested ensembles), and this growing-shrinking process can promote a better <sup>fi</sup>ne-tuning in the synthesis of partitions at each generation. Moreover, MCHPF allows that base partitions of not so good quality when assessed alone still have good chances to take part in high-performance teams, provided that they yield satisfactory levels of increment in the ensemble diversity as a whole [22]. This functionality is not directly available in MOCLE, since in this method <sup>fi</sup>tness-proportionate selection for breeding operates on partitions and not fusion hierarchies, as in MCHPF.

Finally, when it comes to the subject of interpretation of the <sup>fi</sup>nal results delivered by each method, it is fair to conclude that the structures evolved by MCHPF are more amenable to human inspection and interpretation than those evolved by MOCLE. This is because all ingredients required to induce a novel partition are already encapsulated into a single GP individual and thus readily available for visualization. This is an important issue to consider since the easy visualization and interpretation of the clustering results may be instrumental for decision makers to quickly compare the suitability of diverse algorithms [3]. In the case of MOCLE, to realize how a given partition returned at the end of the evolutionary process was in fact synthesized would require a sort of tracking of the fusions performed along all generations of the search process, maybe demanding the facilities of auxiliary visualization tools, such as GAVEL [26].

They are two-dimensional in nature so as to make the visualization of clusters and partitions easy. Two other datasets, iris and glass, taken from the UCI repository [1], are considered as benchmark. As these two datasets have been extensively used, it is possible to compare the results reported here with those delivered in the related literature. The remaining datasets (golub, proteins, leukemia, and lung) are related to real-life, bioinformatics problems, have large dimensions, and are made available elsewhere [2,21,39,42].

The results delivered by the standard version of MCHPF are compared with the results produced by the following algorithms: KM, HAL, HSL, SNN, HBGF, SC, MOCK, and MOCLE. It should be remembered that MOCK and MOCLE also generate as a result Pareto solutions representing different tradeoffs between overall deviation and connectivity. In particular, two versions of MOCLE have been considered, MOCLE-M and MOCLE-H, which differ in terms of the fusion operator used as crossover — MCLA or HBGF, respectively.

The maximum number of generations for each MCHPF run was kept the same for all datasets, namely, $N _ { G } = 1 0 .$ . Although we have experimented with (much) higher values for this parameter, no substantial gains in performance have been perceived. Conversely, for MOCLE and MOCK, $N _ { G } = 5 0 .$ In addition, the value of parameter υ used in the calculus of connectivity (see Subsection 3.2) was set in accordance with the number of samples of the target dataset, that is, $\textstyle v = 0 . 0 5 \times n .$ . Aiming at increasing the levels of diversity of the base partitions used by both MCHPF and MOCLE, the number of clusters produced with the conventional algorithms (i.e. KM, HAL, HSL, and SNN) for each dataset varied in the range $[ K _ { m i n } , K _ { m a x } ] ,$ where $K _ { m i n }$ and $K _ { m a x }$ equal to the smallest number of clusters and twice the largest number of clusters among those of the known structures, respectively.

In order to comparatively assess the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>nal partitions returned by the competing methods, the corrected Rand (CR) index was used [29,41]. This validation index is good for its insensitiveness to the number of clusters in a structure. It measures the similarity between two partitions, say π<sup>a</sup> and π<sup>r</sup>, in this way: values close to 0 mean random partitions and close to 1 indicate a perfect match between the partitions. To be adopted as an external index for our purposes, π<sup>a</sup> had to be one of the <sup>fi</sup>nal partitions produced by one of the contestants while π<sup>r</sup> was one of the known structures of the target dataset.

So, for HAL, HSL, and SNN, which are deterministic, the CR value was calculated between each partition $\pi ^ { S _ { i } } ,$ , from the set of returned solutions $\prod _ { S } = \{ \pi ^ { S _ { 1 } } , \pi ^ { S _ { 2 } } , . . . , \pi ^ { E _ { N S } } \} ,$ , and each structure $^ { E _ { j } } ,$ from the set of known structures $\prod _ { E } = \{ \pi ^ { E _ { 1 } } , \pi ^ { E _ { 2 } } , . . . , \pi ^ { E _ { N E } } \}$ . Then, for each known structure $\Pi ^ { E _ { j } } ,$ , we recorded the highest CR value associated with any partition from ∏ .

Since MCHPF, MOCK and MOCLE are non-deterministic, thirty runs of each method were performed for each dataset, keeping the same initial conditions. Conversely, since SC and HBGF are sensitive to the order of presentation of the base partitions, thirty runs of each method were performed, each time with a random order. Finally, thirty runs of KM were also necessary due to the random initialization of the clusters' centroids. For calculating the CR values associated with all these stochastic algorithms, we have adopted a methodology that resembles that proposed for the deterministic methods. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for each structure $\pi ^ { E _ { j } }$ of a dataset, the highest CR value achieved by any partition produced in each of the thirty runs was recorded and then these thirty CR values were averaged out to yield the <sup>fi</sup>nal, reported score.

## 4.3. Assessing the impact of the hierarchical partition fusions

After performing the thirty runs of MCHPF1 for each dataset, we collected all GP individuals of the thirty <sup>fi</sup>nal populations and grouped them into classes according to the heights of their respective fusion trees. Afterwards, for each of the six resulting classes, $h _ { j } , j = 1 , . . . , 6 ,$ , the CR values of the partitions produced by its associated individuals were averaged out, and these partitions were compared with each of the underlying structures known for the target dataset. These results are summarized in Table 6, where $h _ { m a x }$ denotes the maximum height of any tree pertaining to the thirty <sup>fi</sup>nal populations and K stands for the number of clusters associated with each known structure (refer to Table 3). For instance, if we consider the dataset ds3c3sc6, <sup>fi</sup>ve was the maximum height of any of the <sup>fi</sup>nal hierarchies produced by MCHPF1, and for the hierarchies having this speci<sup>fi</sup>c length the average CR value was the best accomplished among all classes, namely, 0.8058, for K = 3, and 0.5075, for K = 6.

It is rather perceptible that, in general, higher values of CR are positively correlated with hierarchies endowed with at least one partition fusion (i.e. whose heights are equal to or higher than two). The only exception is the dataset iris, for which some of the base partitions were good enough to uncover alone its single underlying structure. Actually, for eighteen out of the twenty-two structures to be revealed, the best results were delivered by hierarchies having three or more levels. In short, these results are interesting because they empirically demonstrate that: 1) partitions constructed via clustering ensembles in general tend to show better quality (CR value) than those delivered by single clustering algorithms (base partitions); and 2) having fusion hierarchies operating on well-chosen subsets of data partitions is a <sup>fi</sup>ne strategy that may yield signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gains in robustness for dealing with the existence of several heterogeneous structures underlying a given dataset, even in relation to single clustering ensembles.

In order to illustrate how novel consensus partitions can be synthesized via the “execution” of the fusion hierarchies evolved by MCHPF, just consider the arti<sup>fi</sup>cial datasets ds2c2sc13 and ds4c2sc8, depicted in Fig. 3. While the latter presents two intrinsic structures, E1 and E2, with two and eight clusters, respectively, the <sup>fi</sup>rst dataset can be segmented into three structures at different resolutions, namely E1 (with two clusters), E2 (with <sup>fi</sup>ve clusters), and E3 (with thirteen clusters). By focusing on the structure E2 for both datasets, one can infer, from Figs. 4 and 5, how the best data partitions returned by MCHPF1 were in fact synthesized. For the second case, a three-level hierarchy of fusion operators was necessary, while, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst, a simple HBGF fusion of three base partitions was enough.

## 4.4. Comparison with conventional clustering algorithms

In this subsection, we compare MCHPF1 with the repertory of basic algorithms used to generate its base partitions. As we have already mentioned, this repertory is heterogeneous enough to yield clusters with different shapes and partitions with different levels of separation between the clusters. While KM and HAL cope well with spherical clusters, HSL and SNN allow the induction of clusters with arbitrary shapes but may fail to succeed when the spatial separation between groups is small.

Table 7 brings the quantitative results in terms of average CR values. It is evident that the conventional algorithms only perform well in those circumstances where the underlying data structures are in conformity with their associated clustering criteria. Moreover, these algorithms do not show a consistent behavior across all datasets and structures, failing considerably in some cases. As a manner to visualize this, let us consider again the arti<sup>fi</sup>cial datasets ds2c2sc13 and ds4c2sc8, depicted in Fig. 3.

For the second dataset, compactness-based algorithms could prevail over those oriented to connectivity. By examining its structures, E1 and E2, it is easy to understand why. Although the two clusters in E1 are not very dense, they are very close to each other, thus hindering the application of the connectivity-based algorithms. On the other hand, the eight clusters in E2 are spherical in shape and well separated to each other, thus facilitating the application of compactness-based algorithms. Conversely, for dataset ds2c2sc13, a different pro<sup>fi</sup>le emerges. All single clustering methods have performed well in eliciting structures E1 and E2, exception given to KM for E2, due to the arbitrary shape of some of its clusters. For E3, however, since most of the clusters assume elliptical forms, HSL and SNN have shown better performance than KM and HAL.

By taking into account the results obtained by MCHPF1, it becomes again evident the gains in robustness incurred by the proposed methodology. Even though the conventional algorithms showed better CR values whenever their clustering assumptions were perfectly met, the GP-based algorithm could outperform, or at least show a comparable level of performance, in thirteen out of the twenty-two structures analyzed. Besides, in none of the cases, MCHPF1 delivered the worst result, meaning that the partitions yielded by its induced hierarchies tend to improve substantially the quality of the base partitions received from the conventional algorithms.

## 4.5. Comparison with advanced clustering algorithms

By observing the results conveyed in Table 8, one can have an idea on how MCHPF has compared with the more advanced approaches. In this regard, we again draw the reader's attention to the levels of robustness incurred by the proposed method: In eleven out of the twenty-two structures analyzed, MCHPF1 could prevail; only in three of these cases, there was a draw; and in no circumstance MCHPF1 ranked at the worst position.

When comparing MCHPF1 solely with the single fusion methods, SC and HBGF, the gains in performance are more promptly noticeable. MCHPF outperformed HBGF in seventeen cases, and for only two there was a tie between them. Against SC, the number of wins was the same, but the ties were higher, viz. three. These results demonstrate that, in general, having fusion hierarchies operating on well-selected data partitions can provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gains in performance (effectiveness and robustness) in relation to single partition fusions.

When contrasted against MOCK, the performance of MCHPF1 is also remarkable. Our method succeeded in fourteen cases, and for only <sup>fi</sup>ve structures there was a tie between them. On the other hand, MOCLE has produced the most competitive results, with MOCLE-H (MOCLE-M) triumphing in seven (six) of the cases. But still in this dispute, our method has prevailed in general, outperforming MOCLE-H (MOCLE-M) in ten (eleven) of the twenty-two structures.

## 4.6. Statistical tests

To have an insight on how signi<sup>fi</sup>cant are the differences in performance exhibited by all the contenders evaluated in the previous subsections, we have resorted to the Friedman test and the Nemenyi test [10]. These non-parametric statistical tests are suitable to compare the performance of different learning algorithms when applied over multiple datasets. The application of the Friedman test requires that the algorithms are ranked in ascending order for each dataset separately, with the best algorithm allotted to the <sup>fi</sup>rst rank. In case of a tie, the average of the ranks is associated to all individuals showing equivalent performance. The null hypothesis of this test is that all the competitors are statistically equivalent, meaning that their average ranks over all datasets are equal. If the null hypothesis of the Friedman test is not veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, Demšar [10] advocates the use of Nemenyi's as a post-hoc test whereby each algorithm alone is compared with all the others. This test infers that the differences in performance between two algorithms are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant if their associated average ranks over all datasets differ in at least one critical difference, i.e. a score that is directly proportional to the number of algorithms in the contest and inversely proportional to the number of datasets used [10].

## 4.7. Comparison among MCHPF variants

Tables 9 and 10 convey the average CR values delivered by the eleven MCHPF variants described in Table 2 for the twenty-two known structures analyzed. The <sup>fi</sup>rst fact to take notice of is that the levels of robustness displayed by the standard con<sup>fi</sup>guration are also presented by the remaining MCHPF variants. As a matter of fact, none of the variants has presented an inconsistent behavior across the datasets like those exhibited by the conventional clustering algorithms (see Subsection 4.4). However, the results reveal that different variants perform better for different datasets, which means that the idea of conceiving and deploying novel variants for different circumstances is worthwhile. In the following, we focus on the <sup>fi</sup>ve variants that have shown the highest average CR values across the 22 structures, viz. MCHPF1, MCHPF2, MCHPF3, MCHPF4, and MCHPF8.

The overall best performance was achieved by MCHPF4. In half of the cases, including ties, this variant has triumphed over the other four best variants. However, the price paid for this accomplishment was the rising in the average computational time (six times higher, on average, than that of MCHPF1) due to an increase in the size of the population (from 50 to 100) and an increase in the maximum heights allowed for the trees of the initial population.

In the second, third, and fourth places come, respectively, MCHPF8, MCHPF2, and MCHPF3, with MCHPF8 prevailing in eight of the cases, including those with a tie. Their settings show some similarities, differing only in some of the dimensions described in Subsection 3.4. For instance, while MCHPF2 makes use of HBGF, MCHPF8 adopts both HBGF and MCLA as consensus functions. Conversely, while MCHPF2 and MCHPF8 adopt the “full” method of population initialization, MCHPF3 adopts a new hybrid strategy. Since in general MCHPF8 performed better than the other two, this result suggests that adopting different consensus functions in the same fusion hierarchy may be useful in some circumstances. Yet, the computational demands incurred by MCHPF8 were 1.22 times higher than those required by MCHPF2 or MCHPF3, which, in turn, were more comparable to that of MCHPF1. Indeed, the main point of departure between MCHPF2/MCHPF3 and MCHPF1 is in the number of <sup>fi</sup>nal solutions returned: Whereas the output of the latter is only the <sup>fi</sup>nal approximation of the Pareto optimal set, all individuals of the last population are returned by the former. This factor should not be neglected: For the dataset iris, the number of hierarchies belonging to the Pareto front was on average only 10.43, almost <sup>fi</sup>ve times lower than the number of individuals of the <sup>fi</sup>nal population (50).

## 5. Final remarks

In this paper, we presented a novel GP-based approach, referred to as MCHPF, hybridizing the advanced strategies of multi-objective clustering and clustering ensembles. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, MCHPF stipulates that novel hierarchical fusion operators for clustering ensembles are automatically designed over simple consensus functions already available in the literature. By considering complementary validation indices in a multi-objective basis, the approach is able to <sup>fi</sup>nd a representative set of high-quality partitions for the different structures that may be available for a given dataset. By varying some conceptual components, eleven different versions of the proposed algorithm have been characterized and empirically assessed.

Also in this work, we highlighted the conceptual aspects that make MCHPF distinctive from MOCLE, a closely-related multi-objective clustering ensemble method [14,16]. Besides, we reported on a series of computational experiments conducted on ten arti<sup>fi</sup>cial and real-life datasets (totalizing twenty-two underlying structures), whereby the performance of MCHPF variants could be estimated in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness. In this context, we could compare the quality of the partitions delivered by the standard version of MCHPF with those produced by basic and advanced clustering methods (of which three are clustering ensembles and two are multi-objective in nature). Overall, the results have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the potentialities of the new approach, mainly in terms of clustering robustness to different sorts of underlying structures.

As future work, we shall investigate the possible application of MCHPF to the complicated tasks of document clustering [4,37], image segmentation [30], and market segmentation [31]. To deal better with ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency issues, mainly when huge distributed data sets are in sight, we plan to incorporate into MCHPF some mechanisms available in recently-proposed scalable frameworks for clustering ensembles [27]. It is also in our plans to devise a semi-supervised version of our approach [23]; to evaluate the potentials of adopting a multi-objective version of grammar-based GP [8,35] in place of the GP engine used here; and to investigate the applicability of MCHPF in the related problems of vector quantization and topographic map formation [5].
