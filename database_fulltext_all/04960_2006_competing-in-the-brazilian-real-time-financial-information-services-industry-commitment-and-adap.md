# Competing in the Brazilian real-time financial information services industry: Commitment and adaptation

Alexandre Barsi Lopes <sup>a,\*</sup>, Nicolau Reinhard <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Cincinnati, College of Business, Information Systems Department, 330 Lindner Hall, ML 0211 Cincinnati, OH 45221, United States <sup>b</sup> Universidade de Sa˜o Paulo, Brazil Received 12 January 2006; accepted 17 February 2006 Available online 12 May 2006

## Abstract

In this study, we examine two very different firms operating in the Brazilian real-time financial information services (RTFIS) industry. We conducted two separate qualitative studies 7 years apart analyzing how a technology-based incumbent reacted to the entry of a challenger with better content-generation capabilities. We concluded that even when facing an entrant with new technologies and environmental changes, commitment to its own IS resources represented the best long-term strategy for the incumbent firm, because the incumbent kept focused on what it did best and invested in the redeployment of its flexible IS resources to redefine its industry processes and activities.

Keywords: Technology-based competition; Resource-based view; Incumbent; Challenger; IS resources; Case study; Developing country; Brazi

## 1. Introduction

We examined the real-time financial information services (RTFIS) industry in Brazil; it specializes in the distribution of ‘‘instantaneous’’ financial news to investors. During a 12-year period, this industry changed from being a quasi-monopoly to becoming a very competitive duopoly, following a significant transformation in the environment that led to considerable changes in the value of the firms’ strategic resources.

We used this industry to help answer two main research questions. First, we looked at what an incumbent should do when simultaneously facing industry deregulation and the entry of a new challenger with different capabilities. Although it has been suggested that the incumbent must change and even ‘‘imitate’’ the new entrant to stay competitive [3], such a strategy might lead to lost focus and lack of commitment to the incumbent’s own capabilities [22]. We also examined the role of IS resources in the incumbent’s quest to remain relevant in the industry.

## 2. Theoretical background

The resource-based view of the firm (RBV) primarily attempted to explain differences in a firm’s profitability that cannot be attributed to industry conditions [33]; firms obtain competitive advantage by using strategies through which they can maximize their effectiveness by capitalizing on unique strengths and assets [34]. As a consequence, they are better able to respond to environmental opportunities, neutralize external threats, avoid internal weaknesses, and maximize the value provided to their customers [4]. Resources are assumed to be scarce and heterogeneously distributed across industries and acquired through limited competition (or even ‘‘luck’’) before they became valuable [11,28]. Resources should be rare among the firm’s competitors and should not be imitable or have equivalent substitutes [2].

Another important concept within RBV is that of capabilities, which can be related to distinctive organizational routines making use of the collection of assets possessed by the firm [44]. The idea of capabilities usually implies that the firm can evolve and develop new sources of competitive advantage, hedging against the obsolescence of existing resources [13,38]. Some firms actively identify, interpret, and act upon early signals from their internal and external environment, and so position themselves to exploit these opportunities well in advance of others, creating competitive advantage [32].

Nonetheless, when firms attempt to develop new resources and capabilities, they also make strong commitments that might not be easily reversed. Firms accumulate resources as a result of actions of learning, investment, and other organizational activities [31]. To create a significant new capability, an organization must make a set of specific and highly complementary investments in new assets, process development, and establishment of intra-organizational relationships. Furthermore, a firm’s commitment to a set of strategic resources, even in times of environmental upheaval, may provide better outcomes in the long term. Firms that do well frequently build around their own successes rather than emulate their rivals [30]. Constant adaptation to change in the environment may lead the firm to sacrifice its distinctive capabilities, which may damages the organizational culture.

Moreover, even internal replication might be difficult because it involves the re-creation of complex and partially tacit processes [36,45]. The situation becomes more difficult for an external imitator because its challenge is not to copy the capability but to acquire that capability so that the imitator can compete in the areas of cost effectiveness and speed. In many cases, acquisition of a firm providing unique resources and capabilities ends up being a good way to change the resource portfolio of the acquiring firm [20]. However, acquisition might also create too many post-acquisition costs and difficulties that would make the incorporation of the acquired resources a laborious process.

## 2.1. IS resources

RBV is not without its critics (e.g., Lado et al. [23] present a discussion of its paradoxes). However, it has been embraced by several scholars, where it has been used to explain how IT might confer advantage. IS resources may be classified into three main categories [42]: outside-in resources (external relationship management and market responsiveness), spanning resources (IS-business partnerships, and IS planning and change management), and inside-out resources (IS infrastructure, IS technical skills, IS development, cost effective IS operations).

It has been proposed that IT only creates value when it leverages existing human and business resources [10,35]. Some see IT assets as relatively easy to be copied while others state that IT infrastructure might not be easy to mimic because of the high-level of integration necessary [6]. Nevertheless, there is a general feeling that competitive advantage can be generated from new combinations of IT, human, and business resources.

## 2.2. Technology-based competition

Several studies have focused on how sudden changes in technology affect competitive behavior; e.g., shifts might be either competence-destroying or -enhancing. Incremental, competence-enhancing innovations initiated by incumbents have been found to reinforce the capabilities of established organizations, while radical, competence-destroying innovations, initiated by new entrants, might lead to new product classes and devaluation of incumbent position [15,41].

In many cases, the incumbent may find it difficult to sustain position in the presence of competence-destroying technologies. The longer the incumbent delays its answer, more enduring the benefits to a firm using the new technology [27]. Highly efficient and developed products might be even more vulnerable because of sudden and unanticipated variations in the environment [1]. The incumbent might be unable to compete effectively and old products in established firms are generally inferior to those of new entrants [40].

The failure of the incumbent to adapt is usually associated to two related issues:

 Organizational inertia and ‘‘core rigidities’’ [26]. Investment in R&D usually follow patterns linked to current methods, including physical ones, of the firm [14]. The stronger its beliefs about what leads to success, more likely changes and discovery of asymmetries might go unnoticed [8].

 Major problems in acquiring and deploying new capabilities, especially when new technology is not close to current one [17]. The incumbent will have to invest in learning the new technology but new entrants might be able to explore it more effectively because they do not have ‘‘old’’ knowledge. Difficulties might also arise from lack of access to expertise or problems in integrating change.

Other studies, however, have found that incumbents can survive and even strengthen their position after a technological disruption. Even when confronted with radical innovation, some incumbent organizations adapt, survive, and regain historic performance levels [16]. Performance of the incumbent may also be better if the firm possesses complementary downstream assets critical to the commercialization of the technology.

## 2.3. Research questions

We investigated an industry that was affected twice by deregulation. Such a change in the environment usually leads to discontinuities and changes in the balance of power. When firms face dramatic changes, those that manage to change strategies rapidly will outperform those that do not. The RBV literature emphasizes that the development of a new capability might require considerable time and a steady commitment. However, learning a new capability might be difficult to accomplish. Thus, we targeted two distinct questions:

1. What is an incumbent to do when its industry is deregulated, especially if the action affects the value of its main resources, and allows a new entrant to bring in diverse and ‘‘hard-to-imitate’’ capabilities?

2. What is the role of IS resources in the competitive interaction between incumbent and challenger?

## 3. Methodology

A case study approach allowed us to examine the causes and consequences of both the development and deployment of strategic resources and capabilities by firms in the industry [46].

## 3.1. Data collection

We used the literature to identify the main variables of interest in the phenomenon being investigated [12]. We conducted our data collection – which involved semi-structured interviews, on-site observation, documentation (e.g., internal analysis, reports, advertisement material, press releases) review, and product demonstrations – at two times: winter 1996 and summer 2003. We did not originally plan to wait 7 years before the second data collection. However, our first collection took place when the industry was in turmoil and the Internet was just starting to be used in Brazil. We decided to revisit the study 7 years later to see what had changed because we felt that enough time had passed for changes seen as likely in our first data collection to take effect. We strived to achieve consistency between data collections, following comparable procedures, including the use of similar interview guidelines [29]. However, for the second data collection, we updated some questions to detect changes that may have affected the industry, such as the diffusion of the Internet.

Although we focused on the two main competitors in the RTFIS industry, in both stages we expanded our data collection and interviewed representatives of all RTFIS firms in Brazil, as well as representatives of information suppliers such as stock exchanges, organizations that provided financial analysis and/or news, and RTFIS clients. All interviewees in supplier firms were members of their upper management but we concentrated on actual users when interviewing clients. In both data collection stages, we used external consultants highly involved in the industry to help us validate the information. In Table 1, we identify interviewees and clarify interview protocol details.

## 3.2. Data analysis

In order to identify the process by which RTFIS companies compete with different sets of strategic resources and capabilities, we used a variety of approaches.

After the initial data collection we conducted a search for representative patterns of the differences and similarities among firms. In particular, we examined statements that would lead us to understand the assets, strategic resources, capabilities, and competences of each firm and how they contributed to the firm’s success [19]. At the end of this process, we sent a report of our analysis, findings, and conclusions to those who had participated. Then, we incorporated their feedback into the case database.

After the second data collection was conducted, we used the case database to create a narrative description of the evolution of the industry over time. With this, we hoped to create a chain of evidence by which we could understand the industry, the causes and consequences of the key events, and each firm’s competitive moves. We also included external events that may have influenced the competitive dynamics, such as the liberalization of the Brazilian information technology (IT) market, dissemination of the Internet, and consolidation of the Brazilian financial industry.

We used information collected from other industry participants (e.g., clients, information suppliers) to triangulate the data from these focus firms. ## 4. The RTFIS industry in Brazil: a historical perspective

These firms act as brokers focusing on immediate (real-time) distribution of financial information to help clients (mostly organizations) make investment decisions in quick-paced and intense environments [47]. Worldwide, this industry is dominated by corporations such as Reuters and Bloomberg.

This industry has a relatively narrow range of offerings, though the volume of information provided is very large because of the many potentially relevant sources. The distributed information can be either generated in-house or acquired from external vendors. Value is added by provision of analytical tools developed internally or provided by third-parties. In general, the information can be classified into:

(1) real time quotes, prices, and indices of investment instruments and securities;

(2) real-time news;

(3) sector and firm analyses; and

(4) raw databases, including companies’ balances, historic data about quotes and prices, and texts of articles from journals and magazines, etc.

To represent the evolution of the RTFIS industry in Brazil, we chose to divide its history into three periods.

## 4.1. Period 1: industry beginnings

In the 1980s Brazil was living under military dictatorship. In this environment, the IT market was ‘‘protected’’ by a state telecommunications service and a reserved market for national companies providing IT goods and services. Access to foreign technology was restricted, or even forbidden [39]. The telecommunication infrastructure was precarious. At that time, the supply of telephone lines was limited and their price excessive. People invested in phone lines and traded or rented lines for profit.

The pioneering Brazilian RTFIS firm TechInc (the names of all companies have been disguised) operated as an electronic data-processing firm that ran accounting and payroll systems for other organizations. Founded in 1973, it soon was apparent that its technological capabilities could be adapted to the distribution of information and it decided to attempt to fulfill the needs of an underserved market. In 1980, the firm reinvented itself as an RTFIS company, distributing financial information obtained from a variety of stock exchanges.

TechInc’s mission was relatively simple – transmit information from point A (stock exchanges, newsrooms) to point B (brokerage houses, investment banks) – but its execution was complex due to the technological restrictions. Consequently, it was forced to develop most of the hardware necessary for its operations. In particular, TechInc was forced to create alternative distribution mechanisms – such as FM and satellite transmission and reception systems – that could bypass the traditional network, especially for the distribution of information to remote parts of the country. TechInc owned more than 4000 telephone lines in the city of Sa˜o Paulo, the country’s financial center; it had bought them at low prices before the rapid increase in demand.

TechInc’s was a first mover in the Brazilian RTFIS industry, virtually unchallenged and able to capture a significant share of the market. Only later did other companies join the industry; however, they faced serious problems establishing their information delivery mechanisms. The same protected market that had created initial difficulties for TechInc benefited them later by making rival networks difficult to implement. Furthermore, foreign companies had only limited opportunities to participate in the market.

Thus, TechInc became a quasi-monopoly, acquiring the largest of its original competitors in 1991. During that time, TechInc also established partnerships with different information providers, obtaining an important role in the management of the electronic network of the largest Brazilian stock market. The partnerships also enabled it to offer its clients an assortment of analytical tools, sector and company reports, news (both national and international), and archives of market information. Moreover, TechInc offered a variety of products, such as e-mail, an innovation at that time. Finally, it established subsidiaries in other South American countries and Europe.

## 4.2. Period 2: deregulation of the IT market

After developing a highly skilled class of IT professionals, the law that limited import of IT components was abolished in 1991. Consequently, it became much easier and cheaper to import telecommunication components.

With the market open to foreign competition, two international RTFIS firms increased their efforts to penetrate the Brazilian financial market, and both eventually experienced strong growth during the mid-1990s. However, these companies targeted large customers, such as banks and brokerage houses with international operations. The level of telecommunication requirements imposed by the international firms and prices of up to 10 times those charged locally, indicated that they were aiming only at niche markets. It became common practice for large investment firms with international operations to use services from both international and a Brazilian RTFIS firms simultaneously. These two sets of firms then struck partnerships where local firms provided local news in exchange for international news.

The end of 1991 saw the arrival of a new, strong competitor when one of the largest news groups in Brazil acquired a small RTFIS firm and created NewsInc, which was only one of the services offered by the parent organization. NewsInc benefited almost immediately from name recognition and an established news-gathering network. With access to foreign products, NewsInc was able to reduce the advantage that TechInc had due to its telecommunications infrastructure. Additionally, because the parent corporation of NewsInc possessed some radio stations, it was relatively easy to add financial information to these FM stations. This was important because NewsInc did not have a large enough telephone-based data communications network; however, it invested in assembling a team of telecommunication experts who could maintain and enhance its communications infrastructure. Thus, the deregulation of the IT market removed its telecommunications network as a strategic resource for TechInc [9].

The financial information portfolio that NewsInc offered included stock, commodities, and currency quotes, market analysis, and news; this was very similar to the portfolio offered by TechInc. However, there two had fundamental differences:

 NewsInc relied on external partners to develop the client-side interface for the delivery of its services, whereas TechInc performed that function in-house.

 NewsInc entered the industry as part of a group that produced its own news items on a national level, leveraging a set of skills and resources very different from those possessed by TechInc. By incorporating these news pieces, NewsInc implemented a radical innovation by changing both the technology and the links between activities, overturning the core concepts of the Brazilian industry.

Up to that time, RTFIS firms had been subjected to the schedules of the news groups that provided the information. To address that need, NewsInc recruited young and talented financial reporters. They were charged with creating a news service in which speed was fundamental and rumors could be reported—two values that were unusual in more traditional newsrooms.

The process of implementing real-time news certainly was not easy at NewsInc. Its reporters had to receive different training, focusing on fast information turnout. Additionally, the rhythm of work was very different; the real-time reporter was in constant pursuit of fast news affecting investment decisions. It took a long time and much effort for this type of journalism to become part of the culture at NewsInc but despite these early difficulties, the benefits of the informationgeneration strategy eventually became clear. At News-Inc, the collection and transmission of news was concentrated in a single organization with a direct focus on RTFIS clients: NewsInc had a faster turnaround time—a fact conceded by a TechInc executive. This faster link between news and the quotes was of particular importance because the value of the data provided by an RTFIS rests on its availability [43], as clients confirmed. Thus, NewsInc introduced a set of new resources and capabilities that the incumbent did not possess.

More importantly, however, its effort to merge content generation with content distribution proved a challenge to other Brazilian news groups. In 1995 another large Brazilian news organization followed NewsInc’s parent corporation footsteps and entered the industry by acquiring another small RTFIS firm. However, although it was part of a large corporation with resources comparable to NewsInc’s, the company quickly terminated its activities. NewsInc had developed capabilities that were very difficult to replicate, because they were systemic and based on a mixture of know-how, organizational culture, and specific dynamic routines.

NewsInc started gaining rapidly on TechInc. The link between the news-gathering and news-distribution processes was an innovation that gave the challenger an advantage that led to increased market share. Moreover, NewsInc took a proactive stance in increasing its market share by offering its services for a considerably lower price than that of TechInc. Soon after this, TechInc matched and eventually undercut the prices of NewsInc. At the start, both companies were charging approximately US\$900.00 per terminal/month for a basic package. However, they could, and frequently did, negotiate price cuts for specific clients. By the mid-1990s the market had little resemblance to the beginning of the decade.

As a reaction to NewsInc’s entry into the market, in 1992, TechInc decided to buy 50% of a news agency that focused on agribusiness information, which is particularly important for local investors. Meanwhile, TechInc continued to mostly distribute the information provided by other Brazilian news organizations, directing its main focus on the technological aspects of the RTFIS industry. As NewsInc gained market share, the situation appeared to become more complicated for TechInc in 1995 when commercial exploration of the Internet started in Brazil, and also in 1997, when the main Brazilian stock exchange consolidated its back office and ordering operations to facilitate the work of the brokerage houses, minimizing the impact of the complementary back-office systems provided by TechInc.

## 4.3. Period 3: deregulation of the telecommunications industry

In 1998 Brazil privatized the telecommunications industry; this led to improved quality and lower prices for communication services. Then the Internet became much more popular and was accepted as an information-distribution mechanism. Also, the global economic crisis between 1999 and 2002 caused consolidation in the Brazilian financial market: the number of banks and brokerage houses fell 40% (from about 250 to 150), as mergers and acquisitions occurred. Furthermore, one of the main Brazilian stock exchanges discontinued its independent operations in 2000.

The type of service offered by TechInc in the 1980s and early 1990s had become a commodity and the Internet had become commonplace. Indeed, TechInc, NewsInc, and international RTFIS firms started using the Internet to distribute information in real time, although keeping proprietary interfaces to receive and manipulate information.

Motivated by the potential market of individual investors and the cheaper communication infrastructure, two smaller Brazilian RTFIS firms concentrated their efforts on a low-price strategy (real time financial information with ‘‘no-frills’’ analysis) and radically reduced the prices of their offerings. This forced both NewsInc and TechInc to lower their margins. Both firms’ prices for basic service subscriptions fell to approximately half (in Brazilian currency) of their cost in 1996. However, price competition never became a competitive weapon, because all price cuts were matched quickly.

Despite all the change in the industry and in the market, there was remarkable stability in both TechInc’s and NewsInc’s assessments of their own strengths: NewsInc focused on news generation and TechInc on technological development. However, NewsInc finally had reached a strong leadership position in terms of its market share.

Some clients we interviewed in the second data collection had previously been TechInc users but had switched to NewsInc. As the main reasons for their change, they cited NewsInc’s ability to deliver information faster and its user friendly system. To survive, TechInc needed to make changes to ensure that its resources matched the market needs. TechInc realized that it still had several capabilities that were not easily replicable, particularly in terms of it technological expertise for the Brazilian RTFIS industry. An important step was the re-conceptualization of its role in the information-distribution process. It attempted to take control of the development and deployment of trading systems, extending its presence to complementary downstream activities. It had deployed its trading solutions in the late 1990s and building on this, it decided to increase its reach to become the largest provider of trading systems for both individual and institutional investors. This made it more onerous for prospective clients to use different systems and made TechInc a predominant player in the downstream activities (e.g., order placement) related to investment decisions. Also TechInc directed its efforts toward developing a completely new system with knowledge management and trading strategy features by building a new, sophisticated workstation that could provide more complete information management and reinforce the firm’s position as technology leader.

After years in development, their system entered the market in the summer of 2003. The introduction of the new system helped TechInc to obtain several new contracts and regain market share. Some of TechInc’s clients acquired the new system so that their own clients could use it directly. In addition, TechInc’s system implemented ‘‘Algorithmic Trading’’, in which traders could determine investment models that would be automatically implemented (according to recent business press, algorithmic trading is ‘‘sweeping the stock market’’ [18]). Finally, the new system also had a ‘‘knowledge management’’ orientation; users could codify and monitor investment models, then program, compare, share among traders, and upgrade different trading strategies to a specific security.

TechInc continues to distribute news from Brazilian and international sources (except those of NewsInc) and in 2002 it acquired the remaining 50% of the agribusiness news agency to become its sole proprietor. In 2003 that agribusiness news agency started offering a new real-time financial news service in an exclusive deal with TechInc. Sometimes TechInc can release financial news faster than NewsInc.

Meanwhile, the economic crisis of 2002 also brought about particular problems for NewsInc: its parent corporation filed for insolvency protection. Furthermore, the readership and profitability of traditional newspaper companies had been falling all over the world. These factors severely limited the investments that NewsInc could expect from its parent corporation and turned it into the main source of income for its parent group. However, NewsInc’s client-side system was starting to show signs of obsolescence: the system had not been updated for 5 years. Furthermore, NewsInc was not able to build a system by itself, needing partners to develop their clientside interface.

Although NewsInc leadership place remains strong, there is no doubt that TechInc has been able to strengthen its position. As the Brazilian RTFIS industry completed its latest change, TechInc had attained a tool to counterbalance NewsInc’s news-gathering and dissemination capabilities.

## 5. Analysis and discussion

We have found that these two companies shared other characteristics. First, both have exhibited high levels of entrepreneurial resources, including foresight, intuition, and alertness. Second, both companies have been able to act quickly to implement tactical maneuvers to complement their deployment of strategic resources and capabilities. What made the Brazilian RTFIS industry interesting, however, were the differences between them.

## 5.1. Incumbent’s adaptive commitment

The first research question of our study asked what an incumbent should do when deregulation occurs, allowing a new entrant with a diverse and ‘‘hard-to-imitate’’ set of capabilities. The literature on techno-based competition and deregulation strongly suggested that change is necessary and the incumbent would be expected to imitate the new entrant under penalty of lost market share. On the other hand, change and imitation are difficult and might cause the incumbent to lose focus on what it does best.

When the market started responding favorably to NewsInc’s, TechInc appeared to have been caught in an ambivalent position. As an experienced player, it immediately recognized the threat posed by News-Incustry [21]. Thus, the incumbent retaliated by acquiring its own news agency. At the same time, however, members of TechInc kept emphasizing the company’s strategic focus on capability evolution based on technology improvement, indicative of either commitment and/or managerial inertia.

On the other hand, TechInc remained remarkably non-committal about its activities in content generation. It took more than 10 years between the acquisition of the agribusiness news agency and its transformation into a financial news agency. Its focus is still not on creating a news agency comparable that of NewsInc. Instead, TechInc appears to be satisfied with operational measures indicating that its own news agency can eventually deliver news to its clients’ workstations faster than NewsInc. Furthermore, TechInc continued to distribute news from local news agencies, expecting that the combination of sources might be comparable to NewsInc’s offering. Thus, TechInc was able to avoid wasting time and effort in integrating diverse cultures and technologies, which could have led to lost focus. So, it benefited from not having to learn processes where its chances of success were minimal.

TechInc was able to focus its attention on what it did best. By looking inward, TechInc managers were able to determine and commit themselves to the real strengths of the firm and then redirect its strategy in a way that maximized the use of its IS resources (e.g., new developments utilizing the Internet, especially in online trading). While NewsInc had always relied on external providers for its client-side systems, TechInc used its IT expertise to produce valuable new products: algorithmic trading systems that could facilitate and improve investment transactions. Thus, TechInc worked to create new products that could turn the tide in its favor by controlling the downstream flow of information and decision-making. This suggests that committing to its strengths might serve an incumbent better than changing strategies.

TechInc and NewsInc started dominating activities that were in different parts of the production chain, thus offering different benefits to their clients. Although capability transformation might be a powerful strategy [25], benefits for the company might be greater when this internal transformation is combined with a reevaluation of the production chain.

Thus, a pattern of indirect competition can emerge. For example, in the case of the Brazilian RTFIS industry, developing internal technology resources might cause a loss of focus for NewsInc; it may again use third-party providers to develop new software. The new software might not have all the features offered by TechInc, but it might be enough to decrease the competitive gap.

## 5.2. The role of IS resources

Our study has presented mixed evidence about the role of IT as a resource. Traditionally, IT has not been considered a strategic resource, because it can be acquired in the market [7,10]. Here, IT infrastructure was a resource only with the artificial barriers imposed by Brazilian IT policy. On the other hand, other insideout IS resources, although supposed to be less beneficial in turbulent and complex environments, were essential for TechInc to respond to changes in the environment. IS development and technical skills allowed TechInc to respond to the threat posed by NewsInc by developing new products. For example, algorithmic trading, incorporated into the TechInc system in 2003, has only recently become a topic of interest in the US.

It has been said that firms would appear to have only three feasible paths to IT-based advantage: reinvent IT advantages perpetually through continuous innovation, move first and erect unassailable first-mover advantages, or embed IT in organizations to produce sustainable resources. In our case, TechInc used its knowledge of the business and IT to reach first-mover advantage with unique innovations that changed the way processes were conducted. With the firm being forced to continuously create new products to adapt to the precarious local infrastructure, TechInc’s IT expertise became a platform for agility [37], allowing the firm to create innovative products and make competitive moves. TechInc needed flexible resources that could help the firm change the processes; IS resources gave it exactly what it required.

Strong management support was required to implement IS resources for competitive advantage [5]. TechInc, across almost a decade, showed a strong belief on IT innovation as a way of limiting the effect of NewsInc’s entry. Therefore, a company must continuously aim at creating technological innovation, first mover advantage, and asset complementarity to generate a constant flow of new products and increase its chance of survival.

## 6. Limitations and conclusions

This study has some limitations:

1. We did not interview a large number of clients of RTFIS firms; interviewing more may have suggested other underlying phenomena taking place in the market.

2. We concentrated our interviews at the top managerial levels of the RTFIS firms; interviewing lower level employees may have provided additional clues about the culture of the organizations.

3. We concentrated our data collection in a single industry and the conclusions may not extend to other industries.

4. NewsInc and TechInc are both still involved in the Brazilian RTFIS industry and there is no clear ‘‘winner,’’. However, TechInc has improved its market share and competitive standing.

Nevertheless, this study fills the need for longitudinal research about the creation and deployment of IS resources. In particular, it provides a comparison of the impact of IS resources in a competitive environment. Also, this study adds to our understanding of the influence of factors related to a developing country context in the development and use of IS resources.

We have found that commitment to company strengths is the best long-term strategy of a firm, because it can focused on what it does best and find ways to redeploy its flexible resources by redefining industry processes and activities. Also, the constrains in a developing country might work as a motivation for a culture of continuous innovation utilizing IS resources.

An interesting research issue is the relationship between the market for strategic resources and capabilities and the market targeted by the focal firms. Here we have seen, how, at first, the markets for the telecommunications infrastructure directly affected the success of firms in the RTFIS industry and how this was later affected by integrated news gathering and distribution.

Another interesting dilemma raised in our study is that of core rigidity, the winner’s curse, and the paradox of success. As NewsInc increased its participation in the market, TechInc remained concerned with its technology capabilities as a way to distinguish itself. Consequently, it would be relatively easy to conclude that TechInc’s concern with technological development obscured the changes occurring in the industry. However, what may have appeared to be rigidity at first was actually commitment. Therefore, we believe it is important to gain a better understanding of the practical differences between organizational inertia and commitment.

One final research question was related to diversification strategies. Currently NewsInc is in the early stages of offering additional information-based services. Meanwhile, TechInc is attempting to expand internationally by offering its trade platform [24]. However, with activities that are complementary in nature, it remains to be seen which strategy will be more successful.
