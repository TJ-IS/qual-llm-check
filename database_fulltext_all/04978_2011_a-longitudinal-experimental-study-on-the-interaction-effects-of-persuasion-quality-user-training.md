# A longitudinal experimental study on the interaction effects of persuasion quality, user training, and first-hand use on user perceptions of new information technology

Gwanhoo Lee <sup>a,</sup>\*, Weidong Xia <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Technology, Kogod School of Business, American University, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Decision Sciences and Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Florida International University, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 12 September 2010 Received in revised form 12 April 2011 Accepted 29 June 2011 Available online 10 September 2011

Keywords: Technology adoption IT innovation Persuasion User training First-hand use Elaboration likelihood model Longitudinal study

## A B S T R A C T

While prior research has investigated the main effects of external factors on user perceptions of a new IT, little work has been into the interaction effect of external factors on user perceptions. In a longitudinal experimental study, we examined the effect of the quality of persuasive argument, user training, and first-hand use on user perceptions of the new technology over time. We found that the effect of argument quality on users’ perceived ease of use was greater when users had no training. However, we did not find the same effect occurred due to perceived usefulness. We also found that first-hand use changed users perceived usefulness more over time when users received high quality arguments or when they had no training. While we found that first-hand use changed users’ perceived ease of use more when users received high quality arguments, first-hand use did not change users’ perceived ease of use differently whether they had or had not received prior training.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations continue to invest in new IT to improve their performance [6,10]. However, the resulting systems often fail to achieve the intended impact on organizational performance in part because the target users do not accept the technology [20]. Even when a technology adoption decision is made at the management level and its use is mandatory, users can still delay, obstruct, underuse, or sabotage the technology if they do not perceive it to be useful or easy to use [5]. We investigated how persuasive arguments, user training, and first-hand experience with technology influence the user’s perception of it in a mandatory use setting.

The theory of reasoned action, the theory of planned behavior, and TAM have been used to try to explain the process of assimilation of IT in the organization [7,12,19]. According to these, user acceptance and adoption of a new technology is ultimately determined by users’ behavioral intention to use the technology which, in turn, is determined by their beliefs and perceptions about it. Perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) are considered important user perceptions that determine user intention to use the technology [11].

User perceptions are, however, subjective and idiosyncratic and may change as users continue to re-evaluate the technology over time. Therefore, it is important to understand how managerial interventions and mechanisms influence user perceptions over time so that the organization can increase the speed of user adoption.

An individual’s perception of new technology does not remain static and should be understood from a dynamic or longitudinal perspective [3,8]. Individuals develop their own idiosyncratic understanding and expectation of the new technology as they are exposed to persuasive arguments and training, and this is likely to change from their first use of the technology over time. Initial persuasion and internal training are important ways to convey the social norm to the users as well as provide the needed knowledge and skills.

Prior research has investigated how persuasion, user training, and first-hand use individually affect how users perceive a new IT. However, little research has examined the interaction effects of these factors on users’ perceptions of a new IT. Our research aimed at filling this gap. As users typically experience all of persuasion, training, and first-hand use through the adoption lifecycle, it is critical for organizations to understand how these three factors interplay. Using a longitudinal experiment, we examined how persuasion, user training, and first-hand use interplay in shaping and changing users’ perceived usefulness and ease of use of a new IT when its use is mandatory. The target technology in our research was a new software engineering tool that facilitates system analysis and design.

## 2. Theoretical background

2.1. Persuasion, training, and first-hand experience in innovation adoption

Innovation adoption is often viewed as a stage-based process; in this, a potential adopter passes from initial awareness and knowledge, to having a favorable or unfavorable perception, to making a decision to adopt or reject it. The introduction of an innovation into an organization creates uncertainty in the minds of potential adopters.

Innovation adoption is an on-going process involving persuasive communication and learning. Change agents who provide persuasive communication are important to the process [17]. User training is an important external factor that influences user perceptions about a new technology [16]. First-hand experience is one of the most important sources of information about the target object [9]. While persuasive communication and user training may be important managerial interventions that initially influence user perceptions of a new technology, users’ actual first-hand use can be an invaluable source when re-evaluating the technology throughout the adoption lifecycle. Persuasion can be considered to be a signaling process, whereas user training and user first-hand use can be thought of as learning processes.

## 2.2. Quality of persuasive argument

Recent IS research has used persuasion theory and the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) to investigate technology adoption [1]. Persuasion is an active attempt to influence people’s action or belief by an overt appeal to reason or emotion. It is one of the most important strategies for influencing beliefs and behavior and is also viewed as a social influence mechanism to form, transmit, and change social norm. In the early stages of innovation adoption, persuasion not only provides information about the target object but also signals to what important other users believe in and expect him or her to do with respect to the behavior.

ELM assumes that there are two basic routes in persuasion: one central to the issue under consideration and the other peripheral to it, emphasizing inessential cues associated with persuasive arguments (such as the length of an argument, source credibility, and attractiveness) [18]. It has been found that personal relevance or importance of an issue is a key factor that determines which persuasion route is used to influence perceptions and beliefs.

The quality of persuasive argument consists of two dimensions: argument valence (the audience’s evaluation of the consequence or outcome of the target object under consideration) and argument strength (the audience’s subjective probability that the target object is associated with that outcome or consequence). Thus, enhancing argument quality requires an increase in both parts. Argument valence can increase from being negative to positive, while argument strength increases when a causal explanation supporting a persuasive argument is provided and is supported by concrete, objective evidence (e.g., statistics), as opposed to abstract, subjective evidence such as personal opinion.

## 3. Hypothesis development

User perceptions are likely to be shaped and influenced by the information received from sources such as management and peers [14]. The quality of the arguments influences user perceptions when they process information and evaluate the credibility and merits of the arguments. Prior work has found that high quality arguments are likely to yield favorable cognitive and affective outcomes via the central route of persuasion, while low quality arguments lead to neutral or negative responses [4]. Therefore, the quality of the arguments was expected to positively affect users perceived usefulness and ease of use of a new IT.

In addition, user perceptions are often shaped and influenced by user training during the early adoption stage. This provides users with conceptual and procedural knowledge about the IT innovations. Prior research suggests that training increases procedural knowledge, which, in turn, affects perceived ease of use, attitudes, and usage [13]. During a typical training session, users receive information about the features and functionalities of the target technology, which allows them to evaluate its merits and demerits. Furthermore, they usually gain some hands-on experience with the technology in the training session, and this allows them to assess the ease of use of the technology. Therefore, user training is expected to affect users’ perceived usefulness and ease of use of the new IT.

If users’ initial perceptions of the technology are formed without user training, their perceptions are likely to be unstable or unreliable over time. Therefore, if users do not have prior training, they are likely to be influenced by the arguments to a greater extent because they may not have other bases on which they can anchor their perceptions. As users evaluate the arguments against the initial perceptions that are relatively stable, their perceptions are likely to be less influenced by the arguments. Therefore, we proposed:

H1. The positive effect of argument quality on users’ perceived usefulness of a new IT will be greater when users do not have prior training than when users have prior training.

H2. The positive effect of argument quality on users’ perceived ease of use of a new IT will be greater when users do not have prior training than when users have prior training.

After adopting a new technology, users continue to learn about it through their first-hand use experience [2]. Users’ pre-usage perceptions are likely to be unstable if their perceptions are based on second-hand information such as persuasive messages. As users gain first-hand experience, they modify and change their initial perceptions. First-hand experience with the technology provides users with concrete sensory information about its strengths and weaknesses. Information gleaned from first-hand experience is more accessible and inspires greater confidence than second-hand information or vicarious experience. As a result, we expected that the effect of argument quality on users’ perceptions decreases over time due to increased experience.

First-hand experience would move users’ idiosyncratic, subjective perceptions towards more objective attributes of the technology. The higher the argument quality, the more likely that the users’ initial perceptions would be based on unrealistic, subjective, overly optimistic views. As a result, with more firsthand experience, users would be likely to make more adjustments to their initial perceptions based on stronger, higher quality persuasive arguments. Therefore, we proposed:

H3. As users’ first-hand use increases over time, users’ perceived usefulness of the new IT based on high quality arguments will change more than those based on low quality arguments.

H4. As users’ first-hand use increases over time, users’ perceived ease of use of the new IT based on high quality arguments will change more than those based on low quality arguments.

In a training session, prospective users gain some hands-on experience with the target technology. As a result, their initial perceptions shaped are likely to be relatively realistic, stable, and close to the objective attributes of the technology. In contrast, if prospective users do not receive training, their initial perceptions are likely to be unrealistic, unstable, and remote from the objective attributes of the technology. Therefore, we expected that, when users gain first-hand experience without prior training, user perceptions are likely to change more than user perceptions originally formed from user training. We therefore proposed:

H5. As users’ first-hand use increases over time, users’ perceived usefulness of the new IT based on no user training will change more than those based on user training.

H6. As users’ first-hand use increases over time, users’ perceived ease of use of the new IT based on no user training will change more than those based on user training.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Target IT

A new software engineering tool was used as our target technology. As one of the leading software engineering tools in the market, it provided comprehensive functions for systems analysis, design, and development. It supported traditional system analysis and design (SAD) methodologies, object-oriented SAD, and many other methods. As such, the tool supported data flow, entityrelationship, object-oriented diagrams, etc.

## 4.2. Subjects

Ninety-two business major undergraduate students at a large U.S. university participated in the study. All subjects were juniors or seniors who were taking an IS analysis and design course. Although the subjects were undergraduates, nearly all of them had work experience in the IS areas. On average, the subjects had two years of part- or full-time work experience, had used computers for about nine years, and had completed two IS development projects.

The subjects were assured that participation would not affect their grades and that all responses would be kept anonymous and confidential. To ensure that there were no confounding effects caused by differences in subjects’ prior knowledge and experience about the software tool, no information about the tool was given to the subjects and it was not made available to them before the experiment. We collected demographic data on their background, prior computer experience, and skills. Results showed that none of the subjects had any experience with the software tool being studied.

## 4.3. Data collection procedure and experimental design

We collected data from the subjects at two different times, as shown in Fig. 1. The first set of data was collected immediately after the lab experiment was administered at time t . The subjects then used the software tool to complete two projects during the next four weeks. The second set of data was collected at the end of this period $( t _ { 2 } ) .$

The lab experiment used a two by two balanced factorial design with four groups of subjects, as shown in Fig. 2. The subjects were randomly assigned to one of the four groups. Each group had twenty-three subjects. The experiment involved two factors: the quality of persuasive arguments and user training. The subjects in the first group were given no user training and a set of low quality persuasive messages about the software tool. The subjects in the second group were given no user training and a set of high quality persuasive messages advocating the adoption of the software tool. The subjects in the third group were given a user training session followed by a set of low quality persuasive messages. The subjects in the fourth group were given a user training session followed by a set of high quality persuasive messages. After the experiment, all subjects were asked to complete a questionnaire that included measures that assessed their perceptions about the usefulness and ease of use of the software tool. All the experiments at $t _ { 1 }$ were conducted on the same day so that the subjects had no or little time to share ideas with their peers about the training session and the questionnaire. In addition, we asked subjects not to share ideas with their peers about the training session and the survey questionnaire until the end of our study (i.e., $t _ { 2 } ) .$ We required the subjects to sign a consent form on this issue.

## 4.4. Experimental manipulations

## 4.4.1. User training

The user training session included a 30-min lecture and demonstration followed by 45-min of practice. One of the authors presented the concepts and functionalities of the software tool and then demonstrated its use by constructing of two data flow diagrams. Using a step-by-step tutorial, the subjects then completed two other data flow diagrams that were similar to those shown to them.

## 4.4.2. Quality of persuasive arguments

We developed two different versions of eight persuasive messages that were intended to more or less advocate the use of the software tool; these are shown in Appendix A. These messages were adapted from various sources, including trade magazines, expert opinions, software brochures, and the vendor’s website. Each message provided information on characteristics and attributes of the software tool.

We manipulated the quality of the persuasive arguments using different degrees of argument valence and argument strength. High quality persuasive arguments had a very positive valence with clear causal explanations supported by objective, concrete evidence. In contrast, low quality arguments had neutral or slightly negative valence with ambiguous causal explanations supported by subjective or less concrete evidence, such as opinions or anecdotal experience. Each pair of low and high quality persuasive messages was approximately of the same length (61 words) to eliminate any confounding effect of different lengths.

The validity of persuasion manipulation was tested using 18 graduate students and university staff members in two pilot tests. Results of the pilot tests suggested that the participants perceived clear differences in argument quality between the low and the high quality messages. Furthermore, participants of the pilot test rated the extent to which the argument messages were easy to understand, on a 7-point Likert scale. The rating results indicated that the messages were easy to understand and that there was no significant difference in understandability between the low and the high quality persuasive messages.

## 4.5. Actual use

In the four weeks following the lab experiment, all subjects were required to use the software tool in completing two projects that had to be analyzed while designing an IS using various diagramming techniques. The use of the new software was mandatory and no alternative was provided for the subjects. The two projects were derived from real-world business processes. The software tool was available 24/7 to the subjects in a dedicated computer lab. The second questionnaire was administered at the end of the fourth week (t ).

## 4.6. Manipulation checks

To ensure that our manipulation of user training was successful, at the end of the training session, we measured three outcomes using 7-point Likert scales: (1) the extent to which the subject became knowledgeable about using the tool, (2) the extent to which the subject understood its functionalities, and (3) the subject’s perceived effectiveness of the training session. Results showed that the subjects rated all three outcomes highly $( M _ { \mathrm { k n o w l e d g e } } = 5 . 9 , ~ M _ { \mathrm { f u n c t i o n a l i t i e s } } = 6 . 8 ,$ $M _ { \mathrm { e f f e c t i v e n e s s } } = 6 . 1 )$ , indicating that the user training was perceived to be effective. There was no significant difference in subjects’ perceived effectiveness of the user training between low and high quality persuasion groups. Therefore, we concluded that the user training and manipulation was effective and valid.

We checked the validity of the manipulation on the quality of the persuasive arguments using a 7-point Likert scale by asking: ‘‘Overall, the statements presented above are strong in advocating the software tool’’. Results suggested that subjects in the high quality persuasion group rated this item significantly higher than those in the low quality persuasion group $( M _ { \mathrm { { s t r o n g ~ p e r s u a s i o n } } } = 6 . 4 ,$ $M _ { \mathrm { w e a k \ p e r s u a s i o n } } = 2 . 6 ; F = 1 8 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Therefore, we concluded that the manipulation was effective and valid.

We also checked subjects’ perceived personal relevance of the software tool by using two measurement items that were intended to assess personal relevance: (1) ‘‘Using this software tool would be relevant to my study in school,’’ and (2) ‘‘Using this software tool would be relevant to my future job.’’ The mean scores of these two items for all subjects, based on a 7-point Likert scale, were 5.4 and 5.6, respectively. These results indicated that the subjects perceived the software tool to be highly relevant to them.

To ensure that the experiment results were not biased due to differences in subjects across the four conditions, we tested demographic differences between subjects. The results suggested that subjects did not differ in their prior computer experience $( F = 0 . 3 4 , \ p = 0 . 8 0 )$ , daily computer use $( F = 0 . 2 3 , p = 0 . 8 7 )$ , work experience $\left( F = 1 . 2 5 , \ p = 0 . 3 0 \right)$ , and the number of IS projects previously completed $( F = 0 . 4 0 , p = 0 . 7 5 )$ . These results suggested that the experiment was not confounded by differences in subjects prior experience and skills in the four groups.

## 4.7. Dependent variables

The dependent variables included users’ perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of the software tool. The measures for these dependent variables were adapted from extant instruments in prior literature [21]. All items were measured using 7-point Likert scales ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree’’. These items are presented in Appendix B.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Validity and reliability of measures

Convergent and discriminant validities of the measures were assessed using exploratory factor analysis. We used data collected from both $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 }$ to test them. As shown in Table 1, all measurement items loaded on their intended construct significantly higher than on the other construct. The factor structure was robust across $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 } ,$ demonstrating similar patterns of factor loadings. Overall, the results supported the convergent and discriminant validities of all measures.

Cronbach’s alpha was computed to assess the reliability of the measures of perceived usefulness and ease of use. The alpha coefficients for perceived usefulness at $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 }$ were 0.80 and 0.85, respectively. The alpha coefficients for perceived ease of use at $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 }$ were 0.90 and 0.86, respectively. All reliability coefficients were 0.80 or higher, thus we considered them to have satisfactory levels of reliability.

## 5.2. Descriptive statistics results

Correlation analyses between perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use at $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 }$ showed that the two dependent variables were significantly correlated both at $t _ { 1 } \left( r = 0 . 5 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ and at $t _ { 2 } \left( r = 0 . 5 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ . We then obtained the mean scores and standard deviations of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use for each experimental group for $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 } .$ As shown in Table 2, Group 1’s (no training/low quality argument) scores were generally lower than the other three groups. On the other hand, Group 4 (training/strong persuasion) consistently scored higher than the other three groups in both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. The scores at $t _ { 1 }$ for all groups and constructs were generally higher than those at $t _ { 2 } .$ Finally, perceived usefulness scores were generally higher than perceived ease of use scores across groups and times.

## 5.3. Results of hypothesis testing

We chose MANCOVA and ANCOVA to test the hypotheses. They are widely used in experimental studies when there is the need to control for covariates and can effectively handle category predictor variables. Furthermore, they are effective for testing interaction effects when the sample size is small. We conducted Kolmogorov– Smirnov tests to check the normality of the residuals and did not find any problems. We also tested for heteroscedasticity of the error terms using White’s tests and found no violations. Therefore, we concluded that these methods were appropriate for data analysis in our research.

Since the dependent variables were significantly correlated with one another, we first conducted MANCOVA by including subject’s prior computer experience in the model for $t _ { 1 }$ and both subject’s prior computer experience and actual use of the software tool for t as covariates because these factors could systematically affect subjects’ perceptions about the software tool [15].

Wilks’ Lambdas suggested significant effects of argument quality $( F = 7 4 . 3 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , user training $( F = 1 9 . 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 ) ,$ interaction of argument quality and user training $( F = 3 . 1 8 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and prior computer experience $( F = 3 . 3 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ on the two dependent variables (perceived usefulness and ease of use) at $t _ { 1 }$ . Furthermore, there were significant effects of argument quality $( F = 1 1 . 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , user training $( F = 9 . 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , prior computer experience $( F = 3 . 8 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and software tool usage $( F = 4 . 3 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ on dependent variables at $t _ { 2 } .$

Since MANCOVA results largely supported significant effects of argument quality and user training, we then conducted ANCOVA for each dependent variable independently to test our hypotheses. As shown in Table 3, the results for $t _ { 1 }$ data suggested that argument quality positively affected both perceived usefulness $\left( F = 4 4 . 9 , \right.$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and perceived ease of use $( F = 1 4 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 ) .$ . User training positively affected perceived ease of use $( F = 3 7 . 1$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ but not perceived usefulness $( F = 0 . 4 6 , ~ p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . The interaction effect of argument quality and user training existed in perceived ease of use $( F = 4 . 5 7 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ but not in perceived usefulness $( F = 0 . 3 6 , ~ p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . ## 6. Discussion

The focus of our study was on examining how persuasion, user training, and first-hand use interplay in shaping and changing users’ perceived usefulness and ease of use of a new IT when its use was mandatory. The experimental results suggest an interaction effect of argument quality and user training on users’ perceived ease of use of the target technology: the positive effect of argument quality is stronger when users receives no prior training than when users receives training. To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has examined this. Once users receive training, thus gaining some knowledge and experience, the effect of argument quality is reduced. Therefore, managers should take into account conditions under which persuasive messages are delivered to users when anticipating the strength of the effect of argument quality.

![](/api/attachments/EFS5D265/fulltext/images/79eaecdedc0393c7bc641c83fe41d7629fb255063be7af62244142d262c3b7ad.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Interaction effect of argument quality and user training on users’ perceived ease of use of the new software tool.

We found that the extent to which users’ first-hand use experience changed users’ perceptions over time depended on whether and how their initial perceptions are formed by persuasive communication and user training. Specifically, firsthand use changed users’ perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of the target technology more when the initial perceptions were influenced by high quality persuasive arguments. The strong positive effect of high quality persuasive arguments on user perceptions diminishes over time as users accumulate direct experience with the technology.

We found that first-hand use changed users’ initial PU of the target technology more when no prior training was provided prior to persuasive communication. However, we did not find the same pattern for users’ PEOU. In general, users’ PU appears to converge to a more realistic perception based on the objective attributes of the technology as the users accumulate first-hand experience. Furthermore, if users do not receive any training during the adoption process, their initial perceptions tend to converge more than if they receive user training. This suggests that user training helps users form a more realistic, objective perception about the usefulness of the technology in the early stage of the adoption process. Thus the adoption process tends to be more stable and smooth.

One limitation of our research was the use of student subjects, which could potentially limit the generalizability of our findings. However, for the purpose of examining user adoption of new IT, studies employing student subjects do not appear to be systematically different from those using organizational users. Most of our subjects were working full-time or part-time and had about 2 years of prior work experience in the IS area. Nevertheless, the subjects were detached from the real-world business context. Thus they might not have had the opportunity to appreciate and understand the full impact of the technology since their use of it might not represent a full professional agenda. For example, the use of student subjects might explain why the interaction effect of argument quality and training on users’ perceived usefulness (H1) was not supported.

Due to our context, the results might only be valid in a particular setting in which the use of the technology was mandatory and alternative software tools were not available.

Although we conducted the experiment over a semester, the subjects’ use of the technology was relatively short in time. In addition, the target technology was a software tool for system analysis and design, which may not generalize to other types of technology.
