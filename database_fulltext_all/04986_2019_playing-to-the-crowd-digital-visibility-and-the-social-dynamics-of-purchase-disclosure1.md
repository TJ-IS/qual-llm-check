# PLAYING TO THE CROWD? DIGITAL VISIBILITY AND THE SOCIAL DYNAMICS OF PURCHASE DISCLOSURE<sup>1</sup>

Lauren Rhue Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, 7621 Mowatt Lane, College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {lrhue@rhsmith.umd.edu}

Arun Sundararajan Stern School of Business, New York University, 44 West 4<sup>th</sup> Street, New York, NY 10012 U.S.A. {digitalarun@nyu.edu}

As people share more of their lives on social media, their consumption and purchase behaviors become increasingly visible to their peers, spawning a new form of digitally enabled conspicuous consumption. We examine how the ensuing dynamics of social commerce affect these visibility choices. We use data from a platform dedicated exclusively to facilitating the display of verified online and offline purchases with optional product commentaries. We use fixed effects and matching techniques to control for endogeneity in commentary and consumer disclosure, while embedding a new text-based model of product similarity into our econometric analysis and capturing product complementarity using network-based measures of similarity. Our findings demonstrate that disclosed purchase patterns are shaped by other users, even those with whom the discloser has no previously stated relationship. We find that the source of attention shapes disclosure behavior, and we uncover a relationship between comments and purchase similarity of disclosed purchases, suggesting tha consumers play to the crowd. The negative relationship between product reviews and purchase disclosure suggest that consumers may disclose a purchase because of a relationship with that particular item and not a desire to habitually reveal purchases. Our results shed new light on the distinction between digital visibility and social buzz, with managerial implications for retailers and social media platforms that support socia consumption.

Keywords: Econometrics, social consumption, social media, network science, online reviews, Facebook

## Introduction

In today’s digital world, consumers are constantly “sharing” information about their activities, from posting selfies and publishing recent event pictures to providing movie recommendations and checking into restaurants or airline clubs. A glance at a Facebook or Instagram feed or a YouTube channel reveals a dizzying array of digitally enabled “conspicuous consumption,” ranging from the more explicit “haul” videos that teenagers use to showcase physical-world purchases to the not-so-subtle advertising of superior personal consumption experiences via social media—from sporting events and fashion shows to vacations and public service. An overlooked by-product of the shift toward digitally mediated social interaction is an increase in the visibility of our commercial choices.

Such visibility might benefit firms and generate positive “buzz” in some cases, but it is also possible that reactions (or non-reactions) during this form of social consumption to specific choices or brands could have a chilling effect on future purchases and decrease engagement with the platform itself. This study investigates how the social dynamics on a platform dubbed the “Twitter for credit cards” shapes whether and how consumers make their consumption behavior visible to others.

Using transaction and interaction data from this social commerce platform and the disclosure of online and offline verified purchases for thousands of users between 2009 and 2011 that it enabled, we uncover new connections between platform-based social interactions and the purchases made conspicuous by consumers. We find that consumers are less likely to divulge their purchases on a social platform if they receive reactions from users with whom they have no connection, with the opposite effect if the feedback they get is from followers, people who have signed up to receive updates about the consumer. We find that consumers who continue to engage with the platform after their first disclosure tend to disclose purchases that are similar to those that received positive feedback.

The relationship we uncover between comments and purchase similarity suggests that consumers play to the crowd. More broadly, our findings point to an interesting and unexplored relationship between visibility choices and social dynamics, highlighting the importance of perceptions of not just friends, but of strangers as well.

This research makes several contributions to the information systems literature. Our work investigates how peer feedback—for people with and without significant social ties— can influence what choices are disclosed online. It provides a first investigation into the dynamics of disclosed purchases for people without a stated social connection. Furthermore, this research examines the large-scale online sharing of verified purchases in a context that does not require ratings, unlike other platforms like Yelp or Amazon in which consumers reveal their consumption but are required to leave a rating or review. This unique context isolates the effect of posting product reviews and receiving feedback on the subsequent choices made by individuals. We also develop a new approach for quantifying product similarity in a setting that involves horizontal product differentiation.

## Background

An intellectual starting point for theorizing about motivations for sharing purchase information among peers comes from the literature on knowledge sharing in organizations. Prior work tells us that employees share their knowledge because they have relevant experience, because they expect reputational benefits, and because they are embedded in the network (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Each of these three drivers of organizational knowledge sharing has an approximate counterpart in our setting of sharing information about product purchases. Consumers with relevant product experiences may feel motivated to share these experiences through online product discussions or electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM). Additionally, consumers may be motivated to transform previously private consumption into public conspicuous consumption (Bearden and Etzel 1982) with the goal of reaping the reputational benefits that come from these acquisition and consumption choices being visible to their peers. Furthermore, the propensity of consumers to continue or discontinue sharing information about purchases may be shaped in part by the nature of their relationships, and the impact their disclosure choice has on this relationship, with the people who provide feedback and/or engage with these purchase disclosures.

## Electronic Word-of-Mouth

Electronic word-of-mouth is a broad concept; one definition that helps focus our discussion is from Hennig-Thurau et al. (2004), who define it as

any positive or negative statement made by potential, actual, or former customers about a product or company, which is made available to a multitude of people and institutions via the Internet (p. 39).

A wide variety of prior research has established how aggregate purchase patterns are influenced by different forms of eWOM, including online product reviews (Chen et al. 2004; Duan et al. 2008a, 2008b; Zhu and Zhang 2010), consumer recommendations (Smith et al. 2005), and retailer reviews (Chatterjee 2001), and how these aggregate purchase patterns generate value for producers and retailers (Shih et al. 2013) and also affect consumers’ opinions and retailers’ profits (Wang et al. 2012).

Our study examines a related yet distinct aspect of eWOM: how decisions made by consumers to disclose purchases are shaped by other users’ opinions of their past purchases. Although much eWOM literature assumes a connection between product purchase, product disclosure, and product review, these activities can be separated. For example, for many years, Amazon.com has tagged reviews as being either verified purchases or not; many prior papers such as Anderson and Simester (2014) have examined product reviews without evidence of purchase. The specific strand of prior research into eWOM that is most relevant to our question is the study of consumer motivation for creating product-specific reviews. Indeed, consumers engage in eWOM for many reasons.

These reasons include both social factors such as concern for others and a desire to build relationships as well as economic factors (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004; Shih et al. 2013; Yoo and Gretzal 2008).

In our data, we notice that a majority of disclosed purchases have no accompanying product reviews. Rather, consumers are sharing details of their purchased products without revealing their opinions of the products, an outcome not explained by any of the eWOM motivations from the literature; the mere disclosure of a purchase contributes little to consumer know-how like eWOM does (Gruen et al. 2006). Our study thus permits the decoupling of the decision to disclose that a purchase has been made from the decision to leave a product review and explores the connection between the act of leaving a review and the desire to disclose future purchases. Since prior studies such as Li and Hitt (2008) have suggested that consumers who leave reviews differ from those who do not, a first natural question for us to ask is whether disclosers (consumers who disclose the purchase but do not leave a review) differ from reviewers (consumers who disclose the purchase and leave a product review) on their continued engagement with the platform. The answer to this question is not immediately clear ex ante. On the one hand, people who leave reviews exert more effort than other consumers, potentially indicating a more engaged and motivated individual who thus could have a higher propensity toward future purchase disclosure; however, reviewers may be motivated more by the desire to share their positive or negative experiences with a particular product and less by other motivations such as status or social factors. Thus, a reviewer may feel less compelled to disclose additional purchases.

Additionally, given that consumers read reviews rather than relying on the summaries (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), products with reviews (which have more content) should generate more conversation than purchase disclosures. This leads us to expect that consumers who leave product reviews are more likely to receive comments from other users on the platform than those who do not, a conjecture we also test later in the paper. If reviewers disengage after their initial visit, then it would indicate a motivation to help others since users with an attention-seeker motivation would continue to disclose purchases in the face of additional comments (Ellison et al. 2006).

## Self-Presentation

## Continued Disclosure

As discussed earlier, our data set contains, on the face of it, evidence that many consumers disclose their purchase history for reasons other than providing helpful product information. One such reason might be embodied in the phenomenon of conspicuous consumption—the use of one’s consumption activities to advertise economic power and/or to gain social status—which has been studied for over a hundred years (Veblen 1899). Esteem, reputation, and self-presentation are motivations for related online behaviors (Bapna et al. 2016; Brennan and Pettit 2004; Kim et al. 2012). Consumers may correspondingly choose to share their purchase history in order to receive attention and/or approval from their peers. Accordingly, we expect that, compared to consumers who do not receive feedback from others, a higher fraction of those consumers who do receive feedback will continue to disclose subsequent purchases.

Prior work has pointed out the possible mediating effects of relationship strength among community members in determining the prevalence of eWOM (Chu and Kim 2011) as well as the types of user-generated content uploaded to social media (Zeng and Wei 2012). It may, therefore, be natural for us to expect that attention from friends will increase the likelihood of disclosing purchases. However, there is also evidence that information from non-friends about products can be more influential than information from friends on consumer choices (Steffes and Burgee 2009), and there may also be a reticence of consumers to disclose what they buy to people whom they know very well. Therefore, we also investigate whether receiving feedback from social network neighbors on past purchases increases or decreases the propensity of consumers to share information about future purchases.

Next, if consumers are motivated to disclose purchases as a way of seeking recognition or esteem (Bernheim 1994; Brennan and Pettit 2011; Kim et al. 2012), one would expect positive feedback on past purchases that have been shared to increase the propensity to share information about future purchases. This hypothesis is also based in part on prior work that has shown how negative reviews have a disproportionate impact on product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). Thus, comment sentiment, particularly negativity, may shape which purchases consumers disclose and have associated with their online identity.

## Disclosure Similarity

There have been a wide variety of prior contexts in which researchers have established the prevalence of conspicuous consumption: to signal wealth (Bagwell and Bernheim 1996; Ireland 1994), to signal affiliation with sustainable products and “green” values (Sexton and Sexton 2011), to signal generositythrough donations (Andreoni and Bernheim 2009), to signal knowledgeability about the products by discovering nonbranded products (Berger and Ward 2010), or to signal uniqueness by acquiring novel items (Ariely and Levav 2000; Berger and Heath 2007; Ratner and Kahn 2002). Thus, if consumers are even partially motivated by self-presentation, then their disclosed purchase histories would focus on tastebased goods (Liu 2007). Moreover, future disclosed purchases would be similar to those that previously received attention. Thus, we expect consumers to share details about purchases similar to those that previously received comments, conditional on their prior purchase disclosures.

Our discussion hinges on a few simplifying assumptions about consumer behavior. We assume that consumers continue to make purchases largely independent of their use of social media and that they have future purchases about which they can share information if they are so inclined. We also assume that consumers who make multiple purchases can choose to share details about just a subset of these purchases.

## Data

Our study uses data from a platform named Blippy, an online social network for the public disclosure of and sharing information about purchases, and initially billed as “Twitter for credit card purchases.” Users joined Blippy by connecting their Blippy account to a credit card or to an account with a participating retailer such as Amazon or iTunes. There was much fanfare and buzz around Blippy in 2009 and 2010 (Tsotsis 2011), but a security breach and uncertainty surrounding their growth and profitability strategies led Blippy to shutter its website in 2011. Although Blippy did not sustain operations, other venues for social consumption have emerged such as Venmo, Mine, and Facebook, indicating that the phenomenon of sharing consumption choices on social media platforms persists after Blippy’s demise.

## Data Source

Our full dataset consists of 1.3 million consumer purchases on the Blippy platform ranging from 2009 to 2011. All of the purchase information shared on Blippy are shown on the consumer’s profile in reverse chronological order. Each purchase shows the retailer, product details, consumer product review (blank if no review written), transaction timestamp, purchase price, and time-stamped comments. Consumers may have multiple products with the same transaction timestamp because consumers often purchase multiple items in a single transaction, so we consider all consumer purchases with the same timestamp to belong to the same receipt.

For each user, we have a username, a profile picture, a user’s description of him/herself, and the user’s social network. The directed social network was shown in the user profile as the list of user-affirmed following/follower ties, potentially imported Twitter and Facebook.

For each product, we gather standardized product characteristics from Blippy such as a product name, product identifier, user-generated product description, and usergenerated tags. Because these product-specific descriptors were standard across all consumer purchases, they form the basis for the distance-based product similarity measure.

Because Products and Retailers are indicator variables, their means are the percentages of those product types and retailers in the dataset. The high percentage of taste-based goods and the large number of individuals who chose not to reveal the prices of their purchases suggest that consumers share purchases that signal their individual tastes, not expensive or exclusive items that signal wealth.

For all subsequent analyses, the dataset is restricted to consumers’ first receipt (i.e., their first set of consumer purchases. We place this restriction in order to best isolate the influence of social reactions and reduce the noise from previous experiences and interactions on Blippy. Even through the dataset is restricted based on the receipt timestamp, comments are always associated with a particular item in the receipt and the items are not identified as belonging to a shopping basket. Therefore, it is possible to examine the effect of feedback to a particular purchase on the decisions of whether to divulge consumption choices and the nature of the product choices that are disclosed. ## Methodology

## Reviews, Comments, and Continued Use

## Identification Strategy

First, we investigate how product reviews affect the propensity for consumers to continue to use the website as well as the propensity for consumers to receive comments. To account for the endogeneity of reviews and continued use, we use coarsened exact matching (CEM) to create a matched dataset to control for observable differences in consumer and item level characteristics between the treatment and control groups (Iacus et al. 2012). Our “treatment” is Review Category, an indicator variable for whether consumers reviewed all purchases in their first receipt. To reduce noise, the dataset is restricted to consumers who either review all their purchases or none of their purchases (48% and 38% of consumers respectively), and the following attributes are used to match consumers: the number of Following and Follower ties, Product Categories, and Retailers. We chose to control for product type and retailer because eWOM is shaped by the product types and attributes of purchases (Mudambi and Schuff 2010) and the social network (Chu and Kim 2011). This matched dataset is used to estimate the next set of models.

## Model Estimation

The first model focuses on the relationship between product reviews and continued use, and the model specification is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname{logit} \left(p \left(\text {ContUse} _ {i, 2} = 1\right)\right) = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text {ReviewCategory} _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {2} \text {ItemCategory} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {3} \text {Retailer} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {4} \text {Month} + \beta_ {5} \text {Degree} _ {i, 1} + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The dependent variable, ContUse, is a binary variable for whether the consumer disclosed another set of purchases to Blippy after the first set of purchases. Review Category, the treatment variable, is an indicator of whether all the consumers’ shared purchases have reviews. ItemCategory includes all the indicator variables for the product category: Application, Album, Book, Beauty, Movie, Other, Product, Song, and TV. Retailer contains indicator variables for the retailer. Month is the month that the consumer purchased items, and Degree is the consumer’s degree in the social network. The unit of analysis is the consumer, shown by the subscript i for a total of N consumers, and the time frame is the first set of consumer purchases (t = 1) for the right-hand side of equation (1) and t = 2 for the dependent variable. We anticipate a negative relationship between reviews and continued disclosure, so we expect a negative coefficient estimate on Review Category to indicate that consumers are less likely to continue to disclose their purchases after leaving a product review.

The second estimation focuses on the relationship between product reviews and comments, and the model specification is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname{logit} \left(p \left(\text {Comment} _ {i, 1} = 1\right)\right) = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text {ReviewCategory} _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {2} \text {ItemCategory} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {3} \text {Retailer} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {4} \text {Month} + \beta_ {5} \text {Degree} _ {i, 1} + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The dependent variable, Comment, is a binary variable for whether the consumer received a comment. Again, Review Category, the treatment variable, is an indicator of whether all the consumers’ disclosed purchases have reviews. Similar to equation (1), ItemCategory includes indicator variables for the product category; Retailer covers the retailer category; Month is the month of consumer purchase; and Degree is the number of incoming and outgoing friendships. We expect a positive relationship between reviews and comments, so a positive coefficient estimate for Review Category would provide support for our supposition.

## The Dynamics of Purchase Disclosure

## Identification Strategy

Second, we examine whether attention in the form of comments influences continued purchase disclosure, and whether the origin and positivity of those comments matter as well. To control for endogeneity, we employ CEM to create another matched dataset. In this analysis, our treatment is the presence of comments, which may be correlated with the consumer’s social network (e.g., more friends may enable some consumers to garner more attention) as well as the attributes of his/her purchases (e.g., some products will naturally gain more attention, regardless of the consumer and his or her connections). Consumer purchases are matched on the following attributes: the consumers’ Following and Followed ties, the number of items on the first receipt, Product Categories, major Retailers, whether the consumer reviewed the products, and the length of those product reviews. These variables are chosen to control for characteristics that could influence the likelihood of receiving comments: how many friends the consumers has, the relative “interestingness” of particular item categories and retailers, and consumers’ investment in reviewing the product. After the matching procedure, we tested the difference in key variables, and Table 2 shows that the treatment and control groups are comparable.

The p-values show no statistically significant differences between the treatment and control groups. Because CEM matches observations within strata with the same coarsened characteristics, the means for all the product category indicator variables are indistinguishable. As shown in Table 2, the variable means indicate the successful matching of observations in the treatment and control groups.

Table 3 shows the correlation among the variables. There is sufficiently low correlation among the key variables to address multicollinearity concerns.

## Model Estimation

To examine the dimensions of comments that shape continued purchase disclosure, we estimate a logistic regression, and the model specification is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {logit} \left(p \left(\text {ContUse} _ {i, 2} = 1\right)\right) = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text {ReviewCategory} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {2} \text {Sentiment} _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {3} \text {Following} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {4} \text {Followers} + \beta_ {5} \text {ItemCategory} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {6} \text {Retailer} _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {7} \text {Month} _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {8} \text {Degree} _ {i, 1} + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

The dependent variable, ContUse, is a binary variable for whether the consumer continued to disclose purchases on Blippy. Comment is Comment Indicator, an indicator of whether the consumer received a comment on any item in the first receipt. We focus on the indicator variable because we examine whether the presence of commentary influences consumer choices instead of how each incremental comment affects consumer choices. Sentiment is Average Comment Sentiment, a measure of comment polarity using the comment text. Comment sentiment is estimated by summing the positive and negative scores assigned to each word in the AFINN dictionary (Nielsen 2011). This list is specifically chosen for its applications in microblogs such as Twitter; Blippy comments and reviews are similar in length and tone to Twitter conversations. Following and Followers are Following Comment Indicator, an indicator of whether the consumer follows any of the commenters, and Follower Comment Indicator, an indicator of whether any of the commenters follows the consumer respectively. Finally, there is the set of control variables: ItemCategories, Retailer, Month, and Degree. These covariates control for time and peer effects.

## The Implications of Product Similarity

## Identification Strategy

We expect that consumers will disclose new purchases that are similar to previous purchases that receive comments. We must quantify product similarity in order to determine whether commentary induces consumers to purchase similar goods—a manner of “playing” to their audience to evoke reactions. The challenge is to find a similarity measure that captures meaningful distinctions among products beyond high-level categories such as music, movies, or sunglasses. To accomplish that goal, we employ two similarity measures: (1) a continuous Euclidean distance measure between constructed item vectors and (2) a continuous network-based measure of product similarity. These two measures are independent and capture two distinct forms of similarity: products with similar attributes and complementary products. By including both measures, we increase the robustness of our findings and avoid making them reliant on a single measure of product similarity. The details on the construction of the product similarity and network-based similarity measures are included in the appendix.

## Model Specification

To examine the effect of feedback on product similarity, we estimate two consumer-level fixed-effects regressions. The first has the item-based measure of product similarity and the second has the network-based product similarity measure. The model specification is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { Similarity } _ {i, (1, 2)} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text { Comment } _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {2} \text { Sentiment } _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {3} \text { Following } _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {4} \text { Followers } _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {5} \text { ItemCategory } _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {6} \text { Retailer } _ {i, 1} + \beta_ {7} N _ {i, 2} + \beta_ {8} \text { SNNPurchase } _ {i, 1} + \\ \beta_ {9} \text { CNPurchase } _ {i, 1} + \varepsilon_ {i} \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

The dependent variable, Similarity, is either the aggregate Product Similarity or Network-based Similarity as described in the Appendix, and the other variables of interest are the same as in equation (3): Comment is Comment Indicator, Sentiment is Average Comment Sentiment, and Following and Followers are Following Comment Indicator and Follower Comment Indicator. The set of covariates include the following variables: ItemCat, Retailers, N (the number of items in the first receipts), SNNPurchase (whether the goods from the consumers’ first receipts were previously purchased by social network neighbors), and CNPurchase (whether commenters previously purchased goods from the consumers’ first receipts). Plus, the model includes consumer-level fixed effects to control for individual-specific heterogeneity in product similarity. These covariates control for time differences among consumers as well as peer effects and effects from item characteristics. Variables and their definitions are summarized in Table 5.

## Continuing to Disclose Consumption

These results examine whether commentary, consumer/commenter relationship, and comment sentiment all influence consumers’ decisions about whether or not to share details about their consumption. The estimation results provide evidence that comments and relationships shape purchase disclosure, as shown by coefficient estimates for Comment Indicator, Following Comment Indicator, and Follower Comment Indicator in Table 7.

First, the significant coefficient estimate on Comment Indicator supports the supposition that comments can shape whether consumers divulge their consumption choices; however, the significantly negative coefficient estimate for Comment Indicator (-0.61) is counter to our expectation, suggesting that consumers who receive comments from the general public are less likely to continue to disclose purchases. This counter-intuitive result (comments make purchase disclosure less appealing) may arise out of the tension between consumers’ interest in sharing personal information and an aversion to sharing information with an unintended audience (Marwick and boyd 2010).

Second, the significant coefficients on the indicators of social network comments suggest that consumer/commenter relationship does influence purchase disclosure choices. There is asymmetry in the effect of the consumer/commenter relationship; Following Commenter Indicator coefficient estimate (-0.70) is significantly negative whereas Follower Commenter Indicator coefficient estimate is significant and positive (1.08). The positive, higher magnitude of the Follower Commenter Indicator as compared to Following Commenter Indicator suggests that consumers place higher esteem on attention from their followers (incoming ties) as compared to the people whom the consumers follow (outgoing ties), perhaps because consumers expect their followers to see their disclosed purchases. Regardless, these estimates are evidence of a potentially asymmetrical effect of followers/following ties on the propensity to disclose consumption decisions.

Third, there is no support for the supposition that the comment sentiment affects engagement. Average Comment Sentiment is not significant, suggesting that consumers may be indifferent about positive or negative comments. Furthermore, many of the comments are neutral because the products spark conversations about the experiences and not necessarily just the products themselves.

Overall, we conclude that comments shape continued engagement and purchase disclosure. These models contain controls for multiple alternate explanations: the types of products, retailers, seasonality, consumers’ social network degrees, and the consumers’ reviews. The indicators for the types of products and retailers enable us to eliminate the theory that users who prefer particular goods are more likely to display those goods. The controls for the year/month of the consumer’s first purchase enable us to control for the initial cohort of users, since perhaps consumers who joined Blippy during its heyday were systematically more likely to continue using the website. The social network connections control for the number of user-affirmed friends on Blippy, and consumers with more social connections may systematically differ from consumers with fewer connections. The variables related to consumer product reviews (the presence, length, and sentiment of the consumer’s product review) indicate consumers enthusiasm for their purchase and their initial investment in the platform. The major caveat is that our data does not allow us to distinguish between consumers who made private purchases and consumers who made no purchases, so we can only interpret the non-continued engagement as some combination of non-disclosed consumption and declining purchase volume.

## The Information Content of Comments

One alternative explanation for these results is that the comments contain information and product recommendations; therefore, consumers’ subsequent purchases are more similar to items with comments because of the information content in the comments and not the mere presence of the comment. To examine this explanation, we assess the comment text and discover that the vast majority of comments, 87%, do not contain any names of any of the items in consumers’ second set of purchases. The remainder of the comments contain more generic terms such as “app,” “love,” “Sephora,” and “like” that are incorporated in other variables such as comment sentiment and product category indicators. In addition, our coefficient estimates are robust to including a variable of intersection of the product names and comments, Comment Information, in the regression. Taken together, there is no evidence that the comments contain information or product recommendations that influence consumers’ future purchase disclosure decisions.

## Discussion

People often use social media to solicit recommendations and opinions (Morris et al. 2010) and to create social capital (Ellison et al. 2007), and in this process, they reveal their consumer choices. To that end, online platforms such as Facebook, Amazon, and Venmo facilitate the “frictionless” sharing of information about consumer purchases. We examine how social dynamics could shape this information sharing, leading to several interesting findings. First, we find that consumers disclose taste-based, experience goods in which the value of the online discussions for other consumers will be higher (Benlian et al. 2012; Greun et al. 2006; Huang et al. 2009). Second, we contribute to the electronic word-ofmouth literature by studying a unique situation in which purchase disclosure is decoupled from product reviews. We find that consumers who leave product reviews are more likely to generate discussion but are less likely to continue to disclose additional purchases. This result suggests that consumer motivations to write product reviews may differ from the motivation to disclose a purchase. Third, we contribute to the social influence discussion by uncovering that feedback from non-friend peers decreases the likelihood of future purchase disclosures, consistent with the theory that the people engage with social media with a particular audience in mind (Marwick and boyd 2010). Because users can interact with all other users on the platform, it is possible to distinguish the effect of feedback induced by friends or social network neighbors (Aral et al. 2009; Bapna and Umyarov 2015) from those induced by other platform peers. When non-friends comment on a consumer’s purchase, s/he may find that “creepy” and choose not to share future purchases, become somewhat risk averse in the purchases s/he reveals, or make fewer similar purchases in the future. In all cases, the platform loses that consumer’s content.

## Limitations

There are a few important limitations in our study. First, we cannot observe consumers’ private purchase decisions, so consumers who cease to publicly disclose their purchases may continue to consume privately or not consume at all, and we cannot distinguish between these two groups. We note, however, that social commerce platforms seek to encourage the engagement and “content production” associated with purchase disclosure, so private consumption and nonconsumption are interchangeable from that perspective. Second, the limited consumer characteristics make it infeasible to discuss or identify the effects of homophily. Third, the dataset may be skewed more heavily toward users who were active at the end of Blippy’s lifespan because it is generated by a breadth first crawl. Fourth, the set of consumers who choose to share their purchases may have extroverted personality traits and may be particularly susceptible to social expectations and feedback. While the behaviors of this consumer population might not represent that of typical consumers, this population is still interesting to firms because of their potential to generate word-of-mouth and influence other consumers’ choices. Fifth, this dataset only captures social reactions on the platform and not offline conversations; however, this limitation indicates that our results capture the lower bound of influence. Even though our dataset is limited to user interaction on a single platform, we still find that social reactions shift purchase disclosure patterns. Sixth, the social network relies on users actively identifying their other friends on the platform so our quantification of the social network may not capture every relationship among users, but every relationship captured is an identified and accurate relationship.

## Conclusion

Platforms with social consumption elements push consumers to publicly disclose purchase details to encourage increased awareness and sales (Duan et al. 2008a) even if consumers do not give overtly positive reviews of the product (Chatterjee 2001); however, this study demonstrates that not all attention is created equal. Firms should reconsider the much soughtafter metric of “engagement” that many social media investments afford and determine whether the ultimate goal is visibility (e.g., more purchases made public) or buzz (e.g., more discussion around a potentially smaller pool of visible goods). The ability to engage and sustain consumers is important for those who manage social platforms because community participation also shapes consumers’ value for the platform (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013).

We also observed that consumers shared information about the purchases related to their “social identity” such as movies and music (Berger and Heath 2007) and this type of purchase disclosure mimics their social media profiles (Liu 2007). As the opportunities for consumers to share their purchasing behaviors with their peers expand in scope and variety, it would be interesting to see if/when consumers divulge purchases that do not have an obvious status or identity component. If consumers choose to disclose their commodity purchases, such as paper towels, would those purchases still be shaped by comments? Is there inherently a signaling component to all purchases or just discretionary, taste-based purchases? These remain fascinating questions for future work.

Many decades of research in marketing, economics, and information systems have given us a keen awareness that the social context is important in visible consumption activities (Andreoni and Bernheim 2009; Bearden and Etzel 1982; Bernheim 1994; Veblen 1899). Consumers are increasingly choosing use social media to disclose their purchasing decisions, as social commerce platforms such as Venmo indicate. Future research should continue to capitalize on the heterogeneity in these social commerce platforms to understand the differential influence of peers with and without social network connections, and with and without shared prior purchases, on consumers’ decisions to disclose their purchase choices.

## Appendix

## Product Similarity: Euclidean Distance Between Items

Since the findings hinge on this product similarity measure, and its construction is somewhat involved, we describe it in some detail. First, we represent products as vectors of keywords from the user-generated tags on the profile pages. These basic product descriptions are simple words and phrases such as “drama” or “a location-based mobile application”; however, the vocabulary (i.e., the set of all words mentioned on any product page) initially contains thousands of words. Next, the keywords are processed by removing common and unique words and appending the user-generated category onto the user-generated keyword to incorporate categorical information. For example, the television show Community is tagged with the phrase “Dan Harmon” in the “Creator” category, so the phrase “Dan Harmon” is changed to “creator\_dan” and “creator \_harmon.” The product vectors with the full vocabulary are thus extremely large, and we therefore use a text-based latent semantic indexing (LSI) model to reduce the dimensionality of the item vectors to 300 dimensions (Bradford 2008). Finally, after creating these product vectors, we measure the dissimilarity of the product vectors using their Euclidean distance. For example, the television shows Community and The Simpsons, based on their product profile pages, are similar products with a Euclidean distance of 7.5. By contrast, the Lady Gaga song Telephone and the Shozu application, based on their product profiles pages, are quite different and receive a higher Euclidean distance of 125. The distance between the product vectors is reversed and then normalized as a percentage of the largest distance to create the Product Similarity score.

Once to the item-to-item Product Similarity measure is calculated, the measure is then aggregated to the consumer level. For each consumer, we calculate the distances between each item purchased in the first receipt with each item purchased in the second receipt, and we record the minimum Product Similarity score. Because the measure is calculated for items in the first and second receipts, only consumers who continue to engage with visible consumption after their first experience (i.e., Continued Use equals 1) receive a Product Similarity score. Our rationale for aggregating the product similarity using the maximum, not the mean, is that if consumers narrow the scope of their visible consumption due to social reactions, then the least similar shared purchases should be more similar for consumers with social reactions than for those without social reactions. The outcome variable of interest in this analysis is the product similarity score of the product vectors of the consecutive consumer purchases.

## Network-Based Product Similarity: Jointly Purchased Items

The second measure of product similarity is based on the consumer network and is independent of product attributes or user-generated keywords. Some products may appear to have divergent attributes despite an underlying complementarity; for example, digital music players and digital albums exhibit superficial differences in product attributes: one is an expensive consumer electronics product and the other is a relatively inexpensive musical good. However, their underlying complementarity is evident by how frequently consumers purchase them together. Therefore, the observation that a set of goods are commonly purchased together by consumers indicates some latent similarity not necessarily observable by the product attributes alone. The more often the items are purchased together, the stronger the latent similarity. To create this measure, for each pair of consecutively purchased items in the dataset, we add the number of other consumers who have purchased both products and use the log-transformation of the total co-purchasers as the Network-based Product Similarity score. If the consumer is the only person in the dataset who purchased these two items, then the Network-based Product Similarity of the two items is 0 whereas if 100 other consumers purchased the same two items then the Network-based Product Similarity is 4.6. This Network-based Product Similarity serves as a robustness check to the attribute-based Product Similarity measure.
