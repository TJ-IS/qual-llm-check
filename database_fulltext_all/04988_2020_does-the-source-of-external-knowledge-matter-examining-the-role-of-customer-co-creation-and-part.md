# Does the source of external knowledge matter? Examining the role of customer co-creation and partner sourcing in knowledge creation and innovation

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Innovation Customer co-creation External knowledge

## A B S T R A C T

External knowledge is widely acknowledged to be critical for organizational innovation. However, we lack understanding of how di<sup>f</sup>erent sources of knowledge namely knowledge from customers and partners in-<sup>fl</sup>uence innovation. Grounded in the knowledge-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, we theoretically develop and empirically test a model of mechanisms through which two forms of external knowledge acquisition i.e., customer co creation and partner sourcing, foster knowledge creation and innovation. We posit that the di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of external knowledge acquisition contribute di<sup>f</sup>erentially toward innovation outcomes. Finally, we examine the moderating role of knowledge dissemination capability on the relationships between external knowledge of both forms. We test our proposed model using data gathered from 655 organizations in two knowledge intensive sectors i.e., <sup>fi</sup>nancial and information technology. Our model explains 65 % of the variance in knowledge creation and highlights that organizations with higher degree of knowledge creation are more likely to innovate through new patents. We extend prior research on customer co-creation and organizational innovation by identifying intervening organizational variables and mechanisms that explicate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of external knowledge on innovation. The results provide important implications for organizations regarding the capabilities needed to utilize external knowledge for innovation

## 1. Introduction

Innovation is key to sustained competitiveness in current dynamic business environments, serving as a salient way to challenge the com moditization of products and services [1,2]. Contemporary organizations are increasingly relying on external knowledge as a means to overcome innovation barriers, such as declining economies of scale, slow speed to market, and creativity blocks [3–6,96,107]. For example, given the changing business environment, long-established companies like General Electric (GE) are adapting innovative practices. Realizing that innovation through global R&D centers employing thousands of researchers may not be su<sup>fi</sup>cient [99], GE has been experimenting with open innovation and leveraging external knowledge e.g., through putting up challenges on GrabCAD. Indeed, external knowledge from customers and partners can o<sup>f</sup>er several advantages for the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s in novation as compared to internal knowledge. These include contributing more diverse ideas [105] and <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand knowledge of requirements [11], as well as reducing costs of innovation as compared to internal R&D, improving new product/service acceptance, and obtaining continuous innovation [81,85].

Yet, even with the increasing use of external knowledge in innovation, there is limited understanding of various sources of such knowledge and if they contribute di<sup>f</sup>erentially toward innovation outcomes. The extant literature has primarily explored the role of partner knowledge [3,105,48,88], while a separate stream of research has examined how and why users or customers contribute to innovation [4,92,60,76]. Although there has been an increased interest in examining the e<sup>f</sup>ects of customer co-creation on innovation, its impact relative to other salient forms of external knowledge, such as partner sourcing, is unclear and understudied [48,90]. Customer co-creation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the direct engagement of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s customers in the design and delivery of its goods and services [37,61]. Examples of customer cocreation can be found in the Lego Group, as well as Sony’s development of its PlayStation 2 with customer ideas [92]. Partner sourcing refers to seeking knowledge from entities such as suppliers, competitors, and universities or research centers [22,22,23]. For example, the automotive industry is replete with instances of car companies’ co-in novation with suppliers [58].

The premise of our study is that co-creation with customers is likely to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent from using partner knowledge for innovation for three key reasons. First, the nature of the innovation contributions di<sup>f</sup>ers in that partners usually provide fewer, speci<sup>fi</sup>c, and better thought-out ideas [25]. For instance, Gattiker et al. [41] noted that B2B partners tend to take into account situational factors and available product attributes while providing innovation inputs. Customers, on the other hand, tend to provide a large number of ideas that may be less feasible to implement (see also [64,94]), e.g., My Starbucks Idea [67]. Second, partners and customers di<sup>f</sup>er in their motivations for contributing innovative ideas. Partners are likely to be ultimately motivated by their respective business bottom lines over partnership ob jectives [57,101]. Partners are more likely to provide only those ideas that: (1) are less central to their core competencies; or (2) may have synergies with their business operations [86]. Partners, especially competitors, proceed with caution in co-creation because of potential risks associated with intellectual property rights and risks associated with losing market share [44]. Customers, on the other hand, are motivated to participate in the co-creation process because they: (1) identify with the products of the organization and want to improve them, (2) enjoy the experience of innovating, (3) want to enhance their reputation, and (4) like to share their experiences with others [39,60,64]. Third, customers usually communicate their innovation ideas through brand communities or through frontline employees of the organization [30,96]. However, partners are more likely to share their ideas directly with employees of the organization with whom they in teract, or even through formal alliances [91]. These di<sup>f</sup>erences have implications for the ways in which the two sources of external knowl edge can be leveraged for organizational innovation and can help address concerns raised by prior research (see [92]) regarding customer involvement in the innovation process.

The objective of this research is to investigate the di<sup>f</sup>erential role of customer co-creation versus partner sourcing in enhancing the knowl edge creation through which an organization can innovate. Although this is not always the case, we suggest that even when an organization is convinced about the need for external knowledge in innovation, it would still need to understand if customer knowledge and partner sourcing can be di<sup>f</sup>erentially leveraged for enhancing innovation. Using the conceptual framework from Nambisan [75], we theorize the intervening organizational mechanisms that translate customer inputs and other external knowledge into innovation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, grounding our work in the knowledge-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (see [46]), our research investigates if two distinct sources of external knowledge in<sup>fl</sup>u ence organizational knowledge creation $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ the ability of an organi zation to produce new knowledge and original ideas that serve its objectives [80]. For example, the di<sup>f</sup>erences in knowledge creation for customer co-creation versus partner sourcing are likely to arise because customers tend to provide a large number of ideas that may not always be feasible [67,84]. This may result in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant additional costs for the organization to process and <sup>fi</sup>lter customer ideas $\mathrm { e . g . , }$ , in the case of Dell IdeaStorm [30], which reduces knowledge creation and resultant innovation.

As part of our theorization, we also identify knowledge dissemina tion capability (KDC) as a key intervening capability that can play a vital role in enhancing organizational knowledge creation and thereby its innovation outcomes. We propose a moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of KDC on organizations’ ability to share and distribute knowledge with those who need it [62,97], helps to share knowledge within the organization e.g., through knowledge management systems (KMS), and increase the number of available ideas to solve a problem at hand. The need for such a capability is likely to di<sup>f</sup>er for di<sup>f</sup>erent sources of external knowl edge. For example, the need for KDC may be higher for disseminating ideas from customers, as compared to partners, because of the higher volume of ideas provided. Thus, we seek to achieve the following

research objectives:

1) Examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of customer co-creation versus partner sourcing on organization’s knowledge creation, and subsequently, innova tion; and

2) Examine the di<sup>f</sup>erential moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of KDC for the relationships between the two forms of external knowledge acquisition with knowledge creation.

Our research makes the following four contributions. First, ou study contributes to the research on organizational innovation by ex amining if the external knowledge acquisition in the form of custome co-creation and partner sourcing in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge creation. Among the limited work at the intersection of knowledge creation and cus tomer-based innovation literatures, Nambisan [75] is notable for setting a broad agenda for researchers to explore the role played by organi zational capabilities in enhancing customers contributions to innova tion. The current research aims to answer this question by theoretically and empirically examining if di<sup>f</sup>erent sources of external knowledge (customer and partners) contribute di<sup>f</sup>erentially to knowledge creation and innovation in organizations. Second, our research serves as a step forward by investigating the intervening organizational processes that convert external knowledge into innovations. A rich stream of literature (e.g. [102],) has explained that external knowledge can be a valuable resource for organizational innovation. To better understand the me chanisms underlying the linkage between external knowledge and in novation, it is important to examine the speci<sup>fi</sup>c capabilities that may help organizations leverage external knowledge for innovation. An explication of these intervening variables is relevant for both re searchers as well as practitioners. For researchers, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can provide a more nuanced understanding of the knowledge creation mechanism. For practitioners, this understanding can allow organiza tions to create knowledge by leveraging both customer and partne knowledge for innovation. Third, our research highlights the role of speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational capabilities facilitated by KMS i.e., KDC, in driving organizational innovation. Prior research (e.g. [22],) has sug gested that KMS, such as online repositories, forums, and yellow pages, can drive innovation. However, research about the speci<sup>fi</sup>c capabilitie supported by KMS that are most bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for innovation is limited and much needed. Our research theorizes and empirically examines if KDC, as enabled by a KMS, can help foster organizational innovation. Thi research responds to the calls by senior information systems (IS) scholars for a more immersive view of information technology (IT) in IS research (e.g., [35,103]). El Sawy [35], for example, argued that “it may be time for a natural shift of emphasis from the Connection view to the Immersion View to the Fusion view as IT continues to morph and augment its capabilities” (p. 588). El Sawy [35] explains the Immersion view of IS as one in which “IT is immersed within the business en: vironment and cannot be separated from work (p. 593) and Fusion view of IS as one in which “IT is not only immersed, but it is fused with the business environment such that they are indistinguishable to ou perception” (p. 591).

In the remainder of the paper, we develop a theoretical model and report on our empirical test of the model utilizing survey and patent data of 655 organizations from two highly knowledge-intensive industries [93] i.e., <sup>fi</sup>nancial and IT, that can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from external knowledge for innovation. We conclude with a discussion of the theoretical and practical implications of our research.

## 2. Theoretical background

In this section, we make use of the knowledge-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm to highlight the important role played by external knowledge sourcing in organizational knowledge creation and innovation. We conceptualize external knowledge sourcing in terms of partner sourcing (i.e., knowledge from suppliers, competitors, and other partners) and customer co-creation. We then review the relevant literature on knowledge creation and innovation to outline the gap in prior research that our study seeks to address.

## 2.1. Role of external knowledge

Incorporating external knowledge for organizational innovation has hurdles. Companies have traditionally considered external knowledge as hard to <sup>fi</sup>nd and risky to rely upon, resulting in the “not invented here” syndrome [26,28]. This and other sources of organizational resistance to external innovations, can result in innovation ine<sup>fi</sup>ciencies [56]. Thus, organizations would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from understanding the mechanisms under which external knowledge could be most e<sup>f</sup>ectively leveraged for innovation. Prior research in various disciplines, such as organizational behavior, IS, and management, has examined organi zational knowledge creation, its processes, and its enablers in some depth (e.g. [65–52],). We aim to build on this research by examining if external knowledge – especially the knowledge acquired through customer co-creation and partner sourcing in tandem – results in knowledge creation and innovation outcomes for the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

The knowledge‐based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, in turn, contends that such specialized knowledge resources drive sustainable competitive advantage [45]. Accordingly, organizations may engage in knowledge sourcing to gain a variety of ideas for value creation [22,49]. Gray and Meister [47] term knowledge sourcing as the seeking of information from di<sup>f</sup>erent entities such as suppliers, government agencies, universities, and research agencies (see also [22,59]). Of particular im portance to the current research is the notion that customers are a key source of knowledge for organizations [48]. This is consistent with prior research, which has found that customers can play an active role in the innovation process [76,96]. Thus, we conceptualize external knowledge sourcing in terms of two constructs in our model i.e., partner sourcing and customer co-creation. Partner sourcing refers to seeking knowledge from entities such as suppliers, competitors, universities, or research centers [22,59] e.g., obtaining speci<sup>fi</sup>c technical know-how from an academic research center. Customer co-creation is the direct engagement of customers in the design and delivery of goods and services produced by a <sup>fi</sup>rm [37,61] e.g., customers providing product ideas on brand communities.

Here, we would like to highlight that the concept of customer cocreation includes, but is not limited to the notion of lead user innovation in the innovation literature [98]. Indeed, lead users are a subset of customers, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as members of a user population who: (1) expect to gain relatively high bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by <sup>fi</sup>nding a solution to their needs, and so may innovate; and (2) are at the leading edge of important trends in a marketplace, and so are currently experiencing needs that will later be experienced by many users in that marketplace. For example, organi zations like 3 M identify lead users to carry out speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation tasks [98]. Customer cocreation, on the other hand, refers to inputs from any customer (not only lead users) toward the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovation. Customers usually have hedonic, reputational, and altruistic motives for cocreation [39] rather than <sup>fi</sup>nancial goals, and may not necessarily have the most current knowledge required to solve the organizational problem.

The knowledge-based view suggests that a <sup>fi</sup>rm can gain competi tive advantage through the application of knowledge resources through its network [63]. Furthermore, Amit and Schoemaker [57] posit that the encompassing construct previously called "resources" can be divided into resources and capabilities, where capabilities are <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and are used to engage resources within the <sup>fi</sup>rm, such as capability to transfer knowledge within the <sup>fi</sup>rm [58]. In the current research, we focus on KDC as a key capability that could assist in the conversion of external knowledge resources for innovation. Consistent with Kingston [62], KDC is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the organization’s ability to share and distribute knowledge as needed (see also [97],). In our context, this applies to knowledge from external partners and customers who are engaged in co-creation with the organization. Knowledge creation refers to the ability of an organization to produce new knowledge and original ideas that serve its objectives [80].

## 2.2. Knowledge creation and innovation

In this research, we consider customer co-creation, partner sourcing, and innovation in the light of the knowledge-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The knowledge-based view considers knowledge as the most valuable resource of an organization [45] and suggests that innovation can be achieved by accessing external knowledge [46]. In conjunction with the shift to a knowledge-based economy (e.g., digitalization, internetworking, virtualization, the role of online networks, and services), the knowledge-based view has gained importance for explaining organizational innovation [11].

Prior research in several disciplines, including management and IS, has examined organizational knowledge creation, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of new knowledge by organizational entities to serve its objectives [80]. Following the classic work by Nonaka [79], subsequent research has explored various enablers of knowledge creation in organizations. For example, Lee and Choi [65] examined the e<sup>f</sup>ects of organizational structure, culture, people, and technology elements on knowledge creation. Based on their empirical study, they found that centralization, collaboration, trust, and IT support contribute toward organizational knowledge creation. While Malhotra et al. [70] explained that collaboration with supply chain partners can create knowledge for the organization, several other studies (e.g. [110]) have explicated the role of individuals e.g., knowledge facilitators, in enabling knowledge creation. However, we observed a lack of research, as also indicated in recent work (e.g. [60],), that links external knowledge, including customer inputs, to knowledge creation in organizations.

At the same time, there is a stream of research (see [11]) on how (e.g. [95],) and why (e.g. [76],) external knowledge sources contribute to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovation. These studies have mainly considered absorptive capacity as the key characteristic of an organization that allows it to assimilate external knowledge for innovation (e.g. [36,38],). However, such <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not provide the whole picture because absorptive capacity is a complex construct that addresses how knowledge is received” and integrated into the <sup>fi</sup>rm. This construct relates to various capabilities that allow a <sup>fi</sup>rm to integrate knowledge [71]. Other studies such as Hagedoorn and Wang [50] considered external knowledge as a whole and related its exploitation for innovation to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s R&D in tensity and R&D collaborations with other organizations. Furthermore, work by Hagedoorn and Wang [50] and other similar studies (e.g. [22],) mainly tried to explore if internal and external sources of knowledge complement each other, rather than examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of external knowledge acquisition as we aim to do.

Among the literature that examined the role of speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of external knowledge in innovation, some studies focused on one particular type or source of external knowledge. For example, Hsueh et al. [112] observed that <sup>fi</sup>rms with stronger business partner networks achieved better innovation performance, without testing the mechan isms of how this happens. In terms of partner sources, while Beers and Zand [66] tested the impact of relationship commitment and diversity with partners on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ service innovation focus, Fang [37] examined the innovation impact of customer co-creation in B2B dyads. Yet, an other set of studies (e.g. [60],) investigated how online tools or platforms enable users or consumers to participate in software innovation. Our review revealed limited work comparing the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of different forms of external knowledge acquisition for innovation. Among the few studies that we found, Vega-Jurado et al. [111] compared two strategies (buying vs. cooperating) and two types of sources (industrial vs. scienti<sup>fi</sup>c agents) in terms of their e<sup>f</sup>ects on innovation, while Kang and Kang [59] compared the e<sup>f</sup>ects of three ways of external knowledge sourcing (information transfer from informal networks, R&D collaboration, and technology acquisition) on technology innovation.

Thus, while the importance of customer involvement and partner sourcing for innovation is acknowledged, our review points to a yet un<sup>fi</sup>lled need for research comparing e<sup>f</sup>ects of these two forms of external knowledge on innovation. Moreover, there is a need to understand the intervening mechanisms through which customer co-creation and partner sourcing in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation.

## 3. Research Model and Hypothesis Development

The goal of this study is to understand the mechanisms that enable customer co-creation and partner sourcing to create knowledge and foster innovation for the organization. We propose that the ideas pro duced through customer co-creation and partner sourcing are likely to increase knowledge creation, though more strongly for partner sourcing than customer co-creation. We also posit that knowledge creation can be strengthened further by e<sup>f</sup>ective KDC. That is, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of cus tomer co-creation and partner sourcing on knowledge creation would be enhanced by KDC. Fig. 1 presents our research model.

## 3.1. External knowledge

With the advent of new IT, organizations can easily move beyond traditional sources for knowledge creation [25,66]. Even the largest R& D active organizations do not rely solely on internal sourcing anymore [24]. Organizations are gathering knowledge relevant to their business problems from a variety of additional sources, such as suppliers, competitors [109], government agencies, universities, and research centers [104]. For example, Apple adopted code from open-source developers to build its new operating system, while SAP and Microsoft set up R&D labs at universities to tap their knowledge [40]. Among competitors, Sony and Samsung have drawn knowledge from each other to develop large LCD panels [44]. Companies also source knowledge from government agencies, for example, Allstate Insurance collaborated with the City of Chicago through an open government data project [72].

The knowledge obtained from such heterogeneous partners is considered more diverse than internally available knowledge, which thereby facilitates idea creation Yan and Azadegan [10]. Also, such sourcing often involves speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge that a partner has, which complements the knowledge creation activities of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. For instance, suppliers can provide expert feedback related to a company’s products or services, which enhances re<sup>fi</sup>nement and knowledge creation Yan and Azadegan [10]. Indeed, diverse knowledge is found to stimulate the creation of new ideas [55], while speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge from partners has helped improve organizational processes for many companies, like Amazon [75]. Therefore, we posit that the greater the ability of an organization to acquire external knowledge from partners, such as suppliers and competitors, the more it can create new knowledge to serve its organizational objectives.

## H1. Partner sourcing is positively related to knowledge creation.

We posit customer co-creation as a second external source of knowledge creation. Present day customers demand a much greater role in customizing the world that surrounds them than before. They not only provide feedback and input on their experiences, they also actively participate in online communities to provide ideas toward creating new products and services that better suit their needs, such as on Dell IdeaStorm [30]. Bendapudi and Leone [76] and Nambisan and Baron [76] de<sup>fi</sup>ne a situation where the customers and employees of the organization interact to develop or improve products, services, or processes as customer co-creation. Through customer co-creation initiatives, organizations can potentially gain access to a large number of new ideas that they can then leverage to address their business problems more e<sup>f</sup>ectively and e<sup>fi</sup>ciently [67].

Knowledge creation involves novel rearrangements of ideas, methods, processes, and strategies, among others [74]. When organi zations involve customers as co-creators, interacting employees and customers are able to challenge traditional problem solving perspectives and discover linkages that have not been discovered before [37]. Acting as partial employees, these customers can in<sup>fl</sup>uence various parts of the problem-solving process, such as through idea generation and/or providing <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand knowledge of their requirements [98]. Customers may thus serve as valuable participants and help in creating new combinations of knowledge, thereby enhancing the knowledge creation of the organization.

## H2. Customer co-creation is positively related to knowledge creation.

The key premise of our work is that co-creation with customers is likely to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent from partner sourcing in its e<sup>f</sup>ect on innovation. We argue that there are two key di<sup>f</sup>erences between customers and partners that would have a bearing on their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on knowledge creation. First, partners tend to provide fewer and better thought-out ideas. Customers, on the other hand, are likely to provide a large number of ideas that may not be well thought-out, as they generally lack business speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge [94,67]. Customer co-creation would, therefore, require a higher level of organizational resources to <sup>fi</sup>lter customer ideas and determine their feasibility, which would have a bearing on the knowledge creation of the organization. Along these lines, Di Gangi and Wasko [30] illustrated that the complexity of the idea has a direct bearing on its implementation. Literature on information overload and cognitive overload (e.g. [78],) also suggests that when people receive too many messages, they are often unable to recognize the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the incoming messages. Collectively, this suggests that such a resource-intensive process may result in a slower conversion of customer ideas into knowledge creation than the con version of partner ideas into knowledge creation.

Second, partners are usually motivated by pro<sup>fi</sup>ts whereas customers typically co-create with <sup>fi</sup>rms because of non-monetary reasons [64,96]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, because partners would like to improve their bottom line, they tend to delve into the business processes that parti cularly address their current task requirement and focus their ideas in a succinct and precise manner [67]. Customers, however, are motivated to co-create because they: (1) identify with products of the organization and want to improve them, (2) enjoy the experience of innovating, (3) want to enhance their reputation, and (4) like to share their experiences with others [39]. Therefore, while customers contribute toward knowledge creation, the e<sup>f</sup>ect is likely to be weaker than for partner sourcing, where business pro<sup>fi</sup>ts may provide a stronger motive.

H3. E<sup>f</sup>ect of partner sourcing is stronger than the e<sup>f</sup>ect of customer co creation on knowledge creation.

## 3.2. Moderating impact of KDC

The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of external knowledge from customers and partners on knowledge creation may depend to a large extent on how accessible this external knowledge is to various entities within the organization. Therefore, when the external knowledge is disseminated broadly within the organization, this external knowledge is more likely to translate into knowledge creation. Built-in processes that facilitate the dissemination of knowledge are important for bringing the new knowledge closer to individuals who are involved in the relevant processes so that they are able to engage in experimentation and en: trepreneurial risk taking [54]. Easy access to new cutting- edge knowledge allows for a novel combination and recombination of knowledge elements derived from connecting people, <sup>fi</sup>rms, and resources [74]. When knowledge is disseminated across functional areas, as well as individuals and groups with diverse frames of reference, it creates an environment that carries a potential for novel recombination. Thus, KDC allows diverse external knowledge to more e<sup>f</sup>ectively enhance knowledge creation. Relatedly, dissemination of knowledge is also essential for transactive memory [80] to develop within the organization, where the awareness of “who knows what” is high and employees do not feel the need to “hide” knowledge. Transactive memory allows open pathways of communication that are essential for enhancing knowledge creation. Therefore, we suggest that KDC strengthens the relationship between external knowledge from partner sourcing or customer co-creation and knowledge creation.

H4. KDC strengthens the positive relationship between partner sourcing and knowledge creation, i.e., the relationship is stronger when KDC is high than when it is low.

H5. KDC strengthens the positive relationship between customer cocreation and knowledge creation, i.e., the relationship is stronger when KDC is high than when it is low.

Innovation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the creation of new products and services not previously available to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s customers, including an addition to the current o<sup>f</sup>erings or a change in existing o<sup>f</sup>erings [73]. It includes changes in products, services, processes, and business models that could cater to new or existing markets. Organizational employees can use their tacit and explicit knowledge in a variety of ways for organiza tional problem solving. A high level of knowledge creation indicates that they may combine new knowledge with their existing knowledge structures to either develop new or transform existing products, processes, or services resulting in innovation. Indeed, such a recombination of diverse knowledge is seen as a wellspring of innovation [74].

Furthermore, the higher the level of knowledge creation, the greater is the choice set of knowledge accessible to employees to innovate. Therefore, we argue that knowledge creation generates more unique ideas that could be translated into innovations e.g., in terms of patents granted for the organization.

H6. Knowledge creation is positively related to innovation.

## 4. Research Method

In this section, we discuss the organizational settings, participants, and procedures for our empirical work, followed by the details of our measures.

## 4.1. Organizational setting, participants, and data collection procedure

The setting for testing the research model presented in Fig. 1 was a sample of organizations based in the United States (U.S.) and in Singapore. The U.S. and Singapore o<sup>f</sup>er similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences that are useful for our study purpose. While both countries have developed economies that rank highly on innovation indices,<sup>1</sup> there are considerable di<sup>f</sup>erences in the size and composition of the two economies. In terms of innovation parameters, the Bloomberg Innovation Index in 2019<sup>2</sup> ranked Singapore highly on tertiary e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, while the U.S. ranked highly in terms of its high-tech companies and patents. Thus, including <sup>fi</sup>rms from these two economies that are similarly innovative but have di<sup>f</sup>erent strengths and weaknesses in terms of innovation inputs and outcomes provide richness to our data. The organizations in our sample provide IT services and <sup>fi</sup>nancial services to their customers. The IT and <sup>fi</sup>nancial industries were chosen. We selected these two sectors because: (1) Both are highly knowledge intensive [93] and contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the economies of the U.S. and Singapore [93]; and (2) these two sectors generate the highest share of startups. Du<sup>fi</sup>n [33] notes that the IT sector was responsible for over 19 % of the new startups worldwide while the <sup>fi</sup>nancial sector was responsible for over 8% worldwide, making them two sectors with the most innova tion. Moreover, <sup>fi</sup>nancial services is one of the largest sectors in the Singapore economy,<sup>3</sup> while the U.S. leads the world in the IT services sector.<sup>4</sup> In total, 655 organizations participated in the study; 399 organizations from the U.S. and 256 from Singapore.

For the organizations in Singapore, we generated lists of companies in the IT and Finance sectors. We used two sources to create our list of IT companies, i.e., Findouter Singapore and Singapore Companie Directory, and the Monetary Authority of Singapore for our list of <sup>fi</sup>- nancial institutions. All the IT and Finance companies listed in these two sources were included. We then emailed the corporate communications contact in each of these organizations to seek participation in our study. If the organization agreed to participate, we asked them to nominate a suitable person (person familiar with the innovation initiatives and patent applications for the organizations) to answer our survey questions. All organizations were given a month to respond to our survey. In between, the non-respondents were contacted by phone to remind them to complete the survey. In all, the entire data collection process lasted about two months. We contacted a total of 615 organi zations in Singapore (506 IT and 109 <sup>fi</sup>nancial companies). Overall, 256 companies provided complete survey responses (207 IT and 49 <sup>fi</sup>nancial companies) resulting in a response rate of 41.62 %.

For data collection in the U.S., we signed a contract with a market research company and obtained a one-time use mailing list of IT and Finance sector project managers. This list was <sup>fi</sup>ltered by the market research company for managers engaged in the company’s knowledge creation and innovation initiatives. We then contacted these employees through email to solicit their participation in the study. A total of 502 organizations were under contract. We received a total of 399 usable responses after eliminating observations with missing data (224 IT and 175 <sup>fi</sup>nancial companies). The descriptive statistics of the combined dataset is provided in Appendix B.

## 4.2. Measurement

All constructs in this study were measured based on items validated in prior research. The items for partner sourcing and KDC were adapted from Pee and Kankanhalli [83] and Gold et al. [43]; customer cocreation from Auh et al. [85]Dobni [31], and Menor and Roth [73]; and knowledge creation from de Pablos [29] and Gold et al. [43]. All these scales utilized seven-point Likert agreement scales. The <sup>fi</sup>nal dependent variable, innovation, was operationalized by measuring the number of successful new patents registered by the organization during the oneyear period leading up to data collection time. Patents in <sup>fi</sup>nancial sector companies mainly included innovations related to new business methods e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nancial services or marketing approaches. Patents in IT companies were for new software for system components, security, or user interfaces. The items constituting the survey are presented in $\mathsf { A p - }$ pendix A.

Because the innovation data were self-reported, we wanted to ad dress any potential common-method bias. To this end, we collected patent application data for a subset of these organizations in terms of patents issued to the organization one year after our data collection. This data was collected from the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) because our organizations were based in di<sup>f</sup>erent countries i.e., U.S. and Singapore.<sup>5</sup> The correlation between the innovation variables operationalized by the two di<sup>f</sup>erent data sources (self-report and WIPO) was very high (0.91).

We controlled for a number of variables in our study. For example, larger organizations are expected to create more knowledge than smaller organizations. Similarly, organizations with higher R&D budgets are likely to create more knowledge than organizations with smaller R&D budgets. Therefore, we controlled for the size of the organization, R&Dand IT investments operationalized by the number of employees, number of employees for R&D initiatives, as well as the dollar amount for R&D, and dollar amount allocated for IT investments, respectively. Because we had organizations from di<sup>f</sup>erent countries $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the U.S. and Singapore), we introduced dummy variables to control for country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c e<sup>f</sup>ects. Finally, we controlled for the slack time the organization provides to its employees, as well as the management support for innovation.

## 5. Data Analysis and results

As part of our preliminary analysis, we screened our data for any outliers, which did not result in cases being excluded. We conducted pooled sample tests to determine if it was appropriate to pool data from organizations in the U.S. and Singapore, and did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any concerns. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted an F-test for variance and found the p value to be greater than 0.10. We then ran a t-test for a di<sup>f</sup>erence in means for independent samples. We found that the sample means were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent either $( \mathbf { p } > 0 . 1 0 )$ . Therefore, we were able to pool the data from two countries. We then tested our data for reliability, convergent, and divergent validity. The Cronbach alpha coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for all our constructs exceeded 0.80, with the lowest being 0.81 for partner sourcing and highest being 0.95 for knowledge creation, thus showing acceptable reliability levels [51]. A principal component analysis with varimax rotation of the independent variables indicated a strong support for convergent validity. All factor loadings were greater than 0.70 and all cross-loadings were less than 0.40. Furthermore, we found that the means of all scales were close to or above 4 and the standard de viations were above 1. The average variance extracted for all variables was higher than the squared correlations between that variable and any other variable. Table 1 shows the relevant correlations. We also ex amined the VIF values for the presence of multicollinearity. All VIF values were under 4, indicating our data did not su<sup>f</sup>er from multicollinearity issues [51]. The results of the factor analysis are presented in Appendix B. Finally, we tested our data for selection bias utilizing Monte Carlo simulations [89,32]. We <sup>fi</sup>rst created 1000 random samples from our data utilizing the Monte Carlo approach. Then, we computed coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for the main e<sup>f</sup>ects, for each of these 1000 samples. We then developed a con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for each of these computed samples. Because our observed estimates were within the 95 % con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval, we concluded that selection bias is not a concern for our data.

To better understand these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we divided our dataset into groups – one representing IT organizations and the other representing <sup>fi</sup>nancial organizations. This higher level of participation as well as the resultant level of knowledge developed in these online communities can explain the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of KDC for IT organizations. Moreover, <sup>fi</sup>nancial organizations tend to be more traditional as compared to the IT organizations that pioneered the open-source concept. IT organization employees, on the other hand, are more likely to share their ideas using online communities. Thus, IT organizations may exhibit a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence of KMS than <sup>fi</sup>nance organizations.

## 6. Discussion

Internet-based communication technologies have greatly reduced the distance between organizations and their partners. These technologies have also brought customers and partners closer to sellers and/or organizations [77]. These closer and instant interactions create opportunities for researchers to revisit how organizations can work with their customers and partners to generate and test ideas for new products, services, and solve complex business problems. Our study contributes to innovation research by examining the role of knowledgerelated antecedents in attaining greater innovation outcomes. We investigated the role of customer co-creation and partner sourcing, in enhancing knowledge creation. A core strength of our study is that we tested our research model using a large and a broad dataset. The results of our study show that di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of external knowledge—- customer co-creation and partner sourcing—contribute di<sup>f</sup>erentially toward knowledge creation. Our model explained 65 % of the variance in knowledge creation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight that organizations creating new knowledge are more likely to innovate through patents.

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

Our study o<sup>f</sup>ers several important theoretical contributions. First, our research contributes to the research on organizational innovation by investigating if customer co-creation and partner sourcing enhance knowledge creation. Prior research (e.g., Van der Aa and Elfring 2002; Yli-Renko et al. 2001) has examined the role of external knowledge in creating organizational innovation. However, very little work has delineated the role of customers from other sources of knowledge. Unlike traditional customers, modern day customers demand a greater role in customizing the world that surrounds them. Such customers are willing to go beyond just presenting summary feedback to actively participating in solving business problems. Yet, the number and the quality of customer ideas may di<sup>f</sup>er from those of partners because of the underlying motivations and core business knowledge. The results of our study show that while these customer e<sup>f</sup>orts are fruitful in enhancing knowledge creation, partner sourcing has a much stronger direct in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on knowledge creation. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to delineate this complex relationship between different sources of external knowledge and organizational innovation initiatives.

Second, our research contributes to the innovation and knowledge management literatures by identifying intervening organizational capabilities that help knowledge creation in organizations. Identifying such capabilities has the potential to provide a more nuanced understanding of knowledge creation, a key enabler of innovation. In doing so, our research explores the moderating and amplifying role of KDC. Organizations are increasingly investing in technologies such as KMS as they see such systems as important drivers of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [100]. However, our understanding of speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational processes that allow organizations to leverage bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of such systems and translate these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts into <sup>fi</sup>rm performance is fairly limited [78,106]. Our research theorizes and empirically examines the role of one organizational process i.e., knowledge dissemination, that typically uses a KMS as its enabler. Highlighting the moderating role of knowledge dissemination capability, we argue that organizations’ ability to disseminate knowledge to their employees using IS (e.g., a KMS) will result in a more innovative culture, greater knowledge creation, and innovation.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Our study provides several important practical implications. First, our research evidences the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of involving customers in developing new innovations. Per the recommendations of the marketing literature, managers usually develop new products in-house and then conduct extensive testing with their customers. For this purpose, managers closely listen to the feedback provided by customers and revise their products to meet customer needs. Although such feedback from customers is highly valuable, our research goes further and recommends managers to partner with customers using communication and collaboration technologies available to them. Such partnerships would promote customers (not just lead users) co-developing product and services with employees of organizations. To facilitate such cocreation, we recommend managers put in place business processes that would allow the integration of customers’ inputs in the product/service development lifecycle. These business processes should be enabled by advanced communication and collaboration technologies that would make customer and employee co-production as e<sup>f</sup>ortless as possible.

Second, our research <sup>fi</sup>nds a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence of KDC. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that organizations that invest in KDC tend to have a higher degree of knowledge creation. Therefore, we recommend managers to invest in technologies, such as a KMS. Such a system could facilitate knowledge dissemination from di<sup>f</sup>erent sources within the organization. This is important for two reasons. First, knowledge or ideas generated in one context may be applicable in another context. Such knowledge if compiled well and made available to employees of the organization may be a source of unexpected knowledge. Second, an employee may need an idea to be explained in several di<sup>f</sup>erent ways before he/she can develop a mental model and inter nalize it. Organizations often lack resources that would allow them to have multiple explanations of a single idea. However, with customer cocreation and sourcing knowledge from multiple partners, organizations may be able to compile multiple explanations in one single area and make it available to employees. Discovery of unexpected knowledge or multiple explanations of one idea may persuade employees to think about novel solutions to mundane problems that they experience on a daily basis. Moreover, IT managers are often criticized for the lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant returns on IT investments. Our research results can help make a case for the IT investments. Based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study, we suggest that investing in KMS technologies to enhance the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of customer co-creation and partner sourcing can increase an organi zation’s innovation outcomes. Therefore, managers could potentially use the number of new successful patents from customer co-creation initiatives as a measure of return for any investments in knowledge management or collaboration tools.

Third, many organizations assess their employees’ involvement in knowledge creation activities by the number of patents they <sup>fi</sup>le. Some companies (e.g., Google) even provide employees with additional compensation as a reward for each successful patent <sup>fi</sup>led. Therefore, most managers involved in new product development or innovation departments are looking to develop knowledge that can be patented. Our research serves as an additional data point highlighting how managers can e<sup>f</sup>ectively create such opportunities for themselves and their employees.

Finally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research suggest that organizations would bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from valuing partner sourcing more than customer co-creation. Indeed, Panzarino [82] recently noted that Apple and Google, two of the biggest competitors are partnering on a COVID-19 contact tracing tool. The logic behind such partnerships is that partner ideas are more likely to be well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned and also more likely to in corporate business speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge and constraints. Moreover, partners can pool resources for knowledge creation if it could be mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for them, which may not be possible in the case of customer co-creation.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research directions

As with other research studies, our research is not without limita tions. Even though the central goal of our research was to examine knowledge creation, we were interested in important organizational outcomes, such as innovation. However, we operationalized innova tion: (1) by measuring the number of patents <sup>fi</sup>led by the organization; and (2) by measuring it as a dichotomous variable indicating low number of patents <sup>fi</sup>led by the organization to high number of patents <sup>fi</sup>led by the organization. Future research could test for the generalizability of our research model by incorporating other operationalizations of innovation and by measuring it as a continuous variable.

The goal of our research was to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of customer co-creation and partner sourcing on knowledge creation and subsequent innovation. Although we did not study the process of how external sources of knowledge contributed to knowledge creation and innovation directly, in examining the di<sup>f</sup>erential contribution of var ious sources of external knowledge provides key insights into our understanding of this important process. Future research should consider examining this process of converting external knowledge to knowledge creation and innovation explicitly. Such examination would not only provide generalizability to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study, but may also provide <sup>fi</sup>ner insights into the knowledge creation and innovation process.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research show that KDC has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of customer co-creation and partner sourcing on knowledge creation. This e<sup>f</sup>ect was small in size. However, we caution against minimizing the implications of this small e<sup>f</sup>ect in light of Abelson’s paradox. Abelson [107] tested the magnitude of the proportion of variance accounted for by player ability but found that for typical data the magnitude of the proportion of variance accounted for is only about 0.003. This small e<sup>f</sup>ect con<sup>fl</sup>icts with the typical sports fans intuition that player ability should account for a lot more of the variance of hits. Abelson’s explanation of this e<sup>f</sup>ect has evolved into what is being called Abelson’s Paradox. He states, “In such cases, it is quite possible that small variance contributions of independent variables in single-shot studies grossly understate the variance contribution in the long run. Thus, one should not necessarily be scornful of miniscule values for percentage variance explanation, provided there is statistical assurance that these values are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly above zero, and that the degree of potential cumulation is substantial” (p. 132). Other examples of potentially cumulative processes provided by Abelson are educational interventions, the persuasive e<sup>f</sup>ects of advertising, and repeated decisions by ideologically similar policy makers. We suggest that the e<sup>f</sup>ect in the context of knowledge creation are cumulative and therefore, while small, have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and important implications for the innovation research.

Organizations implement KDC, such as a KMS, for a variety of reasons. These include implementing best practices within an organization, mapping internal expertise, and creation of knowledge networks (for a review, see [58]). Recently, Zhang [108] argued that organizations struggle to show the return on investments on KDC implementations. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research can serve as another potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t for KDC implementations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research allow organizations to make a case for the return on investments for KDC implementations by showing how such systems can help convert external knowledge into innovation. Future research should conduct <sup>fi</sup>eld studies primarily to study the role of KDC in knowledge creation. These studies could perhaps use a broader set of industry sectors and/or countries. Using a broader dataset may allow researchers to provide a more pronounced e<sup>f</sup>ect size of the e<sup>f</sup>ect of KDC.

While our research focused primarily on inbound ideas, future research should focus on both inbound ideas as well as outbound ideas<sup>7</sup> . Understanding the desorptive capacity (see [68]) of an organization would provide a more enriched understanding of how an organization <sup>fi</sup>ts within an overall industry. Future research should also explicitly delineate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of quality versus quantity of ideas. This would help researchers determine if sheer large number of ideas are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to an organization even if they are not all usable. Strength of ties would present another perspective to look at the quality versus quantity of ideas. Future research could investigate if the strength of the relation ship between the knowledge source (customers or partners) and the organization is important. This would help researchers determine if organizations would create more knowledge with higher quality ties or higher quantity ties. Furthermore, researchers should also examine to what extent organizations actually adopted customer or partner ideas. Qualitative data highlighting examples of such adopted ideas would provide generalizability and validity to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research. Finally, it would be important to establish causality between knowledge creation abilities and innovation. This would necessitate a longitudinal design and we call future researchers to design such studies in this important area of research.

## 7. Conclusions

Organizations invest in a variety of technologies to promote knowledge creation and innovation. While a number of these technologies allow for sourcing knowledge from a variety of sources such as suppliers, government agencies, and R&D <sup>fi</sup>rms, others allow for organizations to work closely with their customers in the problem-solving process. However, our understanding of how and if these investments di<sup>f</sup>erentially result in knowledge creation and innovation is limited. Our research sought to investigate and understand the role of knowledge-related antecedents of innovation. In doing so, we focused on the key factors of customer co-creation, partner sourcing, and knowledge dissemination in knowledge creation. The resulting empirical model presents a richer and more nuanced understanding of how organizations can generate unique knowledge and greater innovation.

Manju Ahuja is the Frazier Family Professor in the College of Business, at the University of Louisville. Her research focuses on issues related to impacts and the use of IT, innovation related to IT, work-life balance, remote work, as well as the management of human resources in IT professions. Dr. Ahuja currently serves as a senior editor at Information Systems Research and previously served as a senior editor at MIS Quarterly and the Journal of the Association for Information Systems.
