technical services provided by call centers are characterized by constant changes in relevant knowledge and a wide variety of support requests. Under this IT problem-solving context, we analyze the learning curve relationship between problem-solving experience and performance enhancement. Based on data collected from a university computing call center consisting of different types of consultants, our empirical findings indicate that (a) the learning effect—as measured by the reduction of average resolution time—occurs with experience, (b) knowledge transfer within a group occurs among lower-level consultants utilizing application-level knowledge (as opposed to technical-level knowledge), and (c) knowledge transfers across IT problem types. The results also have implications for operational decisions about the staffing and problem-solving strategy of call centers.

## 1. Introduction

Because information technology (IT) technical services have become increasingly important, we need a better understanding of learning in the context of IT technical support services. Most previous work on learning, however, has focused on machine- or laborintensive industries, such as truck assembly (Epple et al. 1991), aircraft construction (Benkard 2001), and steel wire production (Lapré et al. 2000). IT support services have certain unique characteristics, such as (1) a rapid pace of evolution in IT and (2) a wide variety of support requests and knowledge required to address them, that pose challenges to learning. In this paper, we study learning by IT knowledge workers in a computing call center to investigate whether learning occurs in the challenging environment of technical support requests.

A better understanding of learning by IT knowledge workers is of value from both a practical and a theoretical standpoint. From a practical standpoint, knowledge of learning behavior permits intelligent staffing and capacity planning. The estimation of learning rates is crucial in the optimal design of call centers (Pinker and Shumsky 2000). Furthermore, there are multiple layers of workers in IT service organizations (Das 2003, Gans and Zhou 2002). These workers are not homogeneous: different employees have different service capabilities in terms of required skill levels and/or knowledge types. Our empirical examination of the learning progress of the different groups as well as knowledge transfer effects has direct bearing on staffing decisions in the dynamic environments encountered in IT call centers (Gurvich et al. 2008).

Of more fundamental value is an empirically grounded understanding of IT knowledge workers’ learning behavior—in particular, one that accounts for IT knowledge types, knowledge transfer among consultants, and knowledge transfer across IT problem types. Based on examination of more than 100 learning curve studies, Dutton and Thomas (1984) argued that the learning rate should not be treated as a constant and emphasized the importance of contingency variables in predicting the learning rate. Following this line of thought, we propose a new knowledge classification based on the job classification of IT knowledge workers—surfacelevel application-level knowledge versus in-depth technical-level knowledge—and analyze its effect on learning rates and knowledge transfer effects. In a departure from previous studies of learning, we allow learning effects and knowledge transfer effects to differ for different types of consultants. This fine-grained characterization of learning in the call center context advances learning theory and provides information about how to staff and organize call centers to promote their performance.

Furthermore, we investigate two kinds of knowledge transfer. First, we examine the knowledge transfer among members within a group. Individual performance can be increased in a group working environment through knowledge transfer or learning from the experience of others (Argote and Ingram 2000, Kogut and Zander 1992). Second, IT problems are categorized into multiple problem types such as networks, operating systems (OSs), and so on. We investigate whether experience accumulated on one problem type influences problem-solving performance on other types. Thus, the three research questions we investigate are as follows: (1) Does learning occur in the task environment of a computing call center? (2) Do different groups of consultants in the call center show different rates of learning and different degrees of knowledge transfer within the group? And (3) does knowledge transfer occur across IT problem types?

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: §2 addresses the IT problem-solving context and research objectives along with the theoretical background. We describe the research site in §3. We specify econometric models along with measurements in §4. The empirical results are presented and the theoretical and managerial implications are discussed in §5. In §6 we conclude with limitations and future research directions.

## 2. IT Problem-Solving

## Context and Research Objectives

Although there is a large body of literature on learning curves, and there are a few studies identifying the influence of observable key elements in IT technical support (Subramanyam and Krishnan 2001), a complete theory of learning in the context of IT technical support services does not exist (Das 2003). Subramanyam and Krishnan (2001) analyzed the impact of consultant-specific and customerspecific factors on individual IT knowledge workers’ performance in a computing call center. However, they did not study knowledge accumulation (or learning progress) in the model. Therefore, as the first step, we review the IT problem-solving context and discuss how we apply learning theory to this context. Here, we derive research questions from (1) what kind of problems consultants are solving, (2) how consultants solve these problems, and (3) the relationship across the problem types.

## 2.1. IT Environment and Knowledge Pool

We need to account for both the uniqueness of the IT environment and the knowledge pool required to solve reported IT problems. Learning theory states that as individuals (or organizations) get more experienced at a task, they become more efficient at it (Yelle 1979). The learning effect assumes implicitly that tasks are identical or similar. This assumption is satisfied in routinely repeated tasks such as in manufacturing work. But the wide variety of support requests generated in the volatile IT environment and the large knowledge pool required to solve those requests may not satisfy the homogeneous task assumption. Thus, our first research goal is to examine whether individual consultants show learning progress in the heterogeneous task environment in a computing call center.

The rapid evolution of IT results in an extremely dynamic environment characterized by upgrades, newer versions or completely new artifacts, and security threats and patches. This has been documented in previous studies using measures such as product life cycle, “freshness” of the product line, and clockspeed (Mendelson and Pillai 1998). For example, in the specific domain of the database management system (DBMS), we can trace the evolution in database technology in terms of the data model (navigational DBMS => relational DBMS => object-oriented DBMS => object relational DBMS => extensible markup language (XML) DBMSs) and in the query language (procedural query language, structured query language (SQL), XML query language (XQL)) supported.

In addition to the volatile environment, IT technical support relies on a wide base of knowledge. The suppliers of IT hardware and software are diverse and IT systems are made up of their components, which include microprocessors, motherboards, dynamic random access memory (DRAM) chip sets, peripherals, PC systems, networks, OSs, office suites, and application software (Kraemer and Dedrick 1998). The large number of combinations requires correspondingly different knowledge pools because of technical compatibility problems and the lack of complete standardization (Gandal 1995, Subramanian and Chung 2003). Our observation of the problem-solving procedures and our interviews with IT consultants support this argument. When an IT problem is reported to a call center, the first step IT consultants take is to understand the compatibility issues related to the combinations of the systems in use. As a result, IT technical support providers have to acquaint themselves with a large number of components and combinations because they are required to help diverse IT end users using different system combinations (whereas computing end users can focus on a small subset of the large set of options available).

Ellis’s (1965) knowledge classification theory, however, would predict that learning would be likely to occur even in the volatile environment of IT technical support work. According to Ellis (1965), learning can be subdivided into two categories: (1) content knowledge and (2) knowledge regarding learning how to learn. First, in the case of content knowledge, the content knowledge accumulated from previous tasks may not be relevant to the performance of the next task. But if the content knowledge accumulated through previous experience is relevant to the content knowledge required to solve a new problem (even though the content knowledge itself may change as a result of technology advancement), IT consultants will show a learning curve of performance improvement with experience.

The second category represents learning associated with how to assimilate or apply particular kinds of information to a new problem, which enhances the logical thinking process. Learners may be able to apply logic developed in one problem to another problem and thereby increase their understanding in a problem domain (Gick and Lockhart 1995, Schilling et al. 2003). The knowledge accumulation in the second category of learning to learn is not susceptible to changes in the knowledge pool. Indeed, some variety may even enhance an individual’s problem-solving ability (Schilling et al. 2003). Consequently, we would expect to see learning even in a heterogeneous task environment.

## 2.2. Group Working Environment in Hierarchical Structure of Consultants

The hierarchical structure of consultants is common in IT technical support services (Das 2003, Subramanyam and Krishnan 2001). Our research site is composed of three hierarchical layers of consultants: the first-level generalists (FirstGs), the second-level generalists (SecondGs), and the thirdlevel specialists (ThirdSs). In a group environment, consultants can increase their problem-solving performance through the knowledge accumulated by other consultants in their group as well as their own experience-based learning. As the second research goal, we examine whether these different groups show different rates of learning and different degrees of knowledge transfer within a group.

To better compare and understand the work conducted by the three groups, we describe the problemsolving process in our research site. Hereafter, we will use interchangeably the terms “layer” and “group.” If a (lower-level) consultant cannot solve a request with his or her individual knowledge, the consultant can refer to the compilation of previously reported problems and solutions developed by other consultants.<sup>1</sup> That is, the consultants can solve problems by utilizing knowledge accumulated in the organization’s memory (Huber 1991). In case the repository is not helpful in resolving a problem, the consultant can solve the request with assistance from other consultants within a group. When lower-level consultants cannot solve the requests even with assistance from other consultants within their group, they can escalate requests to upper-level consultants, along with the transfer of ownership of the request. Here, the assistance from peers is identical to “get help” and the action of escalation corresponds to “give away” in Pentland’s (1992) analysis of organizing moves of technical service interaction.

In a problem-solving hierarchy, higher-level consultants are more likely to find solutions, as noted in Das (2003), because of (1) increasing specialization, (2) proximity to the design process, and (3) greater authority. Unlike generalists, the upper-level consultants in our research site specialize in specific problem types. Furthermore, upper-level consultants have progressively more knowledge related to the design details of systems including software, which may enable them to anticipate problems that arise during use. Also, higher-level consultants can often take actions unavailable to lower-level consultants, such as changing individual user information.

In addition to operational differences across the multiple layers of consultants, such as specialization, the different layers of consultants are in charge of requests requiring different types of knowledge. We subdivide IT knowledge into two sets: (1) application-level knowledge that everybody possesses to use IT for practical purposes and (2) technical-level knowledge that goes beyond the simple usage of IT. For example, users can use a virtual private network (VPN) with secure access to their organization’s network if they know how to install the VPN client on their system and use specific commands and options. Users do not have to know the technical details of how this operation is implemented or executed (e.g., network configuration, file transmission protocols, and cryptographic tunneling protocols). As another example, users may be able to scan and remove computer viruses with antivirus software even if they do not know how vaccines work. The knowledge about suspicious behavior approach or sandbox emulation method is too technical for end users but is essential for developers and IT consultants. In our research site, the FirstGs are responsible for requests that require application-level knowledge. The ThirdSs are in charge of requests that require underlying technical-level knowledge. The SecondGs fall between the two groups—they tend to have more technical knowledge than FirstGs do and less than ThirdSs do.

Given the hierarchical knowledge structure that matches the layers of IT knowledge workers, we expect that learning and knowledge transfer in each group will be contingent on features of the different knowledge sets. Although the classifications do not match up perfectly, application-level knowledge is closer to explicit knowledge (Nonaka 1994, Polanyi 1958), content knowledge (Ellis 1965), and surface knowledge (Hayes-Roth et al. 1983, Turban 1995), whereas technical-level knowledge is closer to tacit knowledge, logical thinking process knowledge, and deep knowledge. Because explicit knowledge is transferred more easily than is tacit knowledge (Zander and Kogut 1995), application-level knowledge should transfer more readily than technical-level knowledge.

Furthermore, learning about one function in application-level knowledge would yield insights into how other closely related functions might work. For example, when one learns how to set the option of VPN settings, he or she is likely to learn other network configuration settings such as certificate authentication or to understand the relevant tools as well as their structure. In contrast, learning of in-depth technical-level knowledge might not lead to a similar kind of knowledge spillover and transfer. For instance, in contrast to the easily accessible, welldocumented materials on VPN client setting, it is difficult to find the solution to problems resulting from the incorrect implementation of a computer networking protocol suite. Thus, we expect that knowledge transfer will be greater within groups that deal with application-level knowledge than among groups that deal with technical-level knowledge.

2.3. Knowledge Transfer Across IT Problem Types In addition to knowledge transfer among members in a group, we investigate another kind of knowledge transfer: knowledge transfer across IT problem types such as virus, OS, and network problems in a computing call center. Our third research goal is to examine whether experience accumulated in one IT problem type influences problem-solving performance in other problem types. From a managerial perspective, understanding knowledge transfer across IT problem types is valuable in determining problem-solving strategy (specialist versus generalist) in a computing call center. Here, we refer to specialization as a strategy where one person is in charge of one problem type without handling other types of problems, and vice versa. A research question of long standing interest in the learning literature is when is it better to foster heterogeneity of experience and when is it better to foster homogeneity (Argote 1999)? This empirical investigation contributes to the literature by answering the question in the computing call center context.

The expectation of knowledge transfer across different IT problem types is motivated by the theory of related variations. Psychological studies of individual learning have demonstrated that related task variation (varying the content or context of the task) enhances the learning process through facilitating the development of more abstract principles (or schema) related to the general class of tasks (Paas and Van Merriënboer 1994, Shea and Kohl 1990). Also, management scholars have reported in contexts different from ours that experience from diverse heterogeneous problems leads to better analyses and responses because of better understanding of the underlying structure of problems (Beckman and Haunschild 2002, Haunschild and Sullivan 2002, Jehn et al. 1999, Schilling et al. 2003). In the IT problemsolving context, the heterogeneous problem-solving experience from diverse IT problem types can increase IT consultants’ understanding of latent schema of IT problems and thereby facilitate the development of abstract concepts embedded in IT problems. Thus, when a consultant solves problems, the consultant’s problem-solving experience gained in one problem type (e.g., OS or virus problems) can have a positive influence on performance in another area (e.g., data network problems).

In sum, the first rationale underlying the knowledge transfer across IT problem types is based on the acquisition of a new level of understanding from various problem-solving experiences. The second is based on the technical relationship among diverse components such as OS, network port, CPU, and graphics card. IT problems are in general a technical combination of multiple components. When an IT consultant solves virus/OS problems (e.g., virus attacking network port and “OS setting pertaining to a network configuration”), the consultant can gain knowledge in the data network area as well. Therefore, we would expect knowledge transfer across IT problem types based on the relationships among the multiple components that make up a computing system.

## 3. Research Site

The research site for this study is a university computing call center. The center provides IT technical support (problem-solving) services and information about computing to a university campus community. Requestors can access the services of the center by walking in, calling on the phone, or sending an e-mail. The center has been using the Remedy system since July 2001.<sup>2</sup> It was introduced in May 2001 and implemented after a two-month period of testing and training. The data for this study cover the period from July 2001 through December 2004. During the time period of our research, more than 150,000 requests were resolved by 119 consultants in the center. Data for our analysis were extracted from the Remedy system in use at the center. The data consisted of (1) a request ID, (2) a consultant ID finally reporting the solution to a requestor, (3) request-created andresolved time stamps, (4) a code indicating the problem type associated with a request (to be discussed), and (5) criticality of a request. In particular, one field of the database is the request trail that enables us to observe all formal actions that are taken by consultants before closing a request.

Knowledge can be accumulated either from an investment in preparation and training (learningbefore-doing) or through experience gained by doing relevant work (Carrillo and Gaimon 2000, Pisano 1994). Learning-before-doing is generally associated with special events such as training programs designed to increase the knowledge level of consultants. Our interviews with managers at diverse levels and all types of consultants indicated that there were no specific training events inducing learning-before-doing during our research period at the center. The level of cumulative knowledge was modified primarily through on-site learning. This represents an appropriate context for examining the learning curve induced from learning-by-doing. The computing environment supported by the center is heterogeneous along various dimensions. For example, operating systems range from HP OpenVMS and Solaris from Sun Microsystems to Microsoft Windows and Apple’s Mac OS. Requestors vary from naïve to expert and have different depths of IT knowledge.

As we mentioned, there are three groups of consultants: FirstG,<sup>3</sup> SecondG, and ThirdS. The first- and second-level generalist consultants (FirstGs and SecondGs) share the same office. A few third-level specialists share a room with other specialists, but most ThirdSs have individual cubicles. Technical support work is organized with FirstG, SecondG, and ThirdS forming a problem-solving hierarchy with problems escalated when necessary (FirstG => SecondG => ThirdS).<sup>4</sup> A FirstG is responsible for problems in many fields requiring surface-level knowledge about applications (Chan et al. 1997, Hayes-Roth et al. 1983, Turban 1995), and a ThirdS solves problems requiring in-depth fundamental technical knowledge while specializing in a specific area such as network infrastructure. Consequently, specialists have deep fundamental knowledge underlying application-level knowledge $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ understanding the IEEE 802.11 protocol stack versus knowing the settings under Windows to obtain wireless Internet access) and are more likely than are the generalists to find solutions to difficult problems.

The work conducted at the center can be organized into three basic steps: (1) problem report and preliminary action, (2) searching for solutions, and (3) closing the request by reporting the solution (please see the online supplement for the details of each step<sup>5</sup>). One of the coauthors spent several weeks at the center observing work and participating in solution-seeking procedures in order to verify real operating procedures and data from the Remedy system.

## 4. Measurements and Econometric Model

## 4.1. Performance Variable

Our focal performance measure is the average resolution time (ART). $A R T _ { i j t }$ is the average of resolution times of all requests resolved by individual i of group $j$ in week $t ,$ where the resolution time (RT) of each request is the elapsed time during the hours of operation of the center between the first reporting of a problem and its resolution. The resolution time is recognized as an important productivity measure in technical support services. First, the labor cost of knowledge workers is the major cost for call center service. Second, customers’ assessment of services is strongly influenced by the resolution time (Zeithaml et al. 1990), especially in situations where the time that requestors spend waiting for a solution represents stoppage of work with consequent financial loss (Das 2003). Furthermore, changes in time required to complete a task has a long history of being used as an indicator of learning in other contexts (Reagans et al. 2005).

## 4.2. Learning Variables

Our study was motivated by a desire to understand learning by IT knowledge workers. The learning model has been applied to diverse units of analysis: individual, group, and organizational levels (Reagans et al. 2005). Because our data allow us to identify the consultants who finally solved the requests, we set the unit of analysis at the individual level. However, as we described, in a group working environment the procedure to obtain assistance from peers is embedded in the solution search procedure. In addition to the face-to-face communication with other consultants, individual consultants can refer to the solutions of previous requests documented in the Remedy system. Therefore, we cannot attribute individual consultants’ performance improvement only to individual learning. Unless we formulate the influence of assistance from other consultants in our regression model, the estimation of the individual-level learning rate may lead to biased conclusions because of omitted variables.

There are two kinds of learning variables in our model: (1) an individual learning variable $( C Q _ { i j t - 1 } )$ to estimate individual learning curves and (2) a knowledge transfer (group learning) variable $( G C Q _ { i j t - 1 } )$ to capture knowledge transfer effects within a group. The individual learning variable is the cumulative request volume solved by consultant i of group j at time t − 1 through all the methods to access the center because consultants gain experience irrespective of the type of consult (phone, walk-in, or e-mail), and the inaccuracy of time stamps for measuring resolution times is irrelevant to the knowledge accumulation process. The performance variable is measured based on requests only through e-mail in order to accurately measure resolution time. The knowledge transfer variable is measured as the cumulative number of requests solved by group j of consultant i through week t − 1 minus $C Q _ { i j t - 1 }$ . We can fully identify the knowledge transfer effects by removing the individual experience of the corresponding consultant from the experience accumulated in the group.

## 4.3. Measurement Error in Resolution Time

Although e-mail requests do not have errors in the time stamp input procedure, resolution times in e-mail requests inevitably include measurement errors entrenched in the problem-solving operational procedure. First, there is a time gap between the time when a request arrives at the center and the time when a consultant starts working on the requests (e1). Second, when a consultant receives a request by e-mail, the e-mail may not contain enough information to solve the request. Then the consultant has to clarify the problem from the sender, causing another type of noise (e2). These two kinds of noise exist for all three groups of consultants.

Third, when a request is solved through the escalation process, the resolution time of the request solved by the upper-level consultants (either SecondG or ThirdS) contains the time consumed by lower-level consultants, which is the third type of noise (e3). There is traditional practice pertaining to e3. In general, lower-level consultants (FirstG or SecondG) decide whether they can solve the problem within the first 15 to 30 minutes after taking on a request. This time horizon is long enough for the consultants to reach the decision because they are familiar with the overall structure of the IT problem. If they feel they would be unable to solve the problem, the center recommends that consultants escalate the problem. Our interviews with generalists confirmed that they followed this practice because lower-level consultants are not penalized for escalating a request.

In Figure 1, the length of the thick line or their summation indicates $R T _ { i t } ^ { * } .$ The dependent variable is the average of resolution times of requests solved by consultant i in week t: $E ( R T _ { i t } ) = \dot { E ( } R T _ { i t } ^ { * } ) + E ( e _ { i t } ) \stackrel { . } { = } A R T _ { i t } .$ . We can formulate the dependent variable with the measurement error of multiplicative form: $E ( R T _ { i t } ) = E ( R T _ { i t } ^ { * } ) \times a _ { i t } ,$ where $a _ { i t } \geq 1$ because the amount of the measurement error of each request $( e _ { i t } )$ is positively correlated with $R T _ { i t } .$ In contrast to the ideal learning equation of ln $( E ( R T _ { i t } ^ { * } ) ) = \beta _ { 0 } + \beta _ { 1 } \pmb { X } _ { i t } + u _ { i t } ,$ the regression model based on the dependent variable with measurement error has ln $a _ { i t }$ on its right-hand side: ln $\begin{array} { r } { ( E ( R T _ { i t } ) ) = } \end{array}$ $\beta _ { 0 } + \beta _ { 1 } \pmb { X } _ { i t } + u _ { i t } + \ln a _ { i t }$ . We have to check a critical assumption, cov $\begin{array} { r } { r ( { \bf X } _ { i t } , a _ { i t } ) = 0 , } \end{array}$ , to ensure an unbiased estimation from the modified equation. In our context, it is obvious that this assumption is satisfied because the measurement error is uncorrelated with our independent variables, including learning variables (X). As a consequence, the usual ordinary least squares (OLS) inference procedures are asymptotically valid. Because $\mathrm { V a r } ( u _ { i t } \dot { + } \ln a _ { i t } ) > \mathrm { V a r } ( u _ { i t } ) .$ , the measurement error in the dependent variable results in a larger error variance than when the dependent variable is not measured with error. But the larger error variance violates none of the assumptions needed for OLS estimation to have its desirable large-sample properties (Wooldridge 2002).

Because the measurement error term is not zero mean $( a _ { i t } \ge 1 )$ , it will lead to the overestimation of the intercepts. But the OLS estimators for learning effects and knowledge transfer effects (slope coefficients) are not affected by the error term and so are consistent and unbiased. Yet we have to consider heteroskedasticity across groups $( \ln a _ { i t } ^ { \mathrm { F i r s t G } } \leq \ln a _ { i t } ^ { \mathrm { S e c o n d G } } \leq$ ln $a _ { i t } ^ { \mathrm { T h i r d S } } )$ and cross-sectional dependence because of the escalation procedure. Furthermore, when FirstGs start working for the center, there are variations in their knowledge levels. Also, the SecondGs and the ThirdSs are full-time staff members, and the majority of the consultants in both groups have had careers in relevant areas that spanned from 7 to 20 years. We could not individually examine their career trajectory because of the inability to obtain what the center considered private personnel information. This previous experience of individual consultants before joining the center may result in the heterogeneity of knowledge levels across consultants. We have to consider these issues in the estimation and its interpretation.

## 4.5. Model Specification

Our model specification uses a standard learning curve power function. The standard form of the learning curve is formulated as $y ( x ) = a x ^ { b } .$ , where y is a performance variable, x is cumulative problem-solving experience, a is the number of hours required to solve the first request, and b is the learning rate.<sup>8</sup> Taking a natural log transformation of both sides and adding covariates of interest and control variables, we obtain the following regression equations:

$$

where $D _ { 0 } = \mathrm { F i r s t G } , D _ { 1 } = \mathrm { S e c o n d G } ,$ and $D _ { 2 } = \mathrm { T h i r d } S ;$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln (P T _ {p -} A R T _ {i t}) = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {p > 1} \beta_ {0 p} P T _ {p} \\ \qquad + \sum_ {p \geq 1} \beta_ {1 p} P T _ {p} \ln (P T _ {p -} C Q _ {i t - 1}) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {p \geq 0} \beta_ {2 p} D _ {p} \ln (P T _ {p -} G C Q _ {i t - 1}) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {m} \alpha_ {m} \mathbf {X} _ {i j t} + \tau_ {i} + u _ {i t}, \quad (\text { Model   2 }) \end{array}

The main objective of Model 1 is to estimate three unique learning curves and knowledge transfer effects by consultant layers based on our performance variable $( A R T _ { i j t } )$ . Here, we split all the consultants into three layers. The model includes the interaction terms between the dummies representing each consultant’s layer and the cumulative number of requests solved by the corresponding consultant (individual learning variable). If $\beta _ { 1 j }$ (negative sign) is significant, then the consultants in the corresponding layer (j) show the individual learning effects. Second, we test whether problem-solving experience accumulated in a group helps increase an individual consultant’s performance. The negative and significant $\beta _ { 2 j } \mathsf { s }$ (the coefficients of the interaction terms of knowledge transfer variables $( G C Q _ { i j t - 1 } )$ with group dummies) indicate the knowledge transfer within group $j .$ Our estimation procedure allows us to determine whether transfer occurs and whether it is positive or negative. The model contains other covariates $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { X } } _ { i j t } ,$ but we omit group dummy variables $( \beta _ { 0 1 } D _ { 0 } + \beta _ { 0 1 } ^ { ' } D _ { 1 } + \beta _ { 0 2 } D _ { 2 } )$ to avoid overspecification, because we include individual fixed effects in the model $( \tau _ { i } )$

In Models 2 and 3, we split the entire data set into eight samples based on the predefined IT problem type classification. The dependent variable, $\bar { P } T _ { p } \_ A R T _ { i t } ,$ is consultant i’s average resolution time for every problem type in week t. The explanatory variable of $P T _ { p - } { \bar { C Q } } _ { i t - 1 } ~ ( P T _ { p - } G C Q _ { i t - 1 } )$ measures the cumulative number of problem type p-related requests solved by consultant i (the group of consultant i) through time t − 1 if the problem type is the same as the dependent variables; otherwise, it is zero. This specification enables us to capture the variation of ART with individual and knowledge transfer variables for every problem type. We can evaluate individual learning effects (knowledge transfer effects within a group) in each problem type with $\beta _ { 1 p } \mathbf { S } \ ( \beta _ { 2 p } \mathbf { S } )$

In Model $^ { 3 , }$ we add total cumulative requests in all problem types $( C Q _ { i t - 1 } )$ in addition to the cumulative problem-solving experience in each problem type $( P T _ { p - } C Q _ { i t - 1 } )$ . This specification enables us to assess whether the overall individual problem-solving performance in a certain problem type improves as the consultant gains experience in other problem types $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ knowledge transfers across IT problem types).

Specifically, we check whether $\beta _ { 3 }$ is significantly negative and the change of $\beta _ { 1 p } s$ from Model 2 to Model 3. Because ThirdSs are focused on a specific problem area and SecondGs are affected partly by specialization in IT problem types, we cannot empirically examine the issue of knowledge transfer across problem types for the SecondGs and the ThirdSs. Thus, Models 2 and 3 are estimated with data from only the FirstG group.

## 4.6. Control Variables

Our model estimation includes various control variables suggested by the literature or through the research site to control for confounding factors and also explore other interesting relationships.

1. Our model allows for the current number of requests solved by consultant i in week $t ( q _ { i t } )$ to affect consultants’ performance through economies of scale. By contrast, the learning variable is operationalized as the cumulative number of requests solved through week t − 1.

2. Each request is given one of four levels of criticality: urgent = 1, high = 2, medium = 3 or low = 4. We calculated and added the average criticality of requests solved by consultant i in week t $( A v \_ c r i t _ { i t } )$ in the regression model.

3. The numbers of requests in each problem type are also included to capture the possible effects of the difference of average resolution time across problem types. That is, our model accounts for the distribution of the number of requests resolved across problem types at an individual level.<sup>9</sup>

4. Changes in a group’s composition can influence performance. We added the two variables to control for this possibility: movement in $\left( M I n _ { t } \right)$ and movement out $( M O u t _ { t } )$ of the knowledge workers in every group.

5. We added Peak to control for the seasonality of the request volume (the first four weeks of a new semester) on an organizational level.

6. We checked the number of consultants engaged in problem solving every week $( C o N u m _ { t } )$ and found some variation. In particular, the number of the FirstGs varies by the time of year even if the variation is very small. Our regression model includes the number of consultants working during week t in each group.

As can be seen from Table $^ { 6 , }$ the coefficients on the individual learning variables $( \beta _ { 1 j } )$ for the three groups (j = 0 for FirstG, 1 for SecondG, 2 for ThirdS) are negative and statistically significant. This indicates that IT consultants demonstrate a significant learning curve on ART as they accumulate individual problem-solving experience. That is, we find that learning effects are applicable even in an IT environment characterized by constant change, a wide variety of support requests, and a broad base of knowledge required to address them. Learning curves are often characterized in terms of a progress ratio (p), which is calculated based on the estimated learning rates, $p = 2 ^ { \beta _ { 1 j } }$ . The progress ratio indicates how much productivity increases for each doubling of cumulative experience. The progress ratio for FirstG is $p = 0 . 9 3 3 ^ { }$ , and the figure for ThirdS is 0.874. When the FirstG (or ThirdS) consultants double problemsolving experience, ART decreases approximately by 6.7% (or 12.5%, respectively). Given the significant learning progress, the managers in a computing call center should appreciate the value of experience as an effective tool for realizing performance enhancements.

The estimated learning rates $\mathbf { \xi } ( t = ( \hat { \beta } _ { 1 m } - \hat { \beta } _ { 1 n } ) /$ $s e ( \hat { \beta } _ { 1 m } - \hat { \beta } _ { 1 n } ) )$ do not differ significantly across the groups: FirstG versus SecondG $( p = 0 . { \dot { 3 } } 9 7 3 )$ , FirstG versus ThirdS $( p = 0 . 2 5 7 5 )$ , and SecondG versus ThirdS $( p = 0 . 7 7 6 0 )$ . These results might come from the mixture of the kinds of problems consultants solve and how they solve the problems. On the one hand, focusing on operational differences (specialist versus generalist), ThirdSs of the upper-level consultant group (more specialized groups) might be expected to have faster learning curves than do the generalists engaged in a diversification strategy (solving all types of problems). Specialization can allow individuals (or organizations) to focus all of their time and energy toward a particular kind of problem and thereby become more proficient in it (Fisher and Ittner 1999, Flueckiger 1976, Von Hippel 1998).

On the other hand, consultants engaged in different layers use different required knowledge to resolve problems. For FirstGs, application-level knowledge is characterized by relatively easy transferability and more knowledge spillover across problems. Therefore, learning about one function on application-level knowledge would yield better insights into how other closely related functions might work. In contrast, learning of in-depth technical-level knowledge might not lead to a similar kind of knowledge spillover. We suggest that the estimated learning rates do not statistically differ because of the influence of these competing forces.

## 5.2. Knowledge Transfer Effects

In Model 1, the coefficient $\beta _ { 2 j }$ for the knowledge transfer effects is negative and significant for the FirstG group, providing evidence of knowledge transfer for FirstGs. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that the similarity between tasks positively affected transfer of knowledge (Darr and Kurtzberg 2000). Although each FirstG consultant solves a variety of problems, the set of problems solved by one consultant is similar to that solved by another. By contrast, each ThirdS consultant has his or her own specialized area of experience and solves problems that differ from other ThirdS consultants’. Rulke and Galaskiewicz (2000) highlighted the role of the ease of shared conceptualization of the problem and the provision of retrieval cues in improving group performance. Each consultant has specialized in a problem type in the ThirdS group and, to a lesser extent, in SecondG so that the weak similarity of tasks across consultants reduces the benefits of knowledge transfer within the group. The specialization strategy employed in ThirdS may not be conducive to knowledge transfer.

Furthermore, knowledge characteristics such as causal ambiguity are important factors that affect the difficulty of knowledge transfer. Ambiguous problems that FirstG consultants are not able to solve are referred upward to ThirdS consultants. Our results suggest that application-level knowledge, which is lower in causal ambiguity than is technical-level knowledge, transferred more than technical-level knowledge. Thus, our results are consistent with Szulanski’s (2000) argument about causal ambiguity reducing knowledge transfer.

## 5.3. Control Variables

The coefficients of the control variables are comprehensible and provide valuable insights. The negative coefficient of current problem volume $( q _ { i t } )$ indicates significant scale effects. That is, as individual consultants confront more problems in a unit of time, their average resolution time decreases.

Turnover hurts the performance of the ThirdS specialist group and, to a lesser extent, that of the SecondG group. Both the number of individuals moving into and the number moving out of the ThirdS group impaired the group’s performance. For the SecondG group, the number of individuals moving into the group harmed its performance. The negative effect of the departure of key individuals reflects the loss of their knowledge; the negative effect of new members reflects the disruption caused by getting them up to speed and integrating them into the groups. Interestingly, neither the number of employees moving into the group nor the number departing hurts the performance of the FirstG group. The FirstG group experienced considerable turnover by design and procedures had been developed to minimize the effect of turnover in this group. Previous work has shown that turnover has a less disruptive effect when it is expected and procedures are in place to deal with it (Rao and Argote 2006, Ton and Huckman 2008).

The positive coefficient of Peak is consistent with the general expectation: resolution times go up during peak periods. The negative relationship between ART and consultant numbers in ThirdS is understandable: the more consultants in each group, the faster the resolution times. Surprisingly, we found that the coefficient of the number of FirstG consultants is positively and significantly associated with ART. A more detailed analysis supplemented with interviews with the manager of the center found that the variation of the number of consultants is intentional rather than a function of the evolution of the center. The center has adopted the operating strategy of changing the number of FirstGs based on the seasonality of requests (e.g., the beginning of the semester or during breaks). The positive coefficient in the regression model appears to reflect the challenges of getting a larger number of FirstG consultants up to speed and properly coordinated. Interestingly, the coefficient of the average criticality is insignificant. There is little variation for the measure because almost all (about 95%) e-mail requests are automatically assigned as level 3 (medium) in criticality.

5.4. Knowledge Transfer Across IT Problem Types In Models 2 and 3, we analyzed ART as a function of IT problem types. We add problem type dummies in addition to individual learning variables and knowledge transfer variables in every problem type. We found that the hours required to solve the first request, individual learning effects, and knowledge transfer effects vary according to the IT problem type.

With the exception of e-mail and OS, the problemtype dummy variables $( P T _ { p } )$ are significant, showing that the hours required to solve the first request across all the problem types are not identical. In Model 2, only the account problem shows significant learning effects, whereas the knowledge transfer effects are significant in six problem types. Thus, both learning effects and knowledge transfer effects depend on the problem type. These results indicate that problem type moderates the learning relationship between experience and performance. A similar conclusion can be drawn from the results in Model 3. One potential explanation of this finding is the difference in the characteristics of each knowledge pool. Differences in knowledge evolution speed or the size of the knowledge pool may cause the learning rates to vary according to IT problem types. The knowledge pool may be so large or abstract that one requires a prohibitively lengthy experience period to benefit from learning, showing relatively slow (or no) learning progress. For example, consultants show neither individual learning nor knowledge transfer effects within a group in Web service problems, which include more diverse areas—e.g., general technical skills, HTML, JavaScript, Perl, Windows environment, Active Server Pages (ASP), UNIX environment, Visual Basic, network protocols (Wade and Parent 2001–2002)—than other problem types such as the account problem type. Similarly, the application software problem that does not show individual learning progress/knowledge transfer deals with a wide spectrum of application software such as Microsoft packages, various statistics packages, multiple DBMSs, graphics software, and media players.

The results from Table 7 elaborate the knowledge transfer effect within the FirstG group that was estimated through Model 1. As noted previously, Models 2 and 3 are estimated with data from only the FirstG group. Although FirstG does not benefit from knowledge transfer within the group in application software and Web service problem types, FirstG benefits from knowledge transfer within the group in the other problem types.

In Model 3, we added the variable of total cumulative requests across all problem types along with cumulative problem-solving experience in each problem type.<sup>10</sup> We can see the evidence of knowledge transfer across IT problem types with a significant coefficient for total cumulative requests in Model $3 ( \beta _ { 3 } = - 0 . 1 3 2 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . This empirically shows that the problem-solving performance in a focal problem type is enhanced not only by experience in the problem type but also by experience gained in other problem types. Here, our identification strategy for knowledge transfer across problem types is rooted in the problem heterogeneity across problem types. The difference of mean and variance across problem types shown in Table 4 as well as different learning progress from Model 2 support the existence of heterogeneity across problem types.

## 6. Conclusion

This study is the first empirical investigation of the nature of learning curves in a computing call center consisting of three hierarchical groups of IT knowledge workers. We found evidence that individuals in all three groups benefitted from their own problem-solving experience. Furthermore, our results show that the first group of generalists benefitted from the experience of other consultants within the group. Thus, the extent of learning transfer depends on the kind of problems being solved (or, equivalently, the knowledge set required to solve problems) and the way the problems are solved. In addition to knowledge transfer across IT knowledge workers, our results provide empirical evidence of knowledge transfer across IT problem types—the performance on a focal problem benefits from experience on other problems. Therefore, we conclude that the specialization strategy does not have to be adopted in the generalist group because IT consultants benefit from solving different types of problems.

In addition to contributing to an empirically grounded study of learning in an IT technical service environment, our study has several implications for managerial practice. First among these is the implication for staffing in IT technical service organizations. Gans and Zhou (2002) showed that a myopic staffing policy could be optimal, where a myopic policy is derived from the optimization of a single-period static model rather than the optimization of a dynamic multiperiod model. A key assumption underlying their analysis is that no learning effects occur. Our estimated learning rates in IT knowledge workers are significant. We also find (1) conditional knowledge transfer effects, (2) positive scale effects in workload, and (3) different turnover effects for different groups. These empirical estimates can be used to dynamically coordinate the potentially conflicting operational goals of high problem-solving performance and operational costs minimization in a computing call center (Bassamboo and Zeevi 2009, Feldman et al. 2008, Pinker and Shumsky 2000).

Learning effects and knowledge transfer are both important resources in performance enhancement. Managers may prioritize one of the two desirable effects through the operational decision on how multiple consultants in a group solve diverse problems (specialist versus generalist). There are a number of implicit and explicit references as to how learning might be positively affected by task specialization because specialization allows individuals to focus all of their time and energy toward a particular kind of problem and thereby become more proficient in it (Fisher and Ittner 1999, Flueckiger 1976, Schilling et al. 2003, Von Hippel 1998). On the other hand, we can boost knowledge transfer within a group by adopting a diversification strategy rather than specialization because of ease of shared conceptualization of the problem and the provision of retrieval cues (Darr and Kurtzberg 2000, Rulke and Galaskiewicz 2000). Our estimation supports the argument that a diversification strategy induces greater knowledge transfer effects. Pinker and Shumsky (2000) showed that the optimal staff mix combines flexible and specialized workers according to characteristics of tasks and organizations. Deciding on the problem-solving strategy requires careful understanding of how well the problem-solving strategies fit the knowledge types used to solve problems. Considering these findings and the knowledge transfer effects across problem types, we can conclude that a diversification strategy for the lower consultants utilizing application-level knowledge is very effective.

There may be alternative explanations for our findings. One explanation is selection bias: a consultant can select the requests he or she is more familiar with because consultants can see the subject/title fields of requests in the queue. That is, consultants could select easier requests to solve as they gained experience. According to this reasoning, selection rather than learning would drive the performance improvements. In our study context, consultants do not benefit by solving more requests or by solving them more quickly. Also, consultants can ask for assistance from other consultants or transfer a request to specialists. Therefore, it is unlikely that they are reluctant to pick up a request in the order received. Furthermore, if consultants generally select requests in order, the problem type distributions should not differ across individual consultants. We performed an analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test whether the proportions of requests solved in every problem type by individuals differed across individuals over time. We do not find statistically significant differences in problem type distributions across individuals. Thus, selection bias does not seem to be a viable alternative explanation in the context.

Another alternative explanation for the performance improvements we observe is technical change. We tried to add calendar time to investigate the influence of technical change associated with the time flow. There is a significant correlation between the knowledge transfer variable and calendar time (0.911 for FirstG, 0.903 for SecondG, and 0.900 for ThirdS), and so we cannot analyze these two factors simultaneously in a regression model. We believe that three characteristics of our site contribute to the high correlation. First, there was continuous operation during the period: neither breakdowns nor interruptions of operation occurred. Second, in contrast to the environments of other studies such as pizza franchises (Darr et al. 1995) or aircraft producers (Benkard 2001), the operation in the center does not have a viable substitute and is not affected by economic conditions such as the appearance of a competitor. Third, a linear increment in experience in the long-term perspective is observed even when there is fluctuation in the short term because of seasonality. We examined potential contributors to performance variation that were expected to be captured with calendar time. The center has used the same Remedy system over the study period. Neither operation nor technical change inside the center was observed. But technical change occurring outside the center, such as the advancement of search engines (Google or Yahoo!), could result in performance gains. Consultants told us that they were unlikely to get assistance from outside sources because they have a better knowledge resource (the archive in the Remedy system database), and most reported problems are related to the unique environment at the university where the center is located. Therefore, the variations captured by calendar time do not seem to be a likely alternative explanation for the increased productivity.

## 6.1. Limitations and Future Research Direction

Although our findings in the learning curve of IT knowledge workers in a computing call center have internal validity, research in additional contexts is needed to determine the generality of our results. Characteristics of our data (e.g., the distinctive operational framework observed at our research site) are not unique to our context. For example, Das (2003) observed a similar structure in his study context. Research in contexts that differ from ours is needed to delineate the boundary conditions for our findings.

We classified the factors that might affect the difference of knowledge transfer observed in consultant groups: (1) what kind of problems to solve (application-level versus technical-level), (2) how to solve the problems (specialist versus generalist), and (3) demographic profile of consultants. The first two factors were highly correlated in our setting so we are not able to determine which factor drove our findings. Research in contexts where these factors are not so highly correlated would be helpful in disentangling the effect of these variables. As for the third factor, FirstGs were students whereas SecondGs and ThirdSs were full-time staff with careers. Including individual fixed effects in our models picks up inherent differences in the productivity of individuals and helps to mitigate this concern. Our estimation does not allow for endogeneity in the managers’ problemsolving strategy decision (specialist versus generalist) and resource allocation methods. Analyzing learning in additional contexts would advance the contingent view of learning that we have advocated here.

We used requests made only through e-mail in measuring performance because request-created time stamps in the other request methods were not accurate. However, e-mail requests may inherently differ from the requests from calling or walking in. The statistics indicate that users are likely to report more critical problems through phone or walk-in than through e-mail requests: the average criticality of e-mail (2.95) is statistically significantly lower than those from the other contact methods (phone: 3.16 and walk-in: 3.05, $p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . In a similar fashion, consultants solving phone and walk-in requests might gain more knowledge if they resolve bigger and more complex problems than problems from e-mail. This will not weaken our findings and is unlikely to affect the managerial relevance of our analyses because e-mail requests account for well over the majority of the requests (70.8%). Future study should incorporate the impact of experience from different request modalities in the learning progress.

Other issues would benefit from additional research. For example, a greater understanding of dimensions of problem relatedness that affect knowledge transfer across IT problem types is needed. Similarly, a greater understanding of whether and how transactive memory systems (or knowledge of who knows what) (Lewis et al. 2005, Liang et al. 1995, Moreland et al. 1998, Wegner 1986) influence knowledge transfer within groups is needed. Future research should attempt to extend our findings by explicitly examining the effect of well-developed transactive memory systems on performance in the

IT technical service environment and by examining knowledge sharing processes (Huber 1991).

In closing, our results indicate that individual learning occurs even in knowledge work in a computing call center characterized by high volatility and a huge knowledge pool. Furthermore, knowledge transfer occurs across IT problem types and within one of the consultant groups. Thus, individual learning and knowledge transfer are significant sources of performance improvements in the call center. These organizations should be designed to facilitate learning and knowledge transfer because such a focus has the potential to improve organizational productivity.
