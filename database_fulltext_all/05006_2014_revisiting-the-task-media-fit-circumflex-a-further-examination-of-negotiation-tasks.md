# Revisiting the Task-Media Fit Circumflex: A further examination of negotiation tasks

Hui-Chih Wang <sup>a,1</sup>, Her-Sen Doong <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, No. 43, Sec. 4, Keelung Rd., Taipei 106, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of Management Information Systems, National Chiayi University, 580 Sinmin Rd., Chiayi 60054, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 26 June 2014 Accepted 1 July 2014 Available online 9 July 2014

Keywords: Task-media fit Negotiation support systems Online negotiation

## A B S T R A C T

McGrath and Hollingshead’s Task-Media Fit Circumflex, which proposed that the negotiations tasks “best fit" highly information-rich channels, is widely accepted for more than two decades. However, this study argues that according to the task nature, different negotiation tasks shall ‘‘best fit’’ different media channels. A 2 - 3 laboratory experiment was conducted to test this argument. Findings suggested that higher process satisfaction, lower cognitive effort and higher negotiation efficiency would be created when text-based systems were used to execute more analyzable tasks. In contrast, less analyzable tasks best fit face-to-face or audio/video-based systems. Managerial implications are discussed accordingly. - 2014 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Negotiation is one of the most crucial business activities [7,26]. The rapid increase in international trade and multi-national organizations has accelerated the prominence of negotiation due to the huge demands for negotiations across countries [29]. Traditionally, the two parties involved in a negotiation had to meet and talk face-to-face. However, the Internet has significantly altered the way people communicate with each other. Today, when there is a need to negotiate over a long distance, the two parties are able to talk on the Internet through a variety of media, choosing between video-based, audio-based and text-based systems [13]. Being acknowledged as a more convenient and cost beneficial approach [18], ‘‘online negotiation’’, the process of negotiating via the Internet, and ‘‘negotiation support systems’’ (NSS), the information systems that support online negotiation, have attracted researchers’ and practitioners’ interest [38]. For example, Smartsettle (http:// www.smartsettle.com/) and Cybersettle (http://www.cybersettle.- com/) are widely used in the business domain, while Inspire (http:// invite.concordia.ca/inspire/) and Negoisst (http://www.wi1.unihohenheim.de/negoisst.html) are often used by academics [32].

According to the media richness theory [3,16], face-to-face communication is the richest channel, followed by the telephone, and finally written text. This is because face-to-face communication is able to deliver more information cues, such as body language, immediate feedback, and emotional interaction via eye contact, as compared with telephone communication [12]. Written text provides even leaner media richness than the telephone, by which the emotional states of negotiators may still be delivered via tone of voice [17]. Generally, it is agreed that the richer the media channel, the better the communication performance. However, some empirical studies have disclosed contrasting results. For example, Kinney and Dennis [15] reported that media richness had no effect on decision quality, time taken to reach a consensus, or user satisfaction. Similarly, Valacich et al. [36] found that communication performance did not differ significantly among four supporting media: face-to-face, video, audio, and text-based. More specifically, Yuan et al. [39] revealed that in an online negotiation situation, the inclusion of video into the text/audiobased system did not create benefits in the negotiation process.

McGrath and Hollingshead [23] thus proposed the ‘‘task-media fit hypothesis’’ based on the concept of the Task Circumplex [11,22]. That is, task performance can only be enhanced if the task fits the supporting media. For example, if the task is to report the current prices of a hundred different products, a text-based message such as an email (a lean medium) may in fact be more efficient than online face-to-face discussion (a rich medium), in which tone of voice or facial expressions may distract users from recording accurate numbers. Although the task-media fit proposition may shed light on results that contradict the media richness theory above, only a small, albeit increasing, number of empirical studies has verified this hypothesis. Moreover, these empirical studies have usually selected two distinct types of task, such as intellective and negotiation tasks [25,34] or idea-generation and negotiation tasks [14], in order to reveal the effects of ‘‘task-media fit’’ on performance. This may be because the Task Circumplex proposes that negotiation tasks have high information richness requirements, while the requirements of intellective/idea-generation tasks are low.

Notably, the current study argues that not all negotiation tasks have high information richness requirements. Different negotiation tasks, depending on their nature, have different information needs [24] and media richness requirements. To verify this argument, a research model was proposed and empirically examined. The research objective is three-fold: first, to verify that negotiation, a conflict resolution task needing high information richness proposed by the Task Circumplex, may still have different information richness requirements based on the nature of the task; second, to confirm the ‘‘fit’’ effects of negotiation task/media channel on the negotiation performance in terms of efficiency and cognitive effort; third, to reveal that when there is a ‘‘good fit’’, negotiators’ satisfaction with the negotiation process and perceived decision quality, which have been neglected in past studies, shall be enhanced. In the following sections, the theoretical background, research hypotheses, methodology, statistical analysis and conclusion are discussed sequentially.

## 2. Theoretical discussion and research model

## 2.1. Media richness theory and task media fit theory

Media richness theory [3] proposes that different media are diverse in their ability to convey various types of information within a given time interval. Each communication channel, according to this theory, has the ability to deliver the following four factors, which determine its media richness level:

 Multiple information cues: The number of ways in which information can be transmitted, such as text (e.g., written words), verbal cues (e.g., vocal inflection), and nonverbal cues (e.g., facial expressions).

 Immediacy of feedback: the level of support of users’ ability to give rapid feedback on the information they receive.

 Language variety: the ability to facilitate engagements involving both numbers and natural language.

 Personal focus: the ability to convey the personal feelings and emotions of the involved parties.

Based on these factors, the theory indicates that face-to-face communication is the richest medium, followed by the telephone and then written communication. This is because when people are communicating face-to-face, they are able to respond to each other immediately, see changes in facial expressions, perceive changing accents or tones, use different oral languages and swiftly switch communication focus at any time. In other words, in terms of communication supported by negotiation support systems, the video mode shall be considered as the richest, followed by the audio mode, and finally the text mode.

Daft and Lengel [3] further argued that using richer media such as face-to-face communication would result in better performance for highly equivocal tasks. Unfortunately, prior studies have failed to support this theory [15,36]. More specifically, Yuan et al. [39] have focused on negotiation tasks and compared different online communication options: text-based, text/audio-based and text audio/video-based. Findings showed that adding video to text audio-based communication could not significantly improve communication efficiency, effectiveness or positive social-emotional communication in a negotiation. Rather, it distracted negotiators from concentrating on the ongoing negotiation task.

McGrath and Hollingshead [23] extended the media richness theory and proposed that tasks undertaken via overly rich or lean communication channels will be less effective if compared with those undertaken via the ‘‘best fit’’ channel. They thus suggested the ‘‘task-media fit hypothesis’’ to map four task types to matching communication channels in order to yield better performance (see Table 1).

The four task categories represent a theoretical continuum of successively increasing degrees of interdependence among members to generate good task performance [20]. For example, generating ideas is a task that may only require the exchange of specific ideas, without the need to evaluate the emotions hidden behind the interactions between group members. In contrast, tasks such as resolving conflicts or negotiating for one’s own benefit, as McGrath and Hollingshead argued, ‘‘may require the transmission of maximally rich information’’. To empirically examine this hypothesis, Kerr and Murthy [14] tested these two types of task and the task performance across two different media channels: face-to-face and computer-mediated. Findings indicated that, on the idea-generation task, computer mediation outperformed the face-to-face channel. However, on the negation task, no significant difference was found between these two supporting channels. Similarly, Mennecke et al. [25] empirically tested intellective and negotiation tasks and the related performance when using four different media channels: face-to-face, videophone, telephone, and synchronous computer mediation. Findings indicated that for the intellective task, no differences were found between these four media channels. For the negotiation task, findings generally supported the taskmedia fit hypothesis: face-to-face or videophone communication outperformed audio or computer-mediated channels. Thus, these two studies have demonstrated contrasting results as to whether the negotiation task demands the richest media channel – face-to-face – as proposed in the Task-Media Fit Circumflex.

## 2.2. Research model

McGrath and Hollingshead [23] indicated that situations in which the information is too rich or too lean for the identified task requirements had ‘‘poor fit’’. They thus proposed that the task of negotiating conflicts of interest was best suited to face-to-face communication, followed by video systems (marginal fit), audio systems (poor fit) and text-based computer systems (poorest fit). However, the current study argues that not all negotiation tasks have high information richness requirements. Rather, different types of negotiation task shall have different ‘‘best fit’’ media channels according to their nature. This argument may also shed light on the conflicting results as to whether negotiation tasks require high information richness, as reported by Kerr and Murthy [14] and Mennecke et al. [25].

To further examine the ‘‘fit’’ effects on negotiation tasks, the current study has proposed two types of negotiation task. More analyzable tasks, in which processes are clearly defined and have predefined solutions [27], can be performed using objective data and information. Less analyzable tasks, in which processes are nonlinear, more equivocal and more personalized [4], are more likely to confuse people during negotiation communication. Prior studies, such as Lim and Benbasat [19], have also applied such task categorization and examined how the users’ perceived equivocality might differ while using the text-based and multimedia channels, respectively. Findings indicated that for highly analyzable tasks, users’ perceived equivocality did not differ between the usage of two media channels, but for less analyzable tasks, users’ perceived equivocality was reduced when applying the multimedia channel. However, the task examined by Lim and Benbasat was a company interview for new employees rather than conflict resolution negotiation. Consequently, the current study aims to go a step further and verify how the ‘‘fit’’ between more/less analyzable negotiation tasks and three types of negotiation media – face-to-face, audio/ video-based systems, and text-based systems – may influence negotiation performance in terms of efficiency, process satisfaction, perceived decision quality, and cognitive effort. The research model (see Fig. 1) is proposed below and hypotheses are presented in the following section.

## 3. Research hypotheses

## 3.1. Efficiency, process satisfaction, and perceived decision quality

One of the most important goals that business negotiators seek to achieve is efficiency: the time taken to complete a task [1]. When the supporting media fit the task, the information communication will be improved and the completion time will be reduced [6]. In support of this, Fuller and Dennis [8] have designed a longitudinal study to reveal that while there is a lower fit between the media channel and the task, individuals will also perceive the process to be less effective and efficient. More specifically, Lim and Benbasat [18] reported that video-based systems are more effective for less analyzable tasks. Consequently, it is hypothesized that:

H1. The time to accomplish the negotiation task will be shorter when there is a good fit between the media channel and the negotiation task.

Goodhue [10] indicated that when there is a good fit between the task and the media channel, greater satisfaction towards the process will be generated. In contrast, if the media channel is too lean or too rich for the supporting task, there is low process satisfaction [8]. As the text-based media channels are more suitable for more analyzable tasks [30,31], individuals may thus perceive the process to be more satisfactory. Consequently, it is hypothesized that:

H2. The negotiators’ satisfaction with the negotiation process will be higher when there is a good fit between the media channel and the negotiation task.

Tan et al. [35] revealed that when the system support provides cognitive resources that match those demanded for the task environment, consumers’ subjective evaluation of the decision outcome (i.e. perceived decision quality) will be enhanced. That is, given that the more analyzable task is less ambiguous [18] and therefore demands fewer cognitive resources, the text-based communication support which helps negotiators to focus on the text content will increase the effectiveness of negotiation. In contrast, to deal with the less analyzable task, which is more demanding in terms of cognitive resources, the audio/video-based communication support, which can offer more cues, will assist the decision-making. Consequently, it is hypothesized that:

H3. The negotiators’ perceived decision quality will be higher when there is a good fit between the media channel and the negotiation task.

## 3.2. Cognitive effort

Cognitive effort is the psychological cost that individuals perceive while processing information [28]. Resource-matching theory indicates that for a given task, individuals’ decisions are influenced by the balance between the cognitive resources available to process the information and those required for the task [2]. That is to say, the information processing will be most efficient and effective when there is a good fit between the media channel and the task [21]. Specifically, Yuan et al. [39] indicated that when the supporting media channel is too lean to present the essential information, negotiators shall expend more cognitive effort to understand their opponents and ensure adequate negotiation performance. Consequently, it is hypothesized that:

H4. The negotiators’ cognitive effort will be lower when there is a good fit between the media channel and the negotiation task.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Subjects and incentives

In this study, a 2 - 3 (negotiation task - media channel) factorial design experiment was undertaken. One hundred and forty-four student volunteers (i.e., 72 dyads) were recruited for this experiment. Each participant was randomly assigned into one of the six groups and was awarded NT\$100 (approximately US\$3.50) at the end of the experiment. The subjects comprised 94 postgraduate students and 50 undergraduate students (87 males, 57 females; mean age 23.3 years), of whom 142 had prior negotiation experience. Further, 122 of these subjects had online negotiation experience (e.g., via email or Yahoo!/MSN messengers).

## 4.2. Experimental design and procedures

The experiment was designed to evaluate and compare the interaction effects of negotiation task types and supporting media types on negotiation performance. To further verify the effect of text-based and audio/video-based NSSs, a NSS implemented in Adobe Flex was developed exclusively for this study. To ensure a consistently high network speed for all NSS users, the negotiation support system was installed on a Win2003 server on the same local area network as the personal computers in the laboratory. Personal computers equipped with Pentium IV CPU and 17-in. LCD monitors were set as the constant system environment for all NSS users. Three types of supporting media were used, as follows:

 Text-based NSS: Negotiators used text only to communicate during the negotiation process (see Fig. 2).

 Audio/video-based NSS: Negotiators used audio and video through a webcam to communicate during the negotiation process (see Fig. 3).

 Face-to-face: Negotiators communicated with each other faceto-face and did not use any information system to support communication during the negotiation process (see Fig. 4).

Moreover, the current study designed two types of negotiation task:

 In the more analyzable negotiation task, negotiators conducted price bargaining on an electronic product.

 In the less analyzable negotiation task, negotiators conducted salary bargaining.

As negotiators only need to consider the price while undertaking the former task, it is thus selected as a more analyzable task. In contrast, while negotiating a salary, more issues, such as the candidate’s manner, personality, education level or professional skills, must be incorporated into the evaluation, rather than considering the level of the salary alone. The salary bargaining is thus selected as a less analyzable negotiation task.

![](/api/attachments/RYTN6A3S/fulltext/images/167a9f64f55cbd31adfde98ed9278c6af265ee0514bd9c8467fe392e01e7d30e.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Illustration of the text-based NSS.

Subjects were randomly assigned into pairs to perform the oneto-one negotiation. Before the process started, the subjects were required to confirm that they did not know their assigned partners. In addition, the paired subjects who were assigned to the treatment using NSS support were seated in separate rooms during the negotiation process.

At the start of the experiment, each subject was asked to read all instructions carefully. Subjects were guided perform the negotiation task on their own and could not speak to others during the negotiation process. After completing the pre-experiment demographic questionnaire, each subject was again randomly assigned into either (1) the more analyzable task group or (2) less analyzable task group. In group 1, one of the paired subjects played the role of a seller of consumer electronics products, while the other acted as the buyer. The more analyzable task to be conducted was price bargaining. In group 2, one of the paired subjects played the role of the company manager who was going to recruit a qualified employee, while the other acted as the candidate who wanted the job. The less analyzable negotiation task to be performed was to determine the job’s salary. Moreover, each of the 36 dyads in groups 1 and 2 was further randomly assigned to use one selected form of communication support: text-based NSS, audio/videobased NSS or face-to-face, to support them during the negotiation process.

At the end of the experiment, 61 out of 72 dyads had reached agreement, with 21, 19 and 21 of these dyads using the text-based NSS, audio/video-based NSS and face-to-face approach, respectively.

## 4.3. Measures

Four constructs were measured in this study. Negotiation efficiency, which was defined as the time each dyad took to accomplish the task. Negotiation process satisfaction, which referred to the negotiators’ satisfaction with the negotiation process, was measured using four items adapted from Spreng et al. [33]. Perceived decision quality, which referred to the negotiators’ confidence in the final negotiation decision, was measured using three items adapted from Tan et al. [35]. Finally, cognitive effort, which was defined as the effort expended while using the NSS or face-to-face communication to negotiate, was measured using four items adapted from Pereira [28].

The initial versions of the scales were pre-tested for content validity by two professors, who served as expert judges. A pilot study was then undertaken to test the scales’ wording and reliability. After the essential revision, the final version of questionnaire was used in the experiment. Table 3 provides the scale items for each construct.

## 4.4. Manipulation check

The manipulation check, which assesses negotiators’ perceptions of task analyzability, was examined using four items developed by Withey et al. [37] on a 7-point Likert scale. These questions were: (1) To what extent is there a clearly known way to complete the negotiation task? (2) To what extent is there a clearly defined body of knowledge that can guide you in completing the negotiation task? (3) To what extent is there an understandable sequence of steps that can be followed in completing the negotiation task? (4) To what extent can you actually rely on established procedures and practices to complete the negotiation task? Cronbach’s a was 0.94 and results showed that there was a significant difference (F = 8.59, p < 0.01) between the more analyzable negotiation task (mean = 3.97, standard deviation = 1.07) and the less analyzable negotiation task (mean = 3.45, standard deviation = 1.05). Therefore, this manipulation was confirmed to be adequate.

Further, for those subjects who were assigned to the treatment of using NSS support, four items adapted from Dennis and Kinney [5] were used to evaluate the perceived media richness of the NSS. Applying a 7-point Likert scale, these items were: ‘‘I could easily explain what I think using this information system’’; ‘‘This information system helped us to communicate quickly’’; ‘‘This information system helped us to better understand each other.’’; ‘‘This information system slowed down our communications’’ (reversed coded). The internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s a = 0.96). Findings indicated that there was a significant difference (F = 152.15, p < 0.01) between the textbased NSS (mean = 3.31, standard deviation = 1.07) and the audio/ video-based NSS (mean = 5.62, standard deviation = 0.73). Thus, manipulations were confirmed to be appropriate in this study.

Cronbach’s a was 0.92 for satisfaction with the negotiation process, 0.85 for perceived decision quality, and 0.92 for cognitive effort. ## 6. Conclusion and implications

This study has empirically demonstrated that not all the negotiation tasks demand the richest media support. Rather, great improvements can be made to online negotiation performance and positive perceptions towards the negotiation process if the support medium selected provides a good fit with the nature of the negotiation task. Moreover, while in general people agreed that the great cognitive efforts that they must expend during negotiation are always demanding, our findings suggest that, by choosing a media channel with ‘‘good fit’’, negotiators can reduce their cognitive effort and enjoy the process more. When negotiators are performing more analyzable negotiation tasks, text-based NSS may meet their needs better, leading to greater satisfaction with the negotiation process, lower cognitive effort, higher perceived decision quality, and less time spent completing their negotiation tasks. For a less analyzable negotiation task, applying face-to-face negotiation or an audio/video-based system will improve negotiators’ satisfaction with the negotiation process, enhance perceived decision quality, and reduce perceived cognitive effort. The completion time will also be shortened. However, employing a mismatching negotiation task/media channel will lead to negative consequences such as lower negotiation process satisfaction and perceived decision quality.

The theoretical contribution of this study is thus twofold. First, as shown in Table 7, this study has added value to McGrath and Hollingshead’s proposition by providing more advanced knowledge of the ‘‘fitting environment’’ of negotiation tasks. Second, while past studies have mainly examined improved performance in terms of shortened time and reduced cognitive effort, this study has revealed that when there is a good fit between the task and the media channel, satisfaction with the negotiation process and perceived decision quality will also be enhanced.

This study may also provide useful implications for company managers in choosing the most efficient media channel through which to conduct their daily negotiation tasks. As managers have been reported to spend a huge amount of time in negotiation every day [9], deciding which media channel they should use to achieve the best negotiation performance has thus become a frequent and very important issue. The present findings have suggested that when they have to undertake more analyzable tasks, such as bargaining over price, shipment quantity, or the delivery terms for the next season, a text-based system will be the most appropriate. In contrast, when they have to conduct less analyzable tasks, such as bargaining over the salary for a new employee or the division of responsibilities in relation to the bonus awarded for a new project, face-to-face or audio/video-based systems will help them to attain better negotiation results.

This study is not without limitations. First, as it employed a laboratory experiment, the external validity of the findings is limited. Second, this study utilized the student sample. Scholars might want to address this issue when applying the findings. Future studies may add value to the existing knowledge by verifying whether there is also a ‘‘good fit’’ between media channels and different types of idea-generation task, which have been proposed by the Task-Media Fit Circumflex to have low media richness requirements.
