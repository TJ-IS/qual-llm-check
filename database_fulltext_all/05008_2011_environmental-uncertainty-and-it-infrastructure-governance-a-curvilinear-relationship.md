This view predicts that firms tend to decentralize IT governance in more uncertain environments. We investigate this issue by studying the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance in a sample of business units from Fortune 1000 companies. The key proposition in this paper is that the relationship between environmental uncertainty and decentralization in IT infrastructure governance is best characterized as a curvilinear relationship. That is, when environmental uncertainty increases from low to high, firms tend to first decentralize their IT infrastructure decisions to the business units to enhance their responsiveness; and then centralize their IT infrastructure decisions to the headquarters as uncertainty increases further, to achieve the benefits of coordination and to mitigate the potential agency problem in uncertain environments. Moreover, the study proposes that business unrelatedness between business units and their headquarters moderates the curvilinear relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance. ## 1. Introduction

IT governance refers to the pattern of decision making for IT-related activities such as strategic IT planning, IT infrastructure management, and application development (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999, Weill and Ross 2005). A central proposition in the IT governance literature is that environmental uncertainty is associated with decentralization in IT governance (e.g., Brown and Magill 1994, Brown 1997). Empirical work in this area, however, presents mixed results. Brown (1997), for example, examined software development and found that uncertain environments drove firms to adopt decentralized governance, whereas other studies on IT infrastructure and strategic IT planning (e.g., Ahituv et al. 1989, Olson and Chervany 1980, Hann and Weber 1996) failed to find significant evidence that environmental uncertainty is associated with more decentralized decision making.

A potential explanation for the mixed empirical findings is that environmental uncertainty and IT governance are multidimensional constructs. The uncertainty in the environment arises from various factors (e.g., dynamism, munificence, and complexity; see Dess and Beard 1984), and different facets of IT (e.g., IT infrastructure, software development, project management, etc.) may have idiosyncratic relationships with different dimensions of uncertainty. For example, flexibility is a key consideration in IT infrastructure whereas speed is a key consideration in project management, to meet the environmental challenges and opportunities (Duncan 1995). The unique characteristics of different aspects of IT suggest that it is necessary to study each facet of IT governance separately. Thus, to develop a deeper understanding of the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT governance, we focus on one specific aspect of IT governance—IT infrastructure governance; i.e., how IT infrastructure governance is influenced by different dimensions of environmental uncertainty. We focus on IT infrastructure governance because IT infrastructure accounts for a large proportion of the investment in IT (Weill and Broadbent 1998), and IT infrastructure serves as the foundation for other IT capabilities (e.g., application development) and IT-based business initiatives (Weill et al. 2002).

A second potential explanation for the mixed empirical findings is that the impact of environmental uncertainty on IT governance is likely to be more involved than that suggested by prior studies. For example, the focus on the advantages of local responsiveness emphasizes the positive relationship between environmental uncertainty and decentralization in IT governance. However, agency theory (e.g., Jensen and Meckling 1992) suggests that environmental uncertainty may discourage decentralization, since it is more difficult for the headquarters to monitor and evaluate business units’ decisions in uncertain environments. Moreover, for multi-business firms, a centralized and standardized platform enables firms to share resources and coordinate activities across different markets that may improve flexibility and responsiveness at the corporate level, and help to mitigate the negative impact of environmental uncertainty. Thus, the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT governance may be influenced by multiple and conflicting forces, and is likely to be more involved than previously suggested.

In this study we propose a curvilinear relationship between environmental uncertainty and decentralization in IT infrastructure governance. We hypothesize that, when environmental uncertainty is relatively low, an increase in environmental uncertainty makes it more likely for firms to decentralize their IT infrastructure decisions to business units, since allowing business units to adjust their infrastructure enables them to better respond to environmental changes. However, when uncertainty is relatively high, a further increase in uncertainty makes it more likely for firms to centralize their IT infrastructure decisions, since centralization enables the headquarters to retain better control of key infrastructure decisions when the environmental changes are harder to predict. Centralization also enables firms to share and coordinate their IT and non-IT (e.g., customer base, production technologies) resources across different business units and improve flexibility and responsiveness in highly uncertain environments.

Using a panel data set containing 1,193 business units in Fortune 1000 companies from 2001 to 2005, we find evidence that supports our hypotheses. The analysis reveals a curvilinear relationship between environment uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance; i.e., a firm is more likely to decentralize IT infrastructure governance to a business unit when the business unit faces intermediate uncertainty, than when the business unit faces very low or very high uncertainty. Moreover, we find that business unrelatedness<sup>1</sup> between the business unit and its headquarters—which captures headquarters’ information disadvantage—positively moderates the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance. This suggests that the curvilinear relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance is stronger when the business units and its headquarters are in different industries than when they are in the same industry. Thus, the key contribution of this paper is to provide a more refined understanding of the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance. The rest of this paper is organized as follows: §2 presents the hypotheses, §3 describes the empirical study, and §4 concludes the paper.

## 2. Theoretical Foundation and Hypothesis Development

Environmental uncertainty has long been studied in strategic management and organization theory literatures. Based on a factor analysis of a comprehensive set of environmental variables, Dess and Beard (1984) concluded that three dimensions of the environment contribute most to uncertainty: dynamism, munificence, and complexity. Dynamism refers to the volatility and unpredictability of changes in the business environment that a firm has to deal with (Keats and Hitt 1988). Industries with higher uncertainty in demand, for example, are more dynamic. Munificence refers to the opportunities for growth in an industry (Dess and Beard 1984). In munificent environments, firms tend to adopt strategies and structures that can help them to capture these growth opportunities. Complexity refers to the number and heterogeneity of task-environment elements that a firm has to deal with (Dess and Beard 1984). The larger the number and the greater the heterogeneity of the entities (e.g., competitors) a firm has to deal with, the more complex the environment. These three dimensions represent the uncertainty in a firm’s environment in terms of three related but orthogonal constructs. Dynamism can be viewed as the uncertainty on the demand (consumer) side, complexity as the uncertainty on the supply (competition) side, and munificence as the uncertainty that is driven by the longer-term trends in the industry. These three dimensions have also been analyzed in the IS literature (e.g., Wade and Hulland 2004).

## 2.1. Environmental Uncertainty and IT Infrastructure Governance

Centralization of IT infrastructure provides the benefits of economies of scale as the same IT platform can be leveraged across different business units, thereby reducing the unit cost of IT infrastructure for each business unit. The benefits of economies of scale are salient when different business units operate in more certain (i.e., less dynamic, munificent, and complex) environments. In these environments it is not difficult for the corporate headquarters to understand the needs of different business units and to optimize resource allocation from an organizational standpoint. A firm-level procurement of IT infrastructure can also provide more bargaining power (over the IT vendors) than separate business unit-level procurements (Turban et al. 2006). Thus, the benefits of scale lead to centralization in more certain environments.

A second important consideration in the choice of IT infrastructure governance is the need for responsiveness that can be provided by local information processing. When an industry becomes more dynamic, more complex, or fast-growing, a firm has to respond to a larger and a more diverse set of suppliers, customers, and competitors, in a more timely fashion. As the information to respond to these needs usually resides with the business units, a centralized governance structure would require business units to transmit local information up the hierarchy to centralized decision makers. The information transmission process introduces delays and distortions that affect the quality and timeliness of decision making (Jensen and Meckling 1992, Alexander 1991). Thus, business units are in the best position to make IT infrastructure decisions to respond appropriately and expeditiously to their environmental challenges and opportunities (Anand and Mendelson 1997, Nault 1998). Centralization and standardization of the IT infrastructure may also fail to meet the varying needs of different business units. Therefore, as uncertainty (i.e., dynamism, munificence, and complexity) increases from low to the intermediate level, the benefits of adaptation and responsiveness provided by more decentralized IT infrastructure governance are likely to outweigh the benefits of economy of scale provided by centralization, and leads to more decentralization of IT infrastructure governance.

Decentralization of IT infrastructure governance, however, raises issues of control. Agency theory notes that the interest of agents (i.e., business units in this research context) may not always align with corporate goals. Business units may take decisions that are in the best interests of the local divisions, but such decisions may not necessarily be in the overall interests of the firm (Jensen and Meckling 1992, Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991). Therefore, decentralization incurs costs of control since the firm has to make extra efforts to monitor the decision processes and outcomes, or to provide incentives to mitigate the agent’s private interests. However, costs of control often do not outweigh the benefits of local information processing, when uncertainty increases from low to the intermediate level. This is because with relatively low uncertainty, the headquarters can easily monitor and evaluate business units’ decisions, ex post. In such environments outcome-based controls (Kirsch 1996, 1997) can be used as a cost-efficient mechanism to regulate business units. The ability to monitor a local unit’s decisions and implement outcome-based controls allows headquarters to use more decentralized IT infrastructure governance without escalating the agency cost.

However, when uncertainty increases from the intermediate to high level, the benefits of local information processing become less significant. This is because the dynamism of the environment, the unique needs of new business opportunities, or the environmental complexity, are likely to be beyond the capabilities of individual business units to respond appropriately. The costs of control, on the other hand, increase significantly as uncertainty increases from the medium to high level. This is because large environmental changes are more likely to cause conflicts between local managers’ short-term preferences and the firm’s long-term strategies. More decentralized decision-making to satisfy diverse (and oftentimes conflicting) business constituencies may create a set of independent systems that might satisfy individual units but not the firm as a whole (Shpilberg et al. 2007). Kahai et al. (2001), for example, found that large firms tend to centralize decision-making regarding IT infrastructure to avoid unrestricted proliferation of different standards. Here, proliferation of different standards is an agency cost, since although local standards may serve individual business units’ well, they often complicate firm-level integration. In this regard, more centralized IT infrastructure governance can be used to control the agency cost. The agency problem is also particularly severe in highly uncertain environments as the headquarters may not be able to design outcome-based reward systems to align business units’ incentives with the interests of the firm (Jensen and Meckling 1992, Foss and Laursen 2005). To regulate business units in highly uncertain environments, firms have to rely more on behavior-based controls (Eisenhardt 1985, Gupta and Govindarajan 1991), which can be very costly. Thus, firms may switch from decentralization to recentralizing IT infrastructure governance to mitigate the agency problem as uncertainty increases from the intermediate to the higher levels.

In highly uncertain environments, centralization also brings the benefits of coordination. For example, in highly uncertain environments centralized governance reduces the cost of coordination across different units of multi-business firms (Tushman and Nadler 1978). Similarly, centralization facilitates cross-unit synergies by enabling the sharing and coordination of (IT and non-IT) resources across different units of a multibusiness firm. These benefits are more valuable in highly dynamic, munificent, and complex environments as different business units can share centralized resources that provide flexibility and risk sharing at the firm level (Adams et al. 2008). For example, in the casino/gaming industry, Harrah’s centralized data warehouse allows its different casino properties to have a universal view of its consumers so that the customer base can be leveraged across different properties (Lee et al. 2006). Such sharing and coordination of resources reduces risk in highly uncertain environments as firms can shift resources across business units as opportunities arise and, thereby, mitigate the impact of the uncertain environment. Considering these forces, as uncertainty increases from the intermediate level, the control and coordination benefits of centralization may outweigh the benefits of adaptation and responsiveness provided by decentralization. Thus, firms tend to re-centralize as the environmental uncertainty changes from the medium to high level. We thus propose:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1.</sup> Environmental uncertainty has a curvilinear relationship with decentralization in IT infrastructure governance.

## Impact of Business Unrelatedness

The choice of IT infrastructure governance is also influenced by the business unrelatedness<sup>2</sup> between a business unit and its corporate headquarters. If the headquarters and the business unit are in different industries, their businesses are more unrelated to each other. In such a case, the headquarters has less information and knowledge to identify the resource needs of the business unit. Thus, as environmental uncertainty increases, the need for business unit managers’ local knowledge is higher, which encourages decentralization. However, the headquarters and the business unit being in unrelated businesses also means that the headquarters has less information and knowledge to monitor and evaluate the business unit’s decisions, if it allows more decentralized IT infrastructure decision making. Thus, in highly uncertain environments the need to control agency costs that encourage centralization are also stronger. This suggests that the curvilinear relationship between environmental uncertainty and decentralization in IT infrastructure governance is likely to be stronger when business unrelatedness is higher.

In contrast, when the headquarters and the business unit are in the same industry, their businesses are more related and the headquarters has more information and knowledge about the resource needs of the business unit. Thus, corporate managers are able to make timely and appropriate decisions on behalf of the business unit, in the case of centralized IT infrastructure governance. Moreover, even if the corporate managers were to delegate infrastructure decisions to the business unit managers, they have the information and knowledge to monitor and evaluate the business unit’s decisions. Thus the agency problem is not a significant issue in this case. Therefore, the need for delegation and the distinction between centralization and decentralization is not crucial when uncertainty increases. As a result, the curvilinear relationship is likely to be weaker when the headquarters and the business unit have related businesses. Therefore, we propose:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.</sup> The business unrelatedness between the headquarters and the business unit strengthens the relationship between environmental uncertainty and decentralization in IT infrastructure governance.

## 3. Empirical Evidence

## 3.1. Data

The data on IT governance is obtained from the CI Technology Database (CI). This database contains information about the IT infrastructure in over 500,000 sites in the United States and Canada. The CI database identifies each site as the headquarters (represented as “Ultimate Headquarters”), as a business unit (represented as “Divisional Headquarters”), or as a plant. For each business unit, the CI database records whether the business unit itself or the headquarters has the decision rights regarding three components of the IT infrastructure: PC, server, and network. If the business unit, instead of the headquarters, has the decision right for a certain component (e.g., PC, server, or network), we consider the governance of this part of the IT infrastructure as being decentralized. Otherwise, we consider the governance of this part of the IT infrastructure as being centralized. Our data set contains 1,193 unique business units and

2,279 unit-year observations. All the business units belong to large, multidivisional Fortune 1000 companies and the data is from 2001 to 2005. Using data at the business-unit level has some advantages. Brown (1997) suggests that for large enterprises with different business environments across different business units, factors at the business-unit level, rather than at the enterprise level, provide a better indication of the governance relationship between individual business units and their headquarters.

We complement our data set by using several other data sources. First, we use the Compustat database to measure the environmental uncertainty for each business unit, in terms of the dynamism, munificence, and complexity faced by each business unit (details are provided below). Second, we use the SDC Platinum Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A) database to measure firms’ growth through M&A activities. We incorporate this firm-level control variable since prior literature suggests that firms that grow mainly through acquisitions are more likely to adopt decentralized IT infrastructure governance (e.g., Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999). Finally, we use the Corporate Affiliations database from Lexis-Nexis to derive a measure of corporate structure. We incorporate this firm-level control variable since prior literature suggests that centralized (decentralized) IT infrastructure governance is more likely to be adopted in firms with centralized (decentralized) corporate governance structure (e.g., Brown and Magill 1994).

## 3.2. IT Infrastructure Governance

We use a binary variable ITDec to indicate whether a firm adopts a more centralized or a more decentralized IT infrastructure governance for a given business unit. As a firm may adopt different IT infrastructure governance modes for its different business units, our unit of analysis is a business unit. Also, since there are three types of IT infrastructure decisions recorded for each business unit (i.e., PC, server, and network), if at least two decisions are made by business unit managers, we set ITDec = 1, which means that the firm adopts more decentralized IT infrastructure governance. Otherwise, if at least two decisions are made by the headquarters, we set ITDec = 0, which means that the firm adopts more centralized IT infrastructure governance.<sup>3</sup> Thus, our dependent variable reflects the degree of centralization/decentralization instead of pure centralization or pure decentralization. The value of 1 for the dependent variable indicates that the firm chooses a “more decentralized” mode (i.e., more decision rights rest with the business unit) and the value of 0 indicates that the firm chooses a “more centralized” mode (i.e., more decision rights rest with the corporate headquarters).<sup>4</sup>

Key IT infrastructure decisions can be made by various parties in the organization, e.g., senior executives, business unit managers (or line managers), or IS managers. It is worth noting that IT governance involves decision rights along two dimensions: Vertically—decisions can be decentralized from corporate-level executives to business unit managers; and horizontally, decisions can be made by the IT department or line managers. In this paper, we only consider the vertical dimension. The main reason is that the nature of IT infrastructure investment often requires the business and IT executives to coordinate with each other in making IT infrastructure decisions (Weill et al. 2002). Moreover, the specific separation of responsibilities between the IT and line managers at the corporate and business-unit level is not observable in our data set.

## 3.3. Environmental Uncertainty

We follow the existing literature (e.g., Dess and Beard 1984, Keats and Hitt 1988, Wade and Hulland 2004) and characterize environmental uncertainty in terms of dynamism, munificence, and complexity of the environment. Dynamism refers to the volatility and unpredictability of the changes that a business unit has to deal with (Keats and Hitt 1988). Following Keats and Hitt (1988), we use the volatility of industry sales and the volatility of industry operating income to measure dynamism. Sales volatility is measured using a two-step procedure. First, the natural logarithm of the total sales of four-digit NAICS industries is regressed against an index variable of years, over a period of five years. Then the antilog of the standard error of the regression coefficient is used as the measure for sales volatility. The intuition is that the standard error of the regression coefficient is an estimate of the unpredictability of the sales growth rate. Volatility of industry operating income is derived in a similar way.

Keats and Hitt (1988), we use the growth in industry sales and the growth in industry operating income to measure munificence. Sales growth is measured using a two-step procedure. First, the natural logarithm of the total sales of four-digit NAICS industries is regressed against an index variable of years, over a period of five years. Then the antilog of the regression coefficient is used as the measure for munificence. The intuition is that the regression coefficient is an estimate of the sales growth rate. The growth rate of industry operating income is derived in a similar way.

Following prior literature (e.g., Dess and Beard 1984, Keats and Hitt 1988), we adopt three indicators to measure complexity. The first two measures are well-known measures of market concentration: fourfirm concentration ratio (measured as the ratio of the total sales of the top four firms in an industry to the total industry sales) and the Herfindahl index (e.g., Hitt 1999). In a concentrated industry, a small number of firms dominate the industry, where each firm knows who its competitors are and how they are likely to behave. Thus, a more concentrated industry is less complex. Since a large value of concentration means less complexity, we use the log value of the reciprocal of the four-firm concentration ratio and the log value of the reciprocal of the Herfindahl index as the corresponding measures of complexity (i.e., large values of these measures mean more complexity). The third measure of complexity reflects Grossack’s (1965) dynamic measure of industry concentration. This measure is estimated from a regression of current-year market shares of all firms in a given industry upon their market shares five years ago. The reciprocal of the regression coefficient indicates the decrease or increase of monopoly power in the industry and is used as the third measure of complexity. A large value of this measure (i.e., a small regression coefficient) signifies a loss of market shares and a decrease in monopoly power of large firms due to the growth of smaller firms, the entry of new firms, or some combination of such factors, that makes the environment more complex. On the other hand, a small value of this measure (i.e., a large regression coefficient) signifies a trend toward dominance by fewer firms, making the environment less complex (Keats and Hitt 1988). The above measures of dynamism, munificence, and complexity are reflective measures of three different dimensions of uncertainty. Specifically, the measures used for each dimension are manifestations of the respective first-order construct; they are interchangeable; and they co-vary with each other (see Table 1 for correlations across the measures). For example, the two measures for dynamism—volatility of industry sales and volatility of industry operating income—are highly correlated with each other and are interchangeable in reflecting the degree of unpredictability faced by a business unit. These characteristics meet the conceptual criteria developed by Jarvis et al. (2003) and Petter et al. (2007). Thus, the two measures for dynamism are reflective indicators of dynamism. Similar arguments apply to the measures for the munificence and complexity constructs as well.<sup>5</sup>

Using principle component analysis (PCA), we extract three factors from the measures identified above (see Table 1 for factor loadings). The pattern of factor loadings supports the existence of three dimensions corresponding to dynamism, munificence, and complexity. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supports the overall validity of the three-factor model (Chi-square = 404018; GFI = 0095; CFI = 0081; NFI = 0081). Cronbach Alphas for the three factors are above 0.6 (dynamism = 0062; munificence = 0061; complexity = 0062), suggesting acceptable reliability for exploratory analysis (Dess and Beard 1984). We then convert the multi-item measures of each dimension of uncertainty into single-item measures. Specifically, for each dimension of uncertainty (i.e., dynamism, munificence, and complexity), we take a weighted average of the measurement items (weighted by their loadings in the underlying principle components) as the single-item measure (e.g., Chin et al. 2003).<sup>6</sup>

## 3.4. Moderating Variable

We use the business unrelatedness between the headquarters and the business unit as a moderating variable. Business unrelatedness is measured as the difference between a business unit and its headquarters’ NAICS industry codes. If a business unit is operating in an entirely different industry from the headquarters, the headquarters has much less information and knowledge about the business unit’s local environment; i.e., the headquarters’ information disadvantage is larger. However, if the business unit and the headquarters are in the same industry, the headquarters has the information and knowledge about the business unit’s industry environment and is able to monitor and evaluate the business unit’s decisions. We assign business unrelatedness to zero if the business unit is in the same four-digit NAICS industry as its headquarters (i.e., low business unrelatedness); one if the business unit is in the same two-digit NAICS industry group as its headquarters but the business unit is in a different four-digit industry (i.e., medium business unrelatedness); and we assign business unrelatedness to two if the business unit and its headquarters are in different two-digit NAICS industry groups (i.e., high business unrelatedness).<sup>71</sup> <sup>8</sup>

## 3.5. Control Variables

We also incorporate a set of control variables in our empirical models. First, we use the log values of the firm’s total number of employees and the business unit’s total number of employees as control variables for the firm and business unit size. Second, we use the ratio of IT employees to the total number of employees in the business unit as a control variable for the IT knowledge at the business unit level. Third, using the SDC Platinum Mergers and Acquisitions database, we calculate the average value of mergers and acquisitions for each firm over the past five years. The ratio of average M&A value to the firm’s operating income (obtained from Compustat) is taken as a control variable for the firm’s growth mode. If this measure is large, it indicates that the firm enters new markets mainly through mergers and acquisitions, rather than through internal development (Busija et al. 1997). Fourth, using the Lexis-Nexis Corporate Affiliations database, we calculate the total number of subsidiaries that report directly to the headquarters of each firm. Then we divide this number by the total number of subsidiaries of the firm, and take this ratio as the measure of the headquarters’ control. If this measure is large, it means that a firm’s headquarters has more control over its subsidiaries (Collis et al. 2007) and that the corporate structure is more centralized. The results first show that the coefficients of the main control variables are consistent with the predictions of the existing literature on IT governance. Table 3 (see Model 1) indicates a positive and significant coefficient for Firm Size $( p < 0 . 1 )$ , Site Size $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ IT Knowledge $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 5 ) .$ and Business Unrelatedness $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results indicate that the headquarters in larger firms are more likely to delegate infrastructure decisions to larger business units or to business units with more IT knowledge, or to business units in a different industry from the headquarters. The positive and significant coefficient of Acquisition $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ indicates that firms that grow mainly through acquisitions are more likely to adopt decentralized IT infrastructure governance. The negative and significant coefficient of Corporate Structure $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ indicates that firms with more centralized corporate structures (higher value of variable Corporate Structure) are more likely to adopt centralized IT infrastructure governance. All these findings are consistent with the existing literature and therefore provide support for the validity of the empirical model.

The Curvilinear Relationship Between Uncertainty and IT Infrastructure Governance. Model 2 in Table 3 shows that when we only include the singular terms of the three environmental variables in the model, the coefficients of dynamism and munificence are not significant, and that the coefficient of complexity is negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results indicate that there is no evidence that firms are more likely to decentralize IT infrastructure decisions in more uncertain environments. The negative coefficient on complexity actually indicates otherwise; $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ firms are more likely to centralize IT infrastructure decisions in more complex environments. Model 3 in Table 3 includes the quadratic terms of the uncertainty variables (i.e., dynamism<sup>2</sup>, munificence<sup>2</sup>, and complexity<sup>2</sup>5. The results show that, for dynamism and munificence, the coefficients of the quadratic terms are negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5$ for dynamism and $p < 0 . 0 1$ for munificence) while the coefficients of the singular terms are positive and significant $( p < 0 . 1$ for dynamism and $p < 0 . 0 1$ for munificence). The signs of the coefficients for dynamism and munificence indicate a curvilinear relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance; i.e., decentralization is first increasing and then decreasing with dynamism and munificence. For complexity, the coefficient of the quadratic term is also negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 \bar { 1 } )$ , but the coefficient of the singular term is not significant, indicating a decreasing curvilinear relationship where firms accelerate centralization with increase in complexity. Overall, these results are consistent with Hypothesis 1.

The Moderating Effect of Business Unrelatedness. Model 4 in Table 3 examines the moderating effect of business unrelatedness on the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance. The coefficients of the interactions between business unrelatedness and the singular terms of dynamism and munificence are positive and significant $( p < 0 . 1$ and $p < 0 . 0 5 ,$ , respectively). The coefficients of the interactions between business unrelatedness and the square terms of dynamism and munificence are negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This indicates that when the business unit is in a different industry from the headquarters, the curvilinear relationship between uncertainty and decentralization of IT infrastructure governance is stronger. These results provide support for Hypothesis 2 and suggest that business unrelatedness moderates the relationship between uncertainty and decentralization of IT infrastructure governance. The moderating effect of business unrelatedness on complexity’s relationship with IT infrastructure governance is, however, nonsignificant.

Figure 1 graphically illustrates the curvilinear relationship between uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance and the moderating effect of business unrelatedness. Panel (a) plots how the probability of adopting more decentralized IT infrastructure governance changes with dynamism at high, medium, and low levels of business unrelatedness, when the other variables are at their mean values. At all three levels of business unrelatedness, the probability of decentralization is first increasing and then decreasing in dynamism. For example, consider the case with high businesses unrelatedness. When dynamism increases from 1.6 to 2.575, the probability of decentralization increases at a decreasing rate. This is evidenced by the positive coefficient (0.24) of dynamism and the negative coefficient (−1044) of dynamism × dynamism in Model 3 of Table 3. When dynamism increases from 2.575 to 3.6, the probability of adopting more decentralized IT infrastructure governance decreases at an increasing rate.<sup>11</sup> This implies that when dynamism is below (above) 2.575, the marginal benefit of local responsiveness provided by decentralization dominates (is dominated by) the marginal benefit of control and coordination provided by centralization and therefore, firms tends to decentralize (recentralize) IT infrastructure governance as the environment becomes more dynamic. The moderating effect of business unrelatedness on the curvilinear relationship is illustrated by the different levels of flatness of these three curves. The curve for high business unrelatedness is steeper than the curves for medium and low business unrelatedness, indicating that the curvilinear relationship between dynamism and decentralization of IT infrastructure governance is stronger when the business unit is in a different business from the headquarters.

For complexity, panel (c) of Figure 1 shows weak curvilinear relationships between complexity and the probability of decentralization, for any level of business relatedness. The steepness of the curves in panel (c) is less visually pronounced than the curves in panels (a) and (b). This finding implies that the benefits of control and coordination provided by centralization dominate the benefits of responsiveness provided by decentralization, when firms are in more complex environments. One plausible explanation for this finding is that increase in complexity, which captures increase in competitive pressure, is often accompanied by higher risks of failure and greater resource requirements for the business unit. The higher risks and greater resource requirements demand more control and coordination from corporate headquarters. Thus, the different curvilinear relationship in the case of complexity could be due to the fact that with an increase in competitive pressure, firms might prefer the benefits of control and coordination provided by centralization.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusion

Using a data set of business units, this study presents a curvilinear relationship between environmental uncertainty and decentralization of IT infrastructure governance. The study also finds that this curvilinear relationship is stronger when the business unit and the headquarters are in different industries. The key theoretical implication of this study is that the relationship between environmental uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance is likely to be more complex than that suggested by the prior literature. In particular, this study integrates the local information processing perspective with the control and coordination perspective to present a curvilinear relationship between uncertainty and IT infrastructure governance. The implication for practice is that given the level of environmental uncertainty, managers need to trade off the benefits of local responsiveness provided by decentralization against the benefits of control and coordination provided by centralization as they make IT infrastructure governance decisions.

Further research is needed to generalize the findings of this study to other areas of IT governance. First, the IT governance decision in this study is primarily about hardware/platform. The decisionmaking about other aspects of IT, such as strategic planning, software development, and project management may have different features. For example, the adoption of ERP systems may require centralized governance of selection decisions for software platforms, even though their implementation can be decentralized. Future studies may focus on application development and explore whether environmental factors have similar influences. Second, our empirical approach assesses the hypothesized outcomes of the multifaceted impact of environmental uncertainty in terms of local responsiveness from decentralization, coordination benefits of centralization, and agency costs of decentralization; but we do not observe these intermediary constructs. Although this approach has its merits in the literature (e.g., Wasserman 2006), further research is needed to specifically develop constructs to measure these forces. In this way, future research can further identify the underlying causes of this nonlinearity and shed more light on the multifaceted impact of environmental uncertainty on IT governance.
