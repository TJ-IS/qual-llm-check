## Highlights

-We explore the influences of perceived online community support and member relations on commitment.

-Perceived online community support has different effects on commitment between posters and lurkers. -Trust positively moderates the effect of perceived freedom of expression on commitment among lurkers.

-The norm of reciprocity negatively moderates the effect of perceived freedom of expression on commitment among lurkers.

Abstract: Based on commitment theory and organization support theory, this study explores the effects of perceived online community support on member commitment and how these effects are moderated by members’ social relations in terms of posters and lurkers. The results indicate that perceived online community support had different effects on commitment between posters and lurkers. Trust was found to positively moderate the effect of perceived freedom of expression on commitment among lurkers; on the contrary, the norm of reciprocity was found to negatively moderate the effect of perceived freedom of expression on commitment among lurkers. The theoretical and practical implications are also discussed.

Keywords: perceived community support, trust, reciprocity, commitment, posters, lurkers

## 1. Introduction

With the prevalence of Internet technology currently, social media has changed the means of information sharing and social interaction.<sup>1–3</sup> Social media refers to certain Internet-based applications that build on the Web 2.0 technology and encourage the creation and exchange of user-generated contents $\left( \mathrm { U G C s } \right) . ^ { 4 }$ Examples include online communities, blogs, microblogs (e.g., Twitter), social networking sites (e.g., Facebook), social shopping sites (e.g., Amazon), Wikipedia, and other content-sharing sites (e.g., YouTube). Facilitated by the universal use of social media, online communities have rapidly developed into an important form of human organization.<sup>5</sup> Online communities serve as both communication platforms and social networks where people holding the same interest can share their knowledge and participate in interactive activities with one another to exchange the desired benefits.<sup>6</sup>

Despite the rapid growth of the online community, research has shown that members in online communities have a high rate of turnover<sup>5</sup> and members have much choice to join and quit the community.<sup>7</sup> Data from China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC, 2014) revealed that 33.7% of Internet users registered for more than one online community.<sup>8</sup> Such a characteristic of multiple memberships of Internet users to various communities makes it challenging for any online community to maintain member commitment. Thus, fostering member commitment is essential to sustain an online community.<sup>9</sup> The importance of member commitment to an organization has long been emphasized in the literature.<sup>7,10,11</sup> Member commitment has been found to be associated with members’ active participation behavior. Previous studies have indicated that commitment can lead to online users’ behavior of generating their own content online,<sup>12</sup> and it enhances their intention of continuous knowledge sharing.<sup>13,14</sup>

However, in active and successful communities, most UGCs are contributed by a small number of core members.<sup>15</sup> Most members never or only occasionally contribute UGCs to the community. Understandably, in most of the studies on UGC generation, lurkers, as a majority of online community members, are mostly left out (e.g., Refs. [5,16,17,18]). Nevertheless, in online communities, lurking is also a significant form of member participation behavior.<sup>19</sup> Lurking allows the online content to diffuse to a wide audience. The existence of lurkers does not pose a problem in large and active online communities.<sup>20</sup> As lurkers read the online content created by active users, they are considered indispensable “consumers” of an online community. Lurkers form an integral part of any online community. Although lurkers are the silent majority in an online community, they do have their preferences and judgments, and they may only select the content of their own interest and seek information purposively.<sup>21</sup> However, very little is known about what determines lurkers’ commitment to an online community. More research needs to be conducted to understand the mechanisms behind lurkers’ commitment. Although prior studies suggest that members’ commitment is significant to the online communities, they mainly focus on active members but ignore lurkers.<sup>7,22</sup>

The growing literature contributes to our understanding of members’ commitment in online communities. One stream of research recognizes that community managers should design the online environment so that it encourages contributions from members.<sup>23</sup> Successfully maintaining member activities significantly depends on the instructions and rules instituted in the online community.<sup>24</sup> Another stream highlights the importance of member relations and benefits obtained from social interactions in explaining members’ commitment (e.g., Refs. [7,9]). The commitment theory offers a good understanding of the social nature and structure of an online community, although the online community should demonstrate some unique features, that is, as we argue in this paper, commitment may be “silenced majority” behavior. The organizational support theory concerns traditional organizations, but in the context of online community, organizational support should be understood differently. By combining both theories, the issues in online communities can be well supported in traditional management theories. Moreover, the online community context actually offers scenarios to advance the two theories. There is good indication that commitment in the online community has different characteristics from commitment in the offline world.

However, to the best of our knowledge, the possible joint effects of both perceived community support and member relations have not been examined. In other words, it is not known whether the community support effects were actually different due to different level of member relations and, if so, how these differences actually lead to member’s commitment. The current study intends to bridge the two streams of research on member commitment by considering members’ role (posters vs. lurkers). It aims to examine the influence of perceived online community support and member relations on members’ community commitment from an integrated perspective. More specifically, we distinguish between member roles (posters vs. lurkers) and examine the mechanisms behind their commitment. Thus, we propose the following research questions:

RQ1: What are the influences of perceived online community support and member relations on members’ community commitment?

RQ2: Are the influences of perceived online community support and member relations on members’ community commitment different between posters and lurkers?

Our study distinguishes itself from previous works in two ways. First, while most previous studies on online community member commitment focus on active users, our research aims to develop a more thorough understanding of online community commitment by focusing on commitment of lurkers as a less understood group of online community members compared to posters. Our findings highlight the need to consider not only the posters but also lurkers in studying online community commitment. Second, our study integrated both perceived community support and members’ social relations in understanding member commitment. As perceived community support is often related to members’ relations in online environment, an analysis of the integrated influence of perceived community support and member relations on member commitment could further our understanding of membe commitment in the online context.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Commitment theory

Commitment is a psychological state that characterizes an individual’s relationship with an organization (p. 67).<sup>25</sup> Individuals with commitment to a business or organization are willing to exert considerable effort to benefit the business or organization, to maintain long-term and valuable relationships with the businesses or organizations, and to believe in and accept the organization’s goals and vales.<sup>26</sup> In the organizational context, commitment has been associated with different employee attitudes and behaviors regarding job-related outcomes, such as turnover,<sup>27</sup> job performance,<sup>28</sup> and organizational citizenship behavior.<sup>29</sup>

Although community members are not employees, they have the same discretion in what they need to do, what they want to do, and what they ought to do.<sup>22</sup> Thus, the commitment theory is suitable for understanding members’ behaviors in online communities; moreover, many scholars have found that commitment is also important in online environments. For example, Bateman et al. (2011) investigated why people read threads, post replies, and moderate online discussions from a commitment perspective. Their findings declared that commitment is the critical factor driving continuous online community usage.<sup>22</sup> Chen et al. (2013) also empirically confirmed that member commitment significantly influences member content creation behaviors among members of SNS websites. A review of the online community literature reveals various explanations for online members community commitment, such as perceived social interaction,<sup>7,9</sup> perceived benefits,<sup>30</sup> and perceived social support.<sup>12</sup> Table 1 summarizes some of the studies that have applied commitment in various online contexts. Even though a member can equally acquire benefits from different online communities, their commitment to some online community is higher than others.<sup>20</sup> In this research, we intend to explore these questions by using a theoretical approach that considers both the individual and the community.

## 2.2. Organizational support theory

The organizational support theory holds that employees form a general perception of the extent to which the organization values their contribution and cares about their well-being.<sup>34</sup> Specifically, organizational support can reflect employee perceptions of their organization’s inclination to provide aid when needed and meet their socio-emotional needs. Perceived organizational support of employees will motivate them to reciprocate with affective commitment to the organization.<sup>35</sup> Like traditional organizations, online communities act as a virtual form of organization with their rules and norms. 36 Moreover, members’ voluntary participation in online communities has been considered a typical form of organizational citizenship behavior.<sup>37</sup> Thus, the organizational support theory could be applied to the online community context.<sup>37</sup> In the online community context, perceived community support reflects members’ views on the extent to which the online community facilitates their communication, values their contribution, and cares about them.<sup>38</sup> Members need to determine whether, and to what extent, the online community will facilitate their communication, recognize and reward their effort, and assis them in resolving problems and complaints.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Based on the commitment theory and the organizational support theory, we propose that member commitment can be predicted by perceived community support (i.e., perceived support for member communication, perceived recognition for contribution, and perceived freedom of expression). Specifically, we expect perceived community support to have a positive effect on member commitment. We also explore the mechanisms underlying lurkers’ commitment in comparison with that of posters. Furthermore, we expect that member relations (e.g., trust and norm of reciprocity) moderate the effect of perceived community support on member commitment in terms of posters and lurkers. ## 3.1. Perceived community support and commitment

Perceived community support reflects members’ views on the extent to which the online community facilitates their communication, values their contribution, and cares about them.<sup>38</sup> The current research conceptualizes perceived community support in three dimensions: perceived support for member communication, perceived recognition for contribution, and perceived freedom of expression.<sup>38,39</sup> Perceived support for member communication refers to the extent to which an online community provides its members with the means, ability, and opportunity to communicate.<sup>38</sup> Perceived recognition for contribution refers to the extent to which the online community recognizes and values members’ contribution.<sup>10</sup> Perceived freedom of expression refers to the extent to which a community

Perceived support for member communication is related to a continuous quality improvement system to facilitate member expression and communication. One purpose of community development is to exchange mutual support, which results in an increased sense of belonging and social bonds among their members.<sup>32</sup> This is demonstrated in the following aspects: First, one of the biggest challenges in maintaining an online community is the supply of information,<sup>14</sup> and information provision depends largely on active communication among members.<sup>41</sup> Thus, facilitating member communication is highly valued because members may stop visiting the online community if they cannot receive corresponding responses or effectively communicate with others members. Moreover, if the community actively facilitates members’ communication and makes information easily available to its members, members will maintain a high satisfaction level. Members with high level of satisfaction are more likely to be involved in activities within the community and thus increase their commitment level.<sup>13</sup> Third, active communication and interaction among members can offer the chance for members to closely connect to one another. Intensive interactions among member may affect their evaluation of the service experience,<sup>7</sup> and improved assessment of the service experience fosters members’ sense of belonging in the community and increases their commitment toward the community.<sup>40</sup> Hence, we

propose the following hypothesis:

H1a. Perceived support for member communication has a positive effect on member commitment in an online community.

Online community members can choose to just read the discussion of others, without being obligated to participate in posting UGCs.<sup>42</sup> This commonly occurs in online communities. Many members simply visit the site to read the topics, while others contribute to the community by posting messages. In this study, we label members who actively post messages as “posters,” but we use “lurkers” to refer to members who simply visit the site to get information without active posting behaviors. Although both lurkers and posters would benefit from an online community, posters could be seen as more psychologically connected to the online community than lurkers.<sup>43</sup> Lurkers just participate in online communities mainly to acquire knowledge or enhance personal status.<sup>44</sup> In order to meet their communication needs, posters may interact directly with other members on the message boards, or indirectly interact with others by rating the content.<sup>44,45</sup> Posting is related more strongly to interaction with individuals within the virtual community. Therefore, posters may care more about the communication convenience, which may positively enhance their commitment to the online community. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H1b. Perceived support for member communication has a stronger positive effect on member commitment among posters than among lurkers in an online community.

Perceived recognition for contribution indicates that the online community recognizes and values a member’s contribution.<sup>10</sup> Being recognized and rewarded fairly indicates that an organization cares about the well-being of the employee, resulting in greater affective attachment and feelings to the organization.<sup>27</sup> When employees perceive that their organization values their contributions and sets up formal recognition mechanism for their contributions, they will develop obligations for future reciprocity, which can in turn benefit the organization.<sup>46</sup> These beneficial actions include engaging in organizational citizenship behaviors, making suggestions to improve the organizational effectiveness, and performing an individual’s job tasks better.<sup>47</sup> People would develop positive affection toward individuals or organizations that appropriately offer active feedback and reward for their contribution.<sup>48</sup> Thus, members’ community commitment can be significantly influenced by the rewards for their activities.<sup>49</sup> If the community explicitly expresses that it values and cares for its members’ contribution, members will increase their expectation that their improved performance would be rewarded, which in turn enhances the level of commitment to the community.<sup>30</sup> On this basis, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H2a. Perceived recognition for contribution has a positive effect on member commitment in an online community.

Posters voluntarily spend time and energy to create content and answering questions in online communities. According to the social exchange perspective, members who contribute to the online community will expect to be rewarded in some way important to them,<sup>50</sup> for example, recognition from the online community. If the online community provides proper rewards and gratitude to encourage members for their efforts, posters will gain more satisfaction than lurkers do. Of course, these incentives are usually intangible to lurkers. Posters are more likely to weigh pros and cons when participating in the online community than lurkers are.<sup>20</sup> Therefore, posters may rely more heavily on perceived recognition for contribution in an online community to form commitment.

H2b. Perceived recognition for contribution has a stronger positive effect on member commitment among posters than among lurkers in an online community

Perceived freedom of expression shows the extent to which a community facilitates individuals rights to express diverse opinions.<sup>40</sup> If the community does not embrace negative discussions or opinions, there would be no active participation in community activities, and consequently the level of member commitment to the community will be reduced.<sup>39</sup> Furthermore, members’ community participation is usually goal oriented; as such, social, functional, and amusing experiences are the key factors influencing commitment.<sup>30,51</sup> A community encouraging its member to express diversified viewpoints can assist members to achieve these social, functional, and amusing experiences, thus enhancing the level of member commitment to the community. The online community offers a platform for exchanging both experiential and factual knowledge.<sup>52</sup> If the community provides its members unique opportunities for free presentation, especially when it relates to expressing professional knowledge, it will actually allow the community to function as a shared knowledge platform.<sup>9</sup> The members’ desire to acquire specialized knowledge would be satisfied, thus leading to increased level of commitment to the online community.<sup>44</sup> Hence, we present the following hypothesis:

H3a. Perceived freedom of expression has a positive effect on member commitment in an online community.

With an open and comprehensive community atmosphere, posters are free to create content as a means of self-expression,<sup>53</sup> which influences their commitment to the online community, while lurkers invest time in searching and reading information in the online community and they only consume the content created by posters.<sup>54</sup> Thus, lurkers rarely care for expression freedom, because, unlike the active posters, they are limited to reading what they need and learning something interesting or useful. As a result, lurkers may rely less heavily on perceived freedom of expression in an online community to form commitment. Hence, we present the following hypothesis:

H3b. Perceived freedom of expression has a stronger positive effect on member commitment among posters than among lurkers in an online community.

## 3.2. The role of member relations

## 3.2.1. Trust

Trust is a cornerstone concept in constructing a long-term business relationship and partnership.<sup>55,56</sup> In online communities, trust between members could be recognized as a general attitude toward other members of the online community.<sup>57</sup> It reflects the depth and scope of member relationships and can lead members to repeatedly visit the same community.<sup>58</sup> Online communities generally lack feasible rules, requiring all members to behave in a commonly expected social manner.<sup>59</sup> Thus, trust is viewed as a subjective alternative to govern so that a more friendly relationship environment can be formed in the online community.

In particular, trust is more essential for posters to form commitment through perceived community support in the online community. First, the absence of trust among posters is a major barrier to sustaining online communities<sup>60</sup>; that is, members without mutual trust will be reluctant to devote their time to communicating with others in an online community.<sup>55</sup> Even if the online community has offered an effective communication platform to exchange advanced information and solutions, posters will be reluctant to continue contributing information to the online community if they believe other members are dishonest and not able to keep promises.<sup>61</sup> Second, posters’ community experience will be compromised without a trustworthy environment even though the online community provides the support needed for its members to share their knowledge. When posters trust each other in an online community, they are more likely to become involved in the community.<sup>62</sup> Third, there is little assurance of recognition and rewards from the online community that can reach everyone and there exists friendship among members. Trust in members will make posters feel obligated to contribute to the online community, thus earning a reputation among other members.<sup>13</sup> Thus, the community can effectively facilitate knowledge creation and knowledge sharing. So, it is expected that with higher levels of trust among members, the effect of perceived community support on commitment will be heightened, especially for posters. Hence, we present the following hypotheses:

H4. Trust has a stronger positive moderating effect between perceived community support (perceived support on member communication (H4a), perceived recognition for contribution (H4b), and perceived freedom of expression (H4c)) and commitment among posters than among lurkers.

## 3.2.2. Norm of reciprocity

The norm of reciprocity is a sense of mutual indebtedness and individuals usually reciprocate the benefits they receive from others, ensuring ongoing supportive exchanges.<sup>63</sup> An individual would be indebted to others because of what others have done in previous interactions.<sup>64</sup> Obligations of reciprocity include giving, receiving, and repaying gifts. Particularly, the norm of reciprocity is more essential for posters to form commitment through perceived community support in the online community. First, sharing information in an online community can be regarded as reciprocity among members by giving, receiving, and repaying more useful knowledge.<sup>56</sup> With a high level of reciprocity, posters with a strong sense of perceived community support will justify their time and effort spent in information and knowledge sharing. Moreover, they will be more willing to exchange information and knowledge with other members in the online community, if they are sure to earn a reputation and equivalent rewards in future participation. All of these factors lead to high levels of community commitment.<sup>61</sup> Second, online members’ information-giving behavior can also nurture and increase self-esteem, status, and respect for posters.<sup>55</sup> In turn, with an increased sense of perceived community support, posters will repay the information “gift” by their own sharing behavior in the future and increased community commitment.<sup>65</sup> Hence, we present the following hypotheses:

H5. The norm of reciprocity has a stronger positive moderating effect between perceived community support (perceived support for member communication (H5a), perceived recognition for contribution (H5b), and perceived freedom of expression (H5c)) and commitment among posters than among lurkers.

## 3.3. Control variables

In addition to perceived community support and member relations, we posit that members commitment depends on their gender, age, duration, and education levels. ## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research site

The data were collected from members of a typical online community in Mainland China, pinggu.org, one of the largest education websites in China. Founded in May 2004, pinggu.org had almost seven million members by the end of 2015. It provides a vast number of forums, blogs, and other member communication platforms supporting knowledge exchange. The objective of pinggu.org is to provide an environment in which people who are proficient or interested in economics, management, finance, and statistics can exchange their knowledge or seek help. The community provides demographic information about its members and each member’s posting history.

## 4.3. Data collection

Online interviews via instant messaging with four members and one manager were conducted to

#

modify and clarify the questions in the questionnaire. As a result, several items were reworded and re-sequenced. Furthermore, a two-stage online pilot test was conducted to assess the reliability of measurements. The first online pilot test with a small sample of 30 respondents through the website of SurveyZ.com showed that constructs with both positive and negative statements had very low reliability. This prompted us to reverse all negative statements to positive expressions. The second pilot test with another small sample of 30 responses demonstrated accepted reliabilities for the measures.

Two rounds of data collection were implemented in 2008 and 2014, respectively, using the same survey instrument. From November 13 to 30, 2008, the first survey was conducted. The second round of data collection was conducted from February 4 to 24, 2014. An invitation letter with the link to the online questionnaire was posted as a top thread in most popular forums in Pinggu.org. In total, 520 respondents completed the survey in 2008 and 349 respondents in 2014. However, some of the respondents did not report their username, so we cannot retrieve their posting behavior information. For such cases, without additional user posting behavior information retrieved through the username, we cannot ascertain whether or not they were posters. As the poster/lurker classification is a significant variable tested in this study, we have to exclude cases that had no username available and thus could not be labeled as poster/lurker. Consequently, after excluding cases with excessive missing values, matching the respondents with their online posting data, the final sample comprised 119 and 244 usable responses in 2008 and 2014, respectively. We compared the mean values of each construct for the 2008 sample and the 2014 sample. We found no significant differences between the two groups (see Table 3). The 2008 and 2014 samples were then merged. To create the poster and lurker groups, the cases were rearranged in descending order by the respondent’s number of postings. Then the top one-third of the cases were classified as posters, and the bottom one-third of cases were labeled as lurkers, leaving the middle one-third of cases out as they were less distinctive in the groupings. This resulted in 112 cases in the “posters” group (average number of postings per person is 42) and 114 cases in the “lurkers” group (average number of postings per person is 0). The detailed descriptive statistics of the respondents’ characteristics are shown in Table 4. Among the respondents, 58.85% were males and 41.15% were females. In terms of age, most respondents were 20–30 years old (74.78%). Furthermore, most respondents had a postgraduate degree (41.59%), followed by a bachelor’s degree (40.71%). Moreover, most respondents belonged to the online community for >1 year (87.17%). The nonresponse bias was assessed by comparing early and late respondents.<sup>67</sup> No significant differences between the first third and last third of all respondents were found on the variables in the research model. Accordingly, the nonresponse bias was not a problem in this study.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Common-method bias

This study used three steps to eliminate the common-method bias. First, as suggested by Kuo and Feng (2013), the respondents were informed that there was no criterion of right or wrong answers and they could answer as truthfully as possible. Moreover, respondents were also informed of the anonymity and privacy of this study.<sup>30</sup> Second, Harman’s one-factor test was conducted to test common-method variance.<sup>68</sup> All items were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis to determine whether a single factor emerges or a single factor accounts for the majority of the variance. In our test, the first component with the largest eigenvalue explains 39.21% of variance, which does not exceed 50% of all variance. Third, following Williams et al. (2003)<sup>69</sup> and Podsakoff et al. (2003),<sup>68</sup> in the PLS model, a common-method variable was generated and linked to all constructs’ indicators. Then we calculated each indicator’s variances substantively explained by the construct and by the method. The results show that the average substantively explained variance of the indicators is 0.57, whereas the average method-based variance is 0.001. The substantive variances are greater than their method variances. In addition, most method factor loadings are not significant. The results indicate that the common-method bias is not an issue in this study.

## 4.2. Measurement models

The ordinary least squares (OLS) approach was used with SPSS 19.0 to analyze the measurement model. The measurement reliability and convergent and discriminate validity were assessed according to the guidelines proposed by Fornell and Larcker (1981).<sup>70</sup> The internal consistency reliability was examined by Cronbach's alpha. As shown in Table 5, the Cronbach’s alpha values of the measurement ranged from 0.76 for perceived freedom of expression to 0.87 for commitment, which were above the acceptable threshold of 0.70. To satisfy convergent validity, the factor loadings of all indicators should exceed 0.70, the composite reliability (CR) should be no less than 0.70, and the average variance extracted (AVE) should be no less than 0.50. In this research, all items had loadings >0.70 except for TR1. The AVE scores were within the range 0.54–0.71, and the CR ranged from 0.76 to 0.88. Thus, all criteria for convergent validity were met. To meet the requirement of discriminant validity, the square root of AVE should exceed that construct’s correlation with other constructs. Table 6 shows that the maximum correlation of any pair of constructs was 0.66, while the minimum square root of AVE was 0.73. The square root of AVE exceeded correlations among the constructs. Thus, discriminant validity was demonstrated among the measurements of the constructs.

5. Discussion and implications  
5.1. Discussion of findings

#

This study examined the effects of perceived community support on online community members commitment, and whether and how trust and norm of reciprocity moderate such effects between lurkers and posters. The results indicated that perceived community support dimensions had different significant effects on members’ community commitment. Perceived support for member communication had a positive effect on member commitment, which did not seem to be statistically different between posters and lurkers. These findings suggest that online community members, regardless of whether they post online messages or not, considered perceived support for member communication as being equally important in influencing their commitment to the community. However, both perceived recognition for contribution and perceived freedom of expression had a stronger positive effect on the commitment of lurkers than that of posters. This is an interesting finding and contrary to the research hypotheses (H2b and H3b). Above all, it indicates that lurkers should not be regarded as passive members in an online community. They are still actively evaluating the perceived community support despite being “silent”; their perceptions of the community support in terms of perceived recognition for contribution and perceived freedom of expression contribute more to their commitment to the community. As lurkers read the online content created by active users, they are considered indispensable “consumers” of an online community. Lurkers are a key part of an online community. If the online community offered a helpful environment to facilitate posters to create information, lurkers can acquire the useful information they search for more conveniently. Thus, lurkers also seek recognition for contribution and freedom of expression.

This study also found that trust positively moderated the effect of perceived freedom of expression and commitment with lurkers. Such findings suggested that the more lurkers trust other community members, the larger the effect of perceived freedom of expression in this community on their commitment. In a trustful environment, the trust among community members perceived by lurkers will greatly magnify the effect of perceived freedom of expression on the lurkers’ commitment to the community. Unexpectedly, the results further reveal that the norm of reciprocity negatively moderated the effect of perceived freedom of expression and commitment with lurkers. Such findings indicate that when lurkers perceive a high norm of reciprocity, the effect of perceived freedom of expression on commitment will be reduced. Lurkers actively pay attention to topics of interest to them and will search for relevant content. If lurkers have little confidence in the community support mechanisms to set appropriate rules for encouraging content creation, they need to be additionally reassured of finding useful information they really need in the online community. Previous studies have shown that satisfying personal needs and information needs is the main purpose of lurking.<sup>71</sup> Lurkers do have their preferences and judgments, and they may only select the content of their own interest and seek information purposively. Thus, lurkers may consider recognition of contribution more important as an online community function, which will meet their need of lurking. As a result, lurkers may depend more heavily on the norm of reciprocity in an online community. This suggests that lurkers may value norm of reciprocity more than the perceived freedom of expression in their involvement in the online community, as they do not tend to express themselves in the community other than observing others. When the norm of reciprocity is present in an online community, the effect of perceived freedom of expression on lurkers’ commitment is suppressed; therefore, this suggests that the norm of reciprocity may replace perceived freedom of expression in their interactional effect on lurkers’ commitment to the online community.

## 5.2. Theoretical and practical implications

This study furthers our understanding of the effect of perceived community support on commitment. First, most previous studies on the effect of organization support on commitment have been conducted in the offline context. Studies on the applicability of this effect in online communities are limited. This study extends the applicability of the organizational support theory to the context of online communities. The explanatory power and support of our model indicate that the organizational support theory suitably explains member commitment in online communities. Overall, our study offers a framework for how perceived community support shapes member commitment in online communities.

Second, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the differences between lurkers and posters in terms of the effect of perceived community support on commitment. Perceived recognition for contribution and perceived freedom of expression are more important for persuading a lurker to commit to an online community compared to perceived support for member communication. This extends our knowledge by identifying that members also felt a sense of community while lurking. Lurkers also care about their experience while visiting the online community.

Third, this study contributes to the literature by proposing trust and norm of reciprocity as moderators between perceived community support and commitment. Our study identifies the conditions and mechanisms by which perceived support for member communication, perceived recognition for contribution, and perceived freedom of expression affect member commitment. Specifically, our findings suggest that perceived community support may influence members commitment through members’ psychological feeling and condition, that is, trust and norm of reciprocity. This extends our knowledge on how members are driven to commit to online communities.

Practically, with the increasing competition of online communities, managers of online communities must update their knowledge of maintaining and developing member commitment. Our findings suggest that managers of online communities recognize the importance of perceived community support in ensuring members’ commitment. First, we suggest a design strategy that improves member communication, recognizes member contribution, and encourages diverse expression Functions such as bulletin boards, chat rooms, and mailing service would create a higher level of commitment to members. An interesting finding of this study is that perceived support for member communication has a positive effect on lurkers’ commitment as well. Offering features that facilitate knowledge creation of posters is somewhat common; however, little design effort has been made to enhance lurkers’ experience in online communities. This finding suggests that lurkers’ commitment can be increased if the online community enhances information-seeking features. Online community managers and facilitators should consider creating a better means of recognizing members’ contribution and perceived freedom of expression in the community to sustain the membership.

Moreover, trust in other members in the online community may help motivate lurkers commitment. Online managers should aim to make online communities more trustful. Online community managers can also build real-name authentication systems that allow members to be better acquainted with others in the community. Integrating the real-name authentication system with rating features can also help increase information credibility, which helps increase members’ level of trust

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This research has its own limitations. The first limitation is the mechanisms of lurker’s commitment, that is, why lurkers care more about the recognition for contribution of the community. As a follow-up study, it would be worth exploring how lurkers form and develop commitment in the context of online communities. Further research can take mediation variables into account when considering the mechanisms between recognition for contribution and lurkers’ commitment. Moreover, our sample was composed of self-selected respondents. We only have data on members who chose to reply to our invitation letter and complete the survey. Thus, the sample may not be representative of all community members. Future research should use objective measures and collect data from multiple sources as a research design. Third, our data were collected from members of Pinggu.com. These members may be different from those who use other online communities, as Pinggu.com is a professional knowledge-sharing platform. Thus, further research can consider collecting data from multiple types of online communities.
