# Real Options Models for Proactive Uncertainty-Reducing Mitigations and Applications in Cybersecurity Investment Decision Making

Michel Benaroch<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Martin J. Whitman School of Management, Syracuse University, Syracuse, New York 13210 Contact: mbenaroc@syr.edu (MB)

Received: March 2, 2015 Revised: December 23, 2015; June 1, 2016; December 4, 2016 Accepted: January 19, 2017 Published Online in Articles in Advance: February 22, 2018

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2017.0714

Copyright: © 2018 INFORMS

Abstract. Managerial flexibility, or real options, embedded in information technology (IT) investments allows resolving uncertainty not only by passively waiting for new information to arrive during deferral but also by proactively deploying mitigations. Classic real options models fail to account for the value of proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations, since they assume that uncertainty is fixed or follows a continuous, time-dependent dynamics. We present adaptations of these models that address this shortcoming. In our models, zero or more mitigations can be applied in varying sequences, mitigations have impulse-type efects on uncertainty reduction, and mitigations’ efects can be complementary, substitutive, or synergetic. These traits make the value of mitigations path dependent and conditional on the uncertainty-reduction ability of earlier deployed mitigations. We operationalize the efects of mitigations in the IT and cybersecurity investment contexts. We also apply the adapted models to a real-world cybersecurity investment case from a Japanese company. Investments in multiple cybersecurity mitigations are typically treated as having a multiplicative efect that leads to overinvestment in mitigations. Our models avoid this problem, permitting to lower cybersecurity costs without compromising on loss prevention. More generally, our models allow implementing the real options logic more fully by supporting both passive and proactive IT investment risk management.

## 1. Introduction

Real options analysis has become an important methodology in the information technology (IT) literature. It allows to conceptualize and value the flexibility embedded in uncertain investments as real options. A real option is said to provide managers with the right, not obligation, to change the course of an investment as uncertainty unfolds (Trigeorgis 1996). Broadly speaking, real options analysis has been used to manage IT investment risk (Benaroch 2002, Fichman et al. 2005, Kumar 2002). It has been shown to improve IT investment decision making when there is flexibility to, for example, time the investment launch (Benaroch and Kaufman 1999, 2000) and backsource an outsourced IT service (Benaroch et al. 2010).

An important gap in the IT literature on real options is the lack of treatment of proactive uncertainty-reducing actions facilitated by managerial flexibility. The classic view is that “the value of real options lies in the enhanced ability of the firm to cope with exogenous uncertainty” (Kulatilaka 1995, p. 91), for example, uncertainty about market-set commodity prices in the case of oil exploration (Smith and McCardle 1998) and mining natural resources (Brennan and Schwartz 1985). Under this view, (1) uncertainty is fixed since the firm is presumed to have no control over underlying risk factors; (2) deferral can resolve uncertainty passively using information that arrives with the passage of time; and (3) the value of deferral derives from the ability to avoid loss that could occur if the firm commits early to a poor irreversible investment. For example, when there is flexibility to defer an investment that costs I and has an uncertain payof P, time could bring information indicating whether P is not likely to exceed I, in which case the firm would avoid investing and losing amount P <sup>−</sup> I. Based on this “wait and see” logic, flexibility allows to truncate the bad tail of the distribution of investment outcome. However, IT research traces uncertainty in IT investments to endogenous and exogenous risk factors that might be under the firm’s control, arguing for the “act and see” logic according to which flexibility also permits to take actions—mitigations— for proactively resolving uncertainty (Benaroch 2002, Tiwana et al. 2006, Benaroch et al. 2007). This logic has been used in the context of “active learning” in the study of Otim and Grover (2012, p. 261):

The efect of the resolution of uncertainty on real options exercise decisions with respect to three e-commerce investment options: scale-up, stage and joint investment, and the relationship between exercise of these options and firm performance. The results . . . show that resolution of external (exogenous) [e.g., demand for a new e-service, maturity of e-technology] and internal (endogenous) [e.g., ability to develop in-house vs. jointly with a third-party] uncertainty has a significant efect on option exercise decisions . . . simply waiting without investment in active learning does not create significant value from real options.” (Content in brackets and italics added.)

Another example is flexibility to prototype, where making a small-scale investment permits conducting feasibility studies and technical performance tests to resolve endogenous uncertainty about the firm’s technical ability to complete the full-scale investment (Benaroch and Goldstein 2009). Empirical evidence indicates that IT practitioners manage project investment risk by thinking exactly in terms of proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations (Benaroch et al. 2006a). With that said, however, the IT literature ofers no guidance or models that account for the impact of uncertainty-reducing mitigations on investment value.

Our goal is to introduce into the IT literature financedeveloped adaptations of classic real options models for evaluating optional uncertainty-reducing mitigations that are deployed sequentially at discrete time points. The models we present are close to compound options models for addressing multistaged investment but difer in three important ways on which we elaborate in Section 2.2. The first diference is in the number of exercised options. In compound options models, completing a stage is akin to exercising an option that creates nested growth options on the remaining stages, and undertaking an investment requires all stages (options) to be completed (exercised) sequentially. By contrast, our models treat mitigations as optional—zero or more of them need to be applied (exercised) before undertaking an investment. Another diference is in the impact of options on uncertainty. In compound options models, exercised options do not change distributional parameters of the stochastic outcome variable (Geske 1979, Cassimon et al. 2004) or change them in a continuous and time-dependent manner (Agliardi and Agliardi 2005, Lee and Paxton 2001). In our models, mitigations produce discrete, time-independent shocks to the outcome variable, consistent with the notion that the moment mitigations are applied they have an information-revelation efect on sources of uncertainty under the firm’s control (instead of an over time efect on uncontrollable sources of uncertainty). A third diference is on path dependency. In compound options models, stages (options) are completed (exercised) in a fixed sequence, so the impact of each stage on uncertainty is always the same. In our models, allowing for zero or more mitigations (options) to be exercised permits mitigations to have substitutive, complementary, and synergetic relationships. In other words, our models account for interacting mitigations whose cost and impact on uncertainty depend on the specific sequence of exercised mitigations.

One area that can benefit from the models we present is cybersecurity investment decision making. There is a growing concern over ineficiency of cybersecurity investments (Zhuo and Solak 2015, Srinidhi et al. 2015, Gilligan 2013, Tadjdeh 2015), particularly when multiple overlapping mitigations (countermeasures) are layered to make it harder for attacks to penetrate (Bayuk and Mostashari 2013, Ahlström 2015, Schilling and Werners 2016). New cybersecurity investments are typ ically evaluated in isolation of investments already in place (Carty et al. 2012). Yet, incremental investments are known to have diminishing (or no) benefits beyond a certain point (Gordon and Loeb 2002, Gilligan 2013). In this respect, two weaknesses characterize extant cyber security investment models for selecting a parsimonious and superior subset of overlapping mitigations. First, they rely only on the expected value, rather than probability distribution, of the outcome variable. Second, and more importantly, they overlook conditionality by assuming that mitigations are independent and have a multiplicative preventative efect. This is evident from a review of articles published between 2002 and 2011 (Demetz and Bachlechner 2013) and from more recent models (e.g., Sawik 2013, Tsalis et al. 2013, Yevseyeva et al. 2015, Sarala et al. 2015, Shahpasand et al. 2015, Paulson et al. 2016). Cybersecurity experts link the latter weakness to overinvestment in cybersecurity (Carty et al. 2012, Gilligan 2013, Ahlström 2015). Some experts add that “An active learning approach is required to make prioritized cyberprotection strategies and tactics focused” (Perakslis 2014, p. 396). Using the real options models we present, the cybersecurity investment problem is reframed as one of selecting a subset of uncertainty-reducing mitigations that may have substitutive, complementary, or synergetic relationships. As real options models explicitly account for the distribution of the investment outcome variable, mitigations can be said to favorably alter the distribution of this variable. If the outcome variable of interest is loss expectancy, the product of attacks frequency and loss per attack, mitigations can lower uncertainty about the rate of successfully blocking attacks (e.g., adding authentication) and/or about their loss magnitude (e.g., frequent backups control the cost of recovering from major attacks). We know of two studies that apply real options models to actual cybersecu rity investments (Khansa and Liginlal 2009, Herath and

Herath 2008) but neither handles conditionality among mitigations. Our models address this gap by balancing mitigations’ costs against their incremental (diminishing) uncertainty reduction impacts on cybersecurity loss expectancy. This trait can improve the eficiency of cybersecurity investments. These investments’ costs are real and show on the books while their loss-prevention benefits do not (Kwon and Johnson 2014).

The adapted real options models we present are based on the work of Koussis et al. (2007). We conceptualize the impact of three categories of mitigations useful for (1) learning about the nature of uncertainty, (2) reducing uncertainty by taming its sources, and (3) coping with uncertainty by reducing its negative consequences. We then formalize this notion of impact within the real options logic. Subsequently we present adaptations of classic models for simple call options (single mitigation), compound options (successive mitigations), and asset-for-asset exchange options (concurrent mitigations).

We ofer an applied perspective throughout our exposition by focusing on cybersecurity investment decision making. We operationalize the notion of uncertainty-reducing mitigations using real-world cybersecurity data from the IT literature (Wang et al. 2008, Herath and Herath 2008). We also demonstrate relevance to IT practice (Ghosh and Li 2013) by providing a real-world cybersecurity investment case from a corporate member of Carnegie Mellon University’s INI/CyLab Japan. The case illustrates the application of the adapted real options models along with the estimation of their parameters, including volatility of the underlying asset and impacts of mitigations on volatility. The case also elucidates the models’ benefits in practice as well as highlights potential methodological challenges relating to the interpretation of mitigations’ impact parameters.

This paper makes novel contributions to the IT and cybersecurity investment decision-making literatures. The real options models we present address a class of investment problems not covered to date in the IT literature. In efect, the models enable IT decision makers to implement the logic of real options more fully. They account for embedded flexibility in terms of both the value of passive deferral and the value of proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations applied during deferral. Moreover, our conceptualization of the impact of proactive mitigations on uncertainty fits well with the notion that risk management narrows down the cone of uncertainty as a project investment progresses (Browning et al. 2002). This should help IT practitioners to connect better with the intuition of real options models. In this sense, our work also helps to address a criticism that real options models are not transparent about how the value of options comes about. Along these lines, we also help researchers and practitioners to develop trust in and comfort with the models by providing spreadsheet tools we have used to implement the adapted models and apply them to a real-world cybersecurity investment case. Our contribution to the cybersecurity investment literature is in being the first to operationalize options models for sequential mitigations with conditionality as well as apply them to real-world cybersecurity investments. From a practical standpoint, our models facilitate lowering cybersecurity costs without compromising on loss-prevention potential.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 frames IT investment risk management in relation to uncertaintyreducing mitigations, real options models, and cybersecurity investments. Section 3 adapts classic real options models to the case of one and two mitigations. Section 4 operationalizes and illustrates the concept of cybersecurity mitigations. Section 5 applies the adapted models we present to a real-world cybersecurity investment case. Section 6 provides sensitivity analysis insights into how mitigations’ value varies for model parameters. Section 7 ofers concluding remarks and directions for future research. Appendix A presents and illustrates a lattice model for evaluating more than two sequential mitigations.

## 2. IT Investment Risk Management

This section conceptualizes the IT investment riskmanagement problem. It contrasts the notion of “passive” and “active” risk management, and reviews the types of mitigations deployed in connection with active risk management. It also discusses a respective gap in extant IT real options models and their application to cybersecurity investment problems.

## 2.1. Problem Conceptualization

Let V <sup></sup> P <sup>−</sup> I be the net value of an investment, where P and I are the random investment payof and cost, respectively. In financial economics, the uncertainty surrounding a variable represents the possibility that the variable will deviate from its expected value (Elton and Gruber 1995). For example, take the case of a cybersecurity investment in intrusion detection systems (IDS). If personnel are inexperienced in IDS configuration, the rate of successfully detecting attacks could deviate up or down from expectations. This creates uncertainty over the investment payof, P, or the amount of cybersecurity loss that could be avoided by making the IDS investment.

Managing IT investment risk adds value by lowering uncertainty and/or its negative value consequences. Suppose that an investment made immediately with no risk management has a stochastic value V that is normally distributed (see Figure 1). Under passive risk management, where an investment is deferred until time T, uncertainty can be resolved by new information arriving with the passage of time. This adds value by allowing the firm to avoid a poor investment decision in case new information suggests that V is likely to be negative since payof P is smaller than cost I. This is akin to truncating part of the left (bad) tail of $V ^ { \prime } { \sf s }$ distribution at time T (see Figure 1). The passive approach is consistent with the classical decision theory view (Arrow 1965), which assumes that all alternatives are given (fixed) and their features (probabilities) cannot be changed to afect risk. The decision maker simply picks the best alternative. Under active risk management, managers can also take actions at any time $t , 0 \leq t < T _ { \cdot }$ , to proactively resolve uncertainty. This can favorably alter the distribution of V at time t by contracting the bad tail and/or expanding the good tail of the distribution (see Figure 1). This approach is consistent with the behavioral view of risk, where risk is brought under control by taking specific actions, for example, negotiating uncertainty-absorbing outsourcing contracts (March and Shapira 1987) and lobbying for a change in regulations constraining investment payof P (Benaroch and Kaufman 2000).

Uncertainty-reducing actions—or mitigations—generally help to learn about uncertainty, to prevent or control uncertainty, and to cope with consequences of uncertainty. Learning yields new information for resolving uncertainty. It could be passive, where new information arrives with the passage of time, or proactive, via deployment of mitigations. For example, in cybersecurity investments, passive learning could occur if newly published expert reviews shed light on an emerging security technology or solution, whereas proactive learning could occur through sampling and postaudit Bayesian analysis of log data (Bidgoli 2006). Prevention reduces uncertainty by proactively controlling its root causes. For instance, in cybersecurity investments, adding a layer of authentication can lower uncertainty over the rate of blocking attacks from succeeding. Coping limits the expected damage of risk events once they occur. To illustrate, uncertainty over the magnitude of loss from successful cyberattacks can be lowered by adding a reactive IDS that shuts down compromised hosts/services, restores tampered files, and changes the attack’s content, among other things (Stakhanova et al. 2007).

One can visualize the impact of sequentially deployed mitigations on uncertainty, given that each mitigation favorably alters the distribution of investment value V. The variance of V becomes increasingly smaller, because of the reduction in uncertainty, and the mean of V can become larger because good outcomes may be enhanced. This impact conforms to the traditional intuition that risk management is associated with a cone of uncertainty that gets progressively narrower as more uncertainty is being resolved (Browning et al. 2002).

## 2.2. The Real Options Perspective

The real options literature makes explicit connections with the above risk-management concepts. Consistent with Figure 1, managerial flexibility aforded by diferent real options was shown to truncate parts of the negative tail of the distribution of investment value (e.g., deferral, piloting, and abandonment) or to enhance the positive tail of the distribution (e.g., expansion and scale-up) (Benaroch 2002; Kumar 2002; Fichman et al. 2005; Tiwana et al. 2006; Benaroch et al. 2006a, 2007).

Real options models conceptualize the uncertain payof of an investment as a stochastic state variable P that moves randomly inside a “cone of uncertainty” (see Figure 2). The many possible trajectories of P inside the cone are distributed such that their majority has endpoints around $P ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ mean plus or minus two standard deviations. Classical real options models take the cone of uncertainty to be fixed, akin to passive risk management. These models value flexibility, or real options (defer, pilot, prototype, etc.), embedded in an investment in terms of information that could arrive with the passage of time. For example, deferring a decision to invest cost I allows the passage of time to bring information about the trajectory of payof $P ,$ and if the information suggests that P is likely to be too small to recover cost $I ,$ one would avoid investing and prevent a loss of amount P <sup>−</sup> I. This logic extends to flexibilities to prototype or pilot an investment: making a small upfront investment I $( I ^ { \prime } < I )$ allows the passage of time to bring information on the likelihood of a poor investment outcome, based on which one would decide whether to commit the full investment cost I. In all these cases, flexibility allows to only truncate part of the bad tail of $P ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ distribution without changing its mean or variance (Figure 2).

The IT literature does link, but only conceptually, the value of some forms of flexibility to proactive mitigations they facilitate. For example, the flexibility to prototype allows resolving uncertainty not only based on information arriving with the passage of time but also based on information produced proactively by conducting feasibility studies and technical performance tests (Benaroch 2002, Tiwana et al. 2006, Benaroch et al. 2007). Proactive uncertainty-reducing actions bring information that adds value beyond the value of plain investment deferral. An empirical validation of the options-based risk-management (OBRiM) framework finds that, consistent with active risk management and the behavioral view of risk, many of the mitigations that IT practitioners build into plans for managing IT project risk reduce uncertainty proactively rather than through deferral alone (Benaroch et al. 2006a). Consider examples involving uncertain investment payof $P ,$ the option’s underlying asset, which is not observable like stock prices and must be estimated. In one example, there is exogenous uncertainty over $P$ because of uncertain customer demand and endogenous uncertainty about estimates of P because of reliance on subjective and incomplete information. Managers could acquire information for improving $P ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ estimates by conducting a statistical analysis of investment benefits or by restructuring the investment so partial benefits accrue sooner (Benaroch et al. 2006a). In another example, there is exogenous uncertainty over P because of an uncertain customer adoption rate of point-of-sale debit card services and because of banking regulations that suppress customer adoption in two out of four New England states. Managers could lobby for a regulatory change to lower $P ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ volatility (uncertainty) by up to 50%, without efecting the inherent customer adoption rate (Benaroch and Kaufman 2000). As these examples suggest, applied mitigations reveal new information at the particular time point they are applied, and they are expected to result in a momentary random impact on controllable sources of uncertainty, rather than in a continuous (over time) impact on inherently uncontrollable sources of uncertainty. Technically speaking, mitigations can be said to result in one-time “impulses” or shocks to the state variable, causing the cone of uncertainty to shift up and/or narrow down as seen in Figure 3(b) and 3(c).

Some of the IT literature associates risk management with a proactive deployment of uncertainty-reducing mitigations (Benaroch 2002, Benaroch et al. 2007), however, the classical real options models this literature uses cannot account for such mitigations. This is most apparent in models for evaluating multistage investments as sequential compound options. In compound options models, exercising an option to undertake a multistage investment opportunity requires all stages (embedded options) to be completed (exercised). By contrast, mitigations are optional in that an investment opportunity can be exercised even if none or just a few of them are applied. Moreover, classic compound

These models make conditionality sensitive only to the part of the bad tail of the distribution truncated by exercised options (Benaroch et al. 2006b, 2007). More advanced compound options models assume that completed investment stages resolve uncertainty and, hence, allow distributional parameters of the state variable to evolve continuously, in a time-dependent manner (Agliardi and Agliardi 2005, Lee and Paxton 2001). These models handle conditionality more fully by accounting for changes in the state variable’s distribution. However, time-dependent changes mean that the cone of uncertainty evolves over time, contrary to the momentary impulse-type impact that mitigations are expected to have on the cone of uncertainty. Finally, compound options models cannot account for path-dependent impacts of mitigations on uncertainty. They require that investment stages (options) be completed (exercised) in a fixed sequence, with the impact of each stage on uncertainty reduction being fixed as well. However, mitigations could have substitutive, complementary, and synergetic impacts on uncertainty reduction. When diferent sequences of (zero or more) mitigations can be exercised, mitigations’ costs and impacts on uncertainty reduction could vary with the specific sequence of mitigations being applied (e.g., mitigation j could have a higher/lower impact on uncertainty reduction if applied after mitigation i than if applied alone).

Koussis et al. (2007) ofer finance-developed adaptations of classic real options models that account for proactive actions facilitated by embedded managerial flexibility. Under these models, (1) mitigations have discrete impulse-type impacts on controllable uncertainty sources; (2) zero or more mitigations could be applied in varying sequences; and, (3) mitigations interact such that their costs and impacts on uncertainty are path dependent. The model adaptations of Koussis et al. (2007) are more general than similar finance-developed models (Childs et al. 2002, Pindyck 1993, Huchzermeier and Loch 2001, Martzoukos 2009).<sup>1</sup> However, these and other finance-developed models are not targeted at or operationalized for IT investments (Benaroch and Kaufman 1999). It is the goal of the next sections to present these models as well as operationalize and apply them in the cybersecurity investment context.

## 2.3. Application to Cybersecurity Investments

Cybersecurity investment decision making is one area that could benefit from the real options models we present. “How much should a firm in the private sector invest in cybersecurity activities?” remains a challenging question (Gordon et al. 2015a, p. 3), especially in light of growing concerns over ineficiency of cybersecurity investments (Zhuo and Solak 2015, Srinidhi et al. 2015, Gilligan 2013) and tightening budgets (Brown 2016, Fielder et al. 2016, Libicki et al. 2015).<sup>2</sup> An IBMfunded field study observes that we lack an understanding of how firms prioritize their limited cybersecurity budgets (Moore et al. 2015). A RAND report, commissioned by Juniper Networks, adds that firms are investing more in cybersecurity but many are not optimizing their investment (Tadjdeh 2015). An Intel white paper attributes this concern to (Carty et al. 2012, p. 2):

A tendency to examine the value of new investments in isolation . . . . Considered in isolation, a new investment may appear attractive because it addresses a very large threat . . . . To realistically analyze the value that the investment will deliver within our environment, we need to assess the incremental value that it will add to our existing controls.

Examining the value of investments in isolation is more problematic under the defense-in-depth strategy, according to which one or more overlapping cybersecurity mitigations are layered on an attack path so as to make it harder to penetrate (Bayuk and Mostashari 2013, Ahlström 2015, Schilling and Werners 2016).

Two weaknesses characterize decision-theoretic and game-theoretic cybersecurity investment models for selecting an economically superior subset of security mitigations (Khansa and Liginlal 2009). Decisiontheoretic models start with a list of threats along with their occurrence probability and resulting impact, and proceed to evaluate the cost efectiveness of combinations of mitigations for combating the threats (Rakes et al. 2012, Sawik 2013, Srinidhi et al. 2015, Tsalis et al. 2013, Viduto et al. 2012, Yevseyeva et al. 2015, Sarala et al. 2015, Shahpasand et al. 2015, Paulson et al. 2016). Game-theoretic models focus on strategic attacker-defender interactions in evaluating mitigations’ efectiveness from the perspective of attackers and defenders (Cavusoglu et al. 2008), and recent models specifically address the allocation of resources among possible mitigations (e.g., Nagurney et al. 2015, Paulson et al. 2016, Fielder et al. 2016). One weakness of both types of models is their reliance on the expected value of cyber-loss expectancy (outcome variable) rather than the probability distribution derived from the frequency of attacks and the magnitude of loss per attack. Thus, they do not account for the probabilistic loss-avoidance efect of investment in cybersecurity. A second and more crucial weakness is the assumption that mitigations are independent and have a multiplicative combined efectiveness. This is apparent from a review of models published between 2002 and 2011 (Demetz and Bachlechner 2013) and from recent models (Sawik 2013, Tsalis et al. 2013, Yevseyeva et al. 2015, Sarala et al. 2015, Shahpasand et al. 2015, Paulson et al. 2016).<sup>3</sup> Because mitigations can in fact interact and have a complementary, substitutive or synergetic efect on loss avoidance, assuming independent mitigations leads to an overestimation of the loss-avoidance benefit of mitigations relative to their cost and, thus, to overdeployment of mitigations and lower investment eficiency.

Real options models are a third type of model that can address both of the above weaknesses. Their underlying stochastic modeling approach captures uncertainty in terms of probability distributions, and their optimizing nature can handle sequential mitigations with conditionality. Multiple papers propose applying real options models to cybersecurity investment problems (e.g., Gordon et al. 2015b, Khan 2011, Wan et al. 2012, Neubauer and Hartl 2009, Abbas et al. 2008, Daneva 2006, Tatsumi and Goto 2010, Li and Su 2007). However, we know of only two papers that apply these models in practice. Khansa and Liginlal (2009) look at the concept of security process innovations (SPIs) as a means for creating cybersecurity infrastructure, and develop a model for valuing options embedded in SPI investments. Herath and Herath (2008) address a real-world multistage investment problem that is close in nature to our interest. The first-stage investment is a mitigation involving Bayesian learning with postaudit data from an IDS. The authors compute this stage’s uncertaintyreduction ability and then explain only conceptually that the second-stage investment decision becomes conditional on the amount of uncertainty remaining. The classic real options models they use simply cannot account for conditionality in the same analysis.<sup>4</sup> As we will show in Section 4, this challenge can be overcome by reframing Herath and Herath’s (2008) learning mitigation to fit the adapted real options models we present. Of course, our models go well beyond this example, in that they allow treating cybersecurity countermeasures as proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations, of which zero or more are deployed sequentially with conditionality, and whose costs and uncertainty-reduction efects could be substitutive, complementary, or synergetic.

From an investment eficiency viewpoint, our models are capable of lowering cybersecurity investment cost without compromising loss-avoidance benefits. Our models could make a material diference by going beyond simple return on investment (ROI) calculations that can mislead because cybersecurity investments represent real costs that show on the books while their loss-avoidance benefits do not (Kwon and Johnson 2014, Moore et al. 2015). As one healthcare chief security oficer explains, “ROI is a fallacy. We are a cost center . . . security is a necessary business expense, and the attempt to frame spending as creating business value is doomed to $\mathrm { f a i l } ^ { \prime \prime }$ (Moore et al. 2015, p. 10). In essence, this boils down to an explicit recognition that typical IT investments are about value-creation whereas cybersecurity investments are about loss-limiting (or value-protection).

## 3. Real Options Models with Mitigations

This section presents the Koussis et al. (2007) adaptation of extant real options models to the case of proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations facilitated by the presence of managerial flexibility.

## 3.2. Formalizing the Impact of Mitigations

Real options models assume that the option’s underlying asset, often computed as the net present value (NPV) of investment cash flows, is a stochastic state variable x that is log normal and follows geometric Brownian motion (GBM). This assumption fits well with many IT investments (Benaroch and Kaufman 1999). Cybersecurity investments are no exception, according to ample empirical evidence (Martin 2009, Khansa and Liginlal 2009, Edwards et al. 2016)<sup>6</sup> and the real-world illustrations we ofer in Sections 4 and 5.

Mitigations facilitated by flexibility are conceptualized as proportional jumps of random size that alter the cone of uncertainty by changing the drift (growth) rate and/or volatility of state variable x. These jumps’ arrival is controlled by decision makers (not random) but their size is log-normally distributed.<sup>7</sup> Formally, to account for mitigations, the standard GBM process is “augmented” as follows (Koussis et al. 2007):

$$

## 3.3. Adaptation of Real Options Models to Mitigations

We proceed to present adaptations of common option valuation models to the case of a single mitigation, two successive mitigations, and two concurrent mitigations (Koussis et al. 2007). These are adaptations of Merton’s basic call options model, Geske’s compound options model, and Margrabe’s asset-for-asset exchange options model (Hull 1993). Three or more mitigations can be handled using numeric options models such as the binary lattice model we present and illustrate in Appendix A.

Single Mitigation. For a single mitigation, we adapt Merton’s model for valuing an investment as a call option (Hull 1993). Assume an investment opportunity with a deterministic cost (exercise price) I and a stochastic payof $\tilde { P }$ having volatility $\sigma _ { P }$ and dividend payout rate $\delta _ { P }$ . Further assume an optional mitigation $M _ { 0 }$ that impacts $\tilde { P }$ with parameters $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ and has a deterministic cost $c _ { 0 } .$ The value of the opportunity conditional on deployment of $M _ { 0 }$ at $t = 0$ is denoted

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & F (\tilde {P}, I \mid M _ {0}) = P e ^ {[ - \delta_ {P} T + \gamma_ {0} ]} N (d _ {1}) - I e ^ {- r T} N (d _ {2}) - c _ {0}, \\ & d _ {1} = \frac {\ln (P / I) + (r - \delta_ {P} + (1 / 2) \sigma_ {P} ^ {2}) T + \gamma_ {0} + (1 / 2) \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}{\sqrt {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}}, \\ & d _ {2} = d _ {1} - \sqrt {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

If $M _ { 0 }$ is not deployed, impact parameters $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ are set to zero, and model (3) reduces to Merton’s valuation model (Hull 1993); if in addition $\delta _ { P }$ is set to zero, model (3) reduces to the basic Black–Scholes model (Hull 1993). With $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ set to zero, the intuition of model (3) is as follows. If the option is worth exercising at time T since $P _ { \mathit { T } } - I > 0$ its terminal value is $P _ { T } - I$ and its present value is $P e ^ { - \delta _ { P } T } - I e ^ { - r T }$ , where $e ^ { - [ \cdot ] T }$ is a continuous time discount factor. To consider the possibility that the investment opportunity can be unattractive to undertake $( P _ { T } - I < 0 )$ , P and I are weighted by probabilities $N ( d _ { 1 } )$ and $N ( d _ { 2 } )$ , respectively; $N ( d _ { 2 } )$ is the riskadjusted probability that the option will finish in the money (if $P _ { T } > I )$ , and $N ( d _ { 1 } )$ is the risk-adjusted probability that $P _ { T }$ will exceed the current value of $P$ .

If $M _ { 0 }$ is deployed, impact parameters $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ would be nonzero, and the result is twofold. Parameters $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ push probability $N ( d _ { 1 } )$ up and probability $N ( d _ { 2 } )$ down, increasing the investment opportunity value. If the increase in investment opportunity value exceeds the mitigation cost, $c _ { 0 } ,$ the mitigation adds value.

Successive Mitigations. Assume a stochastic payof ${ \tilde { P } } ,$ a deterministic investment cost $I ,$ and mitigations $M _ { 0 }$ and $M _ { 1 }$ that cost $c _ { 0 }$ and $c _ { 1 }$ and can be deployed sequentially at $t _ { 0 } = 0$ and $t _ { 1 } > 0 ( t _ { 1 } < T )$ , respectively. Following an adaptation of Geske’s compound options model (Hull 1993), we can conceptually equate deploying M with paying $c _ { 1 }$ to obtain an investment opportunity that expires at time $T$ and allows acquiring P<sup>˜</sup> for cost I. Deploying $M _ { 0 }$ at cost $c _ { 0 }$ yields a similar opportunity that, in addition, ofers the flexibility to deploy $M _ { 1 }$ . By this logic, the investment opportunity is treated as a compound option whose value conditional on deployment of $M _ { 0 }$ and $M _ { 1 }$ at $t _ { 0 } = 0$ and $t _ { 1 } > 0$ , respectively, is written as

$$
\begin{array}{l} F (\tilde {P}, I \mid M _ {0}, M _ {1}) = P e ^ {- \delta_ {p} T + \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1}} N (a _ {1}, b _ {1}, \rho) \\ \qquad \qquad \qquad \text {investment payoff} \\ \qquad - I e ^ {- r T} N (a _ {2}, b _ {2}, \rho) - c _ {1} e ^ {- r t _ {1}} N (a _ {2}) \\ \qquad \qquad \qquad \text {investment cost} \qquad \qquad \qquad \text {cost of M_{1}} \\ \qquad - \underset {\text {cost of M_{0}}} {c _ {0}}, \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} a _ {1} = \frac {\ln (P / P ^ {*}) + (r - \delta_ {P} + (1 / 2) \sigma_ {P} ^ {2}) t _ {1} + \gamma_ {0} + (1 / 2) \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}{\sqrt {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} t _ {1} + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}} \\ a _ {2} = a _ {1} - \sqrt {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} t _ {1} + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}, \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} b _ {1} = \frac {\ln (P / I) + (r - \delta_ {P} + (1 / 2) \sigma_ {P} ^ {2}) T + (\gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1}) + (1 / 2) (\sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2})}{\sqrt {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2}}}, \\ b _ {2} = b _ {1} - \sqrt {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2}}, \quad \rho = \sqrt {\frac {\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} t _ {1} + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}{\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2}}}. \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

In model (4), $N ( x , y ; \rho )$ is the bivariate dimensional cumulative normal distribution with correlation coefficient $\rho .$ The first term is the expected payof $P$ conditional on (1) $P > P ^ { * }$ at $t _ { 1 }$ and (2) $\mathbf { \bar { \rho } } _ { P } > I$ at $T ,$ where $N ( a _ { 1 } , b _ { 1 } , \rho )$ is the joint probability of both conditions being met. The critical value $\check { P ^ { * } }$ is found by solving numerically the equation $P e ^ { - \delta _ { P } ( T - t _ { 1 } ) + \gamma _ { 1 } } N ( d _ { 1 } ) \dot { - }$ $I e ^ { - r ( T - \stackrel {  } { t _ { 1 } } ) } N ( d _ { 2 } ) - c _ { 1 } \stackrel {  } { = } 0 .$ , where $d _ { 1 } = ( \ln ( P / I ) + ( r - \delta _ { P }$ + $( 1 / 2 ) \sigma _ { P } ^ { 2 } ) ( T - t _ { 1 } ) + \gamma _ { 1 } + ( 1 / 2 ) \sigma _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } ) / ( \sqrt { \sigma _ { P } ^ { 2 } T + \sigma _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } } )$ and $d _ { 2 } = d _ { 1 } -$ $\sqrt { \sigma _ { P } ^ { 2 } T + \sigma _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } }$ . The second term in model (4) is the expected investment cost if undertaken at $T ,$ where $N ( a _ { 2 } , \bar { b _ { 2 } } , \rho )$ is the probability that the investment will be undertaken. The third term in model (4) is the cost of deploying $M _ { 1 }$ at $t _ { 1 } ,$ , where $N ( a _ { 2 } )$ is the probability that the value of P at $t _ { 1 }$ will exceed $c _ { 0 } .$ . The last term is the cost of deploying $M _ { 0 }$ at time 0.

Note two special cases of model (4). First, assuming only $M _ { 0 }$ is activated at $t = 0$ , the investment value $F \big ( \overline { { ( P , I ) } } \big | \ M _ { 0 } \big )$ is found by setting $c _ { 1 } = \gamma _ { 1 } = \sigma _ { 1 } = 0 ;$ ; in this case, model (4) reduces to model (3). Second, assuming only $M _ { 1 }$ is activated at $t _ { 1 } > 0$ , the investment value $F \big ( \overline { { ( P , I ) } } \big | \overline { { M _ { 1 } } } \big )$ is found by setting $c _ { 0 } = \gamma _ { 0 } = \sigma _ { 0 } = 0$

Concurrent Mitigations. For mitigations that aim to reduce uncertainty over investment payof $P$ and investment cost I, we present an adaptation of Margrabe’s asset-for-asset exchange model (Hull 1993). The option to exchange a stochastic investment payof $\tilde { P }$ that pays dividend rate $\delta _ { L }$ for a stochastic investment cost <sup>˜</sup>I that pays dividend rate $\delta _ { I }$ is essentially a call option on $P$ with exercise price I (or a put option on I with exercise price P<sup>)</sup>. Now, consider two mitigations, $M _ { 0 }$ and $M _ { 1 } ,$ that can be deployed at $t = 0$ . Mitigation $M _ { 0 }$ costs $c _ { 0 }$ and impacts $\tilde { P }$ with parameters $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } ) . ~ M _ { 1 }$ costs $c _ { 1 }$ and impacts <sup>˜</sup>I with parameters $( \gamma _ { 1 } , \sigma _ { 1 } )$ . Adaptation of Margrabe’s asset-for-asset exchange model under the assumptions of the stochastic process in Equation (2) yields

$$
\begin{array}{c} F (\tilde {P}, \tilde {I} \mid M _ {0} \text {   and   } M _ {1}) \\ = P e ^ {- \delta_ {P} T + \gamma_ {0}} N (d _ {1}) - I e ^ {- \delta_ {I} T + \gamma_ {1}} N (d _ {2}) - c _ {0} - c _ {1}, \\ d _ {1} = \frac {\ln (P / I) + (\delta_ {I} - \delta_ {P} + (1 / 2) \sigma^ {2}) T + \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} + (1 / 2) (\sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2})}{\sqrt {\sigma^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2}}}, \\ d _ {2} = d _ {1} - \sqrt {\sigma^ {2} T + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2}}, \\ \sigma = \sqrt {(\sigma_ {P} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}) + (\sigma_ {I} ^ {2} + \sigma_ {1} ^ {2}) - 2 \rho_ {P I} (\sigma_ {P} + \sigma_ {0}) (\sigma_ {I} + \sigma_ {1})}. \end{array} \tag {5}
$$

In model (5), the variance is a function of $\rho _ { P , I } ,$ the correlation coeficient of the natural logarithms of one plus the rates of return on P and I. If the mitigations are not deployed, their impact parameters $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ and $( \gamma _ { 1 } , \sigma _ { 1 } )$ are set to zero, and model (5) reduces to Margrabe’s classic asset-for-asset exchange model (Hull 1993). If, in addition, I is deterministic with a dividend rate of $\delta _ { I } = r ,$ model (5) reduces to Merton’s option model (Hull 1993).

If both mitigations are deployed, $M _ { 0 }$ and $M _ { 1 }$ impact the distributions of P and $I ,$ respectively, as probabilities $N ( d _ { 1 } )$ and $N ( d _ { 2 } )$ are also a function of $( \gamma _ { 0 } , \sigma _ { 0 } )$ and $( \gamma _ { 1 } , \sigma _ { 1 } )$ . Probability $N ( d _ { 1 } )$ is boosted up and $N ( d _ { 2 } )$ is pushed down even more than in model (3). If the increase in investment opportunity value exceeds the cost of the mitigations, $c _ { 0 }$ and $c _ { 1 } ,$ the mitigations add value.

To summarize, we account for the impact of mitigations on the stochastic behavior of outcome (state) variables using adaptations of common analytical real options models. The adapted models address scenarios involving one mitigation, two parallel mitigations, and two sequential mitigations. For more than two mitigations, analytical options models become too complex, and numeric lattice-based models become a practical alternative. Appendix A presents a binomial lattice model along with an illustration of up to four sequential mitigations.

## 4. Adaptation to Cybersecurity Mitigations

We next illustrate the notion of mitigations and their impact on uncertainty in the context of cybersecurity investments. ## 4.1. Prevention and Coping Mitigations

Both prevention and coping mitigations beneficially modify the distribution of cybersecurity loss expectancy. Prevention mitigations tame existing sources of uncertainty by controlling cyberattacks’ frequency and/or loss magnitude. Coping mitigations limit the loss magnitude of attacks.

Wang et al. (2008) ofer an example of these two types of mitigations in connection with the value-atrisk method. The example is based on real-world data on incidents of denial of services (DoS) in a U.S. financial institution. Most incidents afect only individual productivity, by deleting or changing various computing objects $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , user objects, workstation objects, network components, and server components). Fewer incidents are more severe in that they interrupt network services and normal business activities, causing loss of organizational productivity and reputation. These two types of incidents are termed high-frequency–lowloss (HF–LL) and low-frequency–high-loss (LF–HL) incidents. Two cybersecurity strategies are considered. One strategy installs a firewall, a prevention mitigation that lowers the frequency of both incident types by 50%. Another strategy includes both installing an IDS, a prevention mitigation that lowers the frequency of HF–LL incidents by 30%, and increasing the frequency of backups, a coping mitigation that lowers the loss magnitude of the two incident types by 50%. ## 5. Application to a Real-World Cybersecurity Investment

To illustrate and assess the adapted real options models, we present a real-world investment case from a corporate member of Carnegie Mellon University’s INI/CyLab Japan. The corporate member is a Japanese insurance company seeking to determine which of several mitigations to deploy. Published by Bashir and Christin (2008), the investment case ofers detailed data we are able to use to estimate the impact parameters of mitigations and to evaluate diferent sequences of uncertainty-reducing mitigations. Importantly, our analysis of the case augments the original analysis by overcoming the two weaknesses of cybersecurity investment evaluation models we discussed in Section 2.3. The richness of the case also permits us to assess in Section 7 the challenges that our adapted models may present in practice.

## 7. Discussion

This paper provides a solution to the problem of evaluating optional proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations that are facilitated by embedded flexibility. It presents adaptations of classic real options models that account explicitly for the impact of such mitigations on uncertainty and investment value. These models address a new class of investment problems involving conditional mitigations (options) on an underlying asset whose associated uncertainty could change with the exercise of mitigations (options) at discrete time points.

The practical benefits and implications of our models are illustrated in the cybersecurity investment context, and most visibly in how our analysis of a realworld investment case overcomes limitations of the original analysis of that case (Bashir and Christin 2008). To begin with, unlike extant cybersecurity investment evaluation models, our models represent uncertainty in terms of the probability distribution, not just mean, of the outcome variable. We showed that the realworld data in the investment case fit both the models’ assumed probability distributions and the kind of uncertainty-reduction impacts that mitigations have on these distributions. More importantly, extant cybersecurity investment evaluation models assume mitigations (countermeasures) to be independent of one another, whereas our models incorporate conditionality of sequential mitigations as well as substitutive, complementary, or synergetic relationships between mitigations. These features of our models allow IT decision makers to design and evaluate contingent multimitigation deployment plans that reduce cybersecurity investment cost without compromising loss-prevention potential. The broader implication is the improvement in the eficiency of cybersecurity investments. This is important considering that research has been treating cybersecurity as a cost of doing business when, in practice, managers are increasingly facing a challenge of justifying the tangible cost of investing in cybersecurity (which shows on the books) against the intangible benefit of loss-prevention potential (which does not).

The benefits of our models and their practical implications extend beyond the cybersecurity context to IT investment decision making in general. The flexibility provided by any embedded real option appeals to IT decision makers precisely because it allows them to take proactive actions to resolve uncertainty. For example, in the case of deferral, IT decision makers may want to conduct market studies or consumer surveys to resolve uncertainty about the adoption rate of a new IT system or IT service (Benaroch 2002). Our paper is the first to provide models for evaluating the economic impact of such uncertainty-reducing mitigations. In this sense, our paper moves the IT literature in the direction suggested by Adner and Levinthal (2004, p. 76): “The “wait-and-see” setting of financial options represents the extreme case of investment environments, but one for which the methodology is ideally suited . . . it is important to examine what happens to the applicability of options logic as we move away from a world of wait and see to a world of “act and see,” in which uncertainty resolution is endogenous to firm activity.”

To put this implication more broadly, accounting for the impact of proactive mitigations enables IT decision makers to connect better with the intuition of real options models. Our models’ intuitive framing of how mitigations impact the uncertainty of options’ underlying assets fits well with the traditional notion that the cone of uncertainty gets progressively narrower as uncertainty is resolved over the life of an investment project (Browning et al. 2002). In this sense, our work deepens the intuition into two questions often raised by IT decision makers: (1) How does the value of options computed by real options models come about? (2) Why bother with valuing flexibility if we cannot account for the proactive risk-management actions it enables? The basis for these questions goes to the fact that the IT literature discusses diferent options (defer, stage, pilot, etc.) but values only the “deferral” component of these options in terms of how they truncate the bad tail of the distribution of investment outcome. Our models address an important missing link—they account for how proactive risk-management actions favorably transform the probability distribution of investment outcome. More importantly, our models enable IT decision makers to implement more fully the logic of real options. Our models allow IT decision makers to account for the value of embedded flexibility in terms of both the value that passive deferral adds and the value that proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations add when applied during deferral.

Benaroch and Goldstein (2009) illustrate the benefits of our models in another narrower context, namely, bridging the complementary research streams of IT business investment and software engineering. The IT business investment stream focuses on IT investmentstructuring choices facilitated by embedded options (e.g., stage, contract, and expand). The software engineering stream uses real options concepts to rationalize technical software design choices (e.g., software modularization and reuse).<sup>11</sup> These two streams are integrated by treating design choices at the software engineering level as proactive actions (mitigations) that create and enable exercise of real options (expand, contract, abandon, and so on) at the IT business investment level. Our paper is, of course, broader in its motivation, scope, and focus on IT investment risk management in general.

The present paper opens several venues for future research, being that it is the first to address proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations in connection with IT investment decision making. One important venue is empirical validation. While our framing of mitigations and their impact follows the traditional intuition that risk management progressively narrows down the cone of uncertainty, it remains to be seen how well this intuition and its formalization in our models fits the reasoning of IT decision makers in practice. Had we conducted the original data collection and analysis for the realworld cybersecurity case we presented, a useful supplement would have been to interview IT decision makers in the Japanese company about the adapted models’ logic as well as their strengths and challenges. In this vein, valuable extensions to this paper would include case and field studies that apply our adapted models with the active involvement of IT decision makers, in the spirit of past studies on the classic real options logic (Tiwana et al. 2006, Benaroch et al. 2007).

Another venue for future study is the estimation of mitigations’ parameters of impact on uncertainty, in line with work discussing challenges in estimating the volatility of options’ underlying assets (e.g., Benaroch et al. 2007, Ghosh and Li 2013, Avance 2009, Baker et al. 2011). Generally, the approach to these estimation tasks could be data-based, model-based, or expertbased. The data-based approach requires data linking sources of uncertainty to investment payof and cost with and without the intervention of risk mitigations. This approach proved efective in the examples presented in this paper based on real-world cybersecurity data and in the context of managing software development risk (Benaroch and Goldstein 2009). A challenge with this approach is the preprocessing of data to ensure that the log-normality assumption of real options models is met. An example from the investment case we presented is the number of cyberattack categories underlying the ALE distribution we have considered. One alternative is to divide all attacks into two broad categories (e.g., high-frequency-lowloss and low-frequency-high-loss events; Wang et al. 2008). Another alternative that fits our models better is to group only similar attacks into one of multiple categories (e.g., group “service unavailable— user $\mathrm { P C } , ^ { \prime \prime } , ^ { - }$ “service unavailable—email,” and “service unavailable—website” into a ‘service unavailable’ category; Bashir and Christin 2008). Likewise, the modelbased approach requires a model showing what would happen with and without the deployment of mitigations. Benaroch and Goldstein (2009) provide an example where the COCOMO model for software cost prediction is used to estimate the impact parameters for a mitigation aimed at controlling project cost risk. Expertbased estimation is another approach that relies on experts’ typical ability to adapt and calibrate past experiences to new investment scenarios (Jørgensen 2004, Pfleeger et al. 2005). This approach might also provide a sanity check for the conceptualization of mitigations in our adapted real options models, especially since we do not know if experts would interpret the meaning of mitigations’ impact on uncertainty consistently across investment situations. More work in this area could benefit the estimation of model parameters and the empirical validation of the logic of mitigations we presented.

To conclude, the adapted real options models we present in this paper help to close a notable gap in the literature. The models combine several crucial elements—modeling uncertainty using probability distributions, proactive mitigations that beneficially alter these distributions, the cost of mitigations, and the interrelated impacts of mitigations reflected by their nonadditive path-dependent reduction of uncertainty. By combining these elements our models enable firms to rationally plan the sequential yet conditional deployment of uncertainty-reducing mitigations that leads to value maximization. Naturally, more research is needed to apply and validate the formalization of proactive uncertainty-reducing mitigations in realworld settings. We hope that the present paper ofers suficient foundations for IT researchers to pursue these endeavors.

We use the binary lattice model in Appendix $\mathrm { A } . 2$ to evaluate an expanded version of the investment case in Section 5 that includes up to four sequential mitigations.<sup>12</sup> For this illustration, we add two hypothetical mitigations, $M _ { 1 }$ and $M _ { 3 } ,$ , which can be deployed in 1.5 months $( t _ { 1 } \bar { = } 0 . 1 2 5 )$ at cost $c ( M _ { 1 } ) = \$ 0$ and in 4.5 months $( t _ { 3 } = 3 . 7 5 )$ at cost $c ( M _ { 3 } ) = \$ 25 ,000,$ respectively. Table A.2 displays the value of a few additional mitigation combinations on top of those shown in the investment case in Section 5. Two observations are noteworthy. First, although we implemented the binomial lattice model with only five steps $( \bar { N } _ { s } = 5 )$ , the values this model computes for the mitigation combinations considered in Section 5 are about <sup>±</sup>1% from the values computed using the analytical models in Section 3. The valuation accuracy of the binomial lattice models normally improves as the number of lattice steps is increased. A second observation is that, the longer the sequence of applied mitigations is, the smaller the marginal value a single mitigation is likely to add.
