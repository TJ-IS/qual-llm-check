## Abstract

The information systems literature is replete with conceptual frameworks for analyzing strategic applications of information technology (IT). In this article, the strategic impacts of IT investment are studied through the development of a formal economic model. In particular, it focuses on IT-related quality competition in a duopoly, where the services may not be priced initially (e.g., in the financial services sector), and where the benefits may come indirectly (e.g., in the form of interest earned on consumer deposits or float on checking accounts). A firm may have to invest in IT, regardless of its underlying cost structure, as a response to its competitor's investment level. (We analyze the division of technology benefits between the firms and the consumers and study welfare implications for simultaneous and sequential investments.) Both firms prefer sequential over simultaneous investments, even when both have the required technology. While the IT-inefficient firm (one with higher IT cost for a given service quality) has followership incentives, the leadership incentives for the IT-efficient firm depend on the difference in IT cost structures and on the degree of substitutability between the services of the two firms. A preliminary treatment of pricing issues is provided in conjunction with consumer switching cost, which not only has a negative impact on consumer welfare but may also reduce total industry profits. For dynamic markets with new consumers, the negative effect of switching cost on the welfare of existing consumers is reduced when the IT-efficient firm moves first.

Keywords: Strategy, quality competition, duopoly, leadership incentives, switching cost, investment decision

## Introduction

In this article, we develop an analytical model of strategic impacts of information technology (IT) investments. Our objectives are (1) to characterize the impacts of these investments on the market position of a firm in various competitive settings and to derive some normative managerial implications for IT investment (e.g., timing of investment, competitive response, etc.) and (2) to assess the impacts of IT-related competition on industry profits and consumer welfare. The research questions addressed in this article include the following: How are the benefits of a given IT investment shared between the firms and the consumers when the IT-related services are provided free? What are the incentives for simultaneous and sequential investment decisions and for leadership-followership positions? What are the corresponding implications for consumer welfare? What is the effect of consumers' switching costs upon firm profits?

We analyze a duopoly where two firms have the opportunity to improve the quality of their services through investment in IT. The impacts of relative IT efficiency (as embodied in the firms' IT cost functions) on the firms' willingness to invest in IT, and their leadership-followership incentives are studied. Some interesting results with implications for IT management, industry profits, and consumer welfare emerge from the analysis. For example, from the theoretical model it is shown that a firm may have to invest in IT, regardless of its underlying cost structure, in response to the competitor's investment decision. When this is combined with the fact that many of the IT-related services may be provided free, at least in the initial phases of their introduction, we obtain insights on the division of the technology benefits between the firms and the consumers. That is, to maintain their competitive positions, each firm invests beyond what it would invest acting as a monopolist, and the benefits from the additional investments mostly accrue to the consumers. Similarly, we show that in absence of pricing of the IT-related service, sequential investment decisions are preferred to simultaneous moves by both firms. Moreover, the firm with lower IT efficiency has the incentive to be the second mover. The IT-efficient (lower IT cost) firm's willingness to make the first investment decision is shown to depend on the magnitude of the difference in IT cost structures and on the degree of substitutability (cross-elasticity of substitution) between the services of the two firms. These results offer possible explanations for certain phenomena associated with strategic IT investments (such as the propensity of certain firms to be IT leaders, the lack of investment in a widely available technology, etc.). A preliminary treatment of pricing issues is provided in conjunction with consumer switching cost. The impacts of switching cost on the prices charged are studied for both static and dynamic markets.

This model is applicable to situations where one firm has the system expertise to develop a strategic application at a lower cost compared to its competitors. We assume that all firms have access to the same IT but are not equally efficient in using the technology for strategic purposes. While cases of this nature abound in the strategic IT literature, it should be noted that there are other types of strategic IT applications. For example, a firm may not be efficient in using IT but may still realize a substantive gain from an IT investment. Or a strategic IT investment may be due to an IT innovation (see Davamanirajan, et al., 1991). Finally, we consider product/service quality improvement due to an IT investment. We do not consider new product introduction through IT investments.

The balance of this article is organized as follows: The next section provides the motivation for the study and a review of the relevant literature. This is followed by the development of the basic duopoly model studied in this paper. Next, we consider simultaneous and sequential investment decisions in a single period game without technology improvements, followed by an investigation of the impacts of relative IT efficiency upon endogenous leadership-followership incentives, as well as some pricing and switching cost issues. Finally, ongoing research and extensions of this model are outlined, and anticipated contributions and concluding remarks are summarized.

## Motivation for the Study

Since the early 1980s much of the management literature on information systems has been concerned with strategic aspects of IT investments. It has been recognized that IT is a strategic weapon that can be used to secure competitive advantage (see Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Ives and Learmonth, 1984; McFarlan, 1984). Most of the relevant studies in this domain seek to make the case through the development of conceptual frameworks and ex post examples of successful deployment of strategic IT. Among the more familiar illustrations of applying IT for strategic advantage are airline reservation systems (American and United), EDI (electronic data interchange) and interorganizational systems (American Hospital Supply of Baxter Health Care Corporation), “USA Today” (Gannett Publications) and online financial databases (e.g., Dun and Bradstreet). These examples provide a useful focus on important issues in strategic IT applications. Although much progress has been made in this area, many unanswered questions still remain.

First, the sharing of surplus generated by IT between firms and consumers is not well-understood. In particular, when the IT-related services are given free, the division of benefits of the given technology between the firms and the consumers requires a careful analysis. While many researchers advocate IT investment for gaining competitive advantage, more recently, the issue of sustained advantage has been questioned by Clemons and Kimbrough (1987) and Clemons and Row (1989). They suggest that in many situations, IT investments are strategic necessities rather than a means for achieving competitive advantage. Indeed, if much of the surplus resulting from IT investments accrues to the consumers, then this may have strategic implications for the firms in terms of their investment decisions.

Second, the leadership-followership incentives for strategic IT investment and their impacts on industry profits and consumer welfare are not clearly defined. We hypothesize that the ability to develop the strategic system within a reasonable cost limit is an important factor. Thus, we investigate the IT-related cost differences, allowing for substitution between services provided by the competitors, and examine the effect of such differences on leadership-followership incentives. Also investigated is the timing of IT investments and conditions under which firms may decide not to invest in IT even when the technology is available.

We develop more formal models to address some of these issues in order to derive implications for strategic IT investment decisions. $^{2}$

Many of the examples in this article are from the financial services sector (FSS) because IT plays a major role in providing many services in this sector. Electronic funds transfer, direct deposit, pay-by-phone, debit cards, and treasury workstations are but a few examples of IT applications in the FSS. Although many of the IT-related services could be provided without the IT (such as human tellers instead of ATMs), the deployment of IT greatly improves the quality of the service. Further, a variety of new services are made feasible through IT in the FSS. Because the value to consumers of having several services together from a single firm may be higher than getting them from multiple firms, the possibility of providing new services from existing IT facilities poses an interesting research question. Another distinguishing feature of the FSS is the benefit side. Benefits to firms in the FSS often consist of two components: direct and indirect. For example, in the banking sector, the surcharge on the pay-by-phone transactions would provide the direct benefit, while the interest float on each customer payment constitutes the indirect (and often more significant) component. Similarly, the pricing of ATM transactions (if any) would be a direct benefit, while the interest earned on retained deposits provides an indirect benefit.

There are many examples outside the FSS where a firm may not increase price for the improvement in service quality through IT. The major benefit for the firm may come from retaining or expanding its market share. For example, by providing terminals to its drugstore customers, McKesson made it easy for its customers to place orders. The advantage to McKesson was to generate more business (Clemons and Row, 1988). The improvement in service quality due to IT investments may also be used to differentiate the firm from its competitors. For example, General Electric uses an appliance database to support a customer hotline to enhance the quality of its service support, whereas American Express provides advanced travel services (identifying lowest airline fares, monitoring and tracking travel expenses, etc.) for corporate customers through the use of IT (Porter and Millar, 1985).

For the FSS in particular, differentiation of service quality and introduction of new services through IT investment have become important competitive tools (Bakos, 1987; Bresnahan, 1986). For example, Citibank's introduction of ATMs (Glaser, 1988) and Merrill Lynch's cash management account (CMA) system had major impacts on the industry competition by improving the way in which services were then provided to customers. As a result, competitors were forced to provide similar service levels, with much of the resulting benefits going to the consumers. While Citibank and Merrill Lynch may have retained some of the advantages of their early investments, it is not immediately obvious whether making the first move ahead of competitors, in general, leads to higher profits for the individual firms or the respective industries. To analyze situations such as these requires the development of a theoretical basis for analyzing IT investments in competitive situations. In this article, the primary issues addressed by the proposed model are the strategic impact of IT on the quality of services provided. We consider the case of a duopoly where both firms have the opportunity to improve service quality or generate new services through IT investment.

Since the goal of this article is to develop a formal model of strategic IT investment, it is important to consider the relevant economics literature. The industrial organization literature on quality competition is of interest here. The research in this area has generally focused on either monopolistic or perfectly competitive markets for durable goods, where firms choose some combination of quality (durability), price, and quantity (see Levhari and Peles, 1973; Mussa and Rosen, 1978; Sheshinski 1976). However, Gal-Or (1983; 1985; 1987), and Shaked and Sutton (1982) have developed oligopoly models involving quantity-quality and price-quality decisions respectively (see Tirole, 1989, for a summary of the quality competition literature). Our model is different from the above in two ways. First, the cited studies model situations where revenue is generated only from the pricing of products. However, as discussed earlier, a firm may choose to not charge directly for higher quality of service due to IT and may enjoy only indirect benefits. For example, a bank may be able to retain its customer deposits due to its ATM network, which in turn leads to a higher interest income for the bank. Pricing (if any) of the ATM services may account for a relatively small fraction of the total revenue, at least in the initial phases of introducing the services. Second, in contrast with the durable goods considered by earlier studies, the focus in this article is on the quality of services provided. Thus, for our analysis, the quality of services (and not quantity) is the key decision variable. We are interested in investigating the levels of service quality that are provided under various forms of imperfect competition.

## Research Model

Duopoly provides a starting point for research investigating strategic impacts of information technology. Because the services of the firms may not be perfect substitutes for one another, a duopoly or an oligopoly appears to be an appropriate mode of competition for our analysis. For simplicity, we consider the duopoly case, with the anticipation that some of the results obtained from this analysis can provide insights for other kinds of markets (e.g., oligopoly) as well. Another interpretation of the duopoly case might be the largest or dominant firm in a market region versus the competing firms as a group (e.g., Citibank in New York City). In that case, the competing firms may engage in cooperative development of IT-related services (Clemons and Knez, 1988; Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990).

Each firm's demand is considered as a function of its own attribute level (as a measure of service quality), the competitor's attribute level, and the degree of substitutability between the services provided by the two firms. Generally, the quality of services can be represented by a vector of attributes. For example, in banking, the quality of the ATM service may be determined by several factors, including ATM density, functionality, percent uptime, and cash availability. Similarly, in the insurance industry, the quality of settling claims may depend upon the amount of paperwork involved, the size of the settlement, and the time it takes to receive the payment. We consider only one attribute in the model. While this does not allow us to study the substitution possibilities between various attributes, it does not affect the basic phenomenon that we seek to investigate in this article, namely IT investment decisions in a competitive setting. First we consider the case where the services are free of charge. As noted earlier, this is realistic for the FSS as well as other sectors. For example, when first introduced, ATM services were mostly provided free. $^{3}$ Although we do not analyze the firms' choices between free services and pricing schemes, we believe many services are provided free for at least three reasons: (1) the IT services may have indirect benefits (such as interest income on deposits, as mentioned earlier) which, depending on demand, can offset the investment; (2) the quality of services may serve to increase the demand, leading to higher benefits; and (3) the demand function itself may be highly sensitive to IT-related pricing, where consumers consider IT as part of the service and are unwilling to pay a fee for its usage. Pricing issues are considered later in this article.

## Demand functions

We use the following notation for the demand functions:

$d_{i}$ and $d_{j}$ = demand (in terms of number of customers) faced by firms i and j respectively.

$x_{i}$ and $x_{j} = \text{attribute level (i.e., of quality) of the service offered by firms i and j respectively.}$

$\theta =$ cross elasticity of substitution, $0 < \theta < 1$ . $\theta$ is a factor that determines the negative effective of an increase in the competitor's attribute level on each firm's demand. A high $\theta$ implies a high substitutability between the services, and a strong negative impact of the competitor's quality on demand for services. We note that $\theta$ can be considered as the extent to which the services are different in terms of non-IT related factors (such as friendly service, advertising levels, etc.), which are not affected by IT investment and are considered exogenous to the model. Thus $\theta$ ensures that IT-related quality is not the only determinant of demand.

We use a generalized demand function of the form $d_{i} = a + b(x_{i} - < x_{j})$ , where $a$ and $b$ are constants. The demand function is in agreement with the quality competition in a duopoly; improvement in the attribute of a firm has a positive impact on its own demand ( $\partial d_{i}/\partial x_{j} > 0$ ), and a negative impact on the competitor's demand ( $\partial d_{i}/\partial x_{j} > 0$ ) Note that the functional form is similar to the one used by McGuire and Staelin (1986), except for the fact that the McGuire-Staelin model considers the prices charged by the two firms instead of the attribute levels.

Several aspects of this formulation are worth noting. First, consumers are not homogeneous in terms of their preferences. This is modeled through the use of $\theta$ , which “softens” the negative impact of competitor’s quality level. Thus all consumers do not join the firm with higher IT service quality because they find value in non-IT features of the service as well.

Second, the market demand is not static in this model. The total demand is given by $2a + b[(1-\theta)x_{i} + (1-\theta)x_{j}]$ , which implies that both firms can increase their customer base by increasing their quality levels. This feature is often appropriate because in recent times, inter-industry competition is becoming an increasingly common phenomenon. For example, Merrill Lynch's introduction of the Cash Management Account (CMA) broke the traditional barriers between the banking and securities industries. In this vein, we assume that the firms can draw customers from outside the current market and that the outside industry does not react in the short run. $^{4}$ Also, the partial derivative of total demand with respect to $\theta$ is negative. As mentioned above, a low $\theta$ indicates that the services are not substitutable for one another. Thus with low $\theta$ , we expect two non-overlapping markets for the two distinct services. With high values of $\theta$ , the two markets overlap (i.e., the total size reduces) because the services are then essentially the same.

## Benefit

Let R = benefit (revenue earned) per customer. In other words, the benefit of capturing a bigger share of the market (due to IT-based quality enhancement) leads to additional revenue for the firm. For example, if the two firms compete through strategic ATM deployment, then R may be considered as the interest earned on the average deposit per customer. Thus, R includes the time horizon of interest implicitly. In general, R may be considered as proportional to the average deposit level of the customers. Since the service is assumed to be provided free (for the present), R is treated as an exogenous parameter in this model. Later in this article we extend this model by considering pricing issues, whereby the fee charged for the IT-related service adds to the benefit side. In the extended model, we consider consumers as being heterogeneous in terms of their willingness to pay for a given service level.

## Cost components

Improvements in quality may depend stochastically on the IT investment. $^{5}$ However, we consider the mapping to be deterministic. The deterministic relationship is not unrealistic in many situations. It is very often the case that both firms have access to the information technology required to improve the quality of the service. For example, the technology required for “smart” cards is well-known. However, what is more important and interesting is the economic impact of improving service features, such as the functionality of such cards. We consider two types of IT-related cost for improving the quality of services.

## Fixed Cost

The “fixed” cost of improving the quality of the service through IT investment is given by $f_{i}(\Delta x_{i})^{2}$ , where $f_{i}$ is a constant and $\Delta x_{i}$ is the increase in the attribute level of firm i. In other words, improving service quality becomes increasingly difficult as the quality level increases. We can scale the initial value of $x_{i}$ to zero, in which case this cost component is denoted by $f_{i}x_{i}^{2}$ . Such quadratic forms for cost functions are common in the industrial organization literature on product quality competition (Gal-Or, 1987; Moorthy and P'ng, 1990). Clearly, a convex function given by $f_{i}x_{i}^{\beta}, \beta > 1$ , would be a more generalized form to use. However, the algebra becomes unnecessarily complex without new results being obtained.

Of course, the exact form of the cost function is an empirical question, and it depends on the attribute under consideration. For example, if we consider an increase in density of ATMs, then this cost may be linear, at least in given ranges of density values. On the other hand, if response time is the attribute under consideration, a convex form may be a better choice for the cost function. We carry out the analysis with a quadratic “fixed” cost component, with references to what happens when the function is linear.

## Variable Cost

The “variable” cost of an increase in quality is given by $c_{i}x_{i}$ per customer, where $c_{i}$ is a constant. The total variable cost is $c_{i}x_{i}d_{i}$ for firm i. This variable cost may be attributed to higher levels of maintenance activities and/or more specialized labor that may be necessary to improve the quality level. For example, increasing the functionality of credit cards may require more processing per customer.

In the single period model that we develop in the following section, $c_{i}$ , $c_{j}$ , $f_{i}$ , and $f_{j}$ are considered as stationary. However, in a multi-period model, these coefficients should decrease with time with the availability of superior technologies. Also, one firm need not consistently outperform the other in transforming IT investments into increased demand for services, especially when they have comparable cost structures. This issue of role reversal is discussed later in this article.

## Analysis and Results

In this section, we study the decision to set the quality of services, and hence the IT investment levels, provided under various forms of competition, and its implications for producer and consumer surplus, i.e., the division of the technology benefits between the firms and consumers. Consider a single-period game where technology is generally available but where the firms differ in their efficiency of transforming IT investments into attribute improvements. Assume both firms have access to the underlying information technology. For example, both firms may have adequate IT efficiency to improve the quality level through in-house development, or they may be able to buy the technology from an external vendor via outsourcing. If both firms invest in the information technology simultaneously and without collusion, then the resulting equilibrium is given by the pure non-cooperative Nash solution. This solution is obtained by choosing the “best” (profit maximizing) attribute level for each firm, given any attribute level of the competitor. This situation may occur when there are significant early mover advantages and/or when neither firm announces its attribute level before its competitor has done so.

## Simultaneous investments

The profit function for the time period under consideration is

$$
\pi_ {i} (x _ {i}, x _ {j}) = [ a + b (x _ {i} - \theta x _ {j}) ] [ R - c _ {i} x _ {i} ] - f _ {i} x _ {i} ^ {2}.
$$

In this context, the pure non-cooperative Nash equilibrium is a pair of attribute levels $(x_{i}^{*}, x_{j}^{*})$ , such that the pair corresponds to the regular “best response” requirement on part of both i and j. That is, each firm maximizes its profits, given the attribute level of the other, and neither desires to alter its attribute level. Thus, $x_{i}^{*} = \arg\max \pi_{i}(x_{i}, x_{j}^{*})$ ; and $x_{j}^{*} = \arg\max \pi_{j}(x_{i}, x_{j})$ .

Maximizing i's profit function, we determine i's response to j's attribute level (reaction function):

$$
x _ {i} = m _ {i} x _ {j} + K _ {i} \text { where } m _ {i} = \frac {c _ {i} b \theta}{2 (c _ {i} b + f _ {i})} \text { and }
$$

$$
K _ {i} = \frac {R b - c _ {i} a}{2 (c _ {i} b + f _ {i})}
$$

The reaction functions separate out into two components: (1) $K_{i}$ and $K_{j}$ , the attribute levels that will be attained if the corresponding firms were acting as monopolists, and (2) $m_{i}x_{j}$ and $m_{j}x_{i}$ , the additional amount that the firms must invest in the presence of competition. This additional amount depends on the cost structures of the firms as embedded in the reactive coefficients $m_{i}$ and $m_{j}$ . Interestingly, for linear “fixed” cost of improving quality (i.e., $f_{i}x_{i}$ instead of $f_{i}x_{i}^{2}$ ), the reactive component $m_{i}x_{j}$ is independent of the cost structure ( $c_{i},f_{i}$ ) of firm $i$ , although $K_{i}$ still remains dependent on ( $c_{i},f_{i}$ ). That is, irrespective of its IT cost advantage or disadvantage, a firm has to have constant sensitivity ( $=\theta/2$ ) to its competitor's attribute level. Consider ATM density as an example, where the cost is likely to be linear, as mentioned earlier. The reaction function indicates that for each ATM deployed by its competitor, $i$ will react with $\theta/2$ ATMs. The simple exposition highlights a basic rule of staying in business through additional investment, the surplus from which is passed on to the consumer (e.g., see Bresnahan, 1986, for a discussion of consumer welfare in the FSS). This is a phenomenon that Clemons and Kimbrough (1987) would classify as a strategic necessity. Empirical evidence of strategic necessities rather than strategic advantage is found in Banker and Kauffman (1988) and Clemons (1989).

From the reaction functions for i and j, we have the Nash solution in terms of the attribute levels, $x_{i}^{N}$ and $x_{j}^{N}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} x _ {i} ^ {N} = \frac {R _ {j} c _ {i} \theta b + 2 (c _ {j} b + f _ {j}) R _ {i}}{4 (c _ {i} b + f _ {i}) (c _ {j} b + f _ {j}) - c _ {i} c _ {j} \theta^ {2} b ^ {2}} \\ \text { where } R _ {i} = (R b - c _ {i} a) \text { and } R _ {j} = (R b - c _ {j} a) \end{array}
$$

From the Nash solution, we note that the investment level reduces in the presence of a competitor with higher fixed cost coefficient, $f_{j}$ , i.e., $\partial x_{i}^{N}/\partial f_{j} < 0$ . Recall that i is forced to invest beyond its marginal level ( $K_{i}$ ) because of competitor j. With an increase in $f_{j}$ , the level of $K_{j}$ and the reactive coefficient, $m_{j}$ , decrease. This allows i's investment to fall closer to $K_{i}$ .

Also, certain restrictions in the cost structures of the firms in the duopoly $(c_{i}, c_{j}, f_{i}, \text{and } f_{j})$ must be such that the demand functions for both the firms must be positive for the Nash equilibrium. $^{6}$

The next section considers a sequential investment situation and compares the resulting individual firm and industry profit levels with the corresponding levels for the simultaneous Nash game.

## Sequential investments

We now examine a situation where one firm decides on the investment level first, and the other firm decides next, taking the first mover's investment level as given. This case may arise due to the following reasons: One of the firms may have the first access (say, through its R&D investment) to a technology, $^{7}$ while the other firm may acquire the same technology only after the first firm has made its move (e.g., Fidelity's response to CMA was the Fidelity USA Account). We may call this a natural Stackelberg game, where incentives to lead or follow are determined exogenously. When such incentives are endogenous to the model, one firm may wish to wait for the other firm to make the first decision. For the present, we concentrate on properties of the sequential investment equilibrium. We analyze leadership and followership incentives in the next section.

Let $i$ decide its attribute level, $x_{i}$ , first. However, $i$ will do so only after incorporating the reaction function of $j$ in its own decision. As a result, $i$ 's profit function is given as the demand multiplied by the net benefit minus the fixed cost, that is:

$$
\pi_ {i} = [ a + b \{(1 - \theta m _ {j}) x _ {i} - \theta K _ {j} \} ] [ R - c _ {i} x _ {i} ] - f _ {i} x _ {i} ^ {2}.
$$

This profit function is expressed completely in terms of i's decision and is maximized by i to obtain its attribute level:

$$
x _ {i} ^ {S} = \begin{array}{l} 2 R _ {i} (c _ {j} b + f _ {j}) - \theta^ {2} b ^ {2} c _ {j} R + c _ {i} b \theta R _ {j} \\ 4 (c _ {i} b + f _ {i}) (c _ {j} b + f _ {j}) - 2 c _ {i} c _ {j} b ^ {2} \theta^ {2} \end{array}
$$

$$
\frac {2 - \theta^ {2}}{\theta} c _ {f} R _ {i} - c _ {i} R _ {j} + \frac {2}{\theta b} f _ {f} R _ {i} \geq \frac {2}{b} R f _ {i}
$$

Note that management has to consider the fixed and variable cost of both firms in deciding the attribute level. Next, j considers i's level above, as given, and maximizes its own profit function:

$$
x _ {j} ^ {S} = \frac {c _ {j} b \theta}{2 (c _ {j} b + f _ {j})} x _ {i} ^ {S} + \frac {R b - c _ {j} a}{2 (c _ {j} b + f _ {j})}
$$

As expected, the attribute levels of both i and j diminish as the fixed cost of the first mover $(f_{i})$ increases. We consider the attribute levels of both firms under simultaneous and sequential investments.

Proposition 1: The first mover invests less in the sequential mode than in the simultaneous mode. The second mover also invests less compared to its simultaneous decision. $^{8}$

When firms do not charge a fee for the use of the IT-related service, any investment above the monopolistic base levels $K_{i}$ and $K_{j}$ reduces the profit margin of both i and j. With a simultaneous Nash equilibrium, $^{9}$ the highest level of competition is encountered, which results in both firms investing maximum amounts. In a sequential mode, the first mover reduces its attribute level (below the Nash level). This allows the follower to choose an attribute level below the corresponding Nash value.

Clearly, the first mover increases its profits over the Nash solution (because it always has the option of setting to the Nash level). But what happens to the follower's profits? If we can show that the follower earns a profit higher than its Nash value, then sequential investment in IT is desirable from the viewpoint of both firms. Interestingly, this is the case.

Proposition 2: In a sequential investment mode, even the second mover makes more profit in comparison to the simultaneous mode. $^{10}$

Proposition 2 suggests that the firms would attempt to avoid a simultaneous decision on IT investment, even when there is perfect information on the demand for quality of services. This sequential form of investment is preferred by the two firms even when both firms have the underlying technology (e.g., most insurance firms have access to laptop computers for insurance agents).

Next, we consider the difference between leadership and followership quality levels for a given firm and their implications for industry profits and consumer welfare. For simplicity of exposition, we assume that $f_{i} = f_{j} = 0$ . This implies that the firms already have the IT capacity to increase the quality levels of their services. For example, with a given number of ATMs, the cash availability and percent uptime can be improved by increasing the number of service trips to the machines. The cost of improving quality is then determined by $c_{i}$ and $c_{j}$ .

Proposition 3a: If firm / has higher IT efficiency, then its investment level in the followership mode is higher than that in the leadership mode.

Proposition 3b: If and only if the services of the two firms are close substitutes, then the less efficient firm will invest more as a leader than as a follower. The reverse is true when the services are weak substitutes. The threshold value of $\theta$ is given by

$$
\theta_ {t h} = \frac {c _ {i}}{c _ {j}} \frac {R b + c _ {j} a}{R b}
$$

This proposition implies that the customers of the more efficient firm i get higher quality of service when i moves after j. This result may be explained as follows: In the followership mode, i does not anticipate another investment by j as a reaction to its investment. Also, i gets more quality per IT dollar due to its superior cost structure. These two factors lead to a higher investment in the followership mode.

While i always invests less in the leadership mode, the investment level of j (the less efficient firm) depends on the cross elasticity of substitution and the IT cost structure differences. For “sufficiently high” substitutability of services (given by the threshold value of $\theta$ above), j has to invest more as a leader. Under such situations, the effect of the higher attribute level of i (as a follower) upon j's demand becomes more pronounced. Of course, this dependence on the cross elasticity is not observed with i as the leader. Irrespective of the impact of j's attribute level on i's demand, i's superior cost structure determines its investment levels as a leader and follower.

An important issue of interest in the strategic IT investment game is the resulting consumer welfare. Having derived the service quality levels for the investment sequences $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$ , we examine the technology-related benefit that is passed on to the consumers. Because the services are provided free in this basic model, the total consumer welfare may be taken to be $x_{i}d_{i}(x_{i},x_{j}) + x_{j}d_{j}(x_{i},x_{j})$ .

From the definition of consumer welfare above, the total welfare increases for the following cases: (1) i improves attribute level, j does not; (2) j improves attribute level, i does not; and (3) both i and j improve their attribute levels. In this basic model, none of these cases is likely to occur in most situations. The only exception occurs when the less efficient firm mistakenly (see Proposition 4) makes the first move and $\theta$ exceeds $\theta_{th}$ . In that case, both firms provide higher attribute levels (i.e., higher of the respective leadership and followership levels). However, we will see later that strategic IT investments can affect consumer welfare in the presence of switching costs and if an explicit price is charged for IT-based quality improvements.

## Relative IT efficiency and leadership-followership incentives

Having considered industry profits and consumer welfare for different investment sequences, we next investigate the leadership-followership incentives of the firms. The incentives are influenced by many factors, e.g., IT efficiency, switching cost (may provide advantage to the first mover), learning, leapfrogging (whereby the second mover gets a cost advantage), demand uncertainty (whereby the demand gets known only after the first mover's decision), etc. We focus on relative IT efficiency in this section. Switching cost is considered later. We ask the following questions:

Which firm has the incentive to be the leader or the follower? How much do such incentives depend on the magnitude of differences in cost structures and/or cross elasticity of substitution between services?

Since the constant a in the demand function is independent of quality levels, we consider it to be zero to focus more closely on the various equilibrium quality levels.

Proposition 4: The firm with lower IT efficiency has the incentive to make the second move, i.e., its profit is higher as the follower than as the leader.

It is interesting to note that the less IT-efficient firm generates higher profits as a follower than as a leader, regardless of the cost structure differential and the cross-elasticity of substitution between the services. As a leader, i invests less compared to its followership level; therefore, j can also invest less, which is beneficial for its inferior cost structure. As a leader, j loses by setting the quality level first and letting i invest more as a follower due to its superior cost structure.

However, the more efficient firm i does not have an invariant incentive (the way the less efficient firm j does) to lead or follow with an IT investment.

Proposition 5: For any given value of $\theta$ , the more efficient firm $i$ would want to make the first move if the cost ratio is such that $c_{i} / c_{i} > r$ , where $r$ is given by

$$
\theta \left\{1 - \sqrt {1 - \theta^ {2} / 2} \right\} / \left\{(2 - \theta^ {2} - \frac {4 - 3 \theta^ {2}}{\sqrt {2} \sqrt {2 - \theta^ {2}}} \right\}
$$

The proposition implies that a superior cost structure alone does not determine the leadership incentives for the more efficient firm. The degree of substitutability between the services of i and j (note that r is determined by $\theta$ ) plays an important role in determining leadership incentives. r is plotted as a function of $\theta$ in Figure 1.

Our model illustrates the impact of the ordering of competitors' investment decisions. It is sometimes considered anomalous that firms differ in their use of IT $^{11}$ and their propensity to lead with new innovations. The model attempts to offer an explanation of these phenomena as a function of firms' differential costs, and the level of substitutability between the services of the firms. Often an available technology is not widely used in an industry. Propositions 4 and 5 indicate that this could be the response of rational firms. If both firms have followership incentives, the investment in the technology may be delayed or not undertaken.

## Pricing and switching cost

Switching cost is particularly important for IT-related services or products as opposed to a commodity like sand or cement. First, the presence of considerable learning requirements and/or inertia may be detrimental to the process of switching to a new firm. Electronic home banking provides a good example (McFarlan, 1984). Once a customer has learned to use such a system and incorporated all the periodic creditors, he or she will be less inclined to change banks than before. Second, substantial effort and expense may be involved in switching IT-related services. For example, computerized reservation systems for travel agents make it harder to switch to a new vendor. Clearly any realistic model of strategic IT impacts should consider the impacts of switching on factors like firm and industry profit and consumer welfare.

In this section, we introduce some pricing issues and investigate the effects of switching cost on resulting profits and consumer welfare. We are interested in finding how the presence of switching cost affects a given pricing scheme. The questions addressed are the following:

1. Is there a loss of consumer welfare due to the presence of switching cost, or is switching cost fully subsidized by the firms?

2. Do total industry profits increase due to switching cost?

3. What are the differences in impacts of switching cost on static $^{12}$ and dynamic markets?

First we consider a static market with a constant consumer base of size N, and show that consumer welfare is always reduced due to switching costs. The effect of switching cost on industry profits, however, can be either positive or negative, depending on the relative cost efficiency of the firms.

We model the situation as follows: firm i has introduced the IT-related service quality improvement and has chosen an attribute level $x_{i}$ . After j's introduction $^{13}$ of attribute level $x_{j}$ , consumers make a choice between i and j, depending on (1) $x_{i}, x_{j}$ ; (2) the prices $p_{i}$ and $p_{j}$ charged by i and j respectively; and (3) $\gamma$ , a non-IT related parameter that is distributed uniformly in the consumer base. For example, $\gamma$ could be the distance to a bank. Let $\gamma$ be uniformly distributed across consumers between 0 and 1. Also, in order to focus on the prices charged, we assume that the attribute levels are given in this model. $^{14}$ Let s be the cost of switching from i to j per consumer. The utility of a consumer increases with higher quality of services and diminishes with the price charged and the non-IT factor (e.g., distance). The utility of the consumer of type $\gamma$ is assumed to be

$$
\begin{array}{l} \delta x _ {i} - p _ {i} - \gamma c \text {if he or she stays with i and} \\ \delta x _ {j} - p _ {j} - s - (1 - \gamma) c \text {if he or she joins j} \end{array}
$$

where $\delta$ is a constant, and c is the cost incurred when $\gamma = 1$ .

If the consumer of type $\gamma$ stays with i, then the non-IT related cost (from parameter $\gamma$ ) would be $\gamma c$ , while it is $(1 - \gamma)c$ if the consumer joins j. The consumer with $\gamma = 0$ is likely to stay with i because he or she incurs a relatively high non-IT related cost c (in addition to s) by switching to j. Similarly, the consumer with $\gamma = 1$ is likely to switch to j to avoid the cost c. We are interested in finding the equilibrium prices charged and the corresponding impacts of s. $^{15}$ With given $x_{i}, x_{j}, p_{i}$ , and $p_{j}$ , for a consumer to be indifferent between i and j requires that the non-IT related parameter $\underline{\gamma}$ be given by

$$
\delta x _ {i} - p _ {i} - \gamma c = \delta x _ {j} - p _ {j} - s - (1 - \gamma) c
$$

$$
\text { or } \gamma = \frac {\delta (x _ {i} - x _ {j}) - p _ {i} + p _ {j} + s + c}{2 c}
$$

Thus, the consumers with $\gamma < \underline{\gamma}$ will stay with $i$ , while those with $\gamma > \underline{\gamma}$ will switch to $j$ . Therefore, the profit functions for $i$ and $j$ are given by

$$
\pi_ {i} (p _ {i}, p _ {j}) = \frac {\delta (x _ {i} - x _ {j}) - p _ {i} + p _ {j} + s + c}{2 c} [ R + p _ {i} - c _ {i} x _ {i} ] N
$$

$$
\pi_ {j} (p _ {i}, p _ {j}) = \frac {c - \delta (x _ {i} - x _ {j}) + p _ {i} - p _ {j} - s}{2 c} [ R + p _ {j} - c _ {j} x _ {j} ] N
$$

Note that there are two benefit streams in the profit functions, R and $p_{i}$ or $p_{j}$ . R is the indirect benefit of investing in IT, e.g., the interest income from loans made possible by consumer deposits. The equilibrium prices are found out to be the following:

$$
p _ {i} ^ {*} = c + s / 3 + A / 3 - (R _ {j} ^ {*} + 2 R _ {i} ^ {*}) / 3
$$

$$
p _ {j} ^ {*} = c - s / 3 - A / 3 - (R _ {i} ^ {*} + 2 R _ {j} ^ {*}) / 3
$$

$$
\text { where } A = \delta (x _ {i} - x _ {j}), R _ {i} ^ {*} = R - c _ {i} x _ {i}, R _ {j} ^ {*} = R - c _ {j} x _ {j}.
$$

Observe that i charges s/3 more per consumer, while the second mover j charges s/3 less (subsidizes) due to the presence of switching cost s. Examples of firms subsidizing switching costs are often found in the form of interest premium paid by banks for new customers or the waiving of credit card fees for new applicants. This form of pricing has implications for consumer welfare and industry profits.

Proposition 6a: For the static market described above, the total consumer welfare diminishes due to the switching cost s.

Proposition 6b: With identical quality levels, industry profits increase with s if the more efficient firm invests first. However, if the first firm is less efficient, and if the variable cost difference is greater than s, then industry profits decrease due to switching cost.

When the more IT-efficient firm i moves first, it charges s/3 more per consumer. Also, because it retains a larger fraction of the market, the total profit increase for i more than balances the total subsidy offered by the second mover j. In other words, switching cost adds an extra incentive to make the first move and “lock-in” the consumers. The second firm, however, would know that its profit may suffer due to switching cost even if it provides the same quality of service. In sum, the stake for the first move is heightened in the presence of the switching cost.

Note that if the less efficient firm j made the first move, $^{16}$ then the industry might have been worse off due to s. The intuition is as follows: if the IT efficiency of the second firm is “sufficiently” higher (as stated in Proposition 6b), then it will capture a larger fraction of the market, as seen from the equilibrium value of $\underline{\gamma}$ ; and since the second firm subsidizes s/3, the total industry profits go down.

We considered a static market in the above analysis. The situation becomes more interesting when new consumers join either i or j and therefore incur no switching cost. Next, we investigate the impact of switching costs (i.e., the presence of existing consumers) on new consumer surplus and vice versa.

Let $\Delta$ be the fraction of new consumers, i.e., if there are N existing consumers, then $\Delta N$ new consumers join the market, $\Delta > 0$ . With both existing and new consumers, the profit functions of i and j are

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \pi_ {i} & = N \{(1 + \Delta) [ A - p _ {i} + p _ {j} + c ] [ R _ {i} ^ {*} + p _ {i} ] / 2 c + \\ & s [ R _ {i} ^ {*} + p _ {i} ] / 2 c \} \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \pi_ {j} = N \{(1 + \Delta) [ c - A + p _ {i} - p _ {j} ] [ R _ {j} ^ {*} + p _ {j} ] / 2 c - \\ s [ R _ {j} ^ {*} + p _ {j} ] / 2 c \} \end{array}
$$

The equilibrium prices are found to be

$$
p _ {i} ^ {* *} = c + A / 3 + s / [ 3 (1 + \Delta) ] - (R _ {j} ^ {*} + 2 R _ {i} ^ {*}) / 3
$$

$$
p _ {j} ^ {* *} = c - A / 3 - s / [ 3 (1 + \Delta) ] - (R _ {i} ^ {*} + 2 R _ {j} ^ {*}) / 3
$$

Due to the presence of new consumers, i charges only $s/[3(1 + \Delta)]$ more (instead of s/3) due to switching cost s, and j charges $s/[3(1 + \Delta)]$ less, thus reducing the subsidy.

Proposition 7a: With new consumers, the fraction of existing consumers switching firms decreases.

Proposition 7b: With identical quality levels, if the first mover has higher IT efficiency, then the negative effect of switching cost on existing consumers' welfare is reduced due to the presence of new consumers.

Proposition 7c: With identical quality levels, if the second mover is “sufficiently” inferior to the first, then the total welfare of new customers may be reduced.

With a mix of existing and new customers, the first mover reduces the premium due to switching cost to avoid a loss of new business, while the second firm reduces its subsidy due to the presence of customers without switching costs. As a result, fewer exiting consumers switch firms in the dynamic market. The proposition also suggests that in dynamic markets, the total welfare of existing consumers increases when the first mover is more efficient or when the firms have nearly identical cost structures (see Benjamin, et. al., 1984, for a discussion on inter-industry competition and new markets). However, the welfare of new consumers may be reduced if the first mover is sufficiently superior. The explanation is as follows: having an advantage of an existing customer base with switching costs, the first mover will always charge $s/[3(1 + \Delta)]$ more due to $s$ . The second mover, however, charges $s/[3(1 + \Delta)]$ less due to $s$ . Clearly, the total welfare of new consumers then depends upon who captures a larger fraction of such consumers.

## Ongoing and Future Research

In this article, we have developed a simple model of strategic IT impacts on service quality competition. The model makes certain assumptions but provides valuable insight into leader-follower incentives and pricing policy with switching cost. The next step is to generalize the various functional forms assumed in this article. However, beyond such generalizations, the basic model can also be enhanced substantially by incorporating additional features, as outlined below.

## Interdependent multiple periods

An opportunity to use a more cost-efficient technology at the end of a period may have an impact on the initial investment of each firm. Because a firm's investment in a given period may possibly reduce the next period investment required to attain a given service quality, the knowledge of the opportunity to invest in a more efficient technology in the next period should change the current investment level. $^{17}$ The analysis becomes even more interesting when there is a switching of roles with the new technology, i.e., when the less efficient firm in the first period becomes the more efficient firm in the second. $^{18}$ The impacts of the second period on the first period investment can be investigated through a dynamic programming model.

## New services

An IT investment may lead to the introduction of new services that are economically infeasible without the IT. This is particularly true for the FSS, where the services are provided directly through IT deployment. For example, IT has made possible the frequent introduction of new types of policies and products in the insurance industry. There may be an additional value of the availability of a broader range of services, i.e., the consumers' value of getting two or more services together from one firm may be higher than that of getting them separately from multiple firms. Future research will investigate the quality levels of the individual services in the presence of this complementary effect. Our conjecture is that this effect forces the firms to invest more in each service, which further increases the welfare of the consumers.

## Pricing issues

Of late, a transaction fee is charged to some consumers for the use of IT-related services, especially in the banking industry. Clearly, it is important to incorporate the pricing issue in greater detail. With pricing, the situation can be modeled as a two-stage game where the firms first commit to the attribute levels and then decide the prices. The socially optimal quality and pricing decisions can be compared to the outcomes of the sequential game.

## Conclusion

In this article we addressed the problems of strategic IT investment decisions in a duopoly setting where service quality can be improved through IT and where switching costs build inertia into heterogeneous consumers' initial adoption choice. An analytical framework for assessing such investments focused on the difference in the relative IT efficiency of the two firms, as captured in their IT cost functions. This difference impacts the IT investment levels (and the resulting service quality), individual firm as well as industry profits, and consumer welfare.

Within the basic duopoly model, we investigated the division of the IT benefits between the firms and consumers for the case where the services are not priced. We derived implications of providing free services in the context of simultaneous and sequential investment decisions. Regarding the endogenous leadership-followership incentives of the firms (based on their cost structure and cross-elasticity of services), we showed that the IT-inefficient firm has followership incentives. However, for a given cross-elasticity level, a “sufficient” IT-related cost difference between the two firms is necessary to provide leadership incentives to the IT-efficient firm.

Switching cost and its impacts on pricing were also considered. Switching cost has a negative impact on consumer welfare but may either increase or decrease total industry profits. For dynamic markets with new consumers, switching cost has a less adverse effect on the welfare of existing consumers when the first mover has higher IT-efficiency. Ongoing research toward a more general model has been outlined.

The principal contribution of this article is the development of a theoretical basis for analyzing strategic IT investments. The popular literature on strategic uses of IT has advocated that competitive advantage may be obtained through IT investments, while some recent studies have questioned the sustainability of such advantage. In this article, we showed that the outcome of competitive advantage versus strategic necessity depends on several factors, including the relative IT efficiency of the competing firms, the similarity of their products or services, and the opportunity to price the IT-related services.

## Proof of Proposition 7b

Due to a reduction in the first mover's price, the consumer fraction $\gamma^{*}$ gains a total of $N_{\gamma}^{*}\Delta s/[3(1+\Delta)]$ over the static market case, while the total amount lost by the fraction $1-\gamma^{**}$ , which joins the second mover in spite of the reduction in subsidy, is equal to $N(1-\gamma^{**})\Delta s/[3(1+\Delta)]$ . ## Proof of Proposition 7c

The new customer who is indifferent between $i$ and $j$ has a non-IT parameter value $\gamma$ equal to

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Upsilon_ {n e w} & = 1 / 2 + [ R _ {i} ^ {*} - R _ {j} ^ {*} - 2 s / (1 + \Delta) ] / 6 c \\ & = 1 / 2 + [ (c _ {j} - c _ {i}) x - 2 s / (1 + \Delta) ] / 6 c \end{array}

where $x_{i} = x_{j} = x$ . If $(c_{j} - c_{i})x > 2s / (1 + \Delta)$ , then $\underline{\gamma}_{\text{new}} > .5$ . Thus, the majority of new customers pay $s / [3(1 + \Delta)]$ more due to switching cost, while the remainder pay $s / [3(1 + \Delta)]$ less due to $s$ . In sum, the new consumers' welfare reduces if the cost differential is sufficient, i.e., $(c_{j} - c_{i})x > 2s / (1 + \Delta)$ .
