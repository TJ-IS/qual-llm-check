Robert M. Fuller

College of Business Administration, University of Tennessee, Knoxville, Tennessee 37996, rmfuller@utk.edu

Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405, ardennis@indiana.edu

It also suggests that the way teams appropriate technology influences performance. This research examines how fit and appropriation (from the Fit Appropriation Model) influence performance over time. Initially, the results show that fit better predicted performance; teams using poor-fitting technology performed worse than teams with better fitting technology. However, over a short time period (two days in this study), this initial fit no longer predicted performance; performance of teams using better fitting technology remained constant while teams using poor-fitting technology innovated and adapted, improving performance. There are two key findings from this study. First, fit can predict team performance soon after technology adop tion, but initial assessments of fit are temporary as teams innovate and adapt; thus, our current theoretical models of fitting technology to a task likely will not be useful beyond the first use. Second, teams should understand how to better adapt existing technology and work structures. Because our current theories of task technology fit failed to predict performance beyond the first use of technology, we believe that this calls for a reconsideration of what fit means for teams using technology.

## Introduction

For a growing number of teams, the primary method of interaction is through information technology (IT) (Lipnack and Stamps 1997). Task-Technology Fit (TTF) theory proposes that a good fit between the technology, the task, and the team can lead to better performance (Dennis et al. 2001, Goodhue 1995, Zigurs and Buckland 1998). However, teams often evolve in the way they perform a task over time. As TTF does not address change over time, this may limit its predictive ability, at least for collaborative technologies (Majchrzak et al. 2000, McGrath and Hollingshead 1994).

An alternative perspective argues that performance comes from the way in which teams appropriate a technology’s features (i.e., choose how they use it) and the social structures those features enable and how this appropriation changes over time (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). Teams usually have many opportunities to appropriate and adapt the structures that technology enables because repeated tasks dominate organizational life (March and Simon 1958).

The Fit-Appropriation Model (FAM) integrates these two perspectives (Dennis et al. 2001). It contends that team performance is influenced by both TTF and appropriation. While a meta-analysis indicated that FAM may be useful in explaining team performance (Dennis et al. 2001), its central tenets have not been directly tested (Bobko and Stone-Romero 1998, Dennis et al. 2001). Little is known about the boundary conditions under which TTF or appropriation is more or less appropriate for explaining team performance, and how these two perspectives interact.

This study strives to explain how the two major FAM constructs, TTF and appropriation, influence team performance. Can teams overcome the constraints of poor TTF through appropriation to generate acceptable performance? In examining these two perspectives and their implications for research and practice, it is particularly important to understand not only what is relevant, but also when. This allows us to see if these perspectives complement or conflict with each other, and can provide insight on the timing and duration of these effects (Ancona et al. 2001, George and Jones 2000). This research extends FAM by introducing a key boundary condition: time. Time is presented as an important contingency that affects the predictive validity of the key components of FAM.

We find that, initially, TTF influences appropriation and that over time, appropriation influences TTF. Ultimately, appropriation can ameliorate poor fitting technology such that over a longer perspective, poor TTF does not affect team performance. The results of this research can be used to inform practice about how to select team technologies in consideration of time (e.g., duration of tasks or duration of the team), and to inform research about relevant constructs and measures of interest when examining technology-mediated teams either over time, or at different points in time during the team’s life.

## Theoretical Foundation

FAM proposes that team performance is influenced by two factors, the fit between task and technology structures, and the way the team appropriates these structures (Dennis et al. 2001). Team members initially appropriate a technology based on their prior understanding of it and the task. As team members work, they recognize outcomes from their work (e.g., effectiveness, efficiency) and develop perceptions of how well the technology fits the task. These perceptions may trigger changes in the way in which they subsequently appropriate the technology and define the task, to produce new structures in which the technology is used (Dennis and Garfield 2003). For repeated tasks, these changes can influence subsequent outcomes and perceptions of fit. This pattern of behaviors repeats, and perceptions of fit and the appropriations continue to evolve until the team settles into a somewhat stable set of norms (DeSanctis and Poole 1994).

During these initial and later uses of structures, outcomes that suggest a less-than-desired level of TTF may occur (e.g., disagreements, performance problems) that may induce a change in structures appropriated in subsequent time periods (McGrath 1991, Tyre and Orlikowski 1994).

## FAM and Fit Influences on Performance

TTF is the degree to which features of a technology match the requirements of the task and the abilities of the individuals performing it (Goodhue 1995). TTF suggests that technology will have a positive impact on performance only when its functionality is matched to task requirements (Goodhue 1995, Goodhue and Thompson 1995). Poor fit is associated with poor performance. In general, TTF has focused on the task characteristics, technology features, and how these two fit to influence performance.

Task. We define task as “the behavioral requirements for accomplishing stated goals, via some process, using given information” (Zigurs and Buckland 1998, p. 316). Thus, task centers on the activities or behaviors that individuals need to perform to accomplish a goal. Task characteristics are an important factor influencing team performance, and accounting for as much as 50% of its variance (Poole et al. 1985).

Teams can perform many types of tasks. This study focuses on what McGrath (1981) defines as intellective tasks (i.e., problem solving tasks that have correct answers), as these tend to be more interactive and require the participation of team members for success (Dennis 1996). Intellective tasks are cognitive and require coordination; team members need to both share information and discuss its meaning to derive the correct answer. Teams performing intellective tasks should therefore benefit from technologies that facilitate these two behaviors (Dennis 1996, McGrath and Hollingshead 1994).

Technology. Technology is the tool used by individuals to perform tasks (Goodhue 1995). Technology provides a potential set of features that influence how teams choose to perform a task (Dennis et al. 2001, DeSanctis and Poole 1994, Orlikowski 2000). Many different Group Support System (GSS) features have been suggested in prior research (DeSanctis and Poole 1994, Zigurs and Buckland 1998). FAM focuses on two categories of these features, communication and information processing support (Dennis et al. 2001).

Communication support is any feature that supports, enhances, or defines the way in which team members can communicate (Zigurs and Buckland 1998). Research has shown that for certain tasks, the proper fit of communication support to a task can positively influence performance (Daft et al. 1987, Trevino et al. 1990, Zigurs and Buckland 1998).

Information processing support is any feature of the technology that supports, enhances, or defines how the technology facilitates the aggregation, structuring, or evaluation of information (Zigurs and Buckland 1998). This support includes team memory, and problem modeling and outline tools. Some studies found that information processing support improves outcomes (Barkhi 2001), while other studies suggest that there is little to no improvement (Dennis et al. 1997).

Fit. Fit is defined as a normative construct, depicting the match between requirements of a task and the capabilities of the technology to support task accomplishment (Goodhue 1995). Teams using a technology with a better (normative) fit to the task should have better performance (e.g., higher decision quality, less time) than teams working on the task using poorly fitting technology (Chidambaram and Jones 1993, Dennis et al. 2001, Maruping and Agarwal 2004). In other words, the degree to which the technology features fit the requirements of the task will affect performance outcomes, ceteris paribus. Performance includes both effectiveness (i.e., quality of the decisions) and efficiency (i.e., time taken) because both are important in organizations and because it is often possible to trade off one for the other (i.e., to sacrifice quality for time and vice versa) (Dennis et al. 2001). Thus:

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Initially, teams with better tasktechnology fit (vs. poor-fit teams) will (a) generate higher quality decisions and (b) take less time to perform the task.

## FAM and Appropriation Influences on Performance

FAM also proposes that the way in which technology is appropriated impacts team performance. Individual team members, the team itself, the technology, and the task all provide structures that influence how the team uses a GSS (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). Structures are the rules and resources individuals use to create, define, and maintain systems of social interaction (DeSanctis and Poole 1994, Orlikowski 2000).

Appropriation is the team’s selection and use of these structures (DeSanctis and Poole 1994), including how users select and use available technology, task, and other structures through their interactions and how users ascribe meaning to these structures. Although there are many potential structures, not all are appropriated (Orlikowski 2000). Appropriations may or may not be conscious or intentionally rational, but they are visible in what team members say and do (DeSanctis and Poole 1994).

The method by which structures are initially appropriated depends on the prior knowledge and experiences of the individuals in the team. Team member backgrounds and the task influence individuals’ preferences for which structures are used (Majchrzak et al. 2000, Maznevski and Chudoba 2000, Orlikowski 2000). Individuals often attempt to appropriate structures that are salient to them, based on their level of knowledge and familiarity with the structures from prior use. These prior experiences can constrain an individual’s perception of available social structures; their “behavior repertoire” is constrained by previous interactions (Poole et al. 1985, Carlson and Zmud 1999). Carlson and Zmud (1999) found that over time as individuals gained experience (e.g., experience with technology, communication partner, messaging topic, and organizational context), these experiences had a significant influence on perceptions of the appropriateness of a technology, suggesting that preferences to appropriate structures can change over time as experience changes.

Teams have many opportunities to change how they appropriate structures. Teams can choose to continue to enact the same structures or appropriate new ones to address issues such as performance expectations and effort. With repeated interactions, new or emergent structures may evolve, changing the nature of the task, team, and technology, as well as performance (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). Thus, the structures ultimately appropriated by teams can be quite different from those initially selected by the team (Majchrzak et al. 2000, Postmes et al. 2000).

Unexpected events such as performance problems (Majchrzak et al. 2000) or disruptions such as changes to task requirements (Tyre and Orlikowski 1994) often trigger changes in appropriations. Such events make the team aware of a problem and help it determine that change is warranted. When teams become aware of discrepancies or experience disruptions, they will attempt to determine how to change their behavior to address the issue (McGrath 1991). Prior research has found that when teams have discretion in choosing their own work processes, they are more likely to engage in actions to resolve problems (Tesluk and Mathieu 1999), which can result in the use of new structures (e.g., use the technology differently, change roles).

Teams that initially appropriate technology with good initial TTF (which we call “fit teams”) will likely produce acceptable outcomes (Dennis et al. 2001). Teams appropriate new structures only if they believe that the incremental gain in performance is worth the cost of changing the behaviors (Connolly and Thorn 1987). Fit teams will perceive few misalignments and discrepancies. Because they have little stimulus to seek changes, they will be less likely to change the way they have appropriated the technology; they should relatively quickly adopt a routine path of activity—a relatively consistent set of structures (Majchrzak et al. 2000, McGrath 1991, Orlikowski 1996, Tyre and Orlikowski 1994).

In contrast, teams with poor initial TTF (which we call “poor-fit teams”) will experience poor initial performance (Goodhue 1995). Poor performance and problems while performing the task will be perceived as unexpected events that will motivate teams to appropriate different structures to improve performance (Connolly and Thorn 1987, Dennis and Garfield 2003, Majchrzak et al. 2000, Orlikowski 1996). Therefore, the degree of initial fit would influence the number of appropriation changes made by the teams.

Hypothesis 1C (H1C). Initially, teams with better task-technology fit will perform fewer appropriation changes than poor-fit teams.

As tasks are performed repeatedly, behaviors evolve into a routine pattern that the team recognizes as sufficient to perform the task based on experiences and outcomes (DeSanctis and Poole 1994, Gersick and Hackman 1990, Majchrzak et al. 2000). This routine pattern becomes a default path of appropriation— a set of habitual appropriation patterns requiring little discussion (Gersick and Hackman 1990, McGrath 1991). By routinizing activities, teams reduce uncertainty, and increase effectiveness and efficiency (McGrath and Hollingshead 1994). Given no disruptions, teams will attempt to reapply these routine structures with few changes (McGrath 1991, Postmes et al. 2000). Therefore, fit teams would be less likely to have changes in their perceptions of TTF, appropriations, and performance.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). Over time, there will be little change in fit teams’ (a) decision quality, (b) time on task, (c) perceptions of TTF, and (d) appropriation changes.

Teams experiencing poor performance outcomes and unexpected events during appropriation would be more likely to negatively assess the level of task-technology fit. When allowed discretion over their team activities, they will attempt to appropriate different structures to improve fit and performance (Tesluk and Mathieu 1999). When acceptable performance outcomes do not occur, the net gains expected from appropriation changes can be noticeable, so the team will attempt to appropriate different structures in a search for better outcomes (Connolly and Thorn 1987, Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Initially, teams often exhibit many changes in appropriation as they attempt to determine a satisfactory method to perform the task, and then settle into a more stable, routine, pattern with fewer changes (Dennis and Garfield 2004, Majchrzak et al. 2000, McGrath 1991, Orlikowski 1996, Tyre and Orlikowski 1994).

Hypothesis 3 (H3). Over time, poor-fit teams will (a) improve in decision quality, (b) spend less time on task, (c) perceive improved TTF, and (d) perform fewer appropriation changes.

As poor-fit teams appropriate different structures for improvement and fit teams maintain a consistent set of appropriations (little change in appropriations performed), the differences in perceived fit between fit teams and poor-fit teams should decrease over time. These changes should also lead to a decrease in the differences in performance outcomes between fit and poor-fit teams. Ultimately, we expect that performance differences between fit and poor-fit teams will be very minimal. Thus performance differences ascribed to initial (normatively defined) levels of fit may be fleeting as teams appropriate technology, and increase their understanding of how the structures provided by the team, task, and technology interact and impact performance. As a result, it is likely that what may have been originally discounted as poorfitting technology could well become an adequate fitting technology as teams learn how to better appropriate it over time. ## Method

## Participants

Participants were recruited from a senior undergraduate accounting course at large university and received course credit. They were randomly assigned to 4-person teams, which were randomly assigned to a treatment (fit or poor-fit). Average age was 21.8; 67% were male. There were a total of 160 participants, with 20 teams per treatment. There were no all-male or allfemale teams.

## Task

Three tasks (T1, T2, T3) were performed by each team. The three tasks were a derivation of the student admissions task (Dennis 1996) in which the teams select applicants to admit to the university from a set of 10 applicants. The tasks were hidden profile in that each participant did not receive sufficient information to identify correct applicants and thus team members needed to share information. This task was used because all participants should have a familiarity with it (having gone through the process ). The task was intellective in that there was a correct answer. The task required participants to determine the relevant information and relative weighting of that information in terms of its impact on applicant selection.

The tasks were designed with help from the Office of Admissions to reflect admissions standards and assessment criteria at the participants’ university. Participants received the following information about the applicants: SAT math, SAT verbal, overall GPA, academic GPA, GPA trend, rank in school, high school quality, letters of recommendation, extracurricular activities, type of hometown, parents’ academic background, parents’ alumni status, completion of required coursework, if the high school was in state, and any college credit courses taken in high school.

For each task (T1, T2, T3), participants first worked individually to select applicants, and then worked as a team to reach a team decision. They then received feedback on which applicants should have been selected (e.g., “student A was/was not successful in college”) which enabled the participants to rethink their choices, without providing direct guidance on how to solve the task.

## Independent Variable

The independent variable, i.e., the normative, theorybased, task-technology fit, had two levels: fit and poor fit. Fit teams were defined as teams assigned to use a technology better matched to the task based on current theory-based assessments of TTF. Poor-fit teams were teams assigned technology that was not as well matched to the task. We used the definitions of Zigurs and Buckland (1998) for fit and poor-fit in a normative sense: Given a decision task requiring team members to share information, reach consensus on criteria, assess applicants using those criteria, and reach a team decision, a technology that provides higher levels of communication support and information processing support is a better fit and will enable teams to be more effective and efficient (cf. Dennis et al. 2001, McGrath and Hollingshead 1994).

Fit teams were provided communication support using Groove<sup>®</sup>, a peer-to-peer GSS that offers synchronous text messaging, and ViaVideo<sup>®</sup>, an enterprise-quality video-conferencing tool that delivers both video and audio. ViaVideo<sup>®</sup> provides very high quality audio and video (video at 30 frames per second and better-than-telephone audio at 64 kb/sec). ViaVideo<sup>®</sup> was used in continuous presence mode, so participants could view and hear all team members simultaneously. The university’s video-conferencing bridge was used, so that all sessions were centrally recorded. For information processing support, fit teams were provided with a group spreadsheet, an add-in to Groove<sup>®</sup> that enabled teams to structure data in columns and rows, and perform basic mathematical calculations. Prior research suggests that this match of technology to a decision task is a good fit (Dennis et al. 2001, Zigurs and Buckland 1998).

Poor-fit teams were provided communication support using the same Groove<sup>®</sup> synchronous text-based messaging tool, but with no video or audio conferencing. No information processing support was provided. Prior research shows that this match of technology to a decision task impedes teams so that they are less effective and efficient, and thus is considered a poor fit (Dennis et al. 2001, Zigurs and Buckland 1998).

## Dependent Variables

There were four dependent variables. Three (decision quality, task time, and appropriation changes) were measured at the team level, and one (perception of fit) at the individual level. All variables were measured for each of the three tasks (T1, T2, and T3).

Decision quality was the accuracy of the selections submitted by the team for each task (T1, T2, and T3). To develop a set of correct answers for the three tasks, two members of the school’s admissions office separately reviewed the tasks and identified which applicants would be selected for admission. The admission office members agreed on 87.5% of the applicants (Cohen’s kappa 0750). They then met and resolved differences to develop a final list of correct answers for the three tasks. Decision quality was calculated as the number of matches between the team selections and admissions experts’ answers. If a team correctly identified all 10 applicants who should and should not be admitted, it would receive the maximum score of 10. For each incorrect selection (e.g., granting admission to an applicant who should not have been admitted, or declining admission to one who should have been admitted) one point was lost.

Task time was the time in minutes that the team spent on the task from the first team member statement to final submission of a team answer as measured for each task (T1, T2, and T3). Time was measured using the time stamps on the text transcripts and the videoconference recordings.

Appropriation changes were the subset of the appropriations performed by the team (Zigurs and

Buckland 1998) indicating that the team is changing (rather than reapplying) structures (Majchrzak et al. 2000); specifically, the number of appropriations that modify an existing structure or enact a new structure (measured for T1, T2, and T3). Chudoba’s (1999) coding scheme was used to analyze the text and audio-video interactions. The coding scheme focuses on junctures, changes in team activities that indicate changes in appropriations, so the unit of analysis is the interaction thread; all statements related to a given appropriation change are coded as a single event. We used Chudoba’s electronic process (EP) category defined as changes in the way the team approaches the task as a whole, or changes in the definition of the task or task elements. For example, an interaction with the statement “Let’s go through the applicants one by one and discuss the ones where we disagree” would be coded as an EP juncture as it indicates a change about the approach to the task as a whole. The statement “Extracurricular activities are more important than letters of recommendation” would be coded as an EP juncture as it shows the team is changing its definition of task components. One coder analyzed all the data;a second coder, blind to the study objectives, independently coded 30% of the data. The two were in agreement for 96% of the observations (Cohen’s kappa <sub>=</sub> 086), indicating adequate reliability.

To better understand the appropriation changes made over time, the transcript and video codings were further examined to identify the degree that the changes undertaken were more revolutionary or equilibrium changes (Gersick 1991). Revolutionary changes have an significant impact on the deeper interaction structures enacted by the teams, meaning they make substantive changes in the way they interact using the technology. Equilibrium changes are also changes in appropriation, but are smaller in effect.

Perception of task-technology fit is defined as the degree to which individuals perceive a fit between the technology and the task. We used questionnaire items from the TTF and GSS literature, and author-developed items to measure perceived fit for communication support and information processing support (Goodhue 1998, Goodhue and Thompson 1995, Massey et al. 2001). The scales were reliable: communication support fit (9 items, - <sub>=</sub> 090) and information processing support fit (6 items, -  087).<sup>2</sup> See the supplementary materials.

## Procedures

The procedures were the same for both fit treatments except where noted below. The study consisted of two meeting sessions during one week. At the first session, participants filled out a consent form, were randomly assigned to teams, and teams randomly assigned to a treatment. The participants were placed in separate single-person rooms and provided with an introduction to the technology and the task. They then performed a training task similar to the experimental tasks to enable them to develop an initial understanding of the technology and task.

After the training task, participants were provided with the first task (T1). Participants first worked independently to identify their individual admission selections and then worked together as a team to make a team decision. After submitting the team decision, participants completed a post-task questionnaire on their perceptions of fit and then were provided with task performance feedback.

The second experimental session (tasks T2 and T3) was conducted two days later using the same procedures, with a 5 minute break between the tasks. Participants were not allowed to communicate between tasks; therefore, all communication occurred within the context of the task, allowing us to capture all appropriation changes and communications.

## Discussion

This research tested how the two primary FAM predictors of performance, TTF and appropriation, work together over time to influence team performance.

Specifically, we extended FAM by examining time as an important boundary condition on the relative predictive ability of these two components.

Our results show that there are significant limits on the influence of initial TTF on outcomes: The initial TTF mattered only for the first or second task the teams performed. Over a longer time period, appropriation becomes more important than TTF in understanding performance. In this research, over three tasks, fit teams made fewer appropriation changes and maintained consistent outcomes and perceptions, while poor-fit teams made more appropriation changes resulting in improved outcomes and perceptions. Ultimately, this mitigated the initial differences between poor-fit and fit teams so that after the third task, there were no significant differences in performance or perceptions. Fit had an initial impact on team performance, but over a relatively short period of time, appropriation diminished the initial disadvantages created by a poor TTF.

FAM provides some general conclusions about the relationship between fit and appropriation that deserve additional examination in light of the research findings herein (Dennis et al. 2001).<sup>5</sup> First, it proposes that overall, task-technology fit is a necessary but not sufficient condition for good performance; without fit, performance is likely to be poor, but the provision of fit does not by itself guarantee better performance. Second, FAM suggests that where there is no fit, the provision of appropriation support will have little impact on performance; if nonfitting tools are used, helping teams to appropriate them better won’t improve performance.

This research makes several important contributions to extend FAM and team performance research in general. First, it identifies limits for normative fit in FAM and shows how these limits in fit relate to team appropriations and team performance over time. Second, it highlights the importance of appropriation moves for team performance over longer periods of time. We discuss each of these below.

## Does Fit Matter?

In this study, we found that the initial normative level of fit mattered only fleetingly. FAM accurately predicted that the provision of a normative fit can initially cause differences in performance between fit and poor-fit teams. Therefore for new teams or teams with little experience with the technology, the level of fit between the technology and the task will be important for performance. However, contrary to FAM, poor-fit teams were able to overcome this lack of fit through appropriation. Over time, fit did not matter for team performance because poor-fit teams appropriated different technology and social structures to produce performance comparable to (and in some cases better than) fit teams.

We find that this lack of fit provided ongoing influence on the teams in the study. Fit continued to influence the types and amounts of appropriation changes attempted by individuals in the different initial fit conditions. Participants in the poor-fit teams performed significantly more appropriation changes over the course of the experiment, changing the way they worked together and used the available structures to perform the task. This focus on improvement likely caused them to be more aware of the task, the technology used to complete the task, and the manner in which they used it. This awareness led to their many appropriation changes throughout the course of the experiment, which subsequently helped improve their performance.

Therefore, a major consideration in extending FAM is the manner in which perceived fit and performance influence subsequent appropriations. The technology, the task, and the normative fit predicted by past theory remained unchanged over the tasks, but the perceived fit and performance significantly changed, as the poor-fit teams changed their appropriation.

## Does Appropriation Matter?

The analysis of the transcript coding revealed that poor-fit teams performed more revolutionary changes in that they made substantive changes in the way they interacted using the technology. For example, initially, most poor-fit teams used the technology the way they were accustomed to using it in social, dyadic communications, with short, single sentence messages to one another that generally contained one piece of information. They generally performed the first task by first identifying an applicant for discussion (usually, but not always, the first on the list). Then a team member would post an element of information about the applicant which would then be interactively discussed by all the rest of the team. After discussing that piece of information the team moved on to the next piece of posted information about the applicant. Once the team determined that they had discussed enough, they would decide whether to admit the applicant, and then move on to the next applicant. This process continued for all applicants in the task.

After receiving performance feedback on the prior task, poor-fit teams began their team interaction for the subsequent task by discussing their dissatisfaction with the time expended and poor decision quality, and exploring different methods of communicating using the technology. Most commonly, poor-fit teams appropriated different social interactions by sending longer messages with more or all of the information about the applicant. Many poor-fit teams also appropriated different interaction structures, such as not allowing any discussion about an applicant until all team members posted all of the information they knew about the applicant. They then discussed the information as a whole. Throughout these structural changes, poor-fit teams also considered and reconsidered the information elements provided in the task, discussing which elements were relevant, which were not, and which elements worked together to provide a better picture of the applicant (e.g., “rank in school is only relevant if you know how good the school is   ”).

Poor-fit teams made revolutionary changes by appropriating different social interaction structures over the technology to (1) share more information per message, and (2) by restricting interactions and until all of the information had been presented. Likewise, they enacted different structures of interest within the task itself by identifying those that would yield a better result. As a result, by T3, the majority of poorfit teams started by delaying interactions and simply entering the applicant information with no other discussion. Only afterwards did they begin discussions leading to a consensus decision.

Poor-fit teams performed more appropriation changes than fit teams in an attempt to overcome their initial fit disadvantage. Over the tasks, the number of appropriation changes decreased as teams settled on re-enacting structures that they found beneficial. Given the different amount of time elapsed between tasks, the larger change between T1 and T2 could be due to the fact that participants had more time to consider alternate courses of action, while the little time between T2 and T3 constrained some of the potential deliberation participants could have had. Although the difference in time between T1 and T2 may have magnified the difference between the treatments, most virtual teams in organizations have days (not minutes) between interactions. So while the extra time between T1 and T2 might have influenced the results, it is also likely that the lack of time between T2 and T3 muted results.

These results are consistent with what would be predicted using the lens of adaptive structuration theory (AST) (DeSanctis and Poole 1994), the other component underlying FAM. Poor-fit teams, dissatisfied with their prior interactions, enacted different interaction structures that were more appropriate given the potential structures available to them in the technology, the task, and the group. Over time, the original group norms (structures) for communication were replaced by new interaction structures, causing the resultant change in performance. As performance began to improve, we saw fewer appropriation changes as teams re-enacted the newer social structures resulting in fewer appropriation changes, and improved task performance. Thus, contrary to FAM, we see that through appropriation teams can overcome initial poor fit to generate performance. We also see that perceptions of fit, as reflected in unexpected events, may have a role in helping teams determine that appropriation changes are necessary.

Analysis of the video codings indicate that for the most part, fit teams performed T3 in the same way they performed T1, generally by going through the applicants in order of presentation, verbally discussing some of the different information they had on each applicant, entering some of the criteria into the decision support tool, and then coming to consensus on whom to grant admission. Over the tasks, fit teams also appropriated different structures which impacted the way they worked on the task, however, these changes were smaller in effect, suggesting they were more on the scale of equilibrium changes as opposed to revolutionary changes. Examples of these smaller changes include resolving the order in which the applicants would be discussed (based on prioritization criteria, the amount of dissent, or the order on the sheet), and resolving the order in which the participants would present their information to the rest of the team. Likewise, fit teams spent little time debating the importance of the information elements, simply using the same subset of the information throughout the tasks. Teams did not perceive any major unexpected events, and therefore were not as motivated to abandon prior appropriated structures which resulted in their more consistent performance levels across all three tasks. This result is consistent with the predictions of both FAM and AST. Fit teams re-enacted structures that appeared to generate adequate performance. As a result, their performance and perceptions did not significantly change over time.

In summary, we see that the results of this research are mostly consistent with FAM in the short term. As predicted by FAM, initially, team performance is strongly influenced by the level of fit between the technology and the task. Better levels of fit were found to be associated with higher levels of performance over the first set of tasks, while appropriation (or changes in use) did not appear to be associated with higher initial performance. However, contrary to FAM and consistent with AST, we see that over a longer time period, teams can change the appropriation moves they make and improve performance, regardless of a poor level of fit between the technology and the task. While initial levels of fit can influence the degree to which teams perceive the need to change, it is the appropriations undertaken over time that better predicts later team performance on task. This suggests that FAM is better applied in contexts where teams are new or working on single tasks; given that FAM was based on a meta-analysis of mostly single task studies, this is understandable. However, given a longer temporal lens, appropriation plays a strong role in predicting team performance and fit plays a smaller role.

## Implications for Research

There are several implications for future research. First, and most important, predictions from prior TTF theory and research held only for the initial task in our study; prior theory failed to predict outcomes after the teams had used the technology to perform several tasks and had had the opportunity to change the way in which they worked. We believe that this calls for a complete rethinking of what TTF means for teams. Most prior research and theory on TTF for teams— including our own work—was developed and evaluated via the study of a single use of a technology (e.g., Dennis et al. 2001, Zigurs and Buckland 1998). Teams in such studies have no opportunities to assess fit after the task, and revise the social structures they use. New theories of TTF need to consider how teams adapt to poor fit over time.

Prior research has focused on identifying the technology characteristics that can impact task performance. Our findings suggest that additional research is necessary to understand the appropriation moves and adaptations that may impact performance. It has been shown that facilitation to support faithful appropriation of well-fitting collaboration technologies can yield better performance (Dennis et al. 2001, Wheeler and Valacich 1996). But, how do individuals identify problems from poor-fitting technology and act on them? How does TTF, or the lack of it, facilitate or constrain the appropriation of the available technical and social structures (cf. Majchrzak et al. 2001). Additional research can help to understand how teams recognize problems and “self-facilitate” to appropriate structures and technology features to improve performance (Tesluk and Mathieu 1999).

This research also has implications for TTF research beyond the team context. We found fit to have important initial impacts but few long-term effects on team performance. Is this same pattern true for other collaborative technologies such as knowledge management systems, decision support systems, and so on? We believe more research on TTF over longer time periods is called for.

Additionally, research on performance in virtual teams must consider a larger task context, not only the task and technology, but also of the individuals, their level of knowledge and understanding of each other, the task, and the technology and their interactions over time. Our research highlights the fact that individuals, particularly on the poor-fit teams, changed their perceptions of the technology and the manner in which they appropriated it over time. However, given a similar opportunity to develop similar experience bases, fit teams did not change in the same way (if at all). This finding suggests an extension to research that examines the use of technologies over time. For example, Carlson and Zmud’s (1999) Channel Expansion Theory suggests that experiential bases are important in guiding and enhancing individuals’ perception of a communication channel. It may be that that the development of these experiential bases requires a certain level of engagement that was likely triggered by the problems experienced in poor-fit teams. Given the interest in improving virtual team outcomes, research should consider the larger context, interventions, and task or technology designs that can stimulate attention and experience development to help individuals perceive and appropriate technology features.

## Implications for Practice

We believe that this research also has several implications for practice. The results suggest important considerations about systems selection, systems deployment, and change management, particularly for technology-mediated teams such as virtual teams. Conventional wisdom suggests that when considering a new system, managers should seek to maximize the fit between a system’s features and the needs. However, the results of this study indicate that the system with the best initial fit may not lead to better performance over a longer term.

For systems with standard processes and few options for social appropriation (e.g. transaction processing systems) it makes sense to consider the system with the highest degree of fit. However, for systems to support teams (and perhaps other forms of knowledge work) which have higher levels of autonomy and various available social structures, it may be better to find a system that allows flexibility in the features and processes it provides rather than a system whose feature list meets the current requirements. This is not to say that the system does not need to meet the current requirements, but rather that it has the capability to support a variety of ways of working. This research suggests that depending on the duration and expected repetition of the tasks to be performed, system selection criteria solely on the basis of fit may not be the best method for longer term performance, but consideration of the potential manner in which the task can be performed (future appropriations) is important.

This study also suggests certain considerations for systems deployment. It highlights the need for pilot deployment strategies, particularly where collaboration technologies are concerned. Because the performance of the systems can be significantly influenced by the social appropriations and adaptation techniques implemented during their use, pilot testing these systems can provide managers with ideas and suggestions for training to accelerate the successful use of these systems (Dennis et al. 2001). Early pilots of collaboration technology can become experiments to study and determine the best appropriation methods that lead to best practices and performance.

Finally, this research provides insights for practitioners about strategies for change management. It may be difficult to predict the end state of collaboration systems use and the social appropriations by individuals to use them. Therefore, the focus should be on the transition path that these systems take as they evolve in their use. Although the use of pilot deployment strategies can provide insight on the initial set of changes that system use may undergo, over a longer term, other strategies may be necessary to promote appropriate use of collaboration technology or to manage ongoing appropriation processes to minimize the likelihood of divergent outcomes. As FAM argues, training on how to appropriate technology may be the most important type of training for teams; organizations may be better served to train users on how to recognize discrepancies or misalignments that indicate new processes or different technology capabilities might be necessary. Much of the technical training provided today is what could be called “fit” training, where individuals are taught the features of the technology, and how to use them to perform certain defined tasks. Very little training is provided on how to be creative and develop new ways to work using technology or how to change behaviors to improve performance.

Therefore, we believe that it is important to include appropriation training that provides individuals with knowledge on how to recognize the need for change (e.g., discrepancies and misalignments) and how to implement change. This may minimize the negative effects of routine development, where individuals cease to recognize changes in the environment that require attention and a change in behavior (Gersick and Hackman 1990). It can also provide consistency and guidance in the types of appropriations attempted by individuals.

Organizational decisions on technology acquisition, deployment, and change manage have traditionally been based on the concept of fit. This research shows that this perspective may have limitations, particularly in light of newer technologies and tasks that are much more malleable and subject to change. Managers and IT workers can benefit by considering the flexibility of new systems and the need for flexibility in newer forms of work such as knowledge work.

These implications for research and practice should be tempered by limitations inherent in this research. First, this research used a specific type of technology applied to a specific type of task. While we would expect that improvement over time through appropriation could occur across different contexts, it may be that this task and technology is especially sensitive to this phenomena and that other contexts may not experience the same results in the same way. Second, this research involved student participants that may not be generalizable to other populations. Although a prior pilot of this research with graduate students indicated results similar to those presented here, care must be taken when generalizing these results to other populations that may have different levels of knowledge or experience using these types of technologies. Third, the effect size for power used in this research was 0.41, which could be considered a moderate effect. While this effect size was taken from the original analysis performed by Dennis et al. (2001), we recognize that it is larger than the typical, conservative 0.25 effect size typically used for power. We chose this effect size to be consistent with the prior research that this study is intended to test and extend, but recognize it as a potential limitation. Finally, we recognize that our manipulation, level of fit, was only partially successful. The manipulation check indicates that participants perceived different levels of information processing support, but not communication support, which is likely because of the high level of experience participants had with this type of communication technology. However, we still see an effect from the treatment, suggesting that even though the differences in the perceptions of communication support fit were small between the treatments they were large enough to trigger appropriation changes. It also may be that performance outcomes (for which there were significant differences) play a larger role in triggering appropriation changes than do perceptions of fit. Our review of the transcripts and video suggests that poor fit teams made many more changes in the manner in which they appropriated the medium. As a result we believe that their answer likely reflects the fact that they felt they could use the medium, even though it required more work than those who were in the fit condition.

In summary, the results of this study support prior findings in the FAM meta-analysis indicating that initially, teams using poor-fit technologies perform worse than teams using better fitting technologies. However, this research extends those findings by showing that for a collaboration technology, the differences between poor-fit and fit teams quickly dissipate through social appropriations, and that, ultimately, poor-fit teams can be as effective and efficient as fit teams, and perceive similar levels of fit between the technology and the task. In this research, poorfit teams improved while fit teams stayed about the same throughout the study.

The results of this research highlight the limitations in prior research in its consideration of fit at only a given point in time, and extend the FAM by suggesting that the nature of appropriations attempted by teams has a stronger impact on ongoing performance, especially for repeated tasks. A longer view of fit shows that fit can be dynamic, and influenced by individuals’ knowledge, familiarity, and experience with the task, and the technology. This research suggests that fit may be most useful as a predictor of performance in tasks that are constrained by either the features available in the technology or the behavioral options available to the individuals. It also highlights the importance of the social appropriations that can occur over a longer term repeated task, as fit may not matter as much as individuals’ abilities to recognize discrepancies and appropriate different social structures to achieve better performance.
