## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online reviews Manager response Service recovery Response strategy Laboratory experiments

## A B S T R A C T

Previous studies provide mixed conclusions on the prioritization of response resources allocation. This paper examines the relative importance of three targeting strategies—full response, random response, and responding to extreme online reviews. It also explores the two levels of response tailoring (high vs. low) on consumers purchase likelihood by taking review valance into account. For those firms with sufficient budget, results from three laboratory experiments indicate the following; responding to every negative online review using hightailored responses, and selectively responding to positive online reviews, using low-tailored responses is an optimal configuration. Whereas, for firms with limited budget, responding to extreme negative online reviews using high-tailored responses is the optimal configuration

## 1. Introduction

Customers increasingly rely on online reviews to make purchasing decisions [24,34,45]. Statistics show that more than 90 % of online customers read online reviews before making purchase decisions. This is because they perceive such reviews as neutral or reliable information references [23,38]. In a survey conducted by market analysis firm Cone, 87 % of respondents reported that positive online reviews facilitate their purchasing decisions, whereas 80 % reported that negative online re views have led them to change their minds [21]. This provides strong incentives for firms to shape customer opinion and thereby influence their online ratings. Managerial responses have been found to be one of the most effective ways to shape customer opinion [28,59,77]. Mana gerial responses refer to a firm’s efforts to publicly interact with and respond to customer comments on a third-party platform [28].

The presence of managerial response affects consideration sets, customer satisfaction, trust, and future review volume and valance, which ultimately impacts on firm sales [11,29,41,59,64]. Lui, Bartosiak, Piccoli and Sadhya [49] found a positive relationship between response frequency and firm sales, implying that firms should respond to each customer review individually. However, asserting a full response strat egy—one in which a manager responds to every review—is likely too costly for most firms due to the exponential growth of online reviews. In the face of budget constraints, firms will need to respond in a selective manner and determine which types of reviews they need to target and how tailored their responses must be.

In terms of whom to target, previous studies provide conflicting rec ommendations. Some studies propose that managerial response should concentrate on extreme reviews (those online reviews with the lowest and highest evaluations). This is because they found the effect of responding to extreme reviews on firms’ competitive performance to be stronger than when responding to neutral reviews [28,49]. Other studies suggest that responding to extreme reviews (especially negative ones) can be harmful. In so doing, this can encourage further negative reviews, as well as generate far more detailed negative reviews from consumers [12,51].

In terms of how to respond to customer reviews, (and to save time and costs), those firms with insufficient response resources, normally respond to a large number of reviews by providing standard responses drawn from a fixed set of templates. However, there is growing disdain toward template responses among consumers who may perceive oper ators as perfunctory and insincere in addressing customer opinions. Furthermore, the effect of managerial response on customer satisfaction might be negative if responses are inadequate to resolve the customer’s problem [77]. Therefore, recent studies argue that firms should customize managerial response to the contents of a corresponding review [83]. Nevertheless, whether customized managerial responses increase the sales performance remains unclear. Supporters suggest that customized managerial responses address the specific concerns raised by consumers and increase the perception of a conversational human voice [16,56]. Opponents suggest that customized managerial response to positive reviews may lead to psychological reactance, as it reiterates the merits proposed by the reviewer [22,54].

Integrating service recovery literature, affect delivery literature, and Psychological Reactance Theory, this study attempts to accommodate the conflicting views discussed above by taking review valance and response customization into account. We suggest that consumer’s in terpretations toward managerial responses are jointly determined by review valence and the extent of customization of the response. Con sumers express their concerns on service failures by posting negative reviews. In online social media, service recovery typically takes the form of management responses and consumer’s concerns toward failures can be attenuated after receiving management responses [28,41]. For negative reviews, a customized response makes consumers feel that firms pay attention to their concerns [77]. Therefore, a customized response to negative reviews demonstrates a high-quality service re covery that facilitates consumer’s consumption likelihood. Affective delivery refers to an operator’s act of expressing socially desirable emotions during service transactions [2]. According to affective delivery literature, managerial response to positive reviews may facilitate con sumer’s consumption likelihood by showing warmth and gratitude in service. However, studies drawing on Psychological Reactance Theory arrive at competing conclusions. Psychological Reactance Theory argues that when people realize that a free behavior is restricted, they are motivated to change their attitudes and behaviors to restore their freedom and autonomy [4,6]. For positive reviews, where consumers praise certain aspects of the service providers, a highly customized response may focus on that specific topic and reiterate the merits raised by consumers [83]. Hence, a customized response to positive reviews could be interpreted as a promotional message that impedes customers freedom of expression [18,76]. Therefore, a customized response to positive reviews may stimulate psychological reactance that inhibits consumer's consumption likelihood.

Based on an overview of the extant literature, we introduce the notion of response tailoring to capture the practice of customizing managerial response to the contents of a corresponding review [77]. This was then divided into high-and-low-tailoring conditions based on the extent of content similarity between managerial response and the corresponding review. We argue that consumers may respond positively to high-tailored responses to negative online reviews, this being consistent with Affect Delivery Theory [69].Yet, they respond nega tively to high-tailored responses to positive online reviews, this being consistent with Psychological Reactance Theory [6]. These opposing mechanisms suggest that the relative importance of response strategies (full vs. selective response) and how to respond to online reviews (high vs. low tailoring) vary with review valance.

To validate these ideas, we conducted two laboratorial experiments. In Study 1, we divide firms’ response strategies into full and selective categories. Selective response strategies were further divided into two conditions: responding to extreme reviews and randomly responding. The relative importance of various response strategies were compared in both positive and negative review scenarios. Study 2 manipulated highand-low levels of response tailoring. The effects of high-and-lowresponse tailoring on consumer’s consumption intention were compared in both positive and negative review scenarios.

The current study informs theory and practice in several ways. First, previous literature on managerial response has mainly focused on which types of online management responses (apology, defensive, excuse, etc.) are more likely to attract consumers and enhance business performance [41,61,64,70]. We extend this strand of literature by investigating whom to respond to when under budget constraints. Second, we show that the relative importance of response strategies varies with review valance, offering a potential explanation for the divergent findings in the literature [12,49,51]. Third, our analysis on response tailoring adds to the growing prevalence of exploring the effect of response contents on the quality of managerial response [50].

## 2. Theoretical framework

Our study draws on three areas of research: (1) managerial response and consumer purchasing decisions, (2) the underlying mechanism of managerial response, and (3) response strategies under budget con straints. In the subsequent subsections, we discuss each of these areas.

## 2.1. Managerial response and consumer purchasing decisions

Managerial responses to online customer reviews not only affect customers who receive the responses, but may also influence subsequent customers who observe the responses. Managerial responses to less satisfied customers can increase satisfaction and managerial responses to highly satisfied customers can foster loyalty [11]. The literature presents a general consensus that effective managerial responses in crease consumers’ purchase likelihood by building positive brand im ages and increasing future ratings. Studies have found that effective managerial response leads to favorable customer inference of trust worthiness and caring, thereby, helping to build brand image [3,64]. Managerial responses encourage consumers to post more positive re views but fewer negative reviews, as consumers who post positive re views, consider their feedback is appreciated. In contrast, those who post negative reviews know that their feedback will be scrutinized [59]. Proserpio and Zervas [59] studied the impact of management response and found a 0.12-star increase in ratings and a 12 % increase in review volume for responding hotels. Other studies have found that a 1-star drop in Yelp ratings translates to a 5%–9% decrease in a restaurant’s revenue, while an extra half-star rating can lead to a 49 % increase in a restaurant’s revenue [1,48]. Whilst the positive relationship between managerial response and consumer purchase behavior has been widely acknowledged, scholars have offered divergent theoretical explanations for the underlving mechanisms behind this reality. In the following section, we review related literature concerning how managerial response can predict consumer purchase behavior.

## 2.2. Underlying mechanism of managerial response

## 2.2.1. Responding to negative reviews: service recovery

Customers normally express their dissatisfaction by posting negative reviews online via social media platforms or forums. In an online envi ronment, managerial response to a negative review is an example of complaint management or service recovery. Managers can offer a vari ety of resources when responding to negative reviews, ranging from an apology to financial compensation. Also, managers respond to negative reviews to reassure customers that their experience as depicted in the review is unlikely to be repeated [12]. According to service recovery literature, customer behaviors are the salient consequences of their overall psychological evaluations of a service provider’s endeavors in responding to negative reviews [46,53]. Moreover, consumers who leave negative reviews and receive a response from service providers are more likely to perceive the operator as trustworthy and experience greater satisfaction [28]. Studies have also found that effective mana gerial response leads to favorable customer inference of trustworthiness and caring, thereby influencing consumer purchase decisions [3,64]. Because managerial response is publicly available online and will be viewed by potential customers, readers of online reviews can form a perception of the operator’s customer orientation strategies. This can also facilitate purchasing behavior.

## 2.2.2. Responding to positive reviews: affect delivery or psychologica reactance

The affective delivery literature argues that firms can increase the number of returning consumers by showing warmth and gratitude [69]. Affective delivery benefits service providers in several ways, for example, a higher level of customer satisfaction [7], better service quality evaluation [60], and positive feelings toward the service pro viders and their perceived friendliness [69]. Following this logic, oper ators may respond to positive reviews to facilitate consumer repurchase behavior.

Another line of thought, which derives from Psychological Reactance Theory, arrives at competing conclusions. According to Psychological Reactance literature, when responding to online reviews, there are two boundary conditions of affective delivery which firms should be aware of. They are, review valence and response tailoring. Wang and Chaudhry [77] suggest that managerial response to positive reviews has a negative effect on subsequent ratings because consumers view managerial response to positive reviews as a promotional activity. This argument has been confirmed by Zhang, Qiao, Yang and Zhang [83], who argued that “For positive reviews, where consumers praise certain aspects of their experiences, managers might focus on the specific topic and continue to respond to the corresponding advantages associated with the experiences. By doing this, they make potential review readers confirm the meritorious aspects and strengthen the intention to pur chase (p.4)”. According to Psychological Reactance Theory, when peo ple are subjected to strong promotional attempts they may, in turn respond negatively [19]. Thus, the effect of selective response strategy on consumer purchase behavior may outweigh that of full response strategy due to psychological reactance behavior. On the other hand, Groth, Hennig-Thurau and Walsh [27] found that customers’ affect to ward a firm may be negatively influenced if the service provider’s af fective delivery is perceived to be disingenuous, such as in the case of “shallow acting.” Providing standard responses drawn from a fixed set of templates is one example of disingenuous affect delivery, which high lights the importance of response tailoring.

## 2.3. Response strategies under budget constraints

When compared with no response, a significant number of studies have verified that having a certain level of managerial response leads to higher subsequent rating and sale performance [11,12,49,59,85]. Lui, Bartosiak, Piccoli and Sadhya [49] provide explicit evidence that the number of managerial responses is positively associated with sale per formance. However, merely responding is not enough, as improper re sponses can backfire. Instead, firms need to respond to the right type of reviews with the right style and review volume. Albeit, this effect may decrease as response length increases [11]. When responding to nega tive reviews, managers need to be specific by either apologizing or disclosing the improvement of future actions [70]. Following this logic, the conventional wisdom is to respond to each review (full response strategy) and customize each response as much as possible, so as to enjoy the benefits of managerial response.

At present, an overwhelming majority of reviews do not receive re sponses, especially negative ones [42]. Even for those reviews with re sponses, few offer justifications relevant to the reviews. This may be because the volume of consumer reviews grows dramatically as a busi ness expands. Also, the practice of offering a customized response to every review is likely too costly or too ineffective for most firms. Therefore, firms should respond in a selective manner, because their resources being dedicated to managerial response are limited. Foremost, they must decide how and to which reviews to respond. The following is a discussion of possible targeting strategies and the ways to respond to reviews whilst under budget constraints. We searched related studie published in the last 5 years (from June 2014 to July 2019) on Web of Science. Specifically, we first searched the literature using the phrase “online review” combined with “managerial response” as keywords. The results were then narrowed using the keyword phrases “response strat egy” or “targeting strategy.” Finally, we restricted our focus to market ing and information systems (IS) journals and identified a total of nine studies as most relevant to the current study. These nine studies are summarized in Table 1.

## 2.3.1. Targeting strategies

When considering targeting strategies to meet response budget constraints, Wang and Chaudhry [77] and Herhausen, Ludwig, Grewal, Wulf and Schoegel [29] advocate prioritizing response to negative re views. Herhausen, Ludwig, Grewal, Wulf and Schoegel [29] suggest that each negative online review has the potential to become an online firestorm, yet, there are exceptions and this is not the case for every online review. Proserpio and Zervas [59] also found that managerial response to negative reviews increased subsequent rating and the effect of responding to positive reviews on subsequent ratings was negative. They argue that managerial response to negative reviews increase the cost of leaving a negative review. This is because reviewers know that their feedback will be scrutinized. Nevertheless, other studies have revealed that managerial response strategies which focus on responding to negative reviews actually encourage further and more detailed negative reviews from consumers. This lowers subsequent average rat ings [12,51]. A widely used response to those customers with negative experiences, is to grant them compensations, for example, discounts, coupons, cash rewards, etc. However, Ma, Sun and Kekre [51] found that redress-seeking is a major driver of complaints, and managerial responses that focus on responding to negative reviews may actually encourage future negative reviews.

In terms of whom to target under budget constraints, Gu and Ye [28] propose targeting low-satisfaction customers because managerial re sponses can have a greater positive impact on these customers than on ordinary customers. This finding has been further replicated by Sheng, Amankwah-Amoah, Wang and Khan [63]. Lui, Bartosiak, Piccoli and Sadhya [49] had similar findings to those of Gu and Ye [28] and Sheng, Amankwah-Amoah, Wang and Khan [63] argue that responding to extreme reviews is more effective than a random response strategy. Hu, Lin, Qian and Sun [33] suggest that influential customers (i.e., con sumers who have large numbers of followers) outperform randomly responding as promotional targets. A random response strategy in dicates that managers respond to consumer reviews without a clearly defined pattern. This makes it difficult for prospective consumers to create a perception of the products and to vicariously test the quality of those products before consumption. Based on the extant literature, we abstracted and categorized response strategies empirically [11,28,49, 77] and identified the following managerial response archetypes:

• Non-response strategy (NRS): the manager never responds to con sumer online reviews. Service providers devote zero effort and re sources to influence electronic word-of-mouth communication (e-WOM) when adopting an NRS.

• Full response strategy (FRS): the manager responds indiscriminately to all online comments in an effort to signal attention to all cus tomers. Service providers devote maximum effort and resources to influence e-WOM if adopting an FRS.

• Selective response strategy (SRS): the manager selectively responds to consumer reviews. Service providers devote moderate effort and resources to influence e-WOM if adopting an SRS.

a Respond to extreme reviews (SRS-E): the manager selectively re sponds to extreme comments (those reviews with the lowest and highest evaluations).

b Random response strategy (SRS-R): the manager displays no discernible response strategy and responds to customer comments seemingly at will.

## 2.3.2. Response tailoring

Studies have long asserted that the tone of a managers response to reviews can predict and influence business performance. [61,64,70]. A significant number of studies have explored how the tone of managerial response can predict business performance [61,64,70]. When an accused party responds to negative reviews using an analytical format, an apologetic voice leads to greater perceived integrity; when using a narrative format, a denial voice leads to greater perceived integrity [70]. Sparks, So and Bradley [64] argue that, compared with responses in an official tone, managerial responses with a conversational, human voice are more effective. Rose and Blodgett [61] found that responses with assurance of future or corrective action are equally effective. Other studies argue that a timely response yields more favorable customer inference [63,64,78].

A recent line of inquiry has focused more explicitly on how response tailoring; the practice of customizing managerial response to the con tents of a corresponding review, influences business performance [44, 77]. The merits of managerial tailoring have been noticed. High-tailoring managerial responses provide additional useful infor mation for prospective consumers [77] and can increase the perception of a conversational human voice [16]. Furthermore, a specific response that addresses a consumer’s issues or demonstrates empathy can culti vate prospective consumers’ trust and improve the perceived quality of communication [56]. Conversely, a generic or standardized response which appears less relevant to the customer review, expresses a message that the service provider deals with customer complaints in a perfunc tory way. Overall, studies have concluded that specific responses are more effective than generic responses when dealing with negative re views [44,56].

The above studies focus on the direct influence of response tailoring on business performance. The moderating effect of response tailoring, however, has received little attention in the literature. The effectiveness of a given managerial response may vary according to the level of tailoring. Wang and Chaudhry [77] argue that response tailoring am plifies the positive effect of managerial response to negative reviews as it equates to higher-quality complaint management. However, response tailoring attenuates the effect of managerial response to positive re views. Tailored responses to positive reviews can give people the impression as though the manager is replicating and flaunting the merits proposed by the reviewer. As a consequence, this can lead to greater likelihood of reactance. Therefore, in the next sections we attempt to explore how the effectiveness of managerial response varies with the extent of response tailoring.

## 3. Hypotheses development

We formulated our hypotheses to investigate the effects of response strategies and response tailoring on consumers’ consumption likelihood. First, when predicting consumers’ consumption likelihood, we compared the effects of responding to negative reviews alone, with those of responding to positive reviews alone. Next, we focused on the relative importance of response strategies, taking review valance into account.

Finally, we examined the moderating role of response tailoring.

## 3.1. Full response vs. Selective response

Consumers typically express their concerns about specific product or services by leaving negative reviews. The presence of managerial response creates a dynamic and interactive communication between firms and consumers. Responding to negative reviews has long been regarded as an effective strategy for complaint management [57,58,61, 64]. Selectively responding to negative reviews and focusing on neither extreme reviews nor random reviews may lead consumers to speculate that negative reviews with no response, are honest. Also, and impor tantly, they consider by not addressing these negative responses, the firm is involuntarily acknowledging these failings. This inference negatively predicts consumers’ consumption intention. Individuals often look to others as a reference to develop a sense of fairness [31]. Owing to the public nature of online management responses, consumers may find the practice of complaint management unfair, as it merely focuses on a subset of negative reviews. Gu and Ye [28] discovered that customers who do not receive online management responses whilst observing others who do, experience decreased satisfaction with the service provider. Therefore, we expect that when responding to negative online reviews. implementing a full response strategy will lead to greater consumption likelihood than using a selective response strategy. We thus posit the following hypothesis:

H1. When responding to negative reviews, the effect of using a full response strategy on increasing consumers’ consumption likelihood is stronger than using selective response strategies.

Studies highlighting the importance of responding to positive re views argued that firms may establish positive affect and loyalty in their customers by expressing gratitude and warmth [30,52,69]. After receiving feedback from the service provider, consumers who post positive reviews may feel that their feedback is appreciated and conse quently form positive attitudes toward the provider’s products and/or services.

Customers express their satisfaction and highlight the merits of ser vice providers by posting positive online reviews. From the perspective of customer relationship management, responding to positive online reviews is less imperative than responding to negative online reviews. Instead, responding to positive online reviews is more likely to be interpreted as a marketing behavior to influence further customer opinions. This argument echoes Wang and Chaudhry [77], who claim “an MR-N (managerial response to negative reviews) is typically construed as adding value, but an MR-P (managerial response to positive reviews) can be interpreted as a promotional activity (p.164).” The theory of psychological reactance indicates that when people are sub jected to strong persuasive attempts, they may resist the attempt and even move in the opposite direction [5]. The effect of responding to positive online reviews might be negative due to psychological reactance to a firm’s promotional activities.

When responding to positive reviews, consumers’ skepticism may be triggered once they noticed the firm’s marketing motive. Once con sumers sense that a message has a marketing intent, they will no longer consider the message impartial [80]. This can cause consumers to develop feelings of skepticism and doubt the message claims or mistrust the marketers’ motives [9]. Although responding to positive reviews may convey gratitude and warmth, the heightened skepticism will prompt consumers to defend themselves against that gratitude and warmth. McCoy, Everard, Galletta and Moody [55] discovered that perceived skepticism of a promotional activity increases as the fre quency of exposure to that activity also increases concurrently. A full response strategy exhibits higher frequency of promotional attempts than selective response strategies. Therefore, selective response strate gies prevail over full response strategy in predicting consumer’s con sumption likelihood. This is because selective response strategies induce less skepticism. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. When responding to positive reviews, the effect of using selective response strategies on increasing consumers’ consumption likelihood is stronger than if using a full response strategy.

## 3.2. Responding to extreme reviews vs. random response

Hu, Pavlou and Zhang [35] determined that customer reviews display a J-shaped distribution with mostly 5-star ratings and a small number of 1-star ratings. The J-shaped distribution of consumer reviews may be rooted in the fact that prospective customers with lower valu ations are less likely to purchase the product. Whereas, the customer with extreme ratings are more likely to write reviews as compared to those customers with moderate reviews [35]. With most reviews being positive, a 1-star review (extreme negative review) has greater distinc tion visually and is more noticeable [32,40]. Moreover, consumers generally seek different opinions on the same product prior to con sumption [67]. Extreme negative reviews offer unique information by presenting the “other side of the argument.” [10] Managerial response to an extreme negative review is more likely to be noticed by potential consumers. On the other hand, the influence of managerial response differs with the severity of service failure and the degree of perceived injustice [49]. Furthermore, extreme negative reviews that reflect the highest level of service failure should be emphasized. Similar conclu sions were drawn by Gu and Ye [28] who argue that managerial re sponses have a more positive impact on low-satisfaction customers and therefore managerial response should be focused on extreme negative reviews compared with random negative reviews. Thus, we posit the following hypothesis:

H3. When responding to negative reviews, the effect of responding to extreme reviews on increasing consumers’ consumption likelihood is stronger than using a random response strategy.

The relative importance of responding to extreme reviews versus a random response strategy might be reversed when responding to posi tive reviews. As we discussed in Section 2.2.2, managerial response to positive reviews can be viewed as a promotional activity. Prior studie have asserted that consumers’ skepticism about a promotional message can be influenced by the extremity of the message [17]. An extremely positive review conveys unreserved positive attitudes and results in consumers’ doubts of that reviews objectivity. In contrast, a positive, yet less extreme review is relatively conservative and authentic [80]. Con sumers may exhibit higher skepticism toward managerial response to extreme positive reviews than managerial response to less extreme positive reviews. When compared with responding only to extreme positive reviews, random responses may involve less extreme positive reviews. Consequently, this can induce less skepticism. Therefore, we expect that randomly responding to positive reviews leads to greater consumption likelihood compared with responding to extreme positive reviews. Thus, we posit the following hypothesis:

H4. When responding to positive reviews, the effect of using a random response strategy on increasing consumers’ consumption likelihood is stronger than when responding only to extreme reviews.

## 3.3. Response tailoring

Response tailoring refers to the practice of customizing a response to the contents of the corresponding review [77]. As discussed above, responding to negative reviews is generally regarded as a viable complaint management strategy. We argue that a high-tailored mana gerial response to negative reviews reflects a higher quality complaint management practice [65]. A more tailored response reflects the con tents of a review and is more likely to address the specific complaints raised by the consumer [77]. In addition, tailoring responses builds prospective consumers’ trust and encourages positive attitudes toward a firm [44,56]. On the other hand, managerial responses with less tailoring suggest that a firm handles consumer complaints in a perfunctory way. This further influence consumers’ consumption de cisions. Therefore, we expect that response tailoring positively moder ates the relationship between responding to negative reviews and consumers’ consumption likelihood. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5. When responding to negative reviews, the effect of using high tailored managerial responses on increasing consumers’ consumption likelihood is stronger than using low-tailored managerial responses.

In terms of responding to positive reviews, we argue that response tailoring negatively moderates the relationship between managerial response and consumers’ consumption likelihood. Due to the public nature of online interactions, firms respond to positive online reviews to please consumers who write those reviews and present positive images to consumers scrutinizing those responses [11]. High-tailored responses to positive reviews satisfy the consumers who write the reviews and also arouse the observer’s psychological reactance. High-tailored responses to positive reviews is largely regarded as a promotional activity, as it reiterates the merits proposed by the reviewer [54,77]. This redundancy of information makes the manager’s response to positive reviews more unfavorable, leading to increased reactance [22]. The effect of responding to positive reviews on consumers’ consumption likelihood, might be impaired because of the observers’ psychological reactance to high-tailored responses to positive reviews. Based on the above discus sion, we propose the following hypothesis

H6. When responding to positive reviews, the effect of using lowtailored managerial responses on increasing consumers’ consumption likelihood is stronger than using high-tailored managerial responses.

## 4. Research methodology

We conducted two laboratory experiments to test our hypotheses. Study 1 was designed to test H1–H4, while Study 2 was designed to test H5 and H6. Study 1 took place in December 2018. For Study 1, we recruited 181 undergraduate students from an introductory e-commerce course at a large public university in Western China. Study 2 was con ducted three months later in March 2019. We recruited subjects for Study 2 with the assistance of the university’s student union, who publicized the experiment through e-mails and posters. Participation was voluntary, and we secured a total of 89 undergraduate and graduate students for Study 2. In both studies, we asked the subjects to disclose their e-mail addresses for further contact. Next, we manually reviewed our database for duplicate e-mails to ensure that no subjects participated in both experiments.

According to a statistical report on China, over 80 % of the online shopping population are aged between 18–30 years, and more than half of them are college students.<sup>1</sup> Additionally, studies have found the on line behavior of college students does not differ from that of the general population [36,81]. Therefore, college students are appropriate subjects for our research.

## 4.1. Study 1: whom to target

## 4.1.1. Stimulus materials

We conducted a lab experiment to test the hypotheses using reviews of a restaurant sourced from a major review website. We consider two selective response strategies, response to extreme reviews and random response. The effect of full response is compared with response to extreme reviews and random response, respectively. Study 1 adopted a between-subjects’ design: response to negative reviews vs. response to positive reviews and full response vs. random response vs. response to extreme reviews. The experiment resulted in six conditions and subjects were randomly assigned to one of the six scenarios. For all six condi tions, an experimental webpage for a fictional restaurant was created. A hypothesized restaurant name was used to avoid any potential con founding effects of previous brand involvement. We ensured that the information presented on the experimental page was similar to the in formation presented on real e-commerce platforms. A total of 16 reviews with mixed positive and negative ratings (12 positive ratings and 4 negative ratings) were presented. Consumers often deem recent reviews to be more relevant because these are often displayed first and our manipulated reviews were presented as the most up-to-date of the focal restaurant [20,79]. In most e-commerce platforms, products are rated based on a 1–5 rating scale, with 4- and 5-star ratings regarded as pos itive, 1- and 2-star ratings deemed negative, and 3-star ratings typically viewed as neutral. Since the current paper focuses on the effects of both positive and negative reviews, neutral reviews were removed in our experimental environment. Therefore, in our experiment, the 16 manipulated reviews included four 5-star ratings, eight 4-star ratings, two 2-star ratings, and two 1-star ratings.

Two conditions were manipulated in Study 1. The restaurant was manipulated to only respond to negative reviews in the first condition and only to respond to positive reviews in the second condition. In terms of the manipulation of response strategies, each review was responded to by the operator, reflecting the full response strategy. In the random response condition, we randomly selected and responded to four posi tive reviews and two negative reviews. In the last condition, operators addressed only extreme reviews, responding to four 5-star ratings and two 1-star ratings.

To best control and manage the influence of response speed on consumers’ consumption likelihood, all reviews were dated no later than four weeks prior to the experiment. These were listed in descending order by posting date. Previous studies have shown that the quantity and order of the reviews influence purchase intentions [26,37,49,84]. To rule out alternative explanations, the order of all reviews and the number of reviews responded to were the same across the six conditions. We also controlled the reputation of the manipulated restaurants. The ratio of positive reviews to negative reviews is widely used to measure a seller’s reputation in e-commerce settings and therefore remained un changed across the six scenarios [52,61]. Lui, Bartosiak, Piccoli and Sadhya [49] explicitly showed that the number of managerial responses positively predicts sales performance, therefore the number of mana gerial responses was kept constant in all six scenarios. We also controlled the need for cognition, which refers to an individual’s tendency to engage in and enjoy thinking [8]. We drew three items from Tam and Ho [68] in our experiment: “I prefer to do something that challenges my thinking abilities rather than something that requires minimal thought,” “I try to anticipate and avoid situations where there is likely a chance I will have to think in depth about something,” and “I like to have the responsibility of handling a situation that requires a lot of thinking.” Since a manipulated Shaanxi cuisine restaurant was used in our exper iments, consumption likelihood toward the manipulated restaurant was influenced by the participants’ preferences toward Shaanxi cuisine. We therefore developed a 1-item scale (“How do you like Shaanxi cuisine?”) to rule out that bias. Participants were screened out if they answered negatively. All items were rated based on a 9-point Likert scale.

## 4.1.2. Procedures and participants

After the participants browsed the manipulated webpage, they were asked to close the browser and rate their likelihood to dine at the restaurant on a 9-point Likert scale. Participants were asked to rate to what extent they agreed with the following statements: “I would be likely to dine at this restaurant,” “I look forward to trying this restau rant,” and “I would be willing to try this restaurant the next time I am dining out.” These items were adapted from the work of Jiang and Benbasat [39].

Participants of Study 1 included 181 undergraduate students in an introductory e-commerce course at a large public university in Western China. According to the power analysis for a between-subject design, this sample size (i.e., 19–37 subjects in each condition) assures a suffi cient statistical power of 0.8 for a medium effect size (f = 0.25) [15]. The entire experiment lasted approximately 20 min. Participation in the experiment was voluntary, and a guaranteed course credit was provided to each participant. As a cover story, participants were introduced to a fictitious third-party review site (xian-gastronome.com) which provided consumer reviews of local restaurants. The cover story explained that the researchers were working with the site to improve its interface design and that students were needed to aid in this process. Before the experimental tasks, the participants were told that 50 % of them would receive an additional reward of US\$5 based on their performance to motivate them to consider the manipulated website as a real restaurant review formula [75]. Empirical studies typically use monetary in centives to encourage participation; thus, such a reward was not likely to create substantial self-selection bias [72,74]. The participants were surveyed regarding their demographic backgrounds and experience using review websites or applications. To address privacy concerns, all the participants were anonymous [71,73]. The participants’ ages ranged from 17 to 21 years, with a mean age of 20.2 years. A total of 53.3 % of the participants were female. On average, participants had 4.71 years of experience using review websites or applications. No significant differ ence was observed in gender, age, or experience using review websites for participants across the different conditions. Participants were randomly directed to one of the six conditions and were requested to carefully read the consumer reviews and managerial responses presented in each condition. Participants were then asked to report their intention to dine at the focal restaurant.

## 4.1.4. Discussion

Overall, five out of six hypotheses were confirmed in Study 1. Only H2a failed to be supported. The main findings are plotted in Fig. 1a and b. When predicting consumers’ consumption likelihood, our finding suggest that managerial responses which concentrate on negative re views are superior to those concentrated on positive reviews. However, the relative importance of response strategies differs with review valence. When responding to negative reviews, both the full response strategy and responding to extreme reviews outweigh the random response strategy. When responding to positive reviews, the opposite conclusion has been drawn, as random response prevails over both a full response strategy and responding to extreme reviews. The full response strategy is superior to responding to extreme reviews when responding to negative reviews. Whereas, when responding to positive reviews, the relative importance of full response versus responding to extreme re views was not supported. Managerial response to positive reviews is often regarded by consumers as self-promotional because it reiterates the merits proposed by the reviewer [54,77]. Therefore, compared with responding to moderate positive reviews, responding to extreme posi tive reviews can be viewed as a firm’s attempt to self-promote. Psy chological reactance literature suggests that both the strength and intensity of a promotional attempt may lead to resistance behaviors [14, 22]. This is because both full response and responding to extreme pos itive reviews lead to resistance behaviors—the relative importance of the two is thus inconclusive. In the following section, we focus on how response tailoring moderates the relationship between managerial response and consumers’ consumption likelihood.

## 4.2. Study 2: how to target

## 4.2.1. Stimulus materials

In Study 2, we employed a 2 (review valance: positive vs. negative) × 2 (response tailoring: high vs. low) between-subject factorial design. The experimental environment of this study was the same webpage used in Study 1. The experiment procedure was also the same as that of Study 1. Similar to Study 1, Study 2 presented 16 reviews with four 5-star ratings, eight 4-star ratings, two 2-star ratings, and two 1-star ratings. Study 2 generated four conditions and participants were randomly assigned to one of the four. To rule out alternative explanations, the order of all reviews and the number of responses were the same across the four conditions. The key differences among the four conditions were the re view valance and the extent of response tailoring. For example, in the negative review/high-tailoring condition, only negative reviews were responded to and they were responded to in a highly tailored manner.

For this study, we used real reviews from a major e-commerce platform to manipulate the extent of response tailoring. The manipula tion of response tailoring involved two steps. To start with we targeted DianPing, one of the most popular review websites in China. Similar to Yelp, DianPing is a business directory service and crowd-sourced review forum. The site classifies merchants into categories based on the type of products or services provided (restaurants, hotels, books, electronics, etc.). Consistent with Study 1, we restricted our focus to Szechuan cuisine in the “restaurants” category located in Xi’an, China. On December 13, 2018, DianPing listed 519 Szechuan cuisine restaurants in Xi’an. We randomly selected 52 merchants and retrieved all historical reviews for these merchants (4534 total reviews). The sample was then cleaned using the following criteria. First, we eliminated reviews that were extremely short or long. Second, only reviews that received managerial response were retained. Third, since the current study is focused on negative and positive reviews, all neutral reviews were removed. Finally, any reviews which appeared to have an automatic response were removed. The final review dataset was 413. From this pool, three PhD students were invited to independently rate the content similarity between reviews and managerial responses on a 9-point Likert scale— 9 being very similar and 1 being very dissimilar. We further examined inter-rater reliability using Cronbach’s alpha<sup>2</sup> . The Cronbach’s alpha value of the response tailoring rating was 0.733, which is acceptable. Based on the average rating of the PhD students, we selected 29 reviews rated as 9 by the participants, (indicating a high-tailored response) or 1 (indicating a low-tailored response). This step was essential for downsizing the original dataset to an operable scale.

In the second step, we calculated the specific response tailoring scores between reviews and managerial responses using Normalized Google Distance (NGD) [13]. To calculate the response tailoring scores, the similarity of each noun–noun pair between reviews and responses was calculated using NGD [13]. NGD is a semantic similarity measure derived from the number of hits returned by the Google search engine for a given set of keywords. Words with the same or similar meanings in a natural language sense tend to be “close” in units of NGD, while words with dissimilar meanings tend to be farther apart. Given two nouns i and j, the similarity between i and j is defined as follows:

$$
\text { Word } - \text { Word   Similarity } (i, j) = 1 - \frac {\max \left\{\log f (i) , \log f (j) \right\} - \log f (i \& j)}{\log G - \min \left\{\log f (i) , \log f (j) \right\}}
$$

where G represents the total number of webpages in the Google search engine and f (i), f (j), and f (i & j) represent the number of hits returned by the Google search engine for $i , j ,$ and (i & j), respectively. Assume that a specific review (r) and its corresponding managerial response (r’) have m and n nouns, respectively. Based on the above equation, a total of (m×n) similarity values can be obtained for each noun–noun pair be tween r and r’. The response tailoring between r and r’ can be defined a the average similarity value of the total (m×n) noun–noun pairs:

Review

$$
\begin{array}{l} - \text {   Response   tailoring } (r, r ^ {\prime}) \\ = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \text { Word } - \text { Word   Similarity } (i , j)}{m \times n} \end{array}
$$

Following the above approach, we calculated the review-response tailoring score for each of the 29 reviews in our dataset. These reviews were then organized in descending order based on the review-response tailoring score and the responses, with moderate tailoring scores being excluded. The above steps yielded a final set of 16 treatment reviews and their corresponding responses. Table 6 presents an example of the selected reviews and their corresponding responses.

## 4.2.2. Procedures and participants

Similar to Study 1, participants were asked to close the browser and rate their likelihood to dine at the restaurant after browsing the manipulated webpage. In addition, to ensure that the extent of response tailoring was successfully manipulated, participants were asked to rate the extent of response tailoring in each condition. A 3-item scale was developed to measure perceived response tailoring (see details in the next section). All of the items were based on a 9-point Likert scale.

A total of 89 undergraduate and graduate students were recruited for Study 2 from a major university located in Western China. Participation in the experiment was voluntary, and a guaranteed course credit was provided to each participant. Before the experimental tasks, the partic ipants were told that 50 % of them would receive an additional reward of US\$8 based on their performance to motivate them to consider the manipulated website as a real restaurant review formula [75]. The negative high-tailoring condition had 23 participants. The negative low-tailoring, positive high-tailoring, and positive low-tailoring condi tions had 19, 27, and 20 participants, respectively. The whole experi ment lasted approximately 15 min. Among the 89 participants, 48 (53.9 %) were female and 41 were male (46.1 %). Participant age ranged from 17 to 32 years (mean = 23.82 years). They were recruited from eight different departments and represented diverse backgrounds. No signif icant differences were found among the subjects across the eight experimental conditions in terms of their demographic statistics. Ac cording to the power analysis for a between-subject design, this sample size (i.e., 19–27 subjects in each condition) assures a sufficient statistical power of 0.80 for a medium effect size (f = 0.25) [15].

## 4.2.4. Discussion

Overall, Study 2 confirmed the two opposing effects of response tailoring. The main findings are plotted in Fig. 2. First, when responding to negative reviews, high-tailored responses outweigh low-tailored re sponses in predicting consumers’ consumption intention. This finding can be explained by the fact that a high-tailored response has similar contents to a given review and is more likely to address the specific complaints raised by the consumer [56,77]. Second, when responding to positive reviews, low-tailored responses prevail over high-tailored re sponses in predicting consumers’ consumption intention. A nascent line of studies allude that a high-tailored response is more likely to reiterate the merits proposed by the reviewer and can therefore be perceived as redundant and unfavorable [22,54,77]. In the context of e-commerce, this study validates that expectation.

## 4.3. Study 3: robust checks

Because of the small samples within each scenario, the subjects characteristics may confound our results. For example, Seock and Bailey [62] found that females exhibit a higher frequency of online shopping than males. This inherent difference between females and males may influence the consumption likelihood measured in our experiments. Therefore, we controlled subject-level variables (i.e., gender, age, and need for cognition) and replicated our models using analysis of covari ance (ANCOVA). The results are presented in Table 8. The results of the models run with ANCOVA are largely identical to the models run with ANOVA, providing evidence that our findings are robust.

In Study 1, the unbalanced dataset (12 positive ratings and 4 negative ratings were presented to participants) and the contrasting response rate in positive reviews and negative reviews may confound the validity of H1 and H2. Consumers reactions may vary because of the different proportions of reviews being responded to. Also, when compared with negative reviews, the reversed effect in positive reviews, might be caused by the diverse response rate<sup>3</sup> . To rule out this con founding explanation, we conducted a supplementary study (Study 3). The manipulations and experimental procedures of Study 3 mimicked that of Study 1 except for the number of reviews presented. In Study 3, 4 positive ratings and 4 negative ratings (a balanced design) were presented to participants. Study 3 recruits students from the same university as that of Study 1 and receives 113 valid responses. No significant differ ence was observed in demographics for participants in Study 1 and Study 3. The results of the ANOVA tests are summarized in Table 9. The results of our model run with Study 3 are largely identical with that run with Study 1, providing evidences that our results are robust.

## 5. General discussion

This research examined the influence of response strategies and response tailoring on consumers’ consumption likelihood. Firstly, Study 1 demonstrated that managerial responses concentrated on negative reviews lead to higher consumption likelihood than managerial re sponses focused on positive reviews. Next, we compared the relative importance of response strategy, considering full response, random response, and responding only to extreme reviews. Study 2 revealed that response tailoring shows polar effects when responding to negative or positive reviews. Specifically, high-tailored responses outweigh lowtailored responses when responding to negative reviews—a relation ship that becomes inverted when responding to positive reviews.

This study represents a pioneering effort to investigate relative importance of response strategies in the context of third-party e-com merce or review platforms. In recent years, firms have been exploring various strategies for intervening in customer opinion. For example, these can be detailed responses to consumer reviews and sharing posi tive reviews on social media [16,47]. Research generally suggests that the presence of managerial response is beneficial to firm performance [41,49]. However, firms may lack sufficient resources to respond to each review. This is because managerial response is costly, and the quantity of reviews continues to grow. As a result, knowing how to respond and to whom, is critical whilst under budget constraints. Our study focuses on the relative importance of various response strategies. The results offer guidance in determining the optimal response strategy whilst under a limited response budget.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

This paper enriches theory threefold. First, this study investigated firms’ response strategy to online reviews from the perspective of resource constraints. This being an important and timely topic that has received surprisingly little attention in the literature. Previous studies have predominantly investigated managerial response strategy from the perspective of either whom to respond or how to respond (see Table 10 for a detailed review). These studies presuppose that firms have sufficient capacity to respond to individual reviews. Although managerial response has been proved as an effective way of e-WOM intervention, the exponentially growth of online reviews strains the response re sources of firms. These limited resources impact and challenge the operability of these findings. For example, Lui, Bartosiak, Piccoli and Sadhya [49] unearthed a positive relationship between managerial response frequency and firm sales. Li, Cui and Peng [44] posit that firms should customize managerial response to the corresponding review. These findings suggest that firms should respond to the contents of each corresponding review as customized as possible, which seems impos sible. We filled this gap by exploring the optimal response strategy under resource constraints. This study demonstrates that response resource constraints critically affect both whom to target and how to respond.

Second, this study reconciled the competing views on the prioriti zation of response resources allocation (whom to target) by taking review valance into account. Lui, Bartosiak, Piccoli and Sadhya [49] suggest that firms should place emphasis on extreme reviews. This is because the response to extreme reviews on firm performance are stronger, rather than the effect of a full response strategy. While Ma, Sun and Kekre [51] and Chevalier, Dover and Mayzlin [12] argue that responses to extreme negative reviews can bring increasingly more detailed negative reviews. Gu and Ye [28] found that managerial responses to online reviews (both positive and negative) positively influence firm performance. Wang and Chaudhry [77] and Herhausen, Ludwig, Grewal, Wulf and Schoegel [29] elaborate that responses to positive reviews negatively influence firm performance. ## 5.2. Managerial implications

This paper informs practice twofold. First and foremost, this paper provides guidelines for seeking the optimal response strategy under different budget conditions. Studies have reached a consensus that the presence of managerial response increases sales performance in various e-commerce settings [11,41,49]. Studies have also highlighted the importance of response tailoring in determining the quality of mana gerial response [44,56]. Following this logic and assuming a sufficient budget, conventional wisdom suggests responding to each consumer review with a high-tailored response. However, our results challenged this widely accepted opinion by taking review valance into account. One of the key findings of this study is that selective response strategy out weighs full response strategy when responding to positive reviews. We also demonstrated that low-tailored response outperforms high-tailored response when responding to positive reviews. We therefore suggest that, for firms with a sufficient response budget, responding to each negative review with high-tailored responses and selectively responding to positive reviews using low-tailored responses is the optimal configuration. We found that responding alone to negative reviews leads to higher consumption likelihood than responding alone to positive reviews. When responding to negative reviews, a full response strategy outweighs randomly responding or responding only to extreme reviews. We further confirmed that responding to extreme reviews prevails over randomly responding. Therefore, for firms with limited response budget, the optimal configuration is to prioritize responding to extreme negative reviews using high-tailored responses.

This paper also informs the practice of facilitating consumers’ con sumption likelihood through responding to positive reviews. Operators receive a small number of reviews at the early stage of their business and frequently attempt to respond to each positive review in the most detailed way possible. This is because they wish to establish positive affect with consumers by expressing gratitude and warmth. However, these operators may not get the results they seek, as the perception of psychological reactance increases as the intensity of managerial response to positive reviews increases [22,43]. We revealed that responding to positive reviews using a full response strategy is costly and inefficient. Therefore, managers are encouraged to employ selective response strategies while responding to positive reviews. Wang and Chaudhry [77] observed in their data that some of the top hotel chains, such as Hilton, Marriott, Starwood, etc., tailored their responses to 5-star reviews to a greater extent than they do for 4- and 3-star ratings. This strategy is potentially ineffective, as we showed that low-tailored responses outweigh high-tailored responses when responding to positive reviews. Thereby, managers should limit the customization of their response to posi tive reviews.

## 5.3. Limitations and future directions

Our analysis has several limitations that are worth exploring in future research. First, when predicting consumers’ purchase likelihood, the current study examined and identified the relative importance of the two most adopted selective response strategies (responding to extreme reviews and randomly responding to reviews). It would be interesting to further expand upon the relative importance of other selective response strategies. For example, responding to reviews posted by influential consumers (i.e., those with a large number of followers) or responding to reviews expressing specific emotions (i.e., anger, anxiety, or happiness).

Second, this study focuses on how the interaction between online reviews and managerial response jointly influences consumers’ (ob servers’) consumption likelihood. The effect of manager response stra tegies on business performance may vary due to the interpersonal differences of the consumers being neglected. In the process of product evaluation, risk-averse consumers pay more attention to negative re views. Whereas, consumers who prefer risks may place more weight on positive reviews. Thus, the effect of managerial response to negative reviews on consumption intention would be greater than responding to positive reviews for risk-averse consumers, Also, this relationship might be reversed for risk-preference consumers. Meanwhile, keeping in mind, the need for cognition— defined as the tendency for an individual to engage in and enjoy thinking. This need for cognition may weaken the effect of response tailoring on consumers’ consumption likelihood [8]. Future studies are encouraged to further validate the model used in our approach by taking the interposal difference of the respondents into account.

Finally, the current study demonstrated that the relative importance of various response strategies differs with review valance. Future studies may further explore this issue by taking into account both review vol ume and review valence simultaneously. For example, it would be interesting to know the relative importance of various response strate gies under diverse conditions such as: (1) a product with a majority of negative reviews; (2) a product with a majority of positive reviews; and (3) a product with approximately equivalent positive and negative reviews.
