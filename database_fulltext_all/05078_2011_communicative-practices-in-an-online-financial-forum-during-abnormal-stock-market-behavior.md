# Communicative practices in an online financial forum during abnormal stock market behavior

John Campbell <sup>a,</sup>\*, Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Information Sciences and Engineering, University of Canberra, Canberra ACT 2601, Australia <sup>b</sup> School of Information Systems, Technology and Management, University of New South Wales, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 3 January 2008 Received in revised form 17 September 2010 Accepted 4 December 2010 Available online 21 December 2010

Keywords: Online financial forum Communicative practices Manipulation Abnormal trading behavior

## A B S T R A C T

We offered a new perspective on communication in computer-mediated environments by examining communicative practices used during discussion in an online financial forum about a listed company that was experiencing abnormal trading behavior. The analysis of communicative practices showed how the linguistic nature of the online forum allowed covert manipulation of inexperienced and unsuspecting forum participants – against all the norms and rules governing online financial forums. Based on our theoretical understanding of communicative practices, we found a discrepancy between the expectations of and the actual divergence from an ideal communicative environment free from distortion and manipulation that is normally assumed to govern online forums. The discrepancy, which remains largely concealed, can be seen as a major factor in the negative implications of online financial forums. - 2010 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has become a core component of the communication infrastructure for financial markets around the world. The relative low cost and ease-of-access offered by Internet technology has given rise to a host of online investor services now commonly made available by stock exchanges, brokerage firms, banks, and investment advisers. Digital networks provide clear benefits over traditional stock market participation by enabling online access to knowledge resources and online trading accounts. Consequently, they have become common and enduring features of modern stock markets and this has fundamentally altered industry competition by changing the way that financial corporations differentiate their services and product offerings [5].

Apart from being a conduit for factual information from authorized or other trusted sources, the advent of the Internet has also given rise to Internet discussion sites (IDS) where investors and traders share information and learn from oneanother through active or passive participation in forum discussions. Typically these IDS are self organizing groups of individuals who share knowledge and information about a diverse range of topics, but largely those concerned with the trading of shares in publicly listed companies. Financial IDS empower private investors by supporting synchronous and asynchronous digital conversations about key securities-related topics, including trading strategies, tax implications, comparative assessments of stockbroker services, and private research. Unfortunately, the structure and anonymity of IDS can allow some individuals to cultivate and exploit the credulity and enthusiasm of others.

In terms of the influence of these forums, prior research has linked IDS postings with stock market activity [8,12]. For example, several studies have reported that increased IDS posting activity preceded significant changes in next-day trading volume and share price returns (e.g., [1,17]). More recently, an Australian study found that companies identified as takeover targets in IDS discussions were, on average, associated with subsequent positive abnormal stock price returns [6]. Many investors participate in IDS and there is widespread interest within these communities to identify and discourage false information and misuse of discussion forums. This is particularly relevant for low capitalized and thinly traded companies, which are vulnerable to increased stock price volatility because of IDS rumors. Periods of increased share price volatility can have a significant impact on reputation and affect a company’s ability to employ key staff, raise debt, or place equity. From a market perspective, these events can lead to sub-optimal allocation of capital and give rise to material financial loss for individual investors.

Despite the significance of these potential outcomes, social interaction processes within financial IDS and practices employed by participants remain largely unexamined. We do not know how participants establish social interaction and share knowledge, nor what characterizes their communicative practices and how this impacts on participants’ engagement in and their behavior after forum discussions. To examine such practices we adapted a theoretical framework developed by Cecez-Kecmanovic and Webb based on Habermas’ theory of communicative action [4]. This framework – reinterpreted and adapted to the IDS context – classifies communicative practices according to the focus of participants’ postings (e.g. subject matter or self expression) and according to participants’ orientation (e.g. towards mutual understanding or towards influencing others).

Thus we investigated IDS communication as a social interaction process. The communicative practices framework was used to analyze postings in a popular financial forum during a period of abnormal stock price and trading volume. By investigating and interpreting forum interactions (postings) through the lens of communicative practices we tried to answer the research questions:

What type of communicative practices are employed by participants in IDS and to what extent can types of communicative practices indicate manipulation and misuse of discussion forums?

## 2. Communicative practices in Internet discussion sites

In an information efficient market, stock prices fully reflect all available information, preventing any individual from identifying an over- or under-valued security and thereby obtaining a reward greater than normal given the risk of investment. In theory, IDS could be seen as enablers of market information efficiency; they create an environment where securities information can be shared, discussed, and evaluated. Like other Internet forum environments, IDS enable a large number of participants to engage in asynchronous information sharing, thereby overcoming the limitations of face-to-face interaction.

While these forums allow individuals to discuss the merits of different investments and debate market related topics, they also may be misused: some people may intend strategically to influence others using a forum as a way of disseminating misinformation, rumors, and hearsay. Unlike face-to-face interaction where such behavior is more visible (due to non-linguistic cues, etc.) and therefore less of a threat. Internet forums are sometimes assumed to be ideal environments for information sharing and free from distortions. The linguistic nature of the medium and such assumptions (especially among novice and naı¨ve participants) may allow an IDS to become vulnerable to misuse.

The history of asset markets and of stock markets in particular is regularly punctuated by periods of over exuberance or apathy in which otherwise intelligent individuals have succumbed to the herding effect of social behavior. The general use of charting and other technical analysis tools in modern stock markets suggest that traders also perceive that there are patterns of herding behavior in markets that can be exploited with technical indicators [14]. In periods of uncertainty, such behavior can affect a group of investors into making a wrong investment decision [16].

Different factors have been suggested as reducing the effect of errors passed by word of mouth, including credibility, trustworthiness, and enthusiasm of the influencer, and informational factors such as timing, vividness, negativity, and consensus. There is compelling evidence that information can be more easily manipulated in online environments particularly in the early stages of information dissemination when objective viewpoints are scarce [9]. However, very little attention has been given to the kinds of messages used to convey information in online environments the intent of the messages, and how they are interpreted by recipients.

Individuals participate in financial IDS typically to be or become successful stock investors and/or traders. At the same time they try to impress other participants and sometimes manipulate and exert their power [3,13]. As a result, participants’ involvement and communicative practices reflect their aims and the strategies they adopt to achieve their aims. For example, a forum participant whose primary goal is learning and mutual understanding may adopt a strategy of strictly following IDS rules and honestly share information with others while publicly demonstrating a high regard for other community members. Conversely, another participant concerned with maximizing his/her investment returns may be more interested in finding ways to influence others for personal gain and so disregard IDS rules or community norms. Our interest was therefore to understand how communicative practices reflect the different goals and linguistic strategies used to impact participants’ actions.

Social interaction is mediated by language. Internet-based social interaction is more amenable to the analysis of communicative practices due to the textual nature of online exchanges. Communication or messages are mechanisms that reveal the intentions of participants: to share knowledge and co-create meaning that achieves better mutual understanding or influences and manipulates others. An examination of the role and power of messages in establishing and maintaining social interaction and enabling knowledge sharing is therefore necessary in order to gain insight into communicative practices and participants’ behavior within IDS. A prerequisite to an examination is understanding the meaning of messages – as explained by speech act theory; a speech or message has a performative meaning (not what is said but what the saying represents).

## 2.1. Messages constituting IDS social processes

IDS participants exchange messages in order to develop a better understanding and co-construct knowledge of the subject under discussion. They also use other messages to refer to norms and rules specified or assumed to govern their interaction. They may, for example, dispute some rules, provide arguments to change them, and seek agreement from others; they are then referring to a particular kind of knowledge that is defined by norms and rules. Individuals will use yet another type of message to express their expectations, attitudes and feelings about forum communication and their satisfaction with the IDS collaborative processes.

Different types of messages refer to different domains of knowledge and are used for different purposes. Cecez-Kecmanovic and Webb proposed a formal classification of messages according to the knowledge domains and goal orientation of participants. Knowledge domains include (1) subject matter (e.g. new information about a particular stock), (2) norms and rules governing the IDS processes (e.g. participants are obliged to provide reasonable arguments for their opinions) and (3) personal experiences, desires and feelings related to IDS communication. In each of the knowledge domains, participants raise certain types of messages:

(1) Messages addressing the subject matter and the topic of discussion such as:

\- raising claims, providing arguments, reasons, grounds,

\- disputing claims, providing counter-arguments and grounds,

\- seeking understanding either by sharing, expressing or imposing beliefs,

\- seeking clarification,

\- interpreting meaning.

(2) Messages addressing norms and rules governing the discus sion process such as:

\- organizing, regulating or directing the discussion process,

\- establishing or disputing rules of conduct and speech (normative regulation of the discussion process),

\- claiming that some norms or rules are violated (acts that relate practical discourse to accepted norms or rules).

(3) Messages addressing personal experiences, desires and feelings that express:

\- personal views about, assessment of, or expectations from the communication process,

\- an individual reflexive perception of the communication process,

\- personal attitudes to cooperation and respect for others (heedful interrelating, intention to influence others, wish to dominate and exert control, etc.).

Messages cannot be fully explained by reference to knowledge domain only. To understand its performative meaning we must focus on the orientation of forum participants within the communication process. Using Habermas’ reformulation of speech act theory as a guide, it is possible to investigate the action orientation of IDS participants in the communication process. Cecez-Kecmanovic and Webb also identified three dominant goal orientations of participants in collaborative working environments:

(A) Knowledge sharing and co-creation – a wish to interact with others to increase mutual understanding and discuss and test alternative or new ideas and concepts.

(B) Achieving an end – an intention to succeed. For example, to trade successfully.

(C) Self-presentation – attempts to create an impression on others, or portraying a particular image.

The framework of communicative practices presented in Table 1 integrates the two dimensions of communicative analysis into a representation describing the knowledge domains of messages along one dimension, and the dominant action orientation (knowledge creation; achieving ends, and self-presentation) along the other. This framework can be used to investigate the flow of messages in terms of what they do and how they contribute to the construction and sharing of knowledge and maintenance of social interaction in an IDS [7]. More specifically, the framework can be used to explore and assess an IDS in terms of approximating or diverging from an ideal communication environment, which is defined here as an instantiation of Habermas’ ideal speech situation in the context of virtual communities. Similar to this, we defined the conditions of an ideal communication environment as communicative practices free from any kind of distortion, any form of coercion, manipulation, or ideology that excludes all force except that of a more reasoned argument. Habermas maintained that participants in practical discourse situations are aware of the ‘‘degree of satisfaction’’ of these conditions. Given the purpose of IDS, their success within this perspective, can be seen as the degree to which conditions of an ideal communication environment are satisfied from the participants’ perspective.

This framework was used to examine IDS communicative practices and assess them in terms of their similarity with or divergence from the ideal communication environment. Communicative practices diverge from the ideal due to different forms of distortion; this, action by an individual may involve ignoring others and over-insistence on acceptance of personal views and opinions, and thus use of the forum as a platform for personal promotion and gain. Such individuals attempt to serve their own personal interest. The presence of such communicative distortions disrupts collaborative processes and may even cause a complete breakdown of communication in a forum.

On the other hand, individuals predominantly oriented to sharing and co-creating knowledge – assumed to be the main purpose of the IDS – present their views and opinions honestly, often unsuspecting of a different orientation of others. They typically seek useful information and learn by establishing mutual understanding and respect for others. However, communicative practices are far from the ideal. Participants are often unaware of the kinds of distortions of communications that take place, overtly or covertly, that prevent cooperative knowledge sharing, trust building, and cooperation.

The communicative practices framework can help us better understand the ways that forum participants interact, exchange messages and construct text-based virtual communities. This can lead us to see beyond the words and towards comprehending the meaning of messages. In our work, we used the theoretical lens of a communicative practices framework to investigate and discover the ways that IDS may be used to enable and support distorted communication, manipulation, and corrupt practices.

## 3. Method

While our philosophical approach was interpretive, we used multimethod comprising of qualitative method (thematic analysis and coding of forum postings) and quantitative analysis (summary of codes and their comparisons with information event analysis) in accordance with Mingers’ suggestion [15]. As interpretive research, our study did not aim to make empirical generalizations based on a sufficiently large sample. Instead, our goal was to improve understanding of communicative practices in a financial forum and their implications in that context, through a thorough examination of an exemplary case. Only theoretical generalization may be attempted in this kind of research [11].

## 3.1. Study event

We selected a particular event on a popular Australian financial IDS called HotCopper; the event occurred during the last days of what has become known as the dotcom bubble (April–May 2000). This involved a discussion about a company that occurred in parallel with conspicuous abnormal trading in the company’s stock. The selected event was of interest as it exemplified synchronicity of the financial forum discussion and abnormal trading behavior, suggesting a potential link between them. This event was therefore particularly suitable for exploring communicative practices employed by the participants and in understanding their role in manipulating and misusing the forum.

The data was provided in two sets:

(a) the HotCopper forum discussion, which involved 130 participants who contributed more than 600 messages directly related to the focal company over a period of 48 days in April and May 2000; the forum messages were time and date stamped, allowing direct comparison with market activity; and

(b) data about the focal company’s trading volume and stock price change during the observed period, obtained from the Australian Stock Exchange.

The HotCopper financial forum has operated since 1995. Its participants included amateur investors, day traders, and professional brokers, all of whom shared a common interest in stock market investment. Participants posted messages on a range of financial topics on a bulletin-board. Guidelines were provided to help participants conform to government regulations regarding investment advice and also to form the basis for moderating forum behavior. A standard disclaimer notice was attached to every message posted to the forum (see Appendix A); it reminded the reader that some claims might not be accurate and that they should seek professional advice before taking any action.

The HotCopper event was triggered by highly evocative postings about a particular company: Perilya Mines NL (Australian Stock

Exchange code: PEM), which occurred during a period of considerable abnormal trading volume and price volatility. PEM was, at the time, a profitable gold mining and exploration company with a market capitalization of less than \$35 million (AUD). On 22 March 2000 the company announced that it had lodged an application to the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) to amend its company type from no-liability to limited liability, and to change its name to Perilya Limited. In Australia, noliability companies are restricted to mining and exploration activities under the Corporations Act and they cannot undertake other business activities. A change of name and company type at that point in time was usually a precursor to the company embarking on a dotcom business venture. The company was well positioned for such an investment; it owned a profitable gold mining operation, had no debt and held more than \$20 million (AUD) in cash and gold bullion.

## 3.2. Data analysis

Our analysis first focused on the event time-horizon in order to frame and make sense of the unusual trading volume and stock price volatility. Event studies have long been used in finance research to assess how capital markets respond to the release of reports containing accounting information or public announcements. Given its emphasis on the relationship between information and market behavior, an event-based analysis was justified for our study. However, it is just one component of the study; the second component of our study was an in-depth analysis of forum messages examining changes in communicative practices due to abnormal trading volume and stock price behavior. Thus the second component of our in-depth analysis of HotCopper messages was driven by the framework of communicative practices. Our aim was to understand the nature and type of these messages. Out of a total of 634 messages during the period of 48 days we selected 398 posted during the most relevant period of 8 days (from 1 to 7). We then conducted a thematic analysis which coded each of the 398 messages as a particular type of message in the framework (e.g. A1, B1, C1, etc. as in Table 1). To do that, each message was examined in terms of its content and the implied goal orientation of the participant who posted it. This required reading each message and interpreting its meaning and its relation to other postings within the broader context of the forum discussion.

While initial analysis and interpretation of messages was conducted by the authors, the final coding of all 398 messages was undertaken by two independent coders and checked for consistency by the first author. Furthermore, as the second author was not a participant in the forum and therefore did not have contextual knowledge, she was able to question and check interpretations within the communicative framework. While any interpretation and coding process is inherently subjective, the analysis may be directed towards seeking greater intersubjectivity and, in that sense, more plausible results. When two independent coders were in doubt and coded differently it was clear that underlying assumptions needed to be examined and interpretation checked. By engaging two independent coders and checking and inter-subjectively resolving their differences in assigning codes we were able to achieve a better argued and more plausible coding of messages.

To compare communicative practices in the forum with market activity of PEM stock we summarized our detailed analysis of the codes within the critical period of 8 days. This comparison, together with background detailed interpretive analysis, enabled us to understand and provide deeper insight into communicative practice in the IDS and thereby make claims about their apparent effect on manipulation and misuse of discussion forums.

Information event analysis. Fig. 1 shows the volume and price range data (high, low and close prices) for Perilya Mining NL over the observed time period. Share price volatility and traded volume increased markedly during the 3 days from trading day 0–2 (the escalation period of Fig. 1).

It has been suggested that if investors differ in the way they interpret rumor, time will elapse before consensus is reached and trading volume and price volatility will be higher than normal. This is consistent with findings from earlier IDS studies. In our case share price variability and volume remained relatively high but were in decline for several more days before returning to pre-event levels around trading day 8 – the period labeled as consolidation. In our case, this was followed by a period of price depreciation lasting approximately five more trading days.

Changes in communicative practices between escalation and consolidation periods. We focused here on the type of messages and their flow during the event. Messages by Stucco – exemplified by messages #6, #12, #21, and #35 – were most often a combination of B1 and B3 type and occasionally A2, B2, and C2, C3. Other forum participants responded to Stucco by questioning either his claims (A1) or his honesty (A3). The detailed analysis indicated that Stucco posted messages strategically, pretending to share valuable information and disclosing his personal knowledge about the target Company. He used B1 and B3 messages to achieve this goal in such a way that many other participants, identified as supporters, interpreted them as A1 or A3 respectively (aimed at knowledge sharing, as confirmed by their responses; e.g., messages #70, #73, and #75).

Stucco managed to influence others by being an active poster (e.g. he posted 20 messages related to PEM in day +1) and especially by pretending to act honestly and with shared interest in mind. Several detractors tried to warn others by pointing to Stucco’s apparent stock ramping posts (these messages were mostly A1, A2, and A3 type). One poster accused Stucco of misleading behavior, claiming that Stucco’s actions were ‘‘causing confusion for other traders . . . not to mention the real prospect of lost profit’’ (Message #18). But the voices of detractors did not seem to be strong enough: Stucco’s and his supporters’ messages (mostly of type B and some of type A) dominated the debate.

To understand the nature of the communicative practices, especially in the escalation and consolidation phases, we coded the messages according to our theoretical framework (note, however, that a single message may contain more then one type of message and was coded as such). Examples of text coding using the communicative framework are shown in Appendix A. These phases (escalation: days 0 to +2; and consolidation: days +3 to +7) included approximately 63% of all messages and were key to understanding the observed event. Tables 2a and 2b summarize the goal orientation and knowledge domain dimensions independently. While orientation to knowledge sharing prevailed in both periods (89% and 84% of all postings), those aimed at achieving ends were notably present (36% and 41%). It is of note that the Chisquare tests revealed no significant change in the goal orientation between periods. However, there was a significant change in the knowledge domains referenced. Forum postings during the escalation period favored messages about the subject matter (72%). In contrast, messages posted during the consolidation period favored expression about personal experiences, desires, and feelings (73%). The shift from substantive factual aspects towards more reflexive positions suggested attempts at collective sensemaking activity within the forum community.

Table 3 provides a finer detailed analysis of communicative practices. Overall, it confirms that the knowledge sharing and cocreation category (A1, A2 and A3) remained the most frequent goal orientation of all messages in both escalation and consolidation periods. A significant difference between the two periods for A1 and A3 types indicated a shift from an emphasis on substantive issues (A1 49%) in the escalation period to an emphasis on personal experiences and feelings (A3 45%) in the consolidation period. Orientation to achieving ends (B1, B2 and B3) was less present but still significant, appearing in approximately one-third of all postings during both periods. The frequency of messages was low to negligible for C1, C2, and C3 messages (self-presentation and self-promotion). When this type of message was used it was regularly in conjunction with type B. This further indicated that some IDS forum participants engaged in the debates predominantly to achieve their goals by influencing others.

This finding alone revealed the nature of communicative practices in IDS forum and suggested a substantial degree of divergence from the ideal communication environment. The problem of course was that this particular finding is in sharp contrast to the intended aims and declared purpose of the IDS forum (see posting rules in Appendix A). Purposeful communicative distortions, spreading rumors and other forms of misuse for individual gain are also illegal and subject to regulation by the Australian Securities & Investments Commission [2].

Potential victims are novice, naı¨ve, and unsuspecting participants who take for granted the declared purpose and role of an IDS forum. In the observed period they expressed their desire to learn, especially from a ‘‘knowledgeable trader’’ like Stucco, who was seen by one participant as ‘‘the personality’’ who ‘‘makes this forum what it is’’ (message #630). Neither the disclaimer in each message nor the warnings of other participants were successful in counteracting the effect of Stucco’s well positioned B-type messages on the actions of others.

The proportion of messages aimed at knowledge sharing and co-creation (A1, A2, and A3) was greater during the escalation period. These exchanges reflected a relatively higher proportion of the postings by participants who provided arguments for suspecting the truth of Stucco’s messages, criticized his intention to mislead and deceive others, and reflected on how this was done. The predominance of knowledge sharing and co-creation during both escalation and consolidation indicated that the overall dominant motivation for participation in the discussions during this event was to create and share knowledge. These participants took part in the IDS discussion with the declared intention of the online forum; i.e., to share knowledge and debate investment ideas. The participants had their voice and attitudes expressed in a distinct communicative manner. During the escalation period the major focus was on addressing the subject matter and personal desires, experiences, and feelings (A1 at 49% and A3 at 36%). However there was a proportional increase in posts oriented towards knowledge sharing during the consolidation period by addressing personal desires, experiences and feelings (A3: increasing from 36% to 45%) and a decrease in posts addressing the subject matter (A1 decreasing from 49% to 31%). Both were indicative of sensemaking activity where participants were discussing personal experiences, desires, and feelings and relating them to the subject matter.

The incidence of achieving ends type messages (B) remained relatively stable between the escalation and consolidation periods (34% and 33% respectively). The incidence of these types of messages suggested that, for some participants, a major motivation for participation was to influence others to either invest or not. Although not statistically significant, there was a preference for messages oriented to achieving ends and focusing on the subject matter (B1 at 21%, compared to B2 at 2% and B3 at 13%). However, during the consolidation period the preference switched to messages oriented to influencing others by expressing personal experiences, desires, and feelings (that is B3 at 21% compared to B1 at 19% and B2 at 1%). Messages dealing with the subject matter and factual information (B1), may have better served the purpose of achieving goals in the escalation phase, as this was a key period (days 0 to +2) when other forum participants had to be convinced about the truth of the rumors that were posted. In the consolidation period, messages that dealt with personal experiences, desires, and feelings may have been a means of defending credibility.

## Discussion and conclusions

Similar to other Internet forums, financial IDS expand the possibilities for social interaction and knowledge sharing beyond the limitations of space and time. Historically, stock markets have been mainly the domain of the rich and well-informed. IDS are changing these relations by providing small investors access to information and the knowledge and opinion of like-minded investors. However, these expanded possibilities also open new opportunities for manipulation, deception, and exploitation.

By analyzing the IDS debate triggered by speculation about a particular stock, we identified a connection between the IDS postings and abnormal trading volume and stock price volatility. Our analysis was successful in highlighting the possibility of speculation and misinformation. Stucco, seems to have promoted the stock and apparently sold his shares after convincing others to invest. Some participants consequently suffered a financial loss. The implications of this IDS forum discussion was also directly damaging to the Company whose share price was lower after the event, and indirectly damaging due to loss of reputation by being associated, even if unwittingly, with this event.

The nature of the medium – textual exchange of messages – allows covert strategic behavior in which a participant pretends to use A type messages (aimed at knowledge sharing) but is really using B type messages (aimed at influencing others). Indeed, our analysis of the discussion showed that more than one-third of participants’ postings were B type messages. At the same time many unsuspecting participants expected the forum to conform to its own rules of behavior such as ‘‘No manipulative or deceptive behavior’’ and ‘‘Spreading of rumors will not be allowed.’’ From our analysis we conclude that the discrepancy between the expectations for and the actual divergence from the ideal communicative environment remains largely concealed; this can be seen as the major contributor to negative IDS forum implications.

A major contribution of our study was the revelation of the nature of communicative practices in IDS and the ways in which IDS may be used to enable and support distorted communications and manipulation. Our findings contribute to knowledge about word-of-mouth communication by identifying the kinds of messages and the intent behind them, while examining how they are interpreted and acted upon by others. Our finding is in contrast to earlier research that used a communicative practice lens and highlighted the importance of influencing or persuading others. Our result demonstrates the significance of context in shaping the kinds of communication approaches adopted in financial IDS.

Also, in earlier studies forum participants did not reflect so deeply on the behavior and responsibilities of other members; we conclude this to be related to the particular circumstances concerning the study event.

Our work makes a theoretical contribution by proposing a new understanding of IDS communicative practices and their comparison with the concept of ideal communicative environment in online forums (those free from manipulation, deception, and coercion). This allowed an assessment of IDS forum success in terms of its closeness to the ideal. The use of communicative acts with a strategic intention to influence other participants is typically achieved by dissemination of rumor and misinformation and through the use of multiple personae, etc. Our findings are relevant for regulators, organizers and participants of IDS and other Internet forums as they raise awareness of and explain how IDS can be misused and manipulated for personal gain without the perpetrators being caught despite blatant violation of the forums policies and norms.

## Forum Posting Rules

The forum has the following posting rules on the home page:

1. No manipulative or deceptive behavior.

2. Under no circumstances will “Topic only” posts be accepted.

3. No advertising without approval.

4. Comments to be kept to information on stocks. Flaming, hate messages, character assassination, libel or attacking sponsors will not be tolerated.

5. Posters to avoid spamming the board with multiple posts.

6. Spreading of rumors will not be allowed.

7. Users to make an effort to state if they own the stock.
