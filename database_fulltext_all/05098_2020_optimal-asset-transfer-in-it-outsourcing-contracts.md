# OPTIMAL ASSET TRANSFER IN IT OUTSOURCING CONTRACTS<sup>1</sup>

Shivendu Shivendu Muma College of Business, University of South Florida, 4202 E. Fowler Avenue, Tampa, FL 33620 U.S.A. {shivendu@usf.edu}

David Zeng College of Business and Information Systems, Dakota State University, 820 N. Washington Avenue, Madison, SD 57042 U.S.A. {david.zeng@dsu.edu}

Vijay Gurbaxani The Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine, 4293 Pereira Drive, Irvine, CA 92697 U.S.A. {VGurbaxa@uci.edu}

Many IT outsourcing arrangements include the purchase of the client’s IT assets by the vendor. Asset transfer benefits the client who can recapture some value through the sale and may even negotiate a lower price because the vendor may be more efficient in using these assets. On the other hand, asset transfer creates lockin for the client and limits future contractual options. To study these tradeoffs, we develop a game-theoretic framework wherein asset transfer creates a one-sided switching cost to the client, and vendors have private information both on their intrinsic capabilities, either high or low, and on the level of quality-improving effort they exert. The quality of IT services depends on the vendor’s capability and quality-improving effort. In a two-period model, we show that when quality is verifiable, the client uses asset transfer as a device to design efficient screening contracts, so that a high capability vendor is selected. On the other hand, when quality is non-verifiable, the client mitigates contractual inefficiency by voluntarily locking into a long-term relationship with the vendor and may transfer assets at a lower than efficient level, even to a high-capability vendor. Our results show that asset transfer can play a strategic role in outsourcing relationships, not just an operational one.

Keywords: Adverse selection, asset transfer, IT-services, outsourcing, monopolistic screening, non-verifiable quality

## Introduction

IT outsourcing<sup>2</sup> contracts often involve the transfer of ownership of IT assets—hardware, software, and human capital— necessary for service delivery from the client to the vendor (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998). The client benefits from asset transfer by generating substantial cash from “selling” the IT assets (Elitzur and Wensley 1997). Correspondingly, the vendor also benefits by acquiring specific assets required to deliver the contracted services and by utilizing these transferred assets more efficiently. Efficiency gains arise due to economies of scale and specialization, flexibility in the deployment of human resources, and pooling of IT specific expertise (Joiner 2011; Lee 1996; Roodhooft and Warlop 1999). On the other hand, transferring IT assets to the vendor creates substantial switching costs for the client, such as potential business disruption, loss of tacit knowledge, the opportunity cost of time, and the nonmonetary costs of searching for new vendors and writing a new contract (Barthélemy and Quélin 2006; Gurbaxani 2007; Kern et al. 2002; Whitten et al. 2010). Switching costs increase the possibility of the vendor’s ex post opportunistic behavior and the associated hold-up costs (Lonsdale 2001; Richmond et al. 1992).

Thus, from the client’s perspective, asset transfer in IT outsourcing contracts can be a double-edged sword. On one hand, the vendor may utilize the IT assets more efficiently, leading to a lower price. On the other, IT asset transfer may lead to lock-in, creating opportunities for the vendor to hold up the client resulting in poor quality services (Aubert et al. 1998; Lonsdale 2001). Moreover, in order to deliver high quality services, the vendor may need to make additional relationship-specific investments that are often noncontractual, because they are either unobservable, such as qualityimproving effort, or non-verifiable, such as human capital (Earl 1996; Gurbaxani 2007; Susarla et al. 2010). When vendors need to make additional investments for a client, they are faced with the risk that the client might act opportunistically, say by reneging on the contract or by trying to renegotiate price, leading them to underinvest in the relationship. However, a client might be able to use IT asset transfer as an instrument to mitigate this concern and induce the vendor to make the appropriate investment. In addition, asset transfer might enable the vendor to provide IT services at a lower cost due to operational efficiencies.

This paper develops an analytical model to examine the optimal level of asset transfer in situations in which the client is faced with a tradeoff: on one hand, asset transfer can lead to cost savings and serve as a mechanism to induce the vendor to invest optimal effort, while on the other, it can increase the client’s switching costs and lead to vendor opportunism. In addition, clients also face a challenge in selecting a vendor of appropriate capability from a set of vendors with heterogeneous capability and inducing them to exert qualityimproving effort because vendors have private information both on their capability and on the effort exerted by them. Specifically, we study the following three questions: How should client firms assess the tradeoffs between the benefits accruing from the efficiency gains of asset transfer and the costs related to the vendor’s potential opportunistic behavior? What determines the optimal level of asset transfer in outsourcing contracts? How should client firms design outsourcing contracts in the presence of agency issues given that the quality of IT services is often non-verifiable?

To study the impacts of asset transfer and the associated tradeoffs, we develop a stylized two-period model where the quality of IT services may be non-verifiable, a vendor’s intrinsic capability (simplified here as either high or low) is his private information and quality-improving effort is unobservable by the client. Moreover, asset transfer creates onesided switching costs in that contract termination imposes a switching cost only on the client (Barthélemy and Quélin 2006).

In our setting, when quality is verifiable, that is, there is an objective metric to measure quality, the client uses asset transfer as an instrument to screen vendors in the presence of unobservable vendor capability and effort or asymmetric information. Quality-contingent self-selection contracts with an appropriate penalty provision ensure that the vendors select the contract designed for them and exert quality-improving effort in both periods.

In the situation where quality is observed but there is no objective metric to measure it, that is, quality is nonverifiable, quality-contingent contracts are infeasible. Here, the client offers a menu of take-it-or-leave-it contracts<sup>3</sup> at the beginning of the first period with the provision that the client may extend the contract to the second period. The contract specifies the level of asset transfer, the first-period price, and the second-period price if the contract is extended. The dynamics of the optimal outsourcing contract involve (1) offering a menu of contracts that underpay the vendors in the first period and compensate them in the second period for the prior underpayment; (2) rewarding the vendor in the second period for optimal quality-improving effort exerted in the first period so that the total net compensation in two periods is nonnegative; (3) signaling the client’s commitment to a long-term relationship through asset transfer that imposes a one-sided switching cost on the client if the contract is not extended after the first period; and (4) reassuring the vendor that his “sunk investment” in providing high quality services in the first period will not be appropriated by the client because terminating the contract after the first period hurts the client as well.

Our analysis shows that when quality is verifiable the client uses asset transfer as a strategic instrument to screen IT outsourcing vendors and to incentivize them to exert the optimal level of quality-improving effort. The client offers optimal self-selection contracts in which the level of asset transfer to the high-capability vendor remains the same as that under full information while the level of asset transfer to the lowcapability vendor is lower. When the capability gap between high and low-capability vendors is very large, the client is better off by offering a single contract that only the high-type vendor accepts rather than a menu. Interestingly, when quality is non-verifiable and hence noncontractual, if the client is too focused on reaping the efficiency benefits of asset transfer, an opportunistic vendor may not exert the optimal level of quality-improving effort. In practical terms, the threat of contract termination is not credible when the switching cost created by asset transfer is too large. Under some conditions, the client may even distort the level of asset transfer to a highcapability vendor downward to ensure that the vendor does not strategically exploit the one-sided switching costs. On the other hand, if the one-sided switching cost to the client created by asset transfer is too low, then the client’s ability to hold back payment for the cost of quality-improving effort in the first period vanishes and contracts will fail to induce optimal effort, although the client can still achieve perfect screening.

Our work contributes both to the literature on outsourcing in IS and in economics that studies contracts under asymmetric information. Our model captures the tradeoffs between efficiency and lock-in and provides a framework to determine the optimal level of asset transfer when quality is non-verifiable. We show that asset transfer, beyond the well-understood payoff in efficiency, serves as both a screening device and an instrument to induce quality-improving effort in the presence of adverse selection issues and non-verifiable quality. Asset transfer also resolves moral hazard issues because it enables a client to credibly make a commitment to the vendor that she will not terminate the contract after the first period and appropriate the gain from his unobservable quality-improving effort. One-sided switching costs created by asset transfer act as “insurance” for the vendor that his “sunk investment” in the relationship will not be appropriated by the client. This allows a client to design self-enforcing implicit contracts when quality is noncontractual (Bull 1987; Klein 1985). Hence, the optimal level of asset transfer provides an adequate check on the opportunistic behavior of both parties.

Our work also provides a theoretical explanation for some key empirical findings in the growing outsourcing literature in the information systems field. Gurbaxani (2007) finds evidence that asset transfer is commonly used when investment in assets is required to maintain service quality. We provide a theoretical explanation for this observation wherein we show that client firms use asset transfer to incentivize vendors to exert quality-improving effort, especially when IT-service quality is non-verifiable. Chang et al. (2017) and Chen and Bharadwaj (2009) show that various contractual mechanisms are used to mitigate hold-up risks when switching costs are high. Our model provides a theoretical link between risk mitigation and asset transfer, although we focus only on mitigating the risks from the agency problems and non-verifiable quality of services.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: We review the literature and discuss how this research bridges gaps in the literature. Next, we describe the formal model. We then study the role of asset transfer when quality is verifiable and analyze the role of asset transfer when quality is non-verifiable. We conclude with a discussion of our results, including the implications of relaxing some of our assumptions, along with managerial recommendations.

## Literature Review

Our work is related to four streams of literature in economics and IS: property rights economics, agency theory and selfenforcing contracts, non-verifiability of quality, and IT outsourcing. First, we review the relevant literature that form the basis of our analytical model and then identify gaps in the literature and discuss how our study bridges those gaps.

Our research is informed by the property rights literature (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 1995; Hart and Moore 1990) and the agency theory perspective on asset transfer. The property rights literature suggests that ownership of key production assets provides incentives to make further noncontractual investments in improving the assets. The agency theory perspective focuses on hold-up by the vendor as asset transfer creates switching costs for the client (Susarla et al. 2010). A vendor may exploit this lock-in by underperforming and delivering low quality services (Barthélemy and Quélin 2006). We combine the notion that owning the assets necessary for service delivery increases the owner’s ability to engage in hold-up (Hart 2009) with the notion that asset ownership provides high-powered incentives for relationship specific investments. However, in our model, the incentive provided to the vendor is contract extension when the client observes high-quality service in the first period rather than through residual right of control (and a more favorable sharing of joint output).

The impact of the non-verifiability of quality on contractual efficiency has received considerable attention. Farrell and Shapiro (1989) study optimal contracts when quality is not verifiable and the buyer incurs switching costs. They show that when switching costs are unobservable, long-term contracts can mitigate opportunism, but some inefficiency remains. Bac (2000) developed a two-period model of a buyer–seller relationship with privately informed sellers where quality is not contractible and showed that the buyer’s switching cost in the first period increases the seller’s bargaining power in the second period, allowing the buyer to screen sellers and induce quality-improving effort. Bernheim and Whinston (1998) show that firms may strategically leave some verifiable aspects of performance unspecified when some other aspects of performance are non-verifiable, intentionally creating incompleteness in a well-designed contract. Self-enforcing implicit contracts, which are essentially incomplete, are designed not only to allocate risks associated with uncertainty in the state of the world, but also to prevent holdup (Bull 1987; Carmichael 1989; Klein 1985). When quality is non-verifiable, an outsourcing contract that induces the vendor to deliver high quality services needs to be selfenforcing because quality is noncontractual.

IT outsourcing relationships have some distinctive features, which are key to any theoretical investigation. Strategic, economic, and technological considerations motivate IT outsourcing (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998; Lee 2001). Some critical aspects of the quality of IT services are difficult to measure and therefore to verify (Chang et al. 2017; Gopal and Koka 2009). For example, an externally developed software application is often required to be “user friendly” (Ruparelia 2010). Although this common requirement is valid and reasonable, it is non-verifiable because user friendliness is impossible to measure objectively. This makes evaluation of IT services quality difficult and often subjective (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012; Grover et al. 1996; Ramachandran and Gopal 2010), which leads to non-verifiability of quality (Gopal and Koka 2009; Holmstrom 1985) even when it is perfectly observed. Therefore, some key aspects of quality of IT services are not ex ante contractible, even though the value of outsourcing arrangements is directly related to the quality of service (Whitten and Wakefield 2006).

The quality of IT services also depends on vendors’ intrinsic capabilities (Levina and Ross 2003; Tiwana and Bush 2007), which are heterogeneous, and their private information, leading to an adverse selection problem (Aubert et al. 1998). Moreover, a client often cannot observe a vendor’s qualityimproving effort, leading to moral hazard issues (Aubert et al. 2003). Mitigating the risk of adverse selection and moral hazard is critical to developing successful IT outsourcing relationships (Aubert et al. 1998). These unique features of outsourcing relationships and IT services make vendor selection difficult, even though proper selection is key to successful outsourcing relationships (Bapna et al. 2010; Snir and Hitt 2004).

While vendor reputation partially addresses the informational issues of vendor selection (Banerjee and Duflo 2000), clients also use pilot projects as a screening device to mitigate adverse selection issues (Snir and Hitt 2004). Further, the transfer of IT assets has been shown to have positive efficiency benefits to the client (Elitzur and Wensley 1997; Lee 2001; Lee 1996; Joiner 2011) as well as negative effects such as hold-up and opportunistic behavior by the vendor (Lonsdale 2001). Another impact of asset transfer on clients is that it creates substantial switching costs because significant resources are required to recreate the IT environment when the incumbent vendor is replaced (Kern et al. 2002).

Our model captures these essential characteristics of outsourcing relationships: vendors have private information on their intrinsic capabilities and quality-improving effort, and a client’s payoff is critically dependent on appropriate vendor selection. Our two-period setting is also consistent with the aforementioned findings that probationary or extendibility clauses are used to address the problem of contract incompleteness and mitigate the vendor’s potential for underinvestment while protecting the client against vendor nonperformance (Susarla et al. 2010). While the extant literature in IS has focused on either operational benefits of asset transfer or potential costs of lock-in, our study develops a comprehensive framework. It considers a multitude of aspects, including efficiency benefits and the potential for hold-up and switching costs, and highlights the strategic role of asset transfer. When quality is non-verifiable, an optimal asset transfer contract involves tradeoffs between efficiency and the potential for a vendor’s opportunistic behavior, and client firms use asset transfer not only to screen vendors but also to induce quality-improving effort.

## The Model

We consider a client who wants to contract with a vendor for the provision of one unit of IT services in each of two periods: a first period, and a second, or extended, period. The contract between the client and the vendor is for a maximum of two periods. The vendor delivers IT services to the client through a project team. The vendor’s type describes his<sup>4</sup> project team’s intrinsic capability, which includes technical expertise, the ability to grasp a client’s business processes and objectives, and the flexibility to respond to changes in requirements as business conditions change (Aubert et al. 1998; Lacity and Willcocks 1996). The parameter $\theta \in \{ \theta _ { L } ,$ $\theta _ { H } \}$ denotes whether the project team deployed by the vendor is of low or of high-capability wherein $\theta _ { H } > \theta _ { L }$ The probability that the vendor<sup>5</sup> or project team is of highcapability or type $\theta _ { H }$ is $\mu ,$ and the probability that the vendor is of low-capability or type $\theta _ { L }$ is $1 - \mu .$ The vendor knows the project team’s intrinsic capability $\theta _ { j }$ where $j \in \{ H , L \}$ but the client knows only the probability distribution. Although the vendor’s experience and certifications (like CMM level) are helpful to a client in vendor selection, they still leave scope for heterogeneity in capability at the project team level.

The quality of the delivered IT services depends on the vendor’s intrinsic capability (which is his private information) and on the vendor’s costly quality-improving effort (which is not observed by the client) (Elitzur and Wensley 1997). The quality of delivered IT services may be verifiable and therefore contractible (Bhattacharya et al. 2011; Hasija et al. 2008; Roels et al. 2010), or intangible and therefore, difficult for a third party to verify and, hence, non-contractible (Bapna et al. 2010; Gopal and Koka 2009; Grover et al. 1996). Qualitycontingent contracts are obviously infeasible when quality is non-verifiable, even though the quality of IT services is observed by the client.

Let $\boldsymbol q ( \boldsymbol \theta , \boldsymbol x )$ be the quality of IT services delivered by a vendor of type è who exerts x quality-improving effort, such that $q ( \theta ,$ $x ) = \theta + x .$ An additive model is a common formulation in principal-agent models in economics in the presence of adverse selection and moral hazard (Bac 2000; Gibbons and Murphy 1992; Laffont and Tirole 1986; 1990). This formulation implies that there are no complementarities between vendor capability and effort as far as quality is concerned.

The outsourcing contract offered by the client may also provide for transferring some IT assets (including hardware, software, and human assets) to the vendor. Such asset transfer has three distinct aspects: (1) cash flow from the client’s sale of the assets to the vendor at the beginning of the first period (Lee 1996); (2) a potential reduction in the costs of ITservice provisioning because a vendor may manage IT assets more efficiently (Gurbaxani 2007; Joiner 2011); and (3) switching cost to the client if she decides to terminate the contract at the end of the first period (Bahli and Rivard 2003; Whitten et al. 2010). To study the strategic nature of asset transfer in outsourcing contracts, we assume that the net present value of cash flow attributable to asset transfer equals the net present value of the transferred IT assets. That is, asset transfer has zero net monetary value to both the client and the vendor.<sup>6</sup>

We model the relationship between the client and the vendor as a dynamic game, wherein the client offers a take-it-orleave-it contract with the level of IT asset transfer A, where $A = 0$ represents a contract with no asset transfer and $A \ = 1$ represents a contract with a full complement of asset transfer. The switching cost is a real resource cost which is one-sided since it is incurred only by the client if the contract is terminated in the interim (Barthélemy and Quélin 2006; Gurbaxani 2007; Whitten and Wakefield 2006). Further, asset transfer has operational benefits. It decreases the cost of delivering the services, but this efficiency gain is achieved only if the asset transfer is above a threshold level A. ## Optimal Contracts with the Possibility of In-House Provisioning

The optimal second-best contract given in Lemma 1 is designed such that both types of vendors consider participating. But when no high-capability vendor participates, the client offers the contract to a low-capability vendor with lower than the efficient level of asset transfer to economize on information rent. Therefore, when the client keeps open the option to contract with a low-capability vendor by offering a menu of contracts, she not only receives lower quality IT services from the low-capability vendor (when contracted) due to low capability and distortion in the level of asset transfer, but also pays a higher price to the high-capability vendor (when contracted) due to information rent. This double negative in keeping open the option of contracting with a low-capability vendor when no high-capability vendor participates may outweigh the benefits of offering a menu of contracts. In turn, this may lead to a situation in which it may be optimal for a client to offer a contract that is accepted only by a highcapability vendor, and the low-capability vendor does not participate in outsourcing. In this situation, if no highcapability vendor participates, then the client maintains inhouse IT service delivery (no outsourcing).

## Contracts When Cost of Service Provisioning Increases with Asset Transfer

In the numerical example presented earlier and the contracts developed in Lemmas 1 and 2, the vendor’s cost of service provisioning decreases with the level of asset transfer if it is larger than the threshold level (A). This implies that asset transfer leads to efficiency gains and lower cost of IT-services provisioning. The justification for this conceptualization comes from prior literature and industry reports which find that cost saving is the key objective of IT outsourcing contracts with asset transfer (Joiner 2011; Lacity et al. 1994; Roodhooft and Warlop 1999). On the other hand, studies have also found evidence of strategic objectives for IT outsourcing rather than purely cost reduction (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998). For example, a client firm developing cutting edge machine learning tools may outsource to gain access to the best software developers rather than to merely lower the cost of IT-services delivery. Therefore, there may be situations where a client firm may consider outsourcing IT services even if the cost of service provisioning is increasing in asset transfer.

When the cost of service provisioning is increasing in asset transfer (see “Proof of Lemma $1 \mathrm { A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ in Appendix B for modified assumptions and justification), unlike the situation presented earlier where the efficient level of asset transfer is $A = 1$ , the efficient level of asset transfer is $A = 0 .$ . In other words, if there is full information and the client can observe the vendor’s capability, then she will offer contracts with A = 0. Under unobservable vendor capability and effort, if the client offers a contract with $A = 0$ to both types of vendors, then she needs to provide information rent to the high-type vendor $( c _ { L } ( A = 0 ) \ = \ c _ { H } ( A = 0 ) )$ Since the cost differential is decreasing in asset transfer, the client can economize on information rent by sacrificing efficiency from the lowcapability vendor and offering asset transfer $A _ { L } > 0 .$ This dynamic leads to the structuring of a menu of contracts that successfully screen the vendors<sup>12</sup> (Figure 1a in Appendix B describes the efficiency-information rent tradeoff). ## Non-Verifiable Quality

When quality is non-verifiable, quality-contingent contracts are infeasible. Clearly, prices in either period or contract extension after the first period cannot be contingent on quality. This implies that the client does not have an instrument with which she can condition the extension of the contract at the end of the first period. Moreover, the contract cannot provide for penalty payments (F) contingent on quality. Hence, contracts specified in Lemma 1 are infeasible.

## Role of Asset Transfer under Unobservable Vendor Capability and Effort

To study the role of asset transfer under unobservable vendor capability and effort, when quality is non-verifiable, we first develop benchmark contracts without asset transfer. It is straightforward to show that if the client pays the cost of optimal quality-improving effort in the first period, then the vendor is better off by not exerting any quality-improving effort because the client cannot make payment contingent on any verifiable contracting variable. Since the client knows this, she will not pay for the cost of the quality-improving effort in the first period and hence a rational vendor will not exert any effort. Moreover, since the client has only one contracting instrument, price, she has no ability to screen vendors.

PROPOSITION 2: When quality is non-verifiable and the outsourcing contract has no provision for asset transfer, in the presence of unobservable vendor capability and effort, the client can neither screen vendors nor induce any optimal quality-improving effort.

The economic intuition behind Proposition 2 is that a pricealone contract has no screening power and the client has no choice but to offer the same price contract to both types of vendors in each period. Since there is no asset transfer, the cost of service provisioning by high- and low-capability vendors in each period is c. The rational response of the vendor is to choose $x = 0$ in both periods. The payoff to the vendor is $p _ { j } ^ { \ 1 } \ + p _ { j } ^ { \ 2 } \ - \ 2 c$ where $j \in \{ H , L \}$ To satisfy the vendor’s individual rationality, the client must choose $p _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { ~ l ~ } } + p _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { ~ 2 ~ } } - 2 c$ , and the vendor’s payoff in each period is ${ p } _ { j } ^ { 1 } - c = { p } _ { j } ^ { 2 } - c = 0$ , which is independent of his type. This implies that a price-alone contract has no screening power.

From the above discussion, it is clear that in order to improve contracting efficiency, the client needs an instrument that can (1) help in screening vendors, and (2) incentivize the vendor to exert quality-improving effort in the first period without explicitly getting paid for it. This instrument should credibly signal to the vendor a commitment to not appropriate the gains of the quality-improving effort. One such instrument is asset transfer to the vendor because it imposes a resource cost on the client in terms of a one-sided switching cost, but has no implications for the benefit to the vendor if the contract is terminated after the first period. The client’s self-imposed switching cost may allow her to make a credible commitment of contract extension to the vendor if the vendor exerts effort and the observed service quality after the first period is high. Moreover, asset transfer may allow the client to screen vendors, leading to lowering of the cost of IT-services provisioning. While asset transfer has the potential to be beneficial to the client, the resulting one-sided switching cost also creates the potential for a vendor to exploit the resulting lockin. The vendor may exploit the reluctance of the client to switch vendors due to the switching cost by exerting no quality-improving effort in the first period.

Therefore, the design of any efficient contract with asset transfer should be such that the first- and second-period prices ensure that (1) any vendor who does not exert optimal qualityimproving effort in the first period gets a negative payoff and the client is better off by terminating the contract after the first period; (2) the menu of contracts is incentive-compatible such that both types of vendors are better off by selecting the contract designed for their type; (3) any vendor who truthfully selects a contract offered for his type and exerts optimal quality-improving effort in the first period gets a net nonnegative payoff over the two periods; and (4) the switching cost created by the asset transfer is not so large as to allow the vendor to opportunistically exploit it. The first condition excludes free-riding by vendors. The second condition makes truth telling the best response. The third condition ensures that both types of vendors remain interested in the contract, and the fourth condition may limit the level of optimal asset transfer.

When quality is non-verifiable, the client offers a contract which specifies the level of asset transfer and the first- and second-period price, if the contract is extended. The client must commit to the second-period price at the beginning of the contract period to assure the vendor that he will be rewarded in the second period if he delivers high quality services in the first period. More formally, the contract offered is $\mathrm { I S } = \{ S _ { j } = ( A _ { j } , $ p<sup>1</sup><sub>j</sub> )(extend or terminate)(p<sup>2</sup><sub>j</sub> , if extended)} where $j \in \{ H , L \}$ . In this game, a strategy profile combined with the client’s beliefs about vendors’ types is a Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium if (1) both players’ strategies are optimal given the other player’s strategy and (2) the client’s beliefs about the vendors’ types are consistent in the sense that they are the conditional probabilities that the vendor is the same type when a certain contract is accepted and updated based on Bayes’ rule whenever possible. 1. The client offers a set of contracts: $S = \{ S _ { H } , S _ { L } \}$ , where $S _ { H } = \{ A = 1 , p _ { H } ^ { 1 } = p _ { H } ^ { 1 S B } , p _ { H } ^ { 2 } = p _ { H } ^ { 2 S B }$ , if contract is extended} and $S _ { L } = \{ A _ { L } = A _ { L } ^ { S B } , p _ { L } ^ { 1 } = p _ { L } ^ { 1 S B } , p _ { L } ^ { 2 } = p _ { L } ^ { 2 S B }$ if contract extended}, extends the $S _ { H }$ contract if the observed quality after the first period is at least $\theta _ { H } + x _ { H } ^ { * }$ and extends the $S _ { L }$ contract if the observed quality after the first period is at least $\theta _ { L } + x _ { L } ^ { * } ,$ and terminates the contracts otherwise; where $p _ { H } ^ { 1 S B } , p _ { L } ^ { 1 S B } , p _ { H } ^ { 2 S B }$ , and $p _ { L } ^ { 2 S B }$ are the solutions for the client’s optimization problem.

## Discussion

Given the long-term nature of outsourcing contracts, it is critically important for a client to select the appropriate vendor from a set of vendors with heterogeneous intrinsic capabilities, which is their private information. Therefore, the contracting mechanism must be designed to induce participating vendors to self-select into the appropriate contract and to exert the optimal level of quality-improving effort, which is challenging because quality-improving effort is unobservable. Moreover, the quality of IT services can be nonverifiable and thus noncontractual. This implies that the client cannot make payments contingent on quality or effort nor select the appropriate vendor by observing capability. One way by which the client can address these challenges is to hold back a portion of the payment and release it only when she observes high quality services, while providing credible assurances to the vendor that she will not renege on her promise. Therefore, any design of efficient contracts requires that the client must devise a contractual mechanism that facilitates an inter-temporal exchange of welfare from the first period to the second period (Holmstrom 1982) by including contract extendibility clauses based on observed quality even although it is non-verifiable.

When quality is non-verifiable and price is the only contracting instrument available to a client, asset transfer has dual roles in achieving contractual efficiency. First, the selfimposed switching cost due to asset transfer allows the client to make a credible commitment to the vendor that his qualityimproving effort in the first period will be rewarded in the second period. Second, asset transfer provides an additional instrument to the client to design a self-selection screening contract. The client is able to do so by exploiting the heterogeneity in vendors’ efficiency in service provisioning. Therefore, the client uses the level of asset transfer as a strategic device to resolve adverse selection, induce optimal quality-improving effort, and mitigate contractual inefficiency based on non-verifiable quality.

Transferring IT assets creates switching costs which affect the client asymmetrically. That is, asset transfer imposes a cost only if the client does not extend the contract at the end of the first period. This one-sided switching cost allows the client to hold back the cost of effort incurred by the vendor in the first period and credibly announce a higher second-period price. The second-period price is contingent on the extension of the contract based on the client’s observed level of the quality of services at the end of the first period. By offering a first-period price that is not higher than the cost of provisioning basic IT services, the client makes the contract worthless for any vendor who does not intend to exert qualityimproving effort in the first period. This eliminates freeriding by vendors who know that the client will not extend the contract after observing low quality in the first period and resulting in negative payoff. Further, a low-capability vendor has no incentive to mimic a high-capability vendor by exerting higher than optimal level of quality-improving effort because he strictly gets a net negative payoff.

On the other hand, asset transfer reassures the vendor that the client will not appropriate the benefits of his qualityimproving effort. The client offers an extended-period price such that a high-capability vendor gets positive information rent and a low-capability vendor gets his outside option (payoff that the low-capability vendor would receive if the contract is not awarded to him). Any mechanism based only on price affects the payoffs to both types of vendors symmetrically. That is, the price-alone contract results either in a positive payoff for the low-capability vendor or in the client’s failure to attract the high-capability vendor. Therefore, a contract with price alone can never achieve incentivecompatible self-selection. Further, such a contract cannot induce quality-improving effort because quality is noncontractual.

Since asset transfer creates one-sided switching costs to the client, her ability to achieve efficiency is constrained. Under some conditions, she cannot transfer a full complement of assets to the vendor because it creates excessive switching costs and allows the vendor to hold up the client. If the switching cost for the client is greater than the sum of the cost of optimal quality-improving effort and the cost of service provisioning, then an opportunistic vendor will not exert quality-improving effort in the first period. He knows that the client will be better off extending the contract even if low quality services are delivered. This possibility of opportunistic behavior—when switching costs are excessive—by the vendor limits the client’s ability to achieve the secondbest self-selection contract and may lead to a situation where the client lowers the level of asset transfer to the highcapability vendor. Under some conditions—depending on the interplay of the switching cost, the cost of service provisioning with transfer of the full complement of assets, and the cost of the optimal level of effort—the client is better off by either offering a pooling contract or excluding low-capability vendors from the menu of contracts. Intuitively, vendors view a client’s switching cost not only as an assurance that she will not appropriate the benefits of their quality-improving effort by terminating the contract, but also as a strategic tool to be compensated for the cost of quality-improving effort without actually exerting any. Therefore, the ability of a client to use a particular level of asset transfer as a strategic device is constrained.

The literature suggests other operational explanations for the transfer of IT assets in IT outsourcing relationships such as obtaining immediate cash benefits, increasing operational efficiency, and providing incentives for asset-specific investments (Gurbaxani 2007; Ritwik and Tisnovsky 2007). We combine these operational explanations for asset transfer with its strategic nature and show that the level of asset transfer can serve as an additional instrument in the design of screening contracts. Moreover, we integrate the agency theory perspective on asset transfer with the property rights literature. Asset transfer not only mitigates inefficiencies based on asymmetric information (unobservable vendor capability and effort) and non-verifiability of quality, but also allows the client to exploit gains from the lower cost of service delivery resulting from the vendor’s asset-specific investments.

Our findings make two contributions to the outsourcing literature: First, they provide a framework that enhances our current understanding about the motivations for asset transfer. Second, they examine optimal incentive contracts that consider the tradeoffs between operational efficiency and lock-in created by asset transfer. Client firms use the level of asset transfer to mitigate agency issues where efficient selfselection is achieved via a client’s enhanced commitment to the relationship, which is demonstrated by assuming the switching cost associated with asset transfer. This has some similarities to Williamson (1983) in which a party makes a credible commitment by self-imposing a one-sided cost in case of a breach of promise by offering a “hostage,” and also to Holmstrom (1982) in which managers exert qualityimproving effort in the current period due to a concern for future career prospects.

It is worthwhile to examine how the results change if we relax our assumptions. In our setup, vendors are risk neutral<sup>13</sup> and the dynamics of our contracting mechanism and economic intuition are robust to this assumption. If vendors are risk averse, they may require a higher level of asset transfer to assure them of a client’s commitment to contract extension if optimal quality-improving effort is exerted in the first period, as well as higher future compensation to account for the negative payoff in the first period. Moreover, we assumed that a client’s switching cost is independent of the vendor’s intrinsic capability and depends only on the level of asset transfer. If this switching cost is higher when assets are transferred to a high-capability vendor, the optimal level of asset transfer would be lower than the risk-neutral case. Furthermore, we assumed that the switching cost of the transfer of the smallest feasible complement of assets (A) is larger than the cost of optimal quality-improving effort (assumption 5) to ensure that even with second-best level of asset transfer, the client is able to induce optimal qualityimproving effort. If this assumption was relaxed, the client’s ability to induce optimal quality-improving effort would be constrained because asset transfer may not create sufficient switching costs and the client may not be able to credibly hold back the cost of effort in the first period.

We also assumed that the outsourcing contract covers two periods, and therefore, the client is unable to incentivize optimal quality-improving effort in the second period. This setup is consistent with anecdotal evidence that outsourcing vendors make significant upfront investments and attempt to profit later in a long-term relationship (Outsource Portfolio 2009). Our model can easily be extended into a multi-period setting. While the vendor’s profit-extracting behavior may have a significant negative impact on service quality and even on the relationship (JLARC 2009), clients and vendors would face the same issues after the first period as they faced before the first period in the current model. Accordingly, the qualitative results of our two-period model would not change in a multi-period setting.

## Managerial Implications

While the literature has focused on the efficiency gains of asset transfer to the vendor, we suggest that strategic considerations are extremely important as well. Our results provide the economic rationale for the observation that in outsourcing contracts with asset transfer, vendors often make significant upfront investments in the initial contractual stages and make profits only later in the relationship (JLARC 2009; Kern et al. 2002). One key finding is that transfer of a large complement of IT assets may actually provide a perverse incentive to the vendor to shirk if switching costs are too high. The vendor knows that the client will not terminate the contract even after observing poor quality services resulting in the client being locked into a suboptimal outsourcing relationship. This implies that clients must carefully consider the tradeoff between efficiency gains and switching costs.

When firms outsource IT activities for strategic reasons, the vendors’ intrinsic ability is critical. It is exactly in these situations that clients should use asset transfer to achieve efficient self-selection contracts. This seemingly surprising result is consistent with the structure of IT outsourcing relationships in which IT assets essential for delivering quality services, such as software applications and IT human capital, are frequently transferred to vendors. Interestingly, the switching costs arising from asset transfer help the client establish a long-term relationship with a high-capability vendor who exerts optimal quality-improving effort. Since asset transfer induces the vendor to exert optimal quality-improving effort in the first period, it reduces the possibility of contract termination, and reduces the risks that the client will bear these switching costs. Termination fees<sup>14</sup> are commonly used in IT outsourcing contracts because they also create substantial switching costs for clients. However, termination fees can create perverse incentives for the vendor because the contract provides a monetary payoff if the contract is terminated, which may encourage the vendor to exert zero qualityimproving effort in the first period.

## Limitations and Extensions

There are several potential extensions for future research. This paper focuses on contractual mechanisms to mitigate the risks associated with adverse selection, moral hazard, and non-verifiable quality. Alternatively, a vendor may use some informative signal, such as certification (Gao et al. 2010), to convey private information about his capability and the client can use this signal rather than the level of asset transfer to screen vendors. The analysis of such signaling mechanisms in the context of IT outsourcing relationships may provide useful managerial insights to vendors who have to devise strategies to mitigate information asymmetry. It may also be worthwhile to explore effort monitoring as a possible mechanism, albeit with up-front monetary costs. In our model, we assumed reduced-form moral hazard. Additional insights might emerge from a study of self-selection contracts with asset transfer in the presence of moral hazard.
