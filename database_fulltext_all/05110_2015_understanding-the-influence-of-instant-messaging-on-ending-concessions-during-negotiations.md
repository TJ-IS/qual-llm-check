# Understanding the Influence of Instant Messaging on Ending Concessions During Negotiations

NORMAN A. JOHNSON AND RANDOLPH B. COOPER

NORMAN A. JOHNSON is an associate professor in the Decision and Information Sciences Department in the Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. He holds an MBA in finance from Baruch College and an M.Phil. in business and a Ph.D. in management planning and information systems, both from the City University of New York. His research interests include media and negotiation, virtual worlds, and measurements and analytics. His research has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, and Decision Support Systems.

RANDOLPH B. COOPER is a professor in the Decision and Information Sciences Department in the Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. He received his bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees from the University of California at Los Angeles. His current research interests include examining the influence of different media on negotiating dyads. He has published in a variety of academic journals, including DataBase, Decision Support Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Omega.

ABSTRACT: In many online price negotiations, instant messaging (IM) and audio channels rival each other in use, but IM’s use is on the rise. In these contexts, people who are strangers to each other are inclined to act more competitively. They are driven by self-interest and strive for greater gains from agreement in the form of what is termed “ending concessions.” To date, however, researchers have focused on striving for agreement as the main goal of negotiators. This study uses a selfishness theory to explain how individuals using IM, as compared to those using audio, can encourage their negotiation partners to make ending concessions, and thereby increase individuals’ gains when agreement is reached. We use data from a negotiation laboratory experiment to test a model of ending concessions that is predicted by unrestricted offers and comments that negotiators make over IM and audio. We base our analyses on the contents of the resulting communications. The objects of negotiation are simulated lottery tickets. Our results provide three key insights. First, when using IM, partners appear to interpret offers that include concessions from individuals as attempts to manipulate partners into accepting non-equitable agreements; as a result partners decrease their ending concessions. These interpretations do not appear to occur when using audio, and as a result individuals’ concessions do not decrease partners’ ending concessions. Second, using IM, when individuals disagree with anger directed at partners’ bidding behaviors, partners respond by increasing their ending concessions. Ending concessions are further increased when using audio. Third, and in contrast, using IM, when individuals disagree with emotion that does not include anger, partners respond by decreasing their ending concessions. Ending concessions are further decreased when using audio. These insights provide guidance for practice, and are bases for future research on the use of IM and audio for negotiation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: instant messaging, audio, selfishness, agreement, ending concession, self-interest, deindividuation, media, negotiation experiment, lottery, negative reciprocation.

Negotiation is a process by which a joint decision is made by two or more parties, who perceive that their interests conflict [74]. Often, these parties are sellers and buyers who are engaged in price negotiations, and the online environment provides a means for them to exchange offers as they negotiate. For example, as an alternate marketplace to eBay [22], Bonanza provides an opportunity for buyers and sellers to engage in one-on-one price negotiations using instant messaging (IM) in order to reach agreements [14]. The use of IM for negotiations is growing at a rapid pace [11, 18]. For example, in its first year of operation, Bonanza (then called Bonanzle) attracted about 35,000 registered users, who engaged in tens of thousands of transactions [49]; the site is currently growing at an annual rate of 60% [73].<sup>2</sup> In addition to negotiation sites, individual retail organizations are finding online real-time IM negotiations profitable. For example, WideStorm.com provides IM software that is implemented on organizations’ websites and enables consumers and vehicle dealers to negotiate the price of a vehicle entirely online [33]. This software is currently implemented on the websites of 300 automobile, recreation vehicle, motorcycle, and boat dealerships across the United States [32].

The use of IM to facilitate negotiations has surpassed the use of audio (e.g., telephone) in some instances. For example, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) website on finance and development reports over-the-counter market dealers are using IM instead of audio in bilateral trades in order to exchange quotes and negotiate prices as much as 80 percent of the time [2].<sup>3</sup> This trend, combined with research indicating that the use of IM can affect how interpretations are developed among communicating parties [13, 67], raises the question as to how a negotiation process is altered by the use of IM in contrast to the use of audio.

To be successful in negotiations, the literature prescribes that negotiators must carefully navigate conflicting tendencies to compete and maximize self-interest, on one hand, and to cooperate and maximize joint interest, on the other, in an effort to reach agreement [10]. To some extent then, negotiators are driven by self-interest, and they strive for greater gains from agreement in the form of what is termed “ending concessions” [61]. This idea, along with growing use of IM, motivates our research question: With respect to ending concessions, what insights based on selfishness theory can be used strategically by individuals to provide for greater gain when using IM?

Our research is framed from the viewpoint of negotiators who are inclined to act competitively. In general, competitiveness results from a social comparison process in which individuals attempt to achieve or maintain a superior relative position compared to others [31]. Individuals are more competitive in contexts that are zero sum (the individual can gain only at the partner’s expense), that involve few competitors, and that involve uncertainty regarding the quantity of a resource and/ or the identity of the competitor [31].<sup>4</sup> These contextual characteristics describe many price negotiations over IM, such as those we mentioned above that occur between two individuals who do not know each other.

We are interested in ending concession as an outcome because it is an important goal for negotiators who are acting more competitively. In a price negotiation, an ending concession by a partner occurs when the partner offers a price that is equal to the individual’s offer, and thus necessarily ends negotiations with agreement [61]. If an individual can encourage the partner to make a larger ending concession, ceteris paribus, this then results in a greater gain for the individual. Though many negotiation research studies have examined how agreement is reached, we were unable to find research identifying factors that influence the offering of ending concessions. Research on agreement typically employs cooperation theory, which suggests that agreement can be reached through a process in which negotiators cooperate when partners offer concessions that approximate individuals’ concessions [61].<sup>5</sup> Cooperation theory is based on the positive reciprocation norm in which partners feel obligated to repay favors, such as concessions [34]. However, ending concessions do not involve reciprocation, and the applicability of cooperation theory for our purposes is therefore diminished.

An ending concession by a negotiating partner results in the partner’s providing greater benefit to the individual (agreement at a price that is more favorable to the individual) than to the partner himself or herself (agreement at a price that is less favorable to the partner). This kind of less selfish behavior, wherein one provides benefit to another at his or her own expense, has been studied by researchers in many fields, such as biology, psychology, and economics. Our work is guided by aspects of the social psychology selfishness literature that apply to communication within negotiating dyads because of our specific interest in understanding how communication by an individual within the IM and audio contexts affects the degree to which the partner exhibits less selfish behavior (in the form of greater ending concessions) during negotiations.

Based on our research model and empirical results, we contribute to an understanding of the negotiation process for media researchers, negotiation researchers, and also researchers specifically examining the use of information technology in the negotiation process. For example, media researchers will be interested in our use of the selfishness perspective because it will help them recognize how self-interested people interpret others’ communications. This understanding can then be employed strategically by individuals to help them alter their communications in order to influence self-interested people. Negotiation researchers will be interested in how we assess the quality of negotiated outcomes when parties are acting competitively.

In contrast to typical quality assessments that measure Pareto efficiency or joint profit, we propose the use of ending concession in order to evaluate agreement quality because it focuses on the amount an individual gains at the partner’s expense. When combined with whether agreement is reached or not, a focus on these two dependent variables will help researchers better understand the actions of more selfinterested negotiators. For researchers interested in the ways that information technology can influence negotiations, we offer our research model, with its findings that are supported by observations from an experiment we conducted.

## Theoretical Background

We are interested in understanding how an individual can encourage his or her negotiating partner to offer more ending concession. To this end, we first develop and test a research model with ending concession by the partner as the dependent variable. This model is informed by findings in the negotiation and communication media literatures, and what is novel about it is based on the social psychology selfishness literature. The novel viewpoint of our model is reasonable because more (less) ending concession by the partner reflects less (more) selfish behavior. Discussions below hypothesize that the media employed as well as some types of communication by the individual to the partner can result in the partner’s acting more or less selfishly and thereby offering less or more ending concession. However, since agreement is the basic goal of negotiations, it would be counterproductive if an individual’s communications that are used strategically to encourage more ending concession by the partner also discouraged agreement. Therefore, we also examine the degree to which communications that promote ending concessions also affect the ability to reach agreement.

## Selfish Behavior

A person’s behavior is more selfish to the degree that it benefits the person with less regard for others [68]. Until the 1980s, scientists’ beliefs regarding people’s behaviors typically assumed self-interested rationality, that is, selfishness [8]. “Whether one spoke to a biologist, a psychologist, a psychiatrist, a sociologist, an economist, or a political scientist, the answer was the same … [even behaviors that appear] to be motivated by a concern for someone else’s needs will, under closer scrutiny, prove to have ulterior selfish motives” [58, p. 28]. However, recent research in these fields is “all at least compatible with the position that [acting less selfishly] is part of human nature. [That is, people have] … other-regarding sentiments … [they] contribute to public goods from which they benefit little … [and they sacrifice] … for others to whom they are not related” [58, p. 29]. For example, in one context, research demonstrated that about 30 percent of people tended to behave more selfishly whereas 50 percent tended to behave less selfishly [27]. And, less selfish behavior occurs even when people are anonymous strangers in one-off interactions [52].

Though researchers in many fields have studied selfishness, we use the social psychology selfishness literature in order to gain insight into the ways that an individual’s communication within the IM and audio contexts affects whether the partner exhibits more or less selfish behavior during negotiations. This is a reasonable theoretical choice because (1) social psychology deals with the influence of social factors (such as communication) on a person, (2) the act of a partner providing greater ending concessions can involve less selfish behavior because the partner is providing greater benefit to the individual (agreement and a better price for the individual) than to the partner himself or herself (agreement but at a worse price), (3) research has shown that contextual factors can affect the degree to which people exhibit more or less selfish behavior [8, 58], and (4) research has specifically linked more or less selfish behavior to the communication medium employed (e.g., [25]).

## Selfish Behavior and Media

Since the early 1970s, researchers have examined the impacts of different media on communications among individuals [83]. For example, Ochsman and Chapnis [57] compared the effects of ten different media on cooperative problem solving, and found a significant difference in the time required to reach a solution depending on whether or not the media allowed audio communication. Of specific interest here are findings in this vast research stream that can help us to understand implications for negotiation partners behaving more or less selfishly as a result of the media used to communicate.

Social presence theory [69], information richness theory [15], and the social identity model of deindividuation effects (SIDE[60]) suggest that the use of IM, as compared to audio, reduces individuals’ abilities to convey nonverbal cues and to provide quick feedback, which significantly reduces relationship and socially oriented communication [36, 70]. These impacts on communication are also supported by cuelessness theory [65] and reduced social cues theory [71], both of which suggest that IM communication can decrease personalization and increase the psychological distance among communicators. As described next, these effects can lead negotiators using IM to behave more selfishly. Reasons for this increase in selfish behavior include the degree to which negotiation partners misinterpret individuals’ communications. Communications between individuals and their negotiating partners consist of offers and comments.

## Offers

Individuals make offers to partners that indicate the amount for which they are willing to purchase or to sell an asset during negotiations. How partners interpret and act in response to such offers may be shaped by the medium they use. Reductions in nonverbal cues associated with IM (such as the inability to convey the pitch and cadence of individuals’ voices) decrease partners’ abilities to evaluate individuals trustworthiness [24]. As a result, partners believe that individuals will take advantage of this decreased evaluation ability. This leads partners to have higher expectations that individuals will exhibit unfair and unethical behaviors [54, 78] aimed at manipulating partners into accepting nonequitable agreements [17, 27]. These negative expectations are heightened when individuals are strangers because of the sinister attribution error [75]. As a result, partners may interpret individuals offers as attempts at manipulation and the partners tend to act more selfishly [8, 59].

When using IM, partners also act more selfishly in reaction to individuals’ offers due to the increased salience of the negative reciprocity norm. This is because the use of IM reduces relationship and socially oriented communication [36, 70], which increases deindividuation [60], and thereby leads to greater conformance with situation-specific norms [60]. Since partners expect that individuals will be unfair and unethical, situation-specific norms that involve negative reciprocity are salient. As a result, partners tend to interpret individuals’ offers as attempts at manipulation and to follow the negative reciprocity norm, which dictates that unfavorable treatment received by a partner should be returned in kind by the partner [23, 34]. (This is distinct from positive reciprocity, which is salient when partners expect individuals to be fair and ethical, and involves the repayment of favors from individuals [23, 34].) Partners are therefore motivated to punish individuals by acting more selfishly in response to what the partners perceive as manipulative offers by individuals [25, 27]. This propensity to punish individuals perceived to be behaving unfairly or unethically is supported by neurobiological research linking such punishment to the activation of the brain’s reward system [27].

In contrast, the use of audio provides nonverbal feedback through, for example, tone of voice, and simultaneous feedback using verbal expressions, such as “uh huh” [81]. As a result, individuals and their partners believe that they are better able to evaluate each other’s trustworthiness; and knowing this, individuals and their partners expect that each other will be more trustworthy [54]. In addition, partners are more aware that they are interacting with another person, which increases their expectations that individuals will behave fairly and ethically, while decreasing partners’ feelings of deindividuation [54, 60]. Audio thereby results in partners interpreting individuals’ offers more in accord with fairness norms (such as positive reciprocity) and acting more in accord with those norms [23]. This behavior is supported with neurobiological evidence that reward circuits are triggered in people’s brains when partaking in less selfish behavior [8].

## Comments

Negotiating individuals make comments in order to defend their offers and interests and to argue against those of their partners [61], and common among these comments are disagreements. We focus on communicated disagreements because they contain negative affect, which involves unpleasant feelings such as tension, rejection, and antagonism [4, 6], and which have been shown to be important in negotiation processes (e.g., [39]). Independent of the medium employed, when an individual communicates negative affect, this can increase the partner’s negative emotional state, which increases the partner’s expectations that the individual will behave unfairly and unethically [45, 79]. As described above, these expectations increase the partner’s tendency to act more selfishly in order to reduce the likelihood of a nonequitable agreement in which they will be taken advantage of by the individual and in order to punish the individual.

However, independent of the medium employed, when disagreeing comments include anger at the partner’s bidding behavior, the tendency by the partner to behave more selfishly can be overcome by strategic considerations[72].<sup>6</sup> This is because expressions of anger by the individual that are focused specifically on the partner’s bidding behavior may signal that the individual has reached his or her concession limit and indicate to the partner the increasing likelihood that agreement will not be reached [72]. A partner using these comments as signals to adjust expectations about what the individual will do is similar to what is referred to as “tracking” [61]. As a result of such signals, a partner is more inclined to act less selfishly in order to increase the likelihood of reaching agreement [72].

There are thus opposite influences on the partner’s behavior, depending on whether or not disagreeing comments by the individual are interpreted by the partner as containing anger directed at the partner’s bidding behavior: individuals’ disagreeing comments that are interpreted by the partner as containing anger at the partner’s bidding behavior can decrease the partner’s selfish behavior while other disagreeing comments can increase the partner’s selfish behavior. However, many studies have shown that media such as IM decrease understanding among communicators due to the reduced number of cues—such as tone of voice—that can be conveyed [e.g., 39, 75]. This can lead to misinterpretations as to whether disagreements communicated by an individual contain anger directed at the partner’s bidding behavior.

Partners using IM are more likely to misinterpret individuals’ anger at bidding behavior as being focused on the partners themselves. This occurs because IM reduces the ability of negotiators to engage in mutual correction processes (such as asking brief clarifying questions), which results in partners making more “egocentrically tainted” assumptions [75]. Further, the increased psychological distance associated with IM is egocentric in that its reference point is the self in the here and now [77]. As a result of this egocentricity, partners tend to interpret individuals anger at the partner’s bidding behavior as anger directed at the partner’s personal identity. There is thus a decreased ability of a partner to identify and correctly interpret the individual’s anger directed at the partner’s bidding behavior and therefore a decreased ability to act strategically with less selfish behavior.

In contrast, the use of audio increases mutual understanding among negotiators [13], increases personalization, decreases psychological distance, and increases mutual correction abilities such that negotiation partners are less egocentric. This will make a partner less likely to misinterpret the existence and focus of the individual’s anger directed at the partner’s bidding behavior; the partner is thus better able to act strategically and behave less selfishly.

## Offers, Concessions, and Selfish Behavior

We have described how the use of IM versus audio can influence whether partners behave more or less selfishly in reaction to individuals’ offers and disagreements and due to the medium’s effect on how partners interpret individuals’ communications. However, our description does not include how we identify more or less selfish behavior in negotiations. We attend to this critical issue next.

In negotiations, individuals have a common goal of reaching agreement and also have divergent goals in that they prefer different forms of the agreement [74]. For negotiations over the price of an object (a type of distributive negotiations), sellers typically prefer higher agreement prices and buyers typically prefer lower ones. Each price offer by an individual can include a concession, in which the individual changes his or her previous offer in support of the partner’s interests and thus results in a reduction of benefit to the individual [61]. Concessions can take three forms. Unilateral concessions are those offered by an individual without an explicit expectation of a subsequent concession by the partner, but with the hope that the partner will reciprocate with a concession [44]. Reciprocal concessions pay back unilateral concessions [50]. Ending concessions are those that go beyond reciprocation and ensure that negotiations end in agreement [61]. When a partner’s ending concession is larger (smaller), this reflects less (more) selfish behavior because it results in the partner claiming a smaller (larger) portion of the agreement price, which suggests that the partner has given more (less) weight to the individual’s outcomes as compared to the partner’s outcomes.

For example, Figure 1 provides potential offers and acceptable price ranges for a negotiation object. Assume that offers and counteroffers proceed as illustrated in Table 1. Once negotiations have reached the region of prices acceptable to both the seller and buyer (the range from 16 to 22 illustrated in Figure 1), it would be reasonable for either negotiator to make an ending concession. For example, assume that instead of countering with 16 (offer 6 in Table 1), the buyer agrees to the seller’s offer of 22, which results in the buyer offering a reciprocal concession of 1 and an ending concession of 6. Though the buyer values the object more than the price of 22, by agreeing to 22, the buyer incurs an opportunity cost of 6, which could have been saved through further negotiation. That is, since the seller’s acceptable range has a lower limit of 16, he or she might have been willing to go all the way down to this price. For example, if the buyer countered with 16 and then agreed to the seller’s new offer of 20 (offer 8 in Table 1), this would result in the buyer offering a reciprocal concession of 2 and an ending concession of 2, and would have reduced the buyer’s opportunity cost to 4. Therefore, rather than making a large ending concession, a partner typically has opportunities to make smaller unilateral and/or reciprocal concessions and continue with negotiations. Thus, larger ending concessions reflect less selfish behavior because they involve greater opportunity costs, and smaller ending concessions reflect more selfish behavior because they involve smaller opportunity costs.<sup>7</sup> As a result, if an individual can encourage the partner to make larger ending concessions (i.e., to be less selfish), this can result in greater gains (less opportunity costs) for the individual.

## Research Model

We are interested in the contrast between IM and audio media. As described earlier, a zero-sum dyadic negotiation between strangers will increase negotiators’ tendencies to act competitively. Thus, we posit a research model from the perspective of an individual who is interested in obtaining greater ending concessions from his or her negotiation partner (see Figure 2). The hypotheses related to our model focus on how the partner’s ending concessions are influenced by concessions and disagreements communicated by the individual. Hypotheses based on the use of IM end with “im”; hypotheses based on the use of audio end with “a”.

## The Influence of Unilateral, Reciprocal, and First Offer Concessions

An important class of social norms in negotiation is fairness, which includes equity (the equitable distribution of resources [80]) and positive reciprocity (the repayment of favors from others [34]). Though not typically examined, first offers can include unilateral concessions [37]. When an individual offers unilateral concessions, either via first or subsequent offers, and/or responds with reciprocal concessions, these communications are typically interpreted by the partner in light of fairness norms as evidence that the individual is negotiating in a fair manner [38, 53] with a concern for the partner’s interests [41, 61]. This encourages the partner to behave in a fair manner as well by acting less selfishly.

However, Dirks and Ferrin [17] suggest that IM use will promote the partner’s expectations that the individual will behave unethically and unfairly; with these negative expectations, the partner may interpret the individual’s unilateral, reciprocal, and first offer concessions as attempts at unfair manipulation. In addition, these negative expectations along with increased deindividuation suggest that when using IM the negative reciprocity norm may be more salient than fairness norms. As a result, we expect that the partner will behave more selfishly—decreasing endingconcession making—in order to decrease the likelihood of being taken advantage of by the individual and in order to punish the individual by negatively reciprocating what he or she perceives to be unfair manipulation and unethical actions by the individual. This results in the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1im: When negotiating using IM, greater first offer concession by the individual will decrease the partner’s ending concession.

Hypothesis 2im: When negotiating using IM, greater unilateral and reciprocal concessions by an individual will decrease the partner’s ending concession.

Compared to IM, audio increases the partner’s expectations that the individual is acting in a fair and ethical manner. An individual’s concessions are therefore more likely to be interpreted by the partner as evidence that the individual is negotiating in a fair manner with a concern for the partner’s interests. This encourages the partner to behave in a fairer manner, that is, less selfishly, and the partner will be more willing to make a larger ending concession. This leads to the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1a: Compared to IM, audio will reduce the negative effect on ending concessions that results from first offer concessions.

Hypothesis 2a: Compared to IM, audio will reduce the negative effects on ending concessions that result from unilateral and reciprocal concessions.

## The Influence of Disagreeing Communication

We focus on the communication of disagreements because they contain negative affect, which, independent of the medium, (1) increases the partner’s negative emotional state, (2) increases the partner’s expectations that the individual’s intentions are unethical and unfair, and (3) thereby increases the partner’s tendency to act more selfishly in order to decrease the likelihood of a nonequitable agreement in which the partner will be taken advantage of by the individual and in order to punish the individual. We distinguish between two types of disagreements: those with anger at bidding behavior and those without anger.

Independent of the medium, when an individual’s angry disagreements are identified by the partner as being directed at the partner’s bidding behavior, the partner is likely to act strategically and decrease his or her selfish behavior by making a larger ending concession in order to increase the likelihood of reaching agreement. However, clarity as to the existence and focus of anger is reduced when using IM, because IM tends to decrease understanding and make negotiation partners more egocentric. As a result, a partner will misinterpret some of the individual’s anger directed at his or her bidding behavior and therefore hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3im: When negotiating using IM, an individual’s communication of anger that is directed at a partner’s bidding behavior will increase the partner’s ending concession.

Compared to IM, audio enables the partner to better understand communicated affect and makes him or her less egocentric. Thus, audio makes clearer both the existence of anger communicated by the individual and whether the anger is directed at the partner’s bidding behavior. Therefore, it is more likely with audio for the partner to correctly identify the individual’s angry communication at the partner’s bidding behavior, to be aware of the strategic considerations associated with the communication, and to offer a larger ending concession. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3a: Compared to IM, audio will increase the extent to which anger directed at bidding behavior will increase ending concession.

Disagreements communicated by an individual that contain emotion (such as tension) but do not contain anger directed at the partner’s bidding behavior can make the partner act more selfishly due to concerns over achieving an equitable agreement and in order to exact punishment. In addition to this emotional impact, these disagreements are cues to the partner that the partner’s selfish behavior is reasonable because the individual’s lack of anger indicates that the individual does not perceive that the partner is being unreasonable. These emotional and strategic influences of disagreeing comments that do not include anger at bidding behavior work together to make the partner exhibit more selfish behavior, and thereby reduce his or her ending concession.

However, clarity as to the existence and focus of anger at bidding behavior is reduced when using IM, because IM tends to decrease understanding. As a result, a partner will misinterpret some of the individual’s disagreements without anger as disagreements with anger at bidding behavior. Nevertheless, we do not expect all of a partner’s disagreements without anger to be misinterpreted by the partner, and therefore hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4im: When negotiating using IM, an individual’s communication that disagrees without anger will decrease the partner’s ending concession.

Compared to IM, audio enables the partner to better understand communicated affect. Thus, audio makes it clearer whether or not disagreements include anger. This should increase the influence that disagreements without anger have on ending concessions, leading to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4a: Compared to IM, audio will increase the extent to which communication that disagrees without anger will decrease ending concession.

## Method

Participants in the experiment, which took place over a couple of months, included 148 freshmen through seniors (40 percent female) in a university with a population of 17,000 students. As participants arrived at the experiment, they were randomly assigned to different rooms and roles: one for sellers and one for buyers.<sup>8</sup> Sellers received instructions about the experimental task via PowerPoint, and completed three practice rounds of negotiation with an instructor. Buyers did the same at the same time in a different room. During these practice rounds, sellers and buyers were encouraged to ask questions if they were uncertain about the task procedures and objectives. In the practice rounds, they were told the following: (1) The experiment began when a lottery ticket, a prize wheel, a timer, and instructions appeared on their computer screens. (2) After negotiations ended, the appropriate prize wheel would be spun in order to determine the number of points the ticket holder had won. (3) Though dyad negotiation partners were in different locations, they would see the same information at the same time on their computer screens. (4) Anyone could initiate negotiation by contacting their dyad partner via the assigned medium, either using instant messaging or orally using a computer supported audio channel. (5) They had three and a half minutes with which to complete their negotiations.<sup>9</sup> (6) Before the time elapsed, the seller had to indicate via the computer what the parties agreed on in terms of the decision to sell or not to sell the ticket. If the seller did not make such an indication and the time elapsed, both the seller and buyer would receive no points.

After the practice rounds, sellers and buyers were randomly assigned to each other in order to form 74 negotiating dyads. The dyads were then randomly assigned to negotiate using either IM or audio. In addition, these dyads were randomly assigned to negotiate over one of two lottery tickets: one with a relatively high and one with a relatively low expected value. The dyads then performed the experimental negotiations. This resulted in a two-factor randomized design. We did not provide any other experimental manipulations, thus allowing relatively natural interactive dynamics to occur, and making our study more relevant to real-world negotiations [19].

## Task

Our object of negotiation is a simulated lottery ticket, with opportunities to win its face value depicted by a prize wheel. Participants in each dyad negotiated the price of the lottery ticket in terms of points. Each participant in a dyad was given \$10 for participating in the experiment and was told that at the end of it, he or she had a chance to receive an extra \$10, depending on the number of points earned as a result of the outcome of the negotiation and the lottery draw for the ticket’s value.

At the beginning of the negotiations, participants saw a prize wheel on their compuer screens that displayed two potential pairs of lottery ticket values: 0 and 12 points (on the left side of Figure 3) and 0 and 34 points (on the right side of Figure 3). Participants were told that after negotiations ended, there was a 50 percent chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–12 value pair and a 50 percent chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–34 value pair. In addition, participants were told that after the value pair was randomly determined, the prize wheel representing that pair would be spun in order to determine whether the holder of the lottery ticket received 0 or more points. For example, if the 0–12 value pair prize wheel was randomly selected, there was a 74 percent chance that the result of spinning the wheel would be 12 points (the shaded portion in Figure 3 on the left) and a 26 percent chance that the result would be 0 points. If the 0–34 value pair prize wheel was randomly selected, there was a 98 percent chance that the result of spinning the wheel would be 34 points (the shaded portion in Figure 3 on the right) and a 2 percent chance that the result would be 0 points. Note that the participants were not provided with these percentages; instead, if they were interested in the percentages, they had to estimate them by examining the prize wheels. After negotiations ended, this resulted in the ticket holder having a 37 percent chance of winning 12 points $( . 5 ~ ^ { * } ~ . 7 4 )$ , a 49 percent chance of winning 34 points $( . 5 ~ ^ { * } ~ . 9 8 )$ , and a 14 percent chance of winning nothing $( . 5 ^ { * } \cdot 2 6 + . 5 ^ { * } \cdot 0 2 )$

## Operationalizations

Unilateral and Reciprocal Concessions. Within each dyad, we identify the partner that made the last offer. Since we are interested in the degree to which the individual makes more unilateral concessions than the partner, we subtract the partner’s unilateral concessions from the individual’s unilateral concessions to create the unilateral concession measure. In the Table 1 example, the buyer made the ending concession. For this dyad, we would therefore subtract the buyer’s unilateral concessions (0) from the seller’s unilateral concessions (5), resulting in a unilateral concession value of 5.

For reciprocal concessions, we also identify the partner that made the last offer. An individual cannot make a reciprocal concession if the partner does not make a unilateral concession. In addition, the total of an individual’s reciprocal concessions cannot exceed the total of the partner’s unilateral concessions. The measure of reciprocal concessions used in the analyses is therefore reciprocal concessions made by the individual minus unilateral concessions made by the partner. The maximum reciprocal concession is thus zero; the value decreases as reciprocal concessions decline. In the Table 1 example, the buyer made the ending concession. For this dyad, we would therefore subtract the buyer’s unilateral concessions (0) from the seller’s reciprocal concessions (0), resulting in a reciprocal concession value of 0.

Ending Concession. Ending concession is our dependent variable. We first identify the partner making the last offer. When agreement is reached, this last offer includes an ending concession if it has a concession amount that goes beyond what is required to reciprocate any outstanding unilateral concessions that have been made by the individual. Thus, ending concession is determined for the partner making the last offer by adding up all the individual’s unilateral concessions and subtracting from that all of the partner’s reciprocal concessions. This result is then subtracted from the concession associated with the last offer. When this difference is greater than zero, it is the ending concession. An example of the determination of ending concession is provided in Table 1, where the buyer makes the last offer of 20, which includes a reciprocal concession of 2 and an ending concession of 2.

First Offer Concession. For each dyad, only the first offer concession of the individual not making the last offer is employed in our analyses. It would be nice to determine this first offer concession by contrasting the individual’s first offer with the degree to which the individual initially valued the lottery ticket. However, researchers have found that once negotiations begin, such reservation prices change [62].

We therefore measure each seller’s level of concession by the difference between a seller’s first offer and the average of all sellers’ first offers. Similarly, we measure each buyer’s level of concession by the difference between a buyer’s first offer and the average of all buyers’ first offers. More specifically, first offer concession was calculated for the individual who did not make the last offer as the difference between that individual’s first offer and the average first offers made by all other individuals who did not make the last offer. This measure of concession varied with the negotiator role because one would expect a seller’s first offer to be higher than a buyer’s first offer. In addition, each dyad bargained over one of two types of lottery tickets with different expected values, and these different values should influence first offers.<sup>11</sup>

When the buyer made the last offer, the seller’s first offer was subtracted from the appropriate average; when the seller made the last offer, the buyer’s appropriate average was subtracted from the buyer’s first offer. Thus, more (less) concession had larger (smaller) values. For example, if the average of all first offers by sellers who did not make a last offer and when the lottery ticket had a high expected value were 27, an initial seller offer of 28 represents a concession of –1. (i.e., 27 – 28); if the same seller initially offered 25, this would represent a concession of 2 (i.e., 27 – 25), with the 2 representing a greater concession.

Disagreement Without Anger. Bales [4] provides two categories of disagreeing comments that do not include anger. The first is category 10, which conveys disagreement, passive rejection, or formality. For example, one individual in our experiment said “Umm. How about 20 then?” and the partner responded with an unemotional “No”; this “No” is a category 10 comment. The second Bales category conveying disagreement without anger is category 11, which includes some emotional content, such as tension, anxiety, or frustration. For example, in our experiment, one individual gasps and then says “That’s still too high, man.” Since category

10 comments are part of the normal give and take of negotiations, we do not expect them to have any systematic effects on ending concessions. Therefore, we focus on category 11 comments.

Because over 1,700 messages were exchanged, nine coders were trained and employed to code these messages. The messages were randomly divided into three sets. Each set of data was made up of messages from about 25 dyads. Three coders were assigned to each set of data; these coders read or listened to each message and, when appropriate, assigned it to Bales’ category 11. All coders were blind to the research hypotheses and worked independently of each other on coding the messages. The average intercoder reliability for category 11 was 0.91.

The relative amount of communicated category 11 disagreement was determined in the following manner for each message. Each coder assigned a value of 1 to a message the coder believed included disagreement according to Bales category 11 and zero otherwise. The coders’ evaluations for this message were then averaged. For example, if the three coders all believed one communication included category 11 disagreement while only two of the three believed another communication included category 11 disagreement, then the first communication would have a value of 1 for category 11 while the second would have a value of 0.67 for category 11. Category 11 communication was only captured for the negotiating individual whose partner made the last offer.

Anger at Bidding Behavior. Bales [4] category 12 reflects anger in that it identifies communication that shows antagonism, intolerance, combativeness, or is belittling or disparaging. For example, in our experiment one individual says “19 … ahh … come on … you said 20, and now you go back to 19 … that’s impossible [in an angry tone]!” Another example of anger occurred when one individual said “You just use mathematics to calculate the expected value [in a belittling tone]!” The first is an example of anger directed at bidding behavior, while the second is of anger directed at the partner him- or herself (i.e., anger at the partner’s ignorance). The same procedure described for category 11 was followed for category 12. The average intercoder reliability for category 12 was 0.92. Only anger communicated by the individual directed at bidding behavior of the partner who made the last offer was employed.<sup>12</sup>

Communication Media. A single variable was employed in which IM communication was represented with a value of 0 and audio communication was represented by a value of 1. An interaction term was calculated for each independent variable by multiplying this communication media variable times the normalized version of that independent variable. For example, our statistical model includes the anger at bidding behavior variable plus an interaction term consisting of the anger at bidding behavior variable times the communication media variable. When these two terms were included, the path coefficient associated with the anger at bidding behavior variable represents the mean influence of that anger on ending concessions when IM was used; the path coefficient associated with the interaction term represents the average change from this mean influence when using audio [82].

Control. In order to correctly interpret our interaction effects, the “main effect” for IM versus audio must be statistically accounted for [80], with a path to our dependent variable.

## Discussion

We set out to answer the following research question: With respect to ending concession, what insights based on selfishness theory can be employed strategically by individuals to provide for greater gain when using IM? In order to test our hypotheses, we used an experiment involving dyads attempting to buy and sell simulated lottery tickets. Our research model and associated experiment resulted in the following implications for research and practice.

Based on findings in literatures associated with negotiation, selfishness within social psychology, and the effects of media on communication, this paper is the first to examine how a partner’s ending concession can be influenced by an individual’s offers and comments and their interactions with communication media. We hypothesized that when using IM, partners tend to interpret individuals’ offers that include first offer, unilateral, and reciprocal concessions as attempts at manipulation, and that these interpretations would decrease partners’ ending concessions. We also hypothesized that when using IM, the influence of individuals’ disagreements that include anger at partners’ bidding behaviors would increase partners’ ending concessions, whereas individuals’ disagreements without anger would decrease partner ending concessions. Our experimental results supported these hypotheses.

## Implications for Media Researchers

Prior work examining the effects of media on group tasks has typically focused on how media affects cooperation and collaboration and thereby influences outcomes such as consensus (e.g., [85]) and higher task performance (e.g., [16]). In addition, some research studies have examined how conflict within groups and its effect on group performance vary with media (e.g., [29, 51]). However, little attention has been given to how media can affect the ability of one individual to influence another during negotiations [5]. Our work employing a selfishness perspective builds on Barry and Fulmer’s [5] suggestion that a key to understanding the impact of media on individuals’ abilities to influence partners is in understanding the different ways in which media affect people’s perceptions of communications.

To that end, the selfishness perspective we used will enable researchers to better understand how self-interested partners interpret individuals’ communications. This understanding can then be employed strategically by self-interested individuals to help them alter their communications in order to influence their partners. Thus, rather than lamenting the lack of cues when using IM and suggesting procedures to overcome the lack of cues (e.g., [38]), researchers can determine how to embrace IM’s characteristics and provide guidance on how an individual can communicate for competitive advantage vis-à-vis a self-interested partner.

In addition to the findings illustrated by our research model and by the selfishness perspective, our research design extends media research in a number of methodological ways. For example, past studies typically assume that different media vary in their abilities to convey certain cues, such as affect, and they then relate the different media to, for example, negotiation outcomes (e.g., [28]). In contrast, we employ a coding scheme offered by Bales [4] in order to specifically examine the amounts of negative affect that are conveyed by IM and audio media, and to determine the influences that these amounts have on negotiation.<sup>14</sup> In addition, past studies typically work with an aggregated level of affect (e.g., [38]) or with one specific component of affect (e.g., [37, 40]). Here, we examine the different influences of two different types of negative affect: disagreement with anger directed at bidding behavior, and disagreement with emotion other than anger (e.g., with frustration). Further, past studies typically work at the group level (e.g., [27, 46]), though an examination at the individual level is important in order to provide a detailed understanding of the negotiation process. In contrast, our research focuses on the individual level, helping us understand how offers and comments by an individual can affect the ending concessions made by his or her negotiating partner.

## Implications for Negotiation Researchers

Many negotiation researchers evaluate dyadic negotiation quality in terms of Pareto efficiency or joint profit [76, 84]. However, such measures are not appropriate for our work because we are interested in quality associated with only one negotiator in a dyad (not both negotiators). This focus created an opportunity for us to evaluate quality in a novel way. We evaluate quality in terms of the individual’s gain at the expense of the partner when agreement is reached, that is, our dependent variable is the size of the partner’s ending concession. However, in order to achieve these gains individuals must also reach agreement with their partners. Therefore, we also examine how our independent variables affect the ability of individuals to reach agreements with their partners; that is, our second dependent variable reflects whether or not agreement is reached. We believe that the consideration of these two dependent variables will help researchers better understand contexts in which one negotiator is or both negotiators in a dyad are behaving more selfishly.

In addition, two key empirical findings in the past four decades of negotiation research associate rapid versus gradual unilateral concessions by an individual on partner reciprocal concessions [42, 61]. Seeking a theoretical explanation of these findings, Kwon and Weingart [43] appear to employ equity theory to predict a buyer’s satisfaction with the purchase of a car when the seller uses three concession tactics: immediate (the seller makes a single large concession with no buyer negotiations), gradual (the seller makes three gradual concessions resulting from persuasive buyer negotiations), and delayed (the seller makes one large concession after persuasive buyer negotiations). They hypothesized that the buyer would be the most satisfied with the agreement price when the seller concedes with delay, and would be least satisfied with the agreement price when the seller immediately concedes and also when the seller concedes gradually. Their data provided partial support, with immediate concession resulting in significantly less buyer satisfaction, although no difference was found between the gradual and delayed concessions.

However, Kwon and Weingart [43] describe their experimental scenario as more competitive, which suggests that a selfishness perspective may be beneficial. From this perspective, the buyer’s satisfaction with the agreement price would result from the buyer’s own gain and therefore the buyer’s opportunity cost associated with agreement. The selfishness view would predict that immediate concession would result in the least buyer satisfaction because, since there was no persuasive bargaining involved, the buyer would likely believe that he or she could have purchased the car at a lower price (buyer’s remorse). However, the selfishness perspective would not view the gradual or the delayed concession tactics as being different because they both resulted from persuasive bargaining and therefore both represented similarly small opportunity costs. Therefore, the empirical results regarding buyer satisfaction appear to be more completely explained by the selfishness view rather than an equity view.

Other research studies have considered the selfishness view, but in face-to-face contexts where actual interactions between negotiators were not examined. In a set of studies, Galinsky et al. [30] found that in a dyadic negotiation over a single issue, individuals who had a regulatory focus, described as promotional, were able to reach agreements with greater benefits to themselves than others who had a regulatory focus, described as preventive [30]. In explaining their results, the authors noted that, these individuals avoided agreements that would have been simply satisficing to them. In addition, they noted that such a naturally occurring focus “leads negotiators to discover mutually beneficial trade-offs and achieve Pareto optimality” ([30], p. 1096) because this focus causes them to probe aggressively for opportunities to achieve their aspirations. Our emphasis on ending concessions in a context that allowed us to examine interactions, revealed that such aggressive probing can take the form of reduced concession making as well as expressing anger at bidding behavior. We also found that the efficacy of these probes was dependent on whether IM or audio media were employed.

## Implications for Practitioners

The goal of negotiation is to reach agreement, and concession making is a key to this end. Of the four kinds of concessions that can be contained in offers (first offer, unilateral, reciprocal, and ending), only ending concessions necessarily end negotiations with agreement. It is therefore a reasonable strategy for an individual to make offers and comments that encourage the negotiating partner to make an ending concession. Additionally, if the individual’s offers and comments result in a larger ending concession by the partner, then the individual can obtain greater gains.

More selfish behavior in negotiation is important because it reduces all types of concession making [61]. Our results suggest that when using IM, negotiating partners tend to be more selfish and may presume that an individual’s concessions are evidence that the individual is behaving unfairly or unethically. The partner’s response to these concessions is to behave more selfishly, which makes larger ending concessions by him or her less likely. We therefore suggest that when negotiating over IM, individuals should not make anything other than relatively small concessions, and these concessions should be made only when required in order to continue the negotiation process. In contrast, when negotiating over audio, an individual’s unilateral and reciprocal concessions reduce the partner’s selfish behavior, making ending concessions by him or her more likely. We therefore encourage negotiators to make larger unilateral and reciprocal concessions when using audio. However, first offer concessions appear to have little influence on ending concessions when using audio. Since the degree to which an individual is better off once agreement is reached depends in part on how much he or she has conceded, we suggest that individuals using audio should not make first offer concessions.

In addition, disagreeing comments can play important roles in encouraging and discouraging ending concessions. When disagreements with anger are directed at the partner’s bidding behavior, the partner appears to treat it as a signal that the individual has reached his or her concession limit and that the likelihood of reaching agreement is in peril. In order to reach agreement, the partner must therefore behave less selfishly and, for example, tends to increase ending concession making. Thus, an individual is well served by communicating angry comments directed at the partner’s bidding behavior, thereby increasing the likelihood that the partner will offer a larger ending concession that concludes the negotiation process with agreement. This holds independent of whether IM or audio is used.

In contrast, comments made by the individual that disagree with emotion other than anger convey negative affect and appear to be perceived by the partner as evidence that there is no reason to change his or her more selfish behavior. Such comments thus reduce the likelihood that partners will make larger ending concessions. We therefore suggest that offers should not include accompanying comments, or at least should not include emotion such as tension; merely communicating a lower or higher price is sufficient. This holds independent of whether IM or audio is used.

## Limitations

Some of the more important potential limitations of our study include reasons for larger ending concessions other than selfish behavior, problems with impasse rates and selection bias, shared history, and asynchronous communication.

There may be reasons other than less selfish behavior for negotiators to make ending concessions. For example, large ending concessions might result because negotiators are not interested in the negotiation task. However, this is not likely here because participants volunteered in order to earn money, which depended partially on “good” negotiation performance. In addition, only one instance was found where transcripts indicated that negotiators wanted to quit (because they were hungry). Large ending concessions might also be made because of time pressure. However, dyads were given 3.5 minutes to complete negotiations (based on results from a pilot study in which no dyad took longer than 3.5 minutes), and no dyad in the current study took longer than 3 minutes. Finally, negotiators might make smaller ending concessions because they enjoyed the negotiation process. If this were the case, we would be less likely to see the media differences that were found. We are thus confident that selfish behavior played a significant role in the making of ending concessions.

There is a fairly high impasse rate variation as a percentage of dyads across conditions in our study. Tripp and Sondak [74] suggest that this variation might be caused by not giving subjects enough time to negotiate. However, as described above, the time limit we used was based on pilot tests and set to ensure that this problem would not occur. Tripp and Sondak also indicate that when variations in impasse rates across conditions occur and when researchers then delete dyads reaching impasse during analysis, a selection bias could be introduced. Selection bias can occur when a portion of a population is excluded from the sample, and it becomes a problem when it affects the relationships found between independent and dependent variables. Our ending concession model posited relationships between independent variables and more or less ending concession. We therefore only examined dyads that reached agreement, since it is only with these dyads that ending concessions can possibly occur. Note, however, that agreement can occur without any ending concession. This happens when a reciprocal concession is the last to occur, as opposed to an ending concession, within dyads reaching agreement; the data used in our ending concession model included these dyads. Therefore, we are confident that there is no bias in our results due to the variation of impasse rates across the media conditions in our study.

If participants were able to recognize each other as friends, this recognition would occur more often with audio, and this could account for some differences in tendencies to act more or less selfishly when using audio as opposed to IM. However, the participants were from a large population of freshmen through senior college students. In addition, they were randomly assigned to buyer–seller roles and as dyad partners. This reduces the chance that dyad partners knew each other. We also examined the negotiation transcripts looking for indications that the partners recognized each other. There were no dyads in which partners appeared to identify each other without an introduction. There were three dyads in which partners introduced themselves. However, after introductions, negotiations continued without any reference to prior history, such as classes, events, people, and so on that they may have had in common.

Our study was based on synchronous encounters between negotiators, and these kinds of encounters are happening with greater frequency over the Internet. However, many asynchronous negotiations are also occurring. For example, the first step in eBay’s dispute resolution process is for buyers and sellers to communicate via e-mail in order to work out their difficulties. This asynchronous negotiation can result in partial refunds when buyers are not satisfied with the items they purchased [63]. Such asynchronous negotiations differ from our IM negotiations in that more time allows for rumination by each individual, which can make problems seem larger [48], can increase angry moods [64], and can increase the psychological investment each negotiator has with his or her arguments [29]. Future studies that permit negotiations over e-mail will offer an opportunity to examine the degree to which our results can be generalized to e-mail negotiations.

Students were subjects in our study, and this raises a question regarding the generalizability of our results to other individuals. However, individuals of demography similar to that of these students are currently involved in purchasing items on websites such as Bonanza (which we reference in the introduction), and thus a concern about the generalizability of our study is less of an issue than it appears. In addition, though students are not necessarily good surrogates with regard to ethical dilemmas (e.g., [7]), they have been found to be reasonable surrogates with regard to financial decisions (e.g., [47]). Finally, research studies on negotiation have often effectively used student subjects as negotiators by training them for the negotiation tasks to be done and by limiting the number of issues over which they negotiate (e.g., [30, 43]). The subjects in our study were adequately trained and their focus was on a single issue—price determination. In sum, we believe that our use of students is reasonable for our experiment.

## Conclusion

The use of instant messaging (IM) for negotiations is growing rapidly, in some instances surpassing the use of audio (such as telephone) communications. This article highlights the issue of how IM and audio affect the ability of one individual to influence a partner during negotiations, which is examined from the viewpoint of negotiators who are inclined to act competitively in price negotiations. Therefore, we are interested in strategies that can be employed by individuals to reap greater gains in the form agreements with larger ending concessions by the partner. We propose a model based on the theoretical notion that, when using IM as opposed to audio, partners tend to interpret individuals’ offers and comments from a more selfish perspective, and that individuals can use this insight to provide offers and comments that will decrease partner selfishness, thereby increase partners’ ending concessions.

For example, this insight is the basis for our following advice to negotiators. When negotiating over IM, individuals’ first offer, unilateral, and reciprocal concessions tend to decrease partners’ ending concessions. Therefore, individuals should make such concessions sparingly, such as only when required in order to continue the negotiation process. This relationship between decreases in partners’ ending concessions and individuals’ first offer, unilateral, and reciprocal concessions did not tend to occur when negotiators used audio. Our advice regarding sparing use of such concessions therefore does not apply during audio negotiations. In addition, when using IM, individuals’ disagreements that include anger at partners’ bidding behaviors tend to increase partners’ ending concessions, whereas individuals’ disagreements that include emotion other than anger (such as frustration) tend to decrease partners’ ending concessions. The strength of these relationships was enhanced when audio was used. Therefore, individuals should consider conveying anger at partner bidding behavior rather than nonangry emotion when communicating disagreements, independent of whether IM or audio is employed.

In addition to insight for negotiators, we provide researchers with a model and a novel way to evaluate negotiation quality when negotiators are competitively inclined. We suggest that negotiation quality should be evaluated in terms of two dependent variables. The first measures an individual’s gain at the expense of his or her partner, while the second measures the likelihood of reaching agreement. Both of these variables are important when evaluating negotiation quality in competitive negotiation contexts.
