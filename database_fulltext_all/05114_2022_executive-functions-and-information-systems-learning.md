# EXECUTIVE FUNCTIONS AND INFORMATION SYSTEMS LEARNING<sup>1</sup>

Yulia W. Sullivan Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University Waco, TX U.S.A., {Yulia\_Sullivan@baylor.edu}

Fred D. Davis Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University Lubbock, TX, U.S.A. {fred.davis@ttu.edu}

Chang E. Koh G. Brint Ryan College of Business, University of North Texas Denton, TX, U.S.A. {Chang.Koh@unt.edu}

Information systems (IS) are complex and effortful, placing ever-greater demands on humans’ executive functions. Executive functions, general-purpose control processes that regulate one’s thoughts and behaviors, are the subject of growing investigation in cognitive psychology. The present research examines the relationship between individuals’ executive functions and IS learning. Using neuropsychological methods from cognitive psychology, we measured three key dimensions of executive functions: working memory, shifting, and inhibition. Two empirical studies were conducted. Study 1 tested the relationship between executive functions and IS learning in a self-paced offline learning environment. Study 2 replicated Study 1 and extended it to include a comparison of two self-paced online learning methods: behavior modeling and text-based learning. Both studies found significant effects of executive functions on IS learning after controlling for known IS learning determinants. Study 2 also showed that declarative knowledge was higher for behavior modeling than for text-based learning. Overall, our research highlights the influence of executive functions on IS learning. This research advances knowledge about determinants of IS learning and opens important research avenues for gaining deeper insights into cognitive mechanisms underlying effective IS learning.

Keywords: Executive functions, working memory, shifting, inhibition, information systems learning, declarative knowledge, post-learning self-efficacy, behavior modeling, neuropsychological assessment task, NeuroIS

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) are fundamentally changing the nature of work, placing new demands on people’s learning processes (Cascio, 2019; Cascio & Montealegra, 2016; Montealegra & Cascio, 2017). In fact, computing capabilities have become ubiquitously embedded throughout our everyday life, changing the intensity and manner by which we interact with technology (Yoo, 2010). We can expect, then, that executive functions will be increasingly relevant to IS skill acquisition. Executive functions are “a set of general-purpose control mechanisms, often linked to the prefrontal cortex of the brain, that regulate the dynamics of human cognition and action” (Miyake & Friedman, 2012, p. 8). Mastery of powerful and complex IS specifically taxes users’ executive functions (Jipp & Ackerman, 2016; Kieras et al., 1999; Meyer & Kieras, 1997). This research investigates the relationship between executive functions and IS learning, which has been overlooked. The present research takes an important first step toward leveraging recent progress in cognitive psychology on executive functions to advance scientific knowledge and research about IS learning.

IS learning involves acquiring the skills to effectively use IS to perform relevant tasks. According to Kieras et al. (1999), the human information-processing system for performing complex person-system tasks depends on a combination of perceptual, cognitive, and motor skills, all orchestrated with the support of working memory. Specifically, most contemporary IS are interactive and rely on perceptual skills to acquire information from system displays, cognitive skills to process and analyze the information (often in combination with retrieving and using relevant information from memory), and motor skills to communicate back to the system via keystrokes and cursor control. All of these skills place demands on executive functions for attentional control and processing. For example, Jipp and Ackermann (2016) found that increasing levels of technologybased automation for complex information analysis and decision-making places increasing demands on users’ information-processing abilities, enhancing performance for those with higher abilities and degrading performance for those with lower abilities. Tams et al. (2018) underscore the importance of executive functions for effective IS use. They demonstrated that IS can dramatically increase the number of interruptions experienced by users, affecting mental workload through their impact on working memory available for performing computer-based tasks and inhibition for concentrating on the tasks at hand while ignoring irrelevant stimuli. These studies suggest that executive functions are particularly relevant for acquiring IS skills.

In this research, we specifically investigate the three core dimensions of executive functions: working memory (also known as updating), shifting (also known as cognitive flexibility or mental flexibility), and inhibition (also known as attention control) (Benedek et al., 2014; Miyake et al., 2000). The unity/diversity model of executive functions (Miyake et al., 2000) suggests that these three dimensions have some common or shared variance (unity) as well as unique variance (diversity). Several independent studies have shown the importance of these three dimensions varies across different domains (e.g., Blair & Razza, 2007; Del Missier et al., 2012). However, it is currently unclear which specific executive functions are involved in IS learning. Therefore, we examine not only the common role of executive functions but also the differential role of specific executive functions for IS learning.

We conducted two empirical studies showing that executive functions are significantly associated with IS learning. Study 1 tested the relationship between executive functions and IS learning in a self-paced offline learning environment. Study 2 replicated Study 1 and extended it to include a comparison of two self-paced online training methods: behavior modeling and text-based learning. Behavior modeling (i.e., observing others performing the target behavior) is among the most effective IS training methods (e.g., Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Johnson &

Marakas, 2000; Simon & Wegner, 1996; Yi & Davis, 2003). Therefore, Study 2 tested whether the importance of executive functions for IS learning generalized from a selfpaced text-based learning method in an offline learning environment to behavior modeling as well as text-based learning in an online learning environment.

Our research has both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, we demonstrate that executive functions are important for IS learning. By bringing executive functions into the IS field and demonstrating their relevance in a typical IS learning context, our paper may stimulate impactful research on the importance of executive functions for a broad range of IS phenomena. From a practical perspective, understanding the relationship between executive functions and IS learning could help researchers, educators, and practitioners develop, implement, and assess training practices to improve IS skills. A better understanding of executive functions in IS learning may also improve the effectiveness of IS learning interventions by targeting a combination of executive functions because different facets of executive functions may facilitate different aspects of IS learning.

## Theoretical Background: Executive Functions

Executive functions are “a set of general-purpose control mechanisms, often linked to the prefrontal cortex of the brain, that regulate the dynamics of human cognition and action” (Miyake & Friedman, 2012, p. 8). Researchers across the literature agree on three core components of executive functions: working memory, shifting, and inhibition (Benedek et al., 2014; Diamond, 2013; Friedman et al., 2008; Miyake et al., 2000).

Working memory refers to one’s ability to monitor incoming information and revise working memory content by replacing obsolete information with new and relevant information for the current task (Benedek et al., 2014; Miyake et al., 2000). Working memory is critical for making sense of events unfolding over time since it requires holding in mind what happened earlier and relating that to what comes later (Diamond, 2013). Doing a mental calculation requires working memory, as does mentally reordering items, translating instructions into action plans, and incorporating new information into current thinking (Diamond, 2013). Working memory reflects the ability to actively maintain knowledge units in the focus of attention (Engle et al., 1999). Thus, it enables individuals to bring conceptual knowledge to bear on learning processes (Diamond, 2013; Smallwood et al., 2007).

Shifting, also known as “switching” or “cognitive flexibility,” refers to one’s ability to shift between two or more cognitive tasks or mental sets (Miyake et al., 2000; Monsell, 2003). Shifting involves a disengagement from a mental activity that has become irrelevant to a new and relevant mental activity (Benedek et al., 2014). It enables individuals to think creatively and see ideas from multiple perspectives (Diamond, 2013). The shifting function supports the flexibility that allows the rapid execution of other tasks when appropriate (Monsell, 2003). Thus, one needs the shifting function to help resolve sudden conflicts and switch to new actions (Obeso et al., 2013).

Inhibition, also known as “attention control” (Norman & Shallice, 1986), refers to the deliberate, controlled suppression of dominant but irrelevant response tendencies (Miyake et al., 2000). The inhibition function enables individuals to selectively focus on what they choose by suppressing attention to extraneous stimuli or unwanted thoughts (Diamond, 2013). For example, successfully suppressing the impulsive behavioral response to check phones and emails while working on a manuscript requires inhibitory control. It should be noted that the concept of inhibition is particularly diverse and may, in different contexts, refer to other conceptualizations, such as resistance to distractor interference (Friedman & Miyake, 2004).

Although prior studies have proposed other dimensions of executive functions, such as multitasking (e.g., Baddeley, 2012), planning, and verbal and nonverbal fluency (e.g., Jurado & Rosselli, 2007), research has shown that these other dimensions depend on working memory, shifting, and inhibition (Diamond, 2013). For this reason, we speculate that differences in IS learning may be traced to these three basic components of executive functions. Early conceptualizations of executive functions suggested that executive functions (except the switching ability) are static and highly heritable (e.g., Friedman et al., 2008). However, recent evidence suggests that executive functions may be somewhat malleable and subject to experience-dependent change (Diamond & Lee, 2011; Blair, 2016; Hsu et al., 2014; Karbach & Kray, 2009).

The construct of executive functions is new in the IS discipline, although related constructs have been investigated in IS literature, such as controlled processing, top-down regulation of attention, and cognitive abilities (e.g., Santhanam et al., 2008; Wan et al., 2012). One aspect of controlled processing that has received much attention from IS researchers is conscious intentions that result from topdown decision-making processes involving beliefs (e.g., perceived self-efficacy), expectations (e.g., performance expectancy), and reflections of past experience (Ortiz de

Guinea & Markus, 2009). According to the executive functions perspective, such conscious intentions are enabled by executive functions that update working memory, shift mental sets to coordinate multiple tasks, and inhibit irrelevant response tendencies to resolve interference (Chatham et al., 2011; Friedman & Miyake, 2004).

The unity/diversity model of executive functions (Miyake et al., 2000) suggests that the three dimensions of executive functions have some common or shared variance (unity) as well as unique variance (diversity). Miyake et al. (2000) also found that these three dimensions contribute differently to more complex executive tasks, suggesting that the relevance of different executive functions may vary depending on the task (Benedek et al., 2014). This unity and diversity perspective of executive functions is illustrated in Figure 1. Therefore, we examined the association between common and distinct dimensions of executive functions and IS learning within a unity and diversity framework.

## Research Model and Hypotheses Development

The proposed model (Figure 2) hypothesizes that executive functions have a direct and significant relationship with IS learning, even after known learner characteristics were included in the model. We conceptualize executive functions as a multidimensional, formatively measured construct. Although Miyake et al. (2000) conceptualized executive functions as a reflective construct, we argue they should be conceptualized as a formative construct because each dimension or subprocess captures differing aspects of executive functions (Petter et al., 2007). To strengthen our argument on the importance of executive functions, we also included the effectiveness of a behavior modeling training approach in our model. We adapted this method to a selfpaced online learning environment and examined its relation to IS learning alongside executive functions.

## IS Learning

Learning effectiveness is commonly assessed using three criteria: cognitive outcomes (e.g., verbal or declarative knowledge), motivational outcomes (e.g., post-learning selfefficacy), and skill-based outcomes (e.g., procedural knowledge, task performance, and automaticity) (Kraiger et al., 1993).

Declarative knowledge is generally used to evaluate learners’ conceptual understanding of the subject (Santhanam et al., 2008). Anderson et al. (1997) defined declarative knowledge as “things that we are aware we know and can usually describe to others” (p. 440). Executive functions are important during this declarative knowledge phase because a great deal of information from the environment and recalled knowledge must be represented in working memory to form cognitive representations of new competencies (Bandura, 2015; Carroll & Bandura, 1990; Yi & Davis, 2003). After competencies are acquired, habitual patterns are routinized, and users can perform the task without conscious monitoring (Diamond, 2013). If automaticity fails to produce desired results, learners reactivate executive functions to identify the problem source and devise better ways of handling task demands.

Self-efficacy involves “beliefs in one’s capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments” (Bandura, 1997, p. 3). Selfefficacy is generally believed to be task-specific and is hypothesized to influence one’s choice of activities, effort expended, persistence, and task performance (Kraiger et al., 1993; Bandura, 2010). In the context of computer skill acquisition, one should distinguish between an individual’s general computer self-efficacy, which captures a range of computer applications, and one’s self-efficacy regarding a specific technology (Marakas et al., 1998; Marcolin et al., 2000; Yi & Davis, 2003). We conceptualize post-learning self-efficacy as an individual’s perception of efficacy in performing a specific computer-related task following a training program. During the acquisition of new knowledge, individuals assess their post-learning self-efficacy based on the goals they set for themselves and the necessary efforts and resources allocated toward those goals. Learners’ postlearning self-efficacy estimations are an important predictor of their later performance (Johnson & Marakas, 2000).

Procedural knowledge refers to rule-based knowledge about how to do a task (Anderson, 1985; McCormick, 1997). Whereas declarative knowledge is represented in memory by a propositional network, procedural knowledge is represented in memory as condition-action rules (Willingham et al., 1989). Procedural knowledge is taskbased; it is a specification for carrying out specific tasks (Olfman & Mandviwalla, 1994).

In this current research, we measure the first two learning outcomes: declarative knowledge and post-learning selfefficacy. Declarative knowledge and post-learning selfefficacy are the learning outcomes associated with the early and intermediate stages of knowledge acquisition characterized by slow and effortful information processing, whereas procedural knowledge is associated with the later stages of learning characterized by relatively smooth, automatic, and effortless performance (Davis & Yi, 2004; Kanfer & Ackerman, 1989; Kraiger et al., 1993). Given that executive functions are most needed when learning requires high levels of effort (Diamond, 2013), measuring declarative knowledge and post-learning self-efficacy is most relevant to our research objective. Therefore, we bound the analysis to declarative knowledge and post-learning self-efficacy.<sup>2</sup>

Although some scholars in the area of person-system interaction have emphasized the importance of user procedural knowledge over declarative knowledge (e.g., van der Meij & van der Meij, 2013), research has shown that training focusing solely on procedural knowledge is more effective when the system is simple to operate. As the complexity increases, learners should form declarative knowledge prior to procedural knowledge because it enables them to build a mental model through assimilating new information (McCormick, 1997; Santhanan & Sein, 1994). Therefore, learning that focuses on declarative knowledge may provide a better opportunity for learners to form a coherent mental model compared to learning that focuses on procedural training (Bell & Kozlowski, 2008; Davis & Yi, 2004; Martocchio & Dulebohn, 1994; Santhanan & Sein, 1994).

## Common Executive Functions and Declarative Knowledge

Cognitive skill acquisition is often characterized as a progression from an effortful declarative stage through intermediate processes of knowledge compilation to arrive at a level of expertise enabling fast and smooth execution of component skills (e.g., Anderson et al., 1997; VanLehn, 1996). According to Kanfer and Ackerman’s (1989) resource allocation theory, the declarative phase of skill acquisition involves “all the requisite memory and reasoning processes that allow the performer [learner] to attain an understanding of the task requirements” (p. 660). During this phase, learners utilize executive functions for multiple purposes: (1) to hold information in their mind and manipulate it to assimilate new knowledge (i.e., working memory function); (2) to flexibly switch their thought perspectives in analyzing problems and solutions (i.e., shifting function); and (3) to filter out task-irrelevant information (i.e., inhibition function) (Diamond, 2013). To illustrate this perspective, consider the following example: to learn a relational database using Microsoft Access, learners must activate their existing knowledge (e.g., knowledge about data and information) and integrate the new concept into the existing knowledge; switch to a new mindset to facilitate the transition to using new tools and techniques (e.g., switch between different database objects, try more than one approach to solve errors, etc.); and inhibit the rules associated with applications already mastered (e.g., create a table using Microsoft Excel) in order to focus on the learning task (e.g., create a table using Access). Together, working memory, shifting, and inhibition contribute to successful learning.

The common role of executive functions on declarative knowledge is needed for at least two reasons. First, IS learning involves unpredictable demands that require situational adjustment (McCormick, 1997). For example, a new system may be used to redesign the structure and responsibilities inherent to a position, thereby increasing the demand upon an individual’s executive functions (Gattiker, 1992). Consider the following scenario: a company is adopting a new enterprise resource planning (ERP) system to centralize the reporting task. This system may change the roles and responsibilities of an office manager, providing the person with more than one scenario to complete a variety of reports with different levels of complexity. Acquiring the relevant perceptual, cognitive, and motor skills to effectively use a new system requires learners to recall the facts about the new system and be mentally flexible in order to apply the new skills to a variety of tasks (Martocchio & Dulebohn, 1994). This observation implies that the acquisition of new IS knowledge depends on executive functions.

Second, IS learning consists of task sets with high cognitive demands (Meyer & Kieras, 1997). Component abilities, such as psychomotor speed and attention, are needed in even the simplest tasks such as data entry; spatial memory and motor skills are important to word-processing performance; and cognitive skills, such as memory, reasoning, attention, and problem-solving, are important for information search (Czaja & Lee, 2003). Learners with low executive functions could be at a disadvantage when they try to learn a new system. Therefore, we argue that executive functions are positively associated with declarative knowledge.

H1: Common executive functions are positively associated with declarative knowledge

## Working Memory and Declarative Knowledge

IS knowledge acquisition demands working memory for learners to understand various learning elements (e.g., task instructions, new features, the work context, and familiarization with task goals) (Hovde & Renguette, 2017). Anderson’s (1982) theory of skill acquisition states that working memory influences the initial declarative knowledge because relatively large amounts of knowledge about the new skill must be acquired and interpreted. For example, when learning a new programming language, learners need to understand the basic elements in a problem (e.g., determine properties and relations of problem elements and establish the initial and final problem states), decompose and sequence problem elements into an outline of the problem solution (e.g., recognize relevant operators and commands and arrange them to form a solution), and translate from natural language to programming code (Shute, 1991). Overall, the acquisition of IS knowledge requires working memory, where information is maintained, updated, and applied, to perform perceptual, cognitive, and motor tasks (Kieras et al., 1999).

Systems’ complexities and dynamic changes in learning content demand working memory for two reasons. First, working memory enables learners to maintain task goals, integrate new information, modify existing schemas, hold in mind what happened earlier, and relate that to what comes next (Baddeley, 2012; Diamond, 2013; Kane et al., 2001)— all skills needed to complete computer-related tasks (Tams et al., 2018). Consider creating a relational database using Microsoft Access. The task could be creating a new table with five different fields, each field needing an assigned data type. Working memory enables learners to visualize which data to enter into each field. It also allows them to remember what has been entered and thus create a symbolic representation of what the table should look like. Poor working memory can lead to difficulties in learning because learners may have less space in working memory for integrating different types of information needed to create new knowledge.

Second, working memory contributes to accurate problem classification by recognizing when a problem requires a particular solution strategy (Cooney & Swanson, 1990; Wiley & Jarosz, 2012). For example, when should one use Queries instead of Forms in Access? Individuals with high working memory are better at selecting important information to include in a problem representation than those with low working memory (Wiley & Jaroz, 2012). Thus, we hypothesize that working memory is positively associated with declarative knowledge.

H2: Working memory is positively associated with declarative knowledge

## Shifting and Declarative Knowledge

Shifting is important for declarative knowledge because learners need to process multiple goals and flexibly switch ways to approach problems and solutions. As technology becomes ubiquitous, the shifting function helps learners keep up with the pace of change. Learners need to flexibly adapt how they approach problems and solutions (Armstrong & Hardgrave, 2007). For example, when users learn how to interact with a virtual assistant, they may need to adjust their tone of voice if the assistant does not understand their request and look up different options when technical problems arise. By actively shifting their mental approach, learners with high shifting ability can move between goals and information contexts.

IS skill acquisition closely parallels the technology capability of “broadening and deepening” (Benlian, 2015). During learning, learners broaden their technology capabilities by engaging in the sensemaking of a diverse set of IS features. To broaden their surface knowledge, they shift back and forth from one system feature to another to familiarize themselves with the system’s overall functionality. When learners deepen their technology skills, they focus on selected subsets of IS features to fully grasp the features’ affordances and effects. Technology broadening relies on the shifting function. Considering our relational database example, learners need to be flexible to adapt to the system environment. For example, they need to frequently switch from the Datasheet view to the Design view, and from creating a table to identifying the redundant data. Here, individuals with high shifting ability will gain more initial knowledge than those with low shifting ability since they are capable of flexibly shifting their attention between features and mental sets when appropriate with minimum switching cost.

The concept of IT mindfulness (Thatcher et al., 2018) also highlights the relevance of shifting for IS learning. According to this concept, users who employ multiple perspectives (a characteristic of users with high shifting ability) hold greater potential to solve problems effectively. They tend to be more mindful and can thus recognize potential applications of feature sets and interact more flexibly with the system’s features in ways that support learning (Thatcher et al., 2018). Therefore, we hypothesize that shifting is positively associated with declarative knowledge.

H3: Shifting is positively associated with declarative knowledge

## Inhibition and Declarative Knowledge

Effective learning requires sufficient deliberate intentions to protect relevant goals from disruption through inhibition (e.g., not looking up every technology option that seems attractive but is irrelevant) (Monsell, 2003). Inhibition protects limited cognitive resources from being diverted to task-irrelevant stimuli or responses (Eysenck et al., 2007). It helps learners balance the resources available with those required for learning (Tams et al., 2018). For example, Jenkins et al. (2016) demonstrated that the presence of online security messages increased security message disregard when such messages interrupt users’ primary tasks, suggesting that people learn to become selective in their use of cognitive resources to distinguish task-relevant from taskirrelevant information (Haider & Frensch, 1999).

Attentional focus is required for learners to attend to essential information when learning an IS (Yi & Davis, 2003). Whereas technology broadening requires shifting, technology deepening (see the technology capability “broadening and deepening” concept—Benlian, 2015) demands inhibition. For example, when learners need to export data from Access to Excel, they need to focus on the export feature and specific database object they want to export while ignoring similar but irrelevant features. Focusing on a specific feature or content helps learners deepen their IS skills and develop higher declarative knowledge.

Inhibition is needed to protect relevant goals not only from external distractions but also from internal distractions (e.g., incumbent system habits, mind wandering or task-irrelevant thoughts, etc.) (Friedman & Miyake, 2004). For instance, learners may have difficulty in inhibiting the previously established computer skills that are no longer relevant to the current learning condition (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). We posit that inhibition helps make the transition to the new system easier, allowing learners to acquire new declarative knowledge (Lin, 2012; Ophir et al., 2009). For example, when learning to create a table in Access, learners with low inhibition may continue to utilize their skills in creating a table in Excel despite significant differences between the two tools. Therefore, we hypothesize that inhibition is positively associated with declarative knowledge.

H4: Inhibition is positively associated with declarative knowledge

## Common Executive Functions and Post-Learning Self-Efficacy

Common executive functions are important for the formation of post-learning self-efficacy to serve as psychological control mechanisms for learners. Bandura et al. (1977) suggested that “psychological procedures, whatever their format, serve as ways of creating and strengthening expectations of personal efficacy” (p. 126). Executive functions enable learners to use cognitive control in response to difficulties and challenging tasks during the learning phase. If learners can flexibly change directions or goals, respond adaptively, and anticipate future goals, then they can conduct more active learning and harbor more positive thoughts about their accomplishment, resulting in high post-learning self-efficacy. For example, learners are asked to create a relationship between two tables in Microsoft Access, but they fail to assign a correct primary key to the tables. Compared to those with low executive functions, those with high executive functions are better at recognizing and remembering the necessary steps to solve the problem, shifting attention between the tools and problem to be solved, and understanding the relevant fields needed to create a correct relationship. Similarly, relative to those with low executive functions, learners with high executive functions are more likely to reach the solution faster and with fewer problems, all of which leads to increased post-learning self-efficacy.

The dynamic characteristics of IS learning (e.g., constant changes in technology, complexity of problems to be solved, and diverse skills needed to perform a task) require learners to be sufficiently motivated to achieve goals in spite of distractions along the way. High executive functions are particularly needed when learners experience failure or errors during IS knowledge acquisition. For example, when learners observe that the results of their keystrokes are not what they expected but fail to identify the cause of errors, learning can be severely limited (Kay, 2007). Those with low executive functions may fail at self-regulation and have low postlearning self-efficacy. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H5: Common executive functions are positively associated with post-learning self-efficacy

## Working Memory and Post-Learning Self-Efficacy

Working memory may lead to high post-learning selfefficacy through two pathways: (1) the use of deep strategies instead of surface strategies (Jonides, 1995), and (2) the effective allocation of cognitive resources (Kanfer & Ackerman, 1989).

Learners with high working memory are more likely to use deep strategies (e.g., making analogies and generalizations, making connections between concepts) that are more useful for processing new information and increasing selfregulation during learning than surface strategies (e.g., focusing only on assessment requirements) (Jonides, 1995). Considering our database example, learners with high working memory may choose to create outlines, charts, or concept maps to understand different database objects, whereas those with low working memory may simply rehearse simple terminology. Consequently, those with high working memory are more engaged with the learning materials, leading to stronger perceptions of post-learning self-efficacy concurrent with greater skill capabilities (Kraiger et al., 1993).

Learners acquire IS cognitive skills by allocating enough resources to comprehend the complexity of a new system and understand different features as a coordinate system (Brar & van der Meij, 2017; Smith & Kosslyn, 2007). For example, to learn how to use machine learning for decisionmaking, learners need to know the rules of inferences, make assumptions associated with the model prediction, and integrate many pieces of information. Individuals with low working memory are susceptible to fail at managing their cognitive resources and tend to set low goals for themselves; they tend to quickly give up in the face of difficulties and settle for mediocre performances (Bandura, 1997, 2015). In contrast, learners with high working memory can make the right choices, mobilize the necessary efforts and resources, and effectively enhance their post-learning self-efficacy (Bandura, 2015). Therefore, we hypothesize that high working memory is associated with high post-learning selfefficacy.

H6: Working memory is positively associated with postlearning self-efficacy

## Shifting and Post-Learning Self-Efficacy

The shifting function can increase post-learning self-efficacy through one’s self-regulatory process (Lee & Bobko, 1994). Goals associated with IS learning are constrained and supported by various systems’ functionalities, with which learners adjust their cognitive strategies to match their perceptions of what the system requires. Consider an example of spreadsheet learning. When users learn how to use Microsoft Excel, they make sense of the process by fitting the new system into a given work structure. The learning goals could be to create a formula, use built-in functions to perform tax calculations, navigate the system space, and select the correct options needed to carry out the task. Learners’ ability to manage multiple goals is associated with their self-regulatory processes to organize and execute courses of actions required to complete a task (Lee and Bobko, 1994) and support the construction of new meaning (Koch et al., 2010).

The shifting function also relates to post-learning selfefficacy by enabling learners to change strategies and perspectives during learning (Diamond, 2013; Serpell & Esposito, 2016). IS provide learners with more than one alternative to solve a problem (e.g., one can use either Design View or Datasheet View to create a table in Microsoft Access). Those with high shifting ability can effectively view a problem from multiple perspectives and flexibly switch attention from solving the problem to reasoning about the domain knowledge itself (VanLehn, 1996). They evaluate the task demands, construct alternative solutions, establish milestones, and create self-incentives to establish post-learning self-efficacy (Diamond, 2013). Thus, we hypothesize that shifting is positively associated with post-learning self-efficacy.

H7: Shifting is positively associated with post-learning selfefficacy

## Inhibition and Post-Learning Self-Efficacy

Inhibition affects post-learning self-efficacy by blocking interruptions and exercising cognitive control over the task (e.g., greater use of self-regulatory strategies) (Zahodne et al., 2015). Interacting with an IS involves many visual and textual stimuli (e.g., pop-up windows that contain error messages or guidance for navigating the system). Simultaneous presentation of multiple sources of information (e.g., instruction materials, the system’s default settings, the system commands, etc.) can easily distract learners and hinder the learning process (Tams et al., 2018). For example, when learners are presented with 10 different tables and instructed to run a query from those tables, they can be overwhelmed by the instruction materials (either text-based or video-based), an error message every time they make an error, and different menu options and rules presented to them every time they select an option. Learners with high inhibition are less susceptible to the negative impacts of technology-mediated interruptions (Tams et al., 2018) and, in turn, are more confident in their ability to use the system.

Although the effect of inhibition on post-learning self-efficacy has not been tested in prior research, some findings point in this direction. For example, Marakas et al. (1998) reported that goal commitment was positively correlated with post-learning selfefficacy. Individuals with high levels of inhibition tend to show high perseverance to stay on task despite distractions and complete the task despite temptations to give up or to move on to more interesting work (Diamond, 2013). For example, when the instruction is to combine fields using the calculated data type in Access, learners with high inhibition will focus on selecting relevant math operations. When they receive errors, they are likely to keep trying and review what they have done wrong. They have strong inhibitions to shield their learning goals from distractions, such as doing another task.

Further, the notion of flow (see Trevino & Webster, 1992) identifies attention focus (i.e., where an individual’s attention is limited to the narrow stimulus represented by the technology) as one of the key dimensions of flow. A similar construct, namely focused immersion, is also considered a critical aspect of cognitive absorption—a flow state in the context of individual behavior toward technologies (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Research has shown that this attention focus, which is highly associated with inhibition (Diamond, 2013), is positively associated with technology use behaviors (Webster et al., 1993) and several learning outcomes (e.g., positive subjective experience, increased learning, perceived behavioral control, and post-learning self-efficacy) (Hoffman & Novak, 1996; Scott & Walczak, 2009). Here, we hypothesize that inhibition is positively associated with postlearning self-efficacy.

H8: Inhibition is positively associated with post-learning self-efficacy

## Self-Paced Online Behavior Modeling

To confirm the generalizability of the role of executive functions in IS learning, we included the effectiveness of selfpaced online behavior modeling training in our model and examined its relation to IS learning concurrently with executive functions. Prior research has shown that behavior modeling or observational learning produces better training outcomes than other IS learning methods do (e.g., Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Johnson & Marakas, 2000; Simon & Werner, 1996). In modeling-based learning, learners watch someone else perform a target behavior and then attempt to reenact it (Yi & Davis, 2003). Although behavior modeling is a popular learning approach in the IS learning domain, prior research has primarily focused on the effectiveness of this method in a traditional IS learning environment. In this research, we examined the effectiveness of this approach in a self-paced online learning environment. Specifically, we compared behavior modeling (i.e., the use of online video-based modeling, in which learners are allowed to self-pace) to text-based learning, in which learners are given text-based instructions to learn at their own pace.

Self-paced online behavior modeling involves a demonstration of desired behaviors through online video representation of the behaviors. This technique is based on social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), which suggests that observational learning results in “knowledge structures representing the rules and strategies of effective action” that “serve as cognitive guides for the construction of complex modes of behavior” (Bandura, 1997, p. 34) (see also Davis & Yi, 2004, p. 509). To the extent that learners are highly motivated and attentive as they observe someone performing the target behavior, they are likely to develop high declarative knowledge (e.g., Yi & Davis, 2003). In a meta-analytic review of behavior modeling training, Taylor et al. (2005) also found that the effects of behavior modeling on declarative knowledge were generally large, and these effects decayed over time, suggesting that the training effects are strongest at the early stage of learning. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H9: Subjects who receive self-paced online behavior modeling training will develop higher declarative knowledge than subjects who receive self-paced online text-based training

According to social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), observing someone else performing the target behavior raises subjects’ perceptions of their own ability to perform it successfully (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Johnson & Marakas, 2000). For example, Johnson and Marakas (2000) compared non-behavior modeling to behavior modeling in a spreadsheet training workshop. Subjects in behavior modeling were found to develop higher post-learning self-efficacy than those in the non-behavioral modeling condition. Although learners do not directly interact with trainers in an online learning environment, we argue that learners still experience feelings of accomplishment by watching the trainers demonstrate the behaviors successfully.

Behavior modeling is less error prone, compared to other methods such as exploration and instruction learning (Hecht et al., 2001; Simon & Wegner, 1996). This benefit is even more prominent in an online setting. For example, when an instructor uses behavior modeling to teach business analytics using SAS in a synchronous online setting, learners do not need to switch as often as they do in a face-to-face setting to observe the instructor’s SAS demonstration. With an online mode of behavior modeling, learners can have their virtual video (e.g., Zoom) running side-by-side with their own SAS program.<sup>3</sup> Simultaneously observing the effective execution of a computer task and practicing the observed behaviors can prevent learners from making unnecessary errors and, in turn, raises learners’ post-learning self-efficacy as they witness a successful demonstration of the task (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). Thus, we hypothesize:

H10: Subjects who receive self-paced online behavior modeling training will develop higher post-learning self-efficacy than subjects who receive self-paced online text-based training

## Controlling for Known IS Learning Determinants Based on Prior Studies

Prior research has shown that other learner characteristics, such as age, motivation, and general computer self-efficacy, can influence the acquisition of IS knowledge (e.g., Bostrom et al., 1990; Santhanam et al., 2016; Webster & Martocchio, 1992). Thus, we included the effects of the most common learner characteristics identified by prior studies in our model. We identified these learner characteristics by conducting a review of the extant IS learning literature that measured declarative knowledge and post-learning self-efficacy as the learning outcomes. We summarized these relevant studies in Appendix B.

Based on this review, we controlled for the effects of gender, age, past experience or perceived familiarity with technology, general computer self-efficacy, computer anxiety, goal orientations, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use on declarative knowledge. Our review also revealed that gender, age, perceived familiarity with technology, general computer self-efficacy, computer anxiety, and goal orientations are the most common determinants of post-learning self-efficacy. Thus, we controlled for the effects of these variables on post-learning self-efficacy in the model. ## Research Method

We conducted two studies to accomplish our research objective outlined in the introduction. Specifically, the first study focused on establishing the link between executive functions and IS learning in a traditional IS learning environment when we controlled for other known learner characteristics. Armed with the evidence of the relationship between executive functions and IS learning from Study 1, we replicated the results of the first study using a slightly different approach in Study 2. While we tested H1-H8 in both studies, we tested H9-H10 in Study 2 only.

## Study 1: Survey

In this study, we combined self-reported measures and neuropsychological assessment tasks from cognitive psychology to test our hypotheses. The learning goal was to understand database concepts (i.e., how to exchange data between Microsoft Access and other applications) using a self-paced tutorial on Microsoft Access. Since Microsoft Access is a popular type of database management system that has been used in previous IS studies, using Microsoft Access as our study context makes the results of our study more comparable to those of existing studies.

## Operationalization of Research Constructs

To measure declarative knowledge, we developed ten multiplechoice questions (see Appendix C, Study 1) that assessed participants’ understanding of the concepts and software features. Each question contained four choices of which one was correct. Possible scores ranged from 0 correct to 10 correct. A power format (i.e., learners were told to take as long as they needed to complete the test) was used because the construct of interest was whether learners acquired declarative knowledge or recall accuracy (Martocchio, 1994). We developed items to measure post-learning selfefficacy by following the procedures recommended by Agarwal et al. (2000). Scales for the remaining constructs were adapted from the literature, and sources for each scale are listed in Appendix D. All self-reported measures were modeled reflectively on 7-point Likert scales.

We used neuropsychological assessment tasks to measure executive functions for two reasons. First, executive functions are difficult to measure using a self-reporting technique. Second, unlike a self-reporting technique, this method provides an objective and standardized measure of one’s executive functions (Anastasia & Urbina, 1997). Given that data collected using a self-reporting approach could be susceptible to multiple measurement biases (e.g., social desirability bias), incorporating objective measures in our study helps minimize these biases and strengthens the validity of the findings.

We use a single task to measure each subprocess of executive functions. This approach has been used in prior studies (e.g., Allan et al., 2008; Benedek et al., 2014; Bull & Scerif, 2001; Jaeggi et al., 2010; Ophir et al., 2009) and is appropriate because it produces reliable and homogeneous scores of executive functions without running the risk of including the tasks with unclear validity (Benedek et al., 2014). This approach also minimizes fatigue effects among participants by omitting the need to complete multiple tasks in one session. Thus, we use a single task that is often regarded as a “gold standard” for the assessment of each subprocess of executive functions (Benedek et al., 2014). We selected the three most common cognitive tasks that have been broadly used to measure executive functions. In performing each task, participants were required to perform a set of computerized tasks in which their performance was measured based on their accuracy (proportion of correct responses) and precision (reaction time measures). The summary of executive functions tasks, including their references, is presented in Table 2. The details of the experimental materials used in the study are presented in Appendix E.

## Assessment of Executive Functions

To measure working memory, we used the n-back task, which requires participants to continuously update their working memory in order to maintain the series of the last n presented elements of information (Benedek et al., 2014). The n-back task is a continuous-recognition measure that presents stimulus sequences, such as letters or pictures. At the cognitive level, this task is thought to involve a number of executive processes, including active maintenance of the last n items and updating new items so that they can be actively maintained (Chatham et al., 2011). The n-back task used in this current study was adopted from Jaeggie et al. (2010). To assess the shifting function, we used the color-shape task, which requires participants to make either color-switching or shape-switching decisions depending on the stimulus. The color-shape task was adopted from Miyake et al. (2004) and Miyake and Friedman (2012). To measure inhibition, we used the Stroop task, which requires participants to inhibit the tendency to produce an automatic response, such as naming the stimulus word (Benedek et al., 2014; Miyake et al., 2000). The Stroop task used in this current study was adopted from Miyake et al. (2000). Details of the experimental procedures are presented in Appendix F.

## Data Analysis and Results

We used SmartPLS (v.3.2.9) for data analysis (Ringle et al., 2015). We used partial least squares (PLS) for assessing both the measurement model and the structural model for three reasons. First, we conceptualized executive functions as a formative construct. Relative to the covariance-based SEM procedure that typically requires indicators in a reflective mode, PLS supports latent variables with formative indicators (Chin, 1998a; Chin, 1998b, Chin and Newsted, 1999). Second, our model is fairly complex with multiple control variables. Complex models can be problematic in a covariance-based SEM analysis relative to fit indices and computation as the degrees of freedom increase with an increasing number of indicators and latent variables (Chin & Newsted, 1999). Third, PLS places minimal demands on measurement scales (e.g., categorical to ratio level indicators can be used in the same model) (Chin & Newsted, 1999). Given that our model consists of several different measurement scales, PLS was deemed appropriate for our current research.

## Measurement Model

We evaluated the psychometric properties of the items for all constructs measured by the self-reported questionnaire as well as the executive functions measure. The declarative knowledge scores were computed as the overall proportion of correct answers to the multiple-choice questions. We dropped Question 6 because of some wording issues, and participants were given one free point. The average item difficulty was 0.50, which indicates that the test was powerful in differentiating between those who did and did not learn (Martocchio, 1994). We also computed the item discrimination index (Understanding item analyses, 2006). As can be seen in Appendix G (Table G1), all discrimination indices were greater than 0.20, suggesting that all items successfully distinguished between those who were knowledgeable and those who were not.

The n-back data were collected for three levels of n-back (2-back, 3-back, and 4-back). Given that the tasks become harder as the n increases, users made more mistakes when they performed 3-back and 4-back tasks than when they did 2- back tasks. As expected, the dependent measures of n-back across the three task levels were highly correlated (ranged from 0.41 to 0.63, p < 0.001).

The analysis results reported for the color-shape task were based on the accuracy in the shift trials, in which the response to the previous trial was also correct. Correct trials immediately following errors were excluded because the correct set might not have been achieved on the prior error trials (Friedman et al., 2008). We first checked whether accuracy and reaction times (RTs) measures went in the same direction or whether evidence indicated a speedaccuracy trade-off (i.e., slower responses have higher accuracy scores). If a speed-accuracy trade-off is present, a convincing conclusion is usually impossible (Bruyer & Brysbaert, 2011). In our study, the correlation between accuracy and RTs among color-shape task observations was relatively high (r > 0.50), suggesting the presence of the speed-accuracy trade-off. To remedy this speed-accuracy trade-off, recent research has recommended the use of composite scores (i.e., accuracy scores divided by RTs multiplied by 100) (Kertzman et al., 2006). Thus, we used the composite scores to adjust accuracy performance for sacrifices in speed as the dependent measure.

The dependent measure of the Stroop task was the proportion of correct responses in the incongruent trials (Khng & Lee, 2014). As expected, participants made more errors in the incongruent trials (??̅ = 0.82, SD = 0.20) than in the control (??̅ = 0.94, SD = 0.13) and congruent trials (??̅ = 0.94, SD = 0.13), suggesting that inhibition is needed in the incongruent trials. Regarding the accuracy data of the Stroop task, the raw proportion of correct responses tended to show a positive skew indicative of a floor effect (Miyake et al., 2000, 2004). We applied an arcsine transformation to achieve a satisfactory level of normality. Although the accuracy and RTs measure went in the same direction, the correlation score was low (r < 0.25). Thus, we concluded that no response time-accuracy trade-off was detected for the Stroop data.

## Structural Model

Since we conceptualized executive functions as a formative construct, we reported the weights for the PLS structural model.<sup>4</sup> Given that our sample size was moderate and we were interested in estimating correlation weights between indicators, we used PLS Mode A estimation for the formative measures (Becker et al., 2013). Data analysis processes involved several hierarchical approaches associating IS learning with the hypothesized predictors. We first estimated the independent effect of executive functions on IS learning in Model 1. We then estimated the effects of control variables (i.e., study time and location) and previously known learner characteristics on IS learning (Model 2, Table 5). Next, we ran the analysis with all variables in the model to check whether the executive functions remained significant (Model 3, Table 5). After doing so, we estimated the change in IS learning by removing insignificant control variables and including executive functions in the model (Model 4, Table 5). The final structural model is illustrated in Figure 3.

The results showed that although gender and general computer self-efficacy were significant in the declarative knowledge model, common executive functions still showed a strong, significant relationship with declarative knowledge, supporting H1. The results also show that general computer self-efficacy, computer anxiety, and mastery orientation remained significant after executive functions were added to the post-learning self-efficacy model. As expected, the effect of common executive functions on post-learning self-efficacy was positive and significant, supporting H5.

Next, we examined the differential relations of executive functions to IS learning. The results are presented in Table 6, and the final structural model is demonstrated in Figure 4. The only significant control variables retained in the final model with declarative knowledge as the dependent variable were general computer self-efficacy and gender. As hypothesized, working memory and shifting functions were positively associated with declarative knowledge, supporting H2 and H3. However, inhibition was not significantly associated with declarative knowledge. Thus, H4 was not supported. As predicted, working memory and shifting functions were significantly associated with post-learning self-efficacy, supporting H6 and H7, respectively. Contrary to our hypothesis, inhibition was not positively associated with post-learning self-efficacy. Thus, H8 was not supported. Although the effect sizes<sup>5</sup> of executive functions on both declarative knowledge (Cohen’s f<sup>2</sup> = 0.11) and post-learning self-efficacy (Cohen’s f<sup>2</sup> = 0.08) were only small to medium, the result still confirms our hypothesis that executive functions play a significant role in increasing IS learning.

Study 1 provided evidence for the common and differential relations of executive functions and IS learning. Although the results of Study 1 supported the majority of our hypotheses, one might argue the findings could be different in other IS learning environments (e.g., online learning). To address this issue, we conducted Study 2 by incorporating self-paced online learning designs in our model. The increased reliance on technology-based learning has led to more learner-centered learning and has given learners unprecedented control over their learning experience (Bell et al., 2017). For this reason, we expected that executive functions would play an even more significant role by helping learners develop effective learning strategies to make better use of their control and ultimately determine how much they learn in an online environment.

## Study 2: Online Experiment

The primary goals of Study 2 were to replicate the findings of Study 1 in a self-paced online learning environment while testing the effect of manipulating the training method from text-based learning to behavior modeling. We conducted an online experiment with a between-subject design by manipulating the methods used for delivering the learning content. Participants were randomly assigned to either the self-paced online behavior modeling condition or the selfpaced online text-based learning condition.

## Participants and Data Collection

We recruited participants from the Prolific.co web platform. Prolific, an established platform for online subject recruitment, has been used as a subject pool in different research areas, including IS (Schneider et al., 2020). Each subject received a US\$12 subject fee for participating in our research. The average duration of the study was 100 minutes.

To reduce common method bias, we gathered the data at two points in time (Podsakoff et al., 2003). At Time 1, we posted a 2-minute screening survey. We told participants that completing this survey would allow us to determine their eligibility for our main study but did not tell them anything about our screening criteria (Sherf & Morrison, 2019). We used the following criteria to identify eligible respondents: (1) had never been diagnosed with color blindness, (2) had never been diagnosed with learning disabilities, (3) used Windows PC to participate in the study, (4) had Microsoft Access 2013 or a newer version installed in their computer, and (5) had basic or no experience in using Microsoft Access. We also applied several demographic filters, including age (i.e., between 18 and 40 years old), current country of residence (i.e., United States), and right-handedness<sup>6</sup> to prescreen participants. These criteria yielded 647 eligible respondents out of 1500 who completed the screening survey.

A day later (Time 2), we invited these 647 people to participate in the main study. They were informed that the study would be automatically closed once the quota (i.e., 85 respondents for each condition) was met. Of the 170 respondents who completed the study, five were eliminated because they did not complete the neuropsychological assessment tasks. Six participants were missing data for one task because of software errors (three participants were missing data for n-back scores and three for Stroop scores). We replaced these missing data with estimates obtained with multiple regression (i.e., Buck’s method of imputation) (Friedman & Miyake, 2004) so that all analyses could be based on the maximum amount of data. Since only the baseline variables were missing in a very small number of cases (3.6%), this approach is suitable because it provides less-biased estimates of parameters for the population than other similar approaches, such as unconditional mean imputation and listwise deletion of individuals with missing data (Little & Rubin, 1987). Analyses of the relationship between executive functions and IS learning were conducted with and without imputation. The replacement of these data points did not change the conclusions from any of the analyses. Therefore, analyses are reported based on all possible data to avoid the loss of statistical power and to use all relevant data available from our sample.

The demographics for each condition and the test of differences between groups are summarized in Table 7. The results of the Chi-Square (X<sup>2</sup>) test indicate no statistically significant differences between two conditions on all demographic variables (i.e., gender, ethnicity, age, and education). All participants had limited prior experience using Microsoft Access. Specifically, 64 participants (38.8%) indicated that they had never used Microsoft Access before, and 101 participants (61.2%) reported basic levels of Microsoft Access skills. The average score of familiarity with Microsoft Access was 3.64 (on a 7-point scale), suggesting that the study participants had a low-level knowledge of Microsoft Access. We found no statistically significant differences between the two conditions on perceived familiarity with Microsoft Access (t = -1.01 (163); p-value = 0.31).

## Procedures

The task sequence completed by participants in Study 2 was similar to Study 1, in which learners first completed the training session and then completed the neuropsychological assessment tasks. The training content used in the experiment was adopted from Microsoft Office online training materials publicly available on the Microsoft support website. The learning modules used in the self-paced online behavior modeling condition consisted of seven segments: (1) introduction to database (4 minutes, 30 seconds); (2) starting a new database from a blank template (4 minutes); (3) building the first table and set data types (4 minutes, 20 seconds); (4) building a table with the table designer (4 minutes, 16 seconds); (5) combining fields using the calculated data type (2 minutes); (6) getting started with table relationships (5 minutes); and (7) creating relationships with the lookup wizard (3 minutes). Each video segment used in the behavior modeling condition was converted into a text-based module, resulting in seven text-based modules. General rules were presented to learners, followed by specific examples and screenshots of the software interfaces in the same order of the behavior modeling condition.

Prior to the learning session, demographics and learner characteristics (i.e., perceived familiarity with Microsoft Access, learning goals, computer anxiety, and general computer self-efficacy) were measured. A 7-point Likert-type scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree”) was used for all items (see Appendix D for details). At the beginning of the training, learners in both conditions were asked to read a pre-training script providing the nature of the training task for the training session (see Appendix K). In both conditions, we asked participants to voluntarily take notes, pay attention, and stay focused. Given that the training was delivered online in an informal learning environment, we also attempted to boost participants’ motivational beliefs and confidence using statements such as “pay attention,” “you are a very capable learner,” etc. (Santhanam et al., 2008).

We held the length of training constant at 40 minutes for all conditions. However, participants were told to pace their own learning by freely allocating their study time over the items within this 40-minute duration. A pre-test confirmed that the 40-minute length was adequate for most subjects with little or no knowledge in databases to complete the training in both training conditions. Learners in the self-paced online behavior modeling condition watched seven video segments for 27 minutes. They then practiced for 13 minutes. Learners in the online text-based learning condition went over the text-based tutorials for 25-30 minutes and practiced for 10-15 minutes. The approximately 15-minute practice time for a 30-minute lesson is also consistent with that of previous studies (e.g., Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Yi & Davis, 2003).

A countdown timer on the screen was made visible to learners to indicate how much time they had left. To ensure that learners paid attention during the training sessions, several attentional check questions (e.g., What was the name of the table created in the video?) were included during the learning phase. Once the training time was over, learners were automatically directed to the Inquisit Millisecond online platform to complete the neuropsychological assessment tasks. The tasks used to measure executive functions were identical to those used in Study 1.

## Measurement Model

As in Study 1, we used PLS in Study 2 to assess both the measurement model and the structural model. The same trimming and transformation procedures for the executive functions data used in Study 1 were also used in Study 2 (see Appendix H). The descriptive statistics of the executive functions and IS learning are presented in Table 8. ## Post Hoc Analysis of Moderating Role of Executive Functions

Compared to text-based training, self-paced online behavior modeling can potentially reduce demands on executive functions (Renkl, 2014). Behavior modeling also helps learners more easily ascertain whether particular actions lead to rewarding or punishing consequences (Allsop et al., 2018; Dunne & O’Dougherty, 2013). Therefore, learners with low executive functions may benefit more from self-paced online behavior modeling training compared to those with high executive functions. Our post hoc analyses of these moderating effects were not significant. Behavior modeling improved declarative knowledge equally for both low and high executive function subjects with no interaction effect.

## Discussion

We conducted two distinct studies to establish the importance of executive functions for IS learning. In Study 1, we found significant relationships between executive functions and IS learning in a self-paced offline learning environment. The relationships remained significant after a host of previously known learner characteristics were included in the model. Study 2 shows that the relationship between executive functions and IS learning generalizes to a self-paced online learning environment. Study 2 also demonstrates the superiority of behavior modeling over textbased learning. The findings from both studies significantly enhance IS learning and developmental research by establishing the importance of executive functions, which should not be overlooked in the design and evaluation of IS learning methods.

## Theoretical Contributions, Implications, and Future Research Directions

We bring a new perspective to IS learning research by suggesting that executive functions, consisting of three specific control processes—working memory, inhibition, and shifting ability—play a significant role in IS knowledge acquisition. Together, these three functions enable learners to acquire perceptual, cognitive, and motor skills to effectively use an IS to perform relevant tasks. Next, we discuss the common and differential involvement of executive functions in IS learning.

## The Common Involvement of Executive Functions in IS Learning

Our research shows that the three dimensions of executive functions correlate and work together to enable IS learning. Our findings suggest that the IS learning tasks such as those used in our studies share some common task requirements, particularly the maintenance of goals and the need to flexibly switch between different types of processing. For example, when learners use Microsoft Access in a self-paced online learning environment, two of their goals are to understand different database objects and identify different data types. To acquire knowledge, therefore, they must actively maintain multiple goals in working memory, flexibly switch between one function to another, and focus on relevant concepts (e.g., foreign key, primary key, etc.).

We are at the point at which nearly all modern work behavior has an IS component (Cascio & Montealegre, 2016). Learners are required not only to consistently adapt to the underlying design and presentation of new systems but also to adjust their skills to adapt to unpredictable work environments. For example, skills acquired from building a relational database for a simple e-commerce website should be adjusted when learners are tasked with building a complex database for a multinational company with multiple product lines. Embracing multiple solutions, adapting to the system’s design and interactivity, and looking beyond the basic requirements are some of the activities involved in IS learning that require executive functions skills to operate in coordination with each other.

## The Differential Involvement of Executive Functions in IS Learning

We make profound contributions to IS learning literature by demonstrating the differential role of three specific executive functions components involved in IS skills acquisition. Our results show that working memory is positively associated with both declarative knowledge and post-learning selfefficacy. Working memory facilitates the encoding of new information, which learners can then construct, access, and use in various ways (Baddeley, 2012). For example, to differentiate the primary keys from foreign keys in database tables, learners need working memory to access the keys functions in one table, hold those functions in mind, and integrate them with other fields in another table. Learners need to continually interact with the system and integrate multiple information cues—processes that require the activation of working memory. Working memory is also responsible for the active maintenance of task goals (Baddeley, 2012). In the previous relational database learning example, the learning goals could be to create a series of tables and define the relationships among tables that should then result in relational tables with no redundant data. The task could also involve additional goals derived from specific problem-solving strategies. People with high working memory may easily keep all goals active throughout the learning process, whereas people with low working memory may fall back on lessspecific goals (e.g., adding table fields based on intuition instead of carefully analyzing the business problem, thus failing to achieve the learning goals).

Turning to the switching function, the findings show that switching is positively associated with both declarative knowledge and post-learning self-efficacy. Learning requires simultaneous processing of multiple information cues (e.g., cues associated with underlying designs and presentations of the system) and active evaluation of problems and solutions from multiple perspectives. Individuals with high shifting ability have more efficient mechanisms of switching back and forth from one mental goal to another (e.g., shifting from one layout to another when exporting a database object to another format).

Regarding the inhibition function, the findings of Study 1 show the insignificant role of inhibition in its relation to declarative knowledge, whereas this relationship is significant in Study 2. This inconsistency could be because participants in Study 2 were restricted to a 40-minute study time, whereas those in Study 1 were not. This time pressure might narrow learners’ focus to the salient features of IS learning that appear most central to completing the learning task. We posit that inhibition plays an important role in an online environment by enabling learners to selectively attend to specific information cues that they receive at any given time. Inhibition entails blocking distracting thoughts as well as shielding learning goals from distractions or interferences that may be more prominent in online learning environments (Chen & Huang, 2014).

Some explanations exist for why inhibition is not associated with post-learning self-efficacy. First, inhibition could play a lesser role than the other two dimensions of executive functions in establishing learners’ self-confidence. IS learning typically involves not only listening and taking notes but also managing multiple goals and performing various computer tasks (Chen, 2002). Inhibition may not be a focal function that one needs to increase post-learning self-efficacy. Second, perhaps the inhibition effect is only observable at the unitary level (shown by the significant weight of the inhibition factor in the formative model) (Friedman & Miyake, 2017). As we continue to better understand how executive functions are associated with IS learning, different forms of inhibition should be investigated. Of particular interest would be attempts to include learner self-control—a subset of the inhibition function—and examine its effect on individual and team learning by using a single or multisite approach.

## Self-Paced Online Behavior Modeling as an IS Learning Approach

We found that behavior modeling leads to higher declarative knowledge than text-based learning. Online video-based modeling assists learners in perceiving temporal changes or movements in the system being learned. It provides learners with an easy-to-follow model, enabling them to work toward meaningful goals in acquiring new IS knowledge (van der Meij & van der Meij, 2014). Our findings imply that behavior modeling can potentially enhance learners’ IS knowledge acquisition in an online setting.

However, we did not observe a significant role of behavior modeling for post-learning self-efficacy. When studying the effect of behavior modeling in a traditional learning environment, Compeau and Higgins (1995) also did not find support for this relationship. A possible reason could be that, when faced with unfamiliar tasks, learners rely more on an indepth assessment of what is required for the knowledge acquisition process and whether they have the appropriate skills (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Gist & Mitchell, 1992). Thus, they rely less on the delivery method to enhance their post-learning self-efficacy.

## Other Variables

The significance of some previously known learner characteristics also implies that executive functions are not the only predictor of IS learning. Other learner characteristics (e.g., mastery orientation, general computer self-efficacy, perceived familiarity, and computer anxiety) likely matter as well. Therefore, IS learning programs intended to improve one’s learning outcomes should consider other learner characteristics.

One might argue that computer anxiety may impair executive functions by preempting the processing and temporary storage capacity of working memory (Eysenck et al., 2007). Although this explanation might be possible, we did not systematically manipulate computer anxiety in our research. Therefore, we do not have sufficient evidence to support this claim. However, prior research has offered strong evidence that executive functions predict learning achievement more consistently than socioemotional factors such as anxiety. For example, Dunkan et al. (2007) found that executive functions are one of the strongest predictors of later school achievement. By contrast, they found that socioemotional factors are generally insignificant predictors of later academic performance, probably because anxious individuals can compensate for the adverse effects of anxiety on processing efficiency of executive functions by increasing their effort and use of resources (Eysenck et al., 2007). Nevertheless, the significant correlation between executive functions and computer anxiety observed in our studies opens an important avenue for further research. We suggest that future research consider experimentally manipulating the level of computer anxiety and/or executive functions to establish a causal relationship.

## The Use of Neuropsychological Assessment Tasks

Another aspect that differentiates our study from previous studies is that we focus on mental processes at the microlevel, which can be directly associated with the functions of the prefrontal lobes of the brain. We introduce neuropsychological assessment tasks that can be used to measure cognitive constructs. This approach would allow researchers to measure learners’ cognitive processes or abilities more objectively than is possible using self-reporting techniques. Thus, we encourage IS researchers to utilize neuropsychological assessment tasks when cognitive constructs are measured in their research.

## Limitations

We note several limitations of our research. First, this is an initial study to investigate the role of executive functions in actual IS learning environments and should be subjected to further testing and improvement. Support for our hypotheses should be tested in different IS learning contexts to increase the external validity. Although the use of Microsoft Access is considered appropriate for our research, it is a popular database management system that has been widely used in industry. Future research is needed to test the model using different types of IS (e.g., from simple to complex systems that may place different demands on executive functions).

Second, evidence indicates that executive function test performance declines with age (e.g., Salthouse et al., 2003). Since we used young adult samples in both of our studies, our dataset might not have enough variability to test the effect of age, evidenced by the insignificant effect of age in the model. Future research should gather data from a more diverse population and examine the moderating effect of age on the relationship between executive functions and IS learning.

Lastly, although neuropsychological tasks are considered valid and reliable measures of executive functions, future research should complement these measures with neuropsychological tools, such as EEG and fMRI. Neuropsychological tools may reveal which brain areas are activated across subjects during IS learning. We call for collaborative research that brings together investigators with expertise in IS, cognitive psychology, neuroscience, and related fields to examine this issue. Future research could also examine the effect of cognitive load (measured using EEG) on the activation of executive functions during IS skills acquisition.

## Agenda for IS Research on Executive Functions

The relationship between executive functions and learning may vary depending on the learning domains (Bull & Scerif, 2001; Peng et al., 2015). Although examining whether IS learning is distinct from other learning domains is beyond the scope of our research, it would be useful for future research to compare the relationship between executive functions and learning in various learning domains. For example, shifting is likely more relevant in IS learning than in non-IS learning. Examining the role that executive functions play in different types of learning may provide additional theoretical insights.

We also acknowledge that not all types of IS learning are homogeneous. There are many types of IS learning (e.g., cloud and mobile computing, big data and machine learning, artificial intelligence-based systems learning, etc.). Future research is needed to tailor the theorizing to different types of IS learning and to assess how technology-supported training may compensate for specific demands on the dimensions of executive functions. Future IS learning research is needed to clearly delineate the conditions under which each dimension of executive functions likely plays a greater or lesser role as a determinant of IS learning or a moderator of the influence of training methods on IS learning. Such a promising research direction awaits a taxonomy of IS learning that does not yet exist. Nevertheless, the current research provides an essential foundational block upon which to build that vision.

Future research is also needed to test the model in various IS learning environments, such as mobile learning, hybridlearning, hypermedia systems, and virtual realities (e.g., Loderer et al., 2020), using different types of learning strategies, such as a generating concept map strategy or a practice testing strategy (e.g., Brod, 2021). The interaction effect between executive functions and different IS learning methods may actually emerge in future studies. Identifying ways in which executive functions may affect how various learning strategies contribute to IS learning seems to be a promising research area. Our research also presents the opportunity for researchers to identify various learning features and conditions (e.g., immediate feedback, the use of synchronous events, and various ways of organizing online materials) that could help those with low executive functions.

Further, procedural knowledge is not measured in our current study. We primarily focus on IS learning before automatic processing takes place—when executive functions play a major role in constructing knowledge. Although we have strong reasons for not measuring procedural knowledge, future studies should put effort into measuring procedural knowledge using a longitudinal study design to demonstrate the diminished effect of executive functions over time.

We also encourage future research to add emotional processes to the analysis of executive functions and IS learning. Learning environments are expected to become more personalized, more attentive to the cultural and social context of learners, and more attentive to learners’ emotions (Graesser, 2020). Future research, for example, could investigate how emotional design for online learning enhances executive functions and, in turn, learning (e.g., Plass et al., 2020). Research on this issue will help us better understand the roles of emotions and cognitions in IS learning.

The link between executive functions and IS learning established here may open the door to discovering new insights about IS design strategies to enhance IS learning by augmenting users’ working memory, shifting, and inhibition abilities. Training methods based on insights about executive functions could make people more attuned to their executive functions and how to better utilize them. For example, a better understanding of executive functions might provide insights that guide IS designs and help users better regulate the interrelationships between conscious processing (involving executive functions) and automatic processing.

Our findings also have implications beyond IS learning. Executive functions are strongly involved in situations requiring planning and decision-making (Dohle et al., 2018). Therefore, they are also relevant for IS effectiveness after the learning phase. Future research could examine how to design IS training to augment users’ executive function abilities and limitations by extending working memory capacity, facilitating task switching (i.e., both detecting the need to switch and reducing the switching costs by maintaining the essential components of multiple task sets), and inhibition (i.e., preventing unnecessary, irrelevant interruptions). We also call for future research to use the concept of executive functions to study other IS-related phenomena, such as technology multitasking, internet addiction, self-regulated learning, and IS use and interventions for breaking and modifying habits.

## Practical Implications

Our findings inform educators and practitioners that individual differences in executive functions are positively associated with IS learning. Over the past decade, we have witnessed substantial changes in the nature of work and organizations, including technological advances that have made jobs more cognitively complex and dynamic (Bell & Kozlowski, 2008; Cascio, 2019). The results of the present research complement prior research by suggesting that organizations should focus on a broader range of skills in deciding which IS skills to develop. If acquiring IS knowledge depends on broader, more general skills (i.e., executive functions skills), then companies, educational institutions, and individuals should invest not only in IS skills but also in executive function skills to obtain improved IS learning. We next integrate our findings with a number of relevant theory bases to propose IS learning intervention strategies (see Table 12).

## IS Learning Interventions Targeting Common Executive Functions

We propose several IS learning interventions to accommodate learners with low executive functions. First, organizations can integrate executive functions training with their IS learning program. Research has shown the effectiveness of executive functions training (e.g., Lumosity, Shute et al., 2015; CogMed computerized training, Diamond & Lee, 2011; and task-switching computerized training, Karbach & Kray, 2009). Moffitt et al. (2011, p. 2694) stated that “interventions that achieve even small improvements in [executive functions] for individuals could shift the entire distribution of outcomes in a salutary direction and yield large improvements in health, wealth, and crime rate for a nation” (see also Diamond, 2013, p. 155). By encouraging users to improve their executive functions, organizations can optimize the outcomes of IS learning and minimize the skills gap among workers.

The mechanisms to help learners monitor their executive functions should also be explored as an IS learning intervention (Keith & Frese, 2005). For example, one could design adaptive guidance to meet the differing needs of learners and augment the interpretation process with futureoriented information that will enhance learning (Bell & Kozlowski, 2008). A training program could utilize monitoring features (e.g., tools that can predict the duration to complete a learning task, check and evaluate the progress of a learning goal, and predict the outcomes of a learning approach) and control features (e.g., tools that can identify specific steps needed to accomplish a learning task). Such features might help learners make more informed decisions regarding learning strategies to adopt in their learning (Schmidt & Ford, 2003).

## IS Learning Interventions Targeting Working Memory

We suggest several learning strategies to assist individuals with low working memory. First, we encourage learners to use external memory aids and computer-based memory aids (e.g., online productivity trackers, electronic reminders, timers, etc.) to enhance memory function (Kapur et al., 2004). Second, training providers and organizations should consider adopting a task analysis approach to IS learning. This task analysis incorporates load information related to IS features, procedural, and figurative sources. The results can be used to create a metric for estimating the cognitive demands of an IS learning task (Arsalidou, 2014) and provide insights on which delivery methods to use to help learners with low executive functions (e.g., breaking down tasks and instructions into small segments, separating procedural information from the theory underlying a specific software function, and frequently repeating important information) (Gathercolde & Alloway, 2008).

Another potential IS learning approach to compensate for low working memory is the use of information control strategies. A common yet simple example of these strategies is the implementation of computerized adaptive testing that adapts to learners’ ability level. The system can be designed to detect signs of memory overload. If working memory failures are detected (e.g., a learner’s error rate is above average), new information presentation should be delayed. Another example of information control strategies is the use of a feedback mechanism to make users more aware of their behavior and to provide individualized evaluation of performance and strategy (Polites & Karahanna, 2013).

## IS Learning Interventions Targeting the Shifting Function

IS learning methods can be designed to enhance learners’ shifting ability. One way to enhance shifting ability is to engage learners in exploration and experimentation during IS learning (Keith & Wolff, 2014). For example, educators can use simulations or online games to help learners explore different features of computer applications in various contexts (Keith & Frese, 2005). Learners are expected to utilize their switching skills and, in turn, actively explore new knowledge on their own (Keith & Wolff, 2014).

IS learning can also be designed to support learners with low shifting ability. Although research has shown that constantly switching contexts between tasks and/or system features is associated with poor learning (e.g., Sana et al., 2013), some switching skills, such as changing perspectives and approaches, are needed to optimize IS learning. Encouraging learners to apply context switching mindfully (i.e., voluntarily attending to particular stimuli based on one’s goal or intention and being flexible enough to adjust to changing demands) may help those with low shifting ability. For example, when learners must operate and switch between two different systems, they can use a switching aid feature, such as a digital sticky note, to add a brief comment and mark where to return. Knowing the “path” to get back will enable users to quickly reconstruct their mental states and possibly minimize the switching costs.

We also recommend an adaptive training management system that can automatically adjust the number of learning variables presented to learners based on the task needs and users’ shifting ability. For example, when users learn how to visualize big data using Tableau, a diagnostic test can be performed at the beginning of the learning stage to identify their switching skills. Based on the result, the number of variable inputs can be manipulated and increased gradually without compromising learning.

IS training could also focus on creating smaller, more modular units of “just-in-time” training. One way to effectively and efficiently support employee-centric training and development is by making access to knowledge more convenient to users (Cascio & Montealegre, 2016). Switching back and forth between learning and doing should be made more natural and effective to reduce switching costs and provide continuous “on-demand” skill development and transfer.

## IS Learning Interventions Targeting the Inhibition Function

A learning platform could be designed to help learners block distracting information. One simple example is the Focus Assist feature in Windows, which allows users to avoid distracting notifications when they need to stay focused. Another example is the hide application feature in Apple Mac that allows users to open only the applications they need for work and to hide everything else. Although these strategies seem to contradict the switching skills interventions, they can be implemented simultaneously. Learners can voluntarily activate multiple features, applications, or contexts that are relevant to learning goals and hide the irrelevant ones.

Another learning intervention that may help computer skills acquisition among those with low inhibition is implementation intentions (Hall et al., 2012). These can increase the salience of action cues, making people more likely to act when relevant opportunities present themselves (Hall et al., 2012; Webb & Sheeran, 2006). Designing a learning platform that can automatically guide learners to set their learning goals, identify possible learning obstacles, and put together plans to help them overcome those obstacles may especially benefit those with low inhibition.

## Behavior Modeling as an IS Learning Method

Organizations and schools worldwide rely upon technologies and advancements in training design and delivery. Our results show that behavior modeling is more effective than text-based learning when it is used to train IS skills, such as database skills, through an online platform. Using this strategy, IS providers, organizations, and educators can provide more interactive learning experiences, thus improving users declarative knowledge.

## Conclusion

In sum, our research demonstrates the importance of executive functions for IS learning. This research lays the foundation for further research to explore the relationship between different aspects of executive functions and IS learning. This research also contributes to a more complete understanding of the role of individual differences in executive functions for IS learning and provides the context for gaining more in-depth insights about the processes, mechanisms, and boundary conditions concerning the effectiveness of training practices.

## Appendix F

## Experimental Procedures to Measure Executive Functions

## N-Back Task

The stimulus material for the n-back task consisted of eight random shapes. The shapes were all shown in yellow and presented centrally on a black background for 500 ms each, followed by a 2500 ms inter-stimulus interval. Participants were required to press a pre-defined key for targets, and their response window lasted from the onset of the stimulus until the presentation of the next stimulus (3000 ms). No response was required for non-targets. Participants completed a total of nine blocks (2-, 3-, and 4-back levels, with each level presented for three consecutive blocks). They first completed 10 trials of a practice session per level n tested and then three blocks of each n-back task—with each block consisting of 20 trials, including 6 target trials and 14 non-target trials. Omissions were counted as errors.

## Color-Shape Task

The color-shape task consisted of pure-task and mixed-task trials. Participants first completed the pure-task trials in which they performed only one task, followed by mixed-task trials in which they alternated between two different tasks—repeat and switch trials. In the mixed-task trials, participants were instructed to indicate whether the color was red or green when the cue was “C” and whether the shape was a circle or a triangle when the cue was “S.” In the repeat trials, participants saw the same letter cue in consecutive order. In the switch trials, two different letter cues were presented one after another. The responses for both color and shape tasks were mapped onto two keyboard-selection rules in a bivalent manner (e.g., the left button for “red” and “circle” and the right button for “green” and “triangle”). Participants were asked to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. At the beginning of the experiment, participants completed two practice blocks of 15 mixedtask trials (plus one start trial) each. If the response was correct, the next trial appeared after a 600 ms intertribal interval (ITI). If it was incorrect, the computer beeped and an error message (i.e., “incorrect”) was displayed for 1000 ms followed by an ITI of 600 ms (Miyake et al. 2004). After the training trials, participants performed two test blocks of 100 (plus one start trial) each. The trial setup was identical to the practice trials except that feedback or an error message was no longer provided for incorrect responses.

## Stroop Task

On each trial of the Stroop task, participants saw words presented in one of four colors: red, blue, green, or black. The task was to name the color in which each word was printed while ignoring what the words said. In this experiment, participants were given three types of trials: (a) incongruent colors condition—30+ trials with a color word printed in a different color (e.g., BLUE printed in a red color); (b) congruent colors condition—30+ trials with a color word printed in the same color (BLUE in a blue color); and (c) control condition—30+ trials with rectangles printed in one of four colors (red, green, blue, or black). Prior to the actual trials, participants received three short blocks of approximately six trials apiece for practice.

## Appendix G

## Item Discrimination Index

The item discrimination index was computed using a Pearson Product Moment correlation between student responses to a particular item and the overall test score (i.e., point-biserial correlation). For an item that is highly discriminating, as expected, learners who responded to the item correctly tended to do well on the test, and the learners who responded to the item incorrectly tended to do poorly on the overall test (Attali & Fraenkel, 2000). The higher the correlation, the more the item results are consistent with the test as a whole. In practice, item discrimination is considered “good” if the index is above 0.30; “fair” if it is between 0.10 and 0.30; and “poor” if it is below 0.1 (Understanding item analyses, 2020).

## Assessment of Common Method Bias

We took several approaches to reduce the common method bias. These included appropriate instrument design and data collection procedures, as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003). First, we controlled for method biases through procedural remedies by separating the measurement of predictors and dependent variables (Podsakoff et al., 2003). We used different measures and response formats for the independent and dependent variables (i.e., self-reported responses to measure the determinants of IS learning outcomes; neuropsychological tasks to measure executive functions; and objective multiple-choice questions to measure declarative knowledge). Using different types of scales and/or tests should reduce the perceived relevance of the previously recalled information in memory (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

We also performed several statistical remedies to reduce common method bias as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003). First, we performed Harman’s single-factor test. It requires conducting an exploratory factor analysis of all measures used in the study. If common method bias exists, a single factor accounting for the majority of the covariance among the measures will emerge (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The test indicated that the first (largest) factor accounted for only 24.54% of the total variance in Study 1 and 23.49% of the total variance in Study 2, showing no indication of the existence of common method bias. Harman’s method bias has been criticized for having insufficient sensitivity to detect a moderate or small level of the common bias effect (Podsakoff et al., 2003). For this reason, we also performed a marker-variable test (Lindell & Whitney, 2001) in Study 1. Although we did not gather marker variables in Study 2, we collected data over two time periods, as described in the “Participants and Data Collection” Section of Study 2. Temporal separation of survey administration reduces common method bias concerns (see Podsakoff et al., 2003).

## Marker Variable Technique (Study 1)

The marker variable technique requires the inclusion of a variable that is theoretically unrelated and dissimilar to other variables in the model. Because the marker variable is assumed to have no relationship with single or multiple variables in the study, common method bias can be assessed based on the correlation between the marker variable and the theoretically unrelated variable (Lindell & Whitney, 2001). “Honesty” was employed as a theoretically unrelated variable. Honesty was measured by the statement “I would never lie to people.” The results (se Table J1) showed no high correlations between the marker and the principal variables. Thus, we conclude that common method bias was not a serious concern.
