## Abstract

Infomediaries such as online travel agencies (OTAs) and review aggregator websites are profoundly changing the structure of the hospitality industry with consequences in the mechanisms of creation and capture of economic value for hotels. By drawing on arguments from resource-based views, transaction cost economics and industrial organisations, we look inside these mechanisms by assessing whether in regard to creation and capture of economic value hotels should pay more attention to diversify the number of OTAs that distribute their services or to improve their reputation through travellers’ reviews posted on TripAdvisor. Gathering data on 62,865 travellers’ reviews on TripAdvisor and on the presence of hotels on multiple OTAs, we built a panel dataset of 355 hotels spanning from 2004 to 2014 and also including hotels’ financial data. Fixed effects regression models are listed on multiple OTA bolsters sales revenue and operating profitability. We demonstrated that for hotels, the online visibility on OTAs counts more on value capture than the online reputation gained on TripAdvisor. Through the presence on multiple OTAs, hotels are expected to cover a presence on multiple market segments and have a higher bargaining power towards each individual distributor. We discuss the implications of these results on the way hotels, OTAs and TripAdvisor can generate and capture value in the vertical chain of the travel industry.

Keywords: Value creation; value capture; OTAs; hotels; online customer reviews.

## 1. Introduction

In the last decade, Internet-related technologies have enabled radical transformations in the structures of many industries [1], causing the rise of new rules and roles (i.e. industry architectures) that circumscribe the division of labour and the distribution of profits along a value chain [2]. In this scenario, the hospitality industry is one of the settings where changes enabled by the Internet are more visible [3]. New mechanisms of intermediation over the Internet are marginalising the role of traditional ‘bricks-and-mortar’ travel agencies and the crowd-sourcing logic through which travellers experiences are created and shared through online reviews and recommendation algorithms [4] and have led to declining sales for publishers of touristic guides such as Lonely Planet [5]. Furthermore, compared with other industries, the hospitality sector has traditionally been a forerunner in experiencing the transition from the physical to the Internet channel for managing customer relationships. Phenomena such as dynamic pricing, infomediation [6], online reservations and recommendation systems have first taken place in this industry and then have been diffused in other sectors of retail and services, enabling changes in business models, competitive strategies and customers’ behaviours. Therefore, studying how the Internet changes value creation mechanisms in the hotel industry can generate relevant and generalisable implications even for other industries.

#

Two typologies of actors have emerged in the infomediation between the end customers and hotels, and they both create economic value by managing information about travellers and hotels (henceforth they are indicated as infomediaries): review aggregators of travel-related contents such as TripAdvisor and Trivago and online travel agencies (OTAs) such as Booking.com and Expedia. OTAs have replaced traditional travel agencies offering customers a broad scope of booking services related to their travels (e.g. hotels, car rental, flights). Today OTAs manage the majority of hotel reservations that are made online [7]. Online travel-related content websites allow travellers to share their experiences and express their recommendations about hotels, destinations and point of interests, and they play a crucial role in forming hotels’ online reputation by leveraging the electronic word of mouth of the ‘crowd’ of travellers. In the case of TripAdvisor, the crowd is constituted by more than 44 million members who have posted more than 100 million reviews. In addition, online travel-related content websites have been able to move further out the frontier among two conflicting dimensions of information like reach and richness [8]. A broader reach of customers can now access at a lower search cost richer information that is customised to their preferences and interests about destinations and hotels.

The two stages of infomediation have a high market concentration because of entry barriers such as network externalities and the high degree of capital intensity and scale economies existing in data management [9]. Referring to recent approaches in industrial organisation that look at the ‘industry architecture’ concept [10], OTAs and users’ review aggregators increasingly play a crucial role in creating economic value in the hospitality industry and in shaping the rules of competition. Specifically, infomediaries assume the role of the least replaceable players and of the ‘guardian of quality’ in the value chain, and they bring more competition for hotels because of the reduction in the search costs for the end customers. Infomediaries thus create value by certifying quality [11] and by favouring lower prices and larger sales volume.

In this scenario, managing online visibility on the new infomediation platforms to attract and retain more travellers is a critical factor for hotels’ profitability. OTAs and review aggregators reduce travellers’ search costs by ranking hotels by price or quality and by bringing more competition among hotels. In this way, they are becoming critical players and online visibility on these platforms is becoming a competitive necessity for hotels. This equates to a redistribution of economic power towards both digital platforms and travellers. The former can capture up to 25% of the price of a room in the form of an intermediation fee charged by OTAs and shared with review websites that convey Internet traffic to them. For customers, the value captured is in lower prices and richer information to guide their decisions about where to go.

From an economic standpoint, hotels face the challenge of capturing the economic value created by the new intermediation mechanisms over the Internet [12]. This challenge consists in balancing a tension between preserving their unit profit margin and pursuing sales growth through a higher and better online visibility. On the one hand, by being visible on the new intermediation platforms, smaller hotels can increase their revenue and their occupancy rates by accessing complementary assets in customer relationship management that can hardly develop on their own given their small size, their weak brand and the limited competencies in web marketing. On the other hand, the increased revenue coming from a better online visibility on infomediation platforms may not necessarily turn out into a greater net profitability for hotels as shown by recent studies [13], given their reduced market power and their reduction in unit profit margin rate due to the commissions paid to OTAs. For small hotels, the intricacies of this challenge lie in the complexity of improving their visibility on the Internet and leveraging it to attract more profitable customers. This activity requires a radical change in marketing competencies because of the ecosystem of visibility on the Internet requires handling new activities (such as search engine optimisation) and new business metrics and is made by a complex array of actors such as search engines, infomediaries, social media, consumer communities and web marketing agencies [14]. Internet presence through a corporate web site and search engines is not sufficient for being visible and attracting profitable customers. Specifically, hotels may use new mechanisms to communicate the value of their product, which in the case of small firms is often based on idiosyncratic features for which the superior quality may not be easily appreciated and assessed online.

The primary aim of this paper is to understand how smaller hotels should manage their online visibility to avoid situations in which the economic value generated is primarily captured by infomediation platforms and does not bolster their net profitability. Although many recent studies have shed light on the mechanisms through which user-generated recommendations generate consumers’ trust and drive sales [15], there is still lack of theoretical and empirical understanding of the conditions under which hotels can capture more economic value through the new mechanisms of intermediation, considering the fact that intermediation occurs in the two separate stages of the value chain described above. First, there is the stage where travellers check quality and the features offered by a hotel, which is primarily controlled by platforms that manage user-generated recommendation systems, such as TripAdvisor. This is the stage where a hotel’s reputation about quality and customer services is formed. Second, there is the stage where customers make an online reservation for a hotel, which is controlled by OTAs. From a managerial perspective, our focus on these two stages sheds light on which online activities hotel’s managers should invest more: either on building commercial relationships with a broad range of OTAs to increase their bargaining power towards this layer of distributors and sell more capacity online or on trying to improve their online reputation and customers’ willingness to pay through better and more customer reviews. This becomes relevant and less straightforward from a theoretical standpoint given the rapid evolution of the Internet ecosystem. In this regard, a crucial point are the mounting concerns about the authenticity of some reviews available on TripAdvisor [16] and the opacity of the popularity algorithms through which hotels are made visible on this platform [17].

We address this problem by reconciling different theoretical views on value creation and capture. First, the concept of ‘industry architecture’ in industrial organisation [10] is used to understand the mechanisms and the economic forces [12] used by infomediation platforms for capturing economic value. Second, we draw on transaction cost economics [18] to identify the conditions in which hotels can increase their bargaining power with infomediation platforms and reduce their specific investments on a certain platform. Third, we use resource-based arguments [19] to understand the dimensions in a hotel’s online visibility on infomediation platforms that are more rare, valuable and costly to imitate, and we sketch out how these dimensions of transparency affect competition dynamics. In this regard, a novelty aspect brought by our study is our focus on hotel’s profitability as the dependent variable through which we observe value capture. Past studies have investigated the effect of online reviews on consumers’ purchasing choices, on their willingness to pay or on sales, leaving unexplored the issue of firms’ ability to capture value from consumer reviews in terms of increased profit. The issue whether online visibility is beneficial from hotels’ profitability assumes importance because of the commission fees they pay to infomediaries and because managing online visibility may require new resources and competencies that can entail additional fixed costs for hotels. In a nutshell, we want to weigh the benefits and the costs of online visibility by understanding whether the increase in revenue it may generate outweighs the growth in unit variable costs in and fixed expenses. Drawing on these theoretical arguments and these empirical data, we demonstrate that a

#

hotel’s presence on a multiplicity of OTAs has a positive and more salient effect on value capture that the dimensions of their visibility on consumer review websites.

We chose the setting of Italian hotels for investigating this topic because it offers some unique points of interest. Italy is the fifth largest destination for international tourism [16], because of its competitive advantage in cultural heritage, landscape beauty and food. These elements allow Italy to attract each year around 50 millions of tourists, mainly from abroad [20], although the structure of its local tourism industry is dominated by small hotels and bed and breakfast structures (B&Bs). On one hand, small hotels’ and B&Bs’ value proposition is more focussed compared with large international chains on giving a ‘true Italian touch’ to customers, but on the other hand, they might have a weaker ability to capture the economic value that stems from online visibility despite their more critical need for these services because of the absence of a strong brand. Given the prominence of tourism for the Italian economy (it counts for about 8.6% of the national GDP), the hotels’ limited ability to capture the economic value brought by information is relevant at the country level as it may be reflected in a reduction of the hospitality industry’s capacity to contribute to the national wealth. The relevance of this issue in Italy is well reflected in the ‘battle’ between Federalberghi, the national trade associations of hoteliers and restaurants, and OTAs and TripAdvisor in front of the national competition authority. In this regard, in 2015, the Italian competition authority has identified a restriction of competition in the clauses enforced by Booking.com and Expedia in their contracts with hotels that impede hotels to offer their services through online travel agencies or any other booking channel (including their own websites) at lower prices or better conditions than those offered on the two infomediation platforms. In the same year, competition authorities in France and Sweden have conducted similar probes towards OTAs. In 2014, the Italian authority has also fined TripAdvisor for failing to do enough to prevent misleading and false reviews [16]. In 2015, the Rome administrative court has cancelled the fine, overturning the ruling of competition authority [21].

To investigate the value creation and value capture mechanisms from a hotel point of view, we developed a dataset on a panel of 355 hotels, observing hotels’ online visibility on TripAdvisor and on the main 13 OTAs entered in the Italian market in over a period of 10 years. This dataset puts together detailed information about the characteristics of hotels’ customer online reviews, hotels’ reply to online customer review, hotels’ listings on the main OTAs, and data on revenue and operating profit performance ranging from 2004 to 2014.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The first section presents the theoretical background for understanding how infomediation platforms and hotels share the economic value. We then propose and test the hypotheses on how hotels might use infomediation platforms to capture economic value. Based on these findings, we conclude by linking back to the literature, outlining limitations, identifying avenues for future research and discussing the implications for theory and practices, and their generalisability to other sectors that are shaped by infomediation mechanisms similar to the ones of the hospitality industry.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1 Sources of value creation in infomediation

In the infomediation stage of the tourism industry economic, value is created in two stages. In the first stage, value is cocreated by travellers (i.e. the crowd) when they provide ratings, reviews and other contents (such as pictures) on review aggregator websites such as TripAdvisor and Trivago. Aggregators’ contribution to such cocreation of value is based on integrating travellers’ reviews and ratings and drawing on these data providing ranking of visibility for hotels, restaurants and destinations in general that are based on what ‘real travellers are saying’. In general, the positive effect of online consumer review in guiding customers’ purchasing decisions has been demonstrated in a variety of industries (for a review see [22]) and has been shown to be particularly effective for sales growth for experiential goods and in the presence of weak brands [23]. In this sense, consumer reviews act as a substitutive mechanism to the one played by brand in building consumer’s trust, in helping firms to play effective differentiation strategies, and in mitigating the effects of information asymmetries between the firm and the customer about the actual quality of the product [24].

By nature of how their ranking algorithms works—through quantification and aggregation—review aggregator websites aim at creating superior economic value by presenting their rankings as an objective fact that reflects the wisdom of the crow and by controlling the customer experience as much as possible. For example, TripAdvisor’s slogan is ‘Get the Truth, Then Go’ and two critical factors for building trust among the users are as follows: (i) soliciting travellers to provide reviews that are accurate, readable, useful and fair and (ii) detecting fake reviews. In this direction, TripAdvisor uses social media mechanisms—such as giving reviewers the possibility to disclose their Facebook identity—to mitigate the tendency of people to provide offensive graffiti-like statements [25] and allow travellers to decide upon the recommendations provided by their friends or by people with similarity in age, interests and social relations. Its mobile application and the GPS data collected by smartphones allow TripAdvisor to further control and support customer experience as through localisation data of travellers, TripAdvisor provides context-aware recommendations of bars, restaurants and point of interest. In so doing, TripAdvisor orchestrates travellers’ touristic experience.

The main effect of the mechanisms enacted by user-generated reviews is a shift in power, influence and economic control from producers of touristic services such as hotels and restaurants to consumers and to the platforms that orchestrate such contents. Power for travellers lies in the fact that reviews support their purchasing decisions [26, 27] and help them in reducing uncertainty among touristic services by accessing more transparent information about quality, price and service features. For TripAdvisor, the power lies in capacity to impose ‘its way of seeing without being able to be seen [17] (p. 20) as its ranking algorithm is kept confidential. As for other online services (similar to the ones provided by Google), the ranking algorithms of TripAdvisor ‘don’t just search and sort reality, they also create it’ [17] (p.25), thereby contributing to the development of a ‘black box of algorithmic culture’ [28] where activities such as booking a room and detecting service failures are no longer under the full control of travellers and hotels, respectively.

Following this shift of power, TripAdvisor assumes the role of providing the best information and guarantee of quality for hotels and destinations [15]. It plays this role in many ways, such as asking travellers to express accurately their ratings and reviews on a number of categories of hospitality such as location, sleep quality, room cleanliness, service or by ‘being embedded’ in the touristic experience of travellers through smartphones and tablets, which enables a provision of real-time and contextaware recommendations. This role becomes crucial for capturing economic value. In this vein, [11] shows that for experience goods where global distribution requires capital intensive investments, is influenced by high cultural and geographical distance between consumers and producers, economic value accrues in the hands of distributors when they take the quality guarantor role on behalf of the producers. This is what is happening presently for supermarket chains with their ‘white-labelling’ campaign on wine and other food products [29], or it is what happened in the eighteenth century when British importers assumed increased economic power in the vertical chain of port wine. TripAdvisor assumes a similar role of quality guarantor. As in the case of wine, tourism and hospitality are based

#

on global value chains and attract a great deal of international sales, and cultural and geographic distance hinders customers from easily identifying quality experiences in their purchasing decisions about the touristic destinations to visit and the hotels where to lodge. These characteristics are especially evident for international tourism in a country like Italy that shows a high number of places to visit and many small hotels with weak brand and limited web marketing capability for communicating their value proposition on the Internet to foreign customers.

By acting as an intermediary between touristic service providers and travellers, TripAdvisor generates economic value for both parties. As described above, for users, value is generated in the form of ‘information’ that is presented as objective facts that support their purchasing decisions and that are aimed at reducing its uncertainty about the service (i.e. the type of room) and the seller (i.e. the hotel). For providers of touristic services, value creations stem from generating and redirecting valuable Internet traffic to OTA (such as Expedia, Hotels.com and Booking.com) or to the hotel’s web site (in the case of large hotel groups) where additional information and booking engines support the actual purchase of travel products and services. The second stage of value generation in infomediation thus occurs on OTAs. In the first stage, value is monetised and captured by TripAdvisor through a clickper-through mechanism. In the majority of cases, hotels do not directly bear a cost for their visibility on TripAdvisor, but this cost is directly sustained by OTAs.

In the second stage of infomediation, OTAs create economic value through three mechanisms. First, for travellers, they build assortment of different services such as flight booking, car rental and hotel reservation [30] and offer a ‘one-stop-shopping’ solution through which they can purchase different services. Hotels’ distribution agreements with OTAs usually foresee to sell a given percentage of their rooms on OTAs, usually at discounted rates, in an exchange of an intermediation fee that may depend on the ranking with which they are visible on the OTA’s web platforms. Second, for hotels, OTAs create economic value by increasing their online visibility and allowing reaching travellers with information about their services and prices. A third mechanism is the fact that OTAs may support hotels in their pricing decisions because of their capabilities in applying dynamic pricing schemes that allow hotels to adjust prices to the condition of the demand. This capability—along with OTAs’ competence to attract Internet traffic—allows improving hotels’ room occupancy rates [31]. OTAs’ main revenue mechanism is an intermediaton fee charged to hotels to which they sell their room, whereas the main external cost is related to pay user-generated review aggregators to bring Internet traffic to their web sites.

## 2.2 Mechanisms of value capture in infomediation

As described in economics and strategic management literature, value capture is a distinct economic process from value generation [12] and refers to a firm’s capacity to retain the economic value that can be generated by providing a certain task. From a hotels’ perspective, capturing value from an increased online visibility on OTAs and review aggregators equates to improving its operating profit position from being simply more visible on the Internet.

At a general level, Lepak et al. [12] described how competition in the supply explains how value slips away from a firm to be shared with other competitors and customers and allows the society as whole to benefit by keeping prices low. In the case of infomediation for hospitality services, the competition mechanism occurs as OTAs put hotels in a situation of strong price competition among them by giving customers the possibility to compare and sort hotels by price. In other words, given the high price competition brought by TripAdvisor and OTAs, the value generated by hotels for providing their hospitality services is shared with such infomediation platforms, online visibility being a strategic

#

necessity [32] for hotels to attract customers, travellers who enjoy lower prices and the society. More concretely, the society may equate with local territories as lower prices applied by hotels attract higher touristic flows, thereby producing a positive impact on other local service business such as restaurants, shops and transportation services. TripAdvisor and Airbnb use a similar argument of value shared with the society when they claimed that in many cities with expensive average prices, their capability to provide lower prices extends the average number of days tourists spend in the city [33–35].

A second general mechanism that explain value capture are isolating mechanisms that can create barriers that may prevent replication of the value-creating task (infomediation in our case) by a competitor. In the case of infomediation for hospitality services, there are many barriers that impede hotels or new entrants to replace the role of OTAs and TripAdvisor. First, their brand is well known among travellers, who increasingly associate their role as the one of ‘quality guardian’ [10]. Second, network effects and two-sided market dynamics [36] prevent hotels and travellers from migrating to other platforms. Third, their algorithms for the popularity index are kept confidential and are continuously refined, and this prevents new entrants from an easy replication of their quality certification capabilities. Finally, by managing online reservations OTAs have an information advantage over hotels in managing loyalty campaigns, in creating special offers and in segmenting effectively their customer’s base for which they can accumulate knowledge that is more valuable.

In the perspective of hotels, given the high market power exerted by review aggregators and OTAs, value capture can stem from increasing the travellers’ willingness to pay and their unit profit margins. As suggested by the previous studies [13, 32, 37], this can occur when firms can differentiate their products through online reviews. This is the case of hospitality services, which can be differentiated both vertically (a five-star hotel provides higher quality service levels than a two-star B&B) and horizontally. In this regard, the Internet and user-generated reviews can help hotels to provide more detailed and accurate information on their service attributes and their unique features, thereby allowing travellers to easily search for the hotel that best fits their preferences, needs and desires. In light of richer information, travellers’ willingness to pay for hotels able to differentiate their hospitality services may increase, and hotels’ profit margins. For example, a family with small kids is likely to pay a premium price for hotels’ amenities and services for children and babies, a young couple is willing to pay more for hotels with spa and romantic atmosphere rooms, or a person travelling for business is typically not interested in hotels with no hi-speed wi-fi connectivity in rooms. Recommendations based on customer reviews can help hotels to reinforce their unique market positioning and their horizontal differentiation because online reviews can reduce service uncertainty for risk-adverse customers, particularly when reviews come from consumers with similar interests and risk attitudes [37]. As stated above, TripAdvisor allows this mechanism by using social networking data from Facebook.

## 2.3 Research focus

Given the importance of value capture mechanisms, our aim is to understand how hotels can drive value their way when they manage their Internet visibility on infomediation platforms. As shown in Figure 1, we explore the linkage between their online visibility and their capacity to create and capture economic value. In this vein, the choice of the dependent variables on which hotels’ online visibility can exert its effect is quite straightforward. Coherently, with similar studies on the effectiveness of online reviews [32] to analyse value creation, we look at hotels’ revenue growth rate, which is expected to benefit from hotels’ access to OTAs’ capability to adjust prices dynamically and to sell their idle capacity. To assess hotels’ capability to capture value, we look at the operating profitability in the form of the hoteliers’ return on sales (ROS). As mentioned in the formula below, the ROS encompasses the multiple effects that hoteliers may enact if they leverage their visibility on OTAs and TripAdvisor. These effects can be given as follows:

1. The capability to increase customers’ willingness to pay by applying differentiation strategies on the offering. This may be reflected in an increase of price from p to $p ^ { : }$ ’.

2. The capability to exploit visibility on multiple OTAs to increase sales volume from $q$ to $q ^ { : }$ , despite the price increase.

3. The capability to bargain more favourable commission fees through visibility on multiple OTAs, which can allow to decrease or keep the ratio of unit variable costs to price (uvc/p) constant.

4. Their capability to manage online presence without any significant increase in their fixed costs (FC).

If most of these conditions hold true, then, ceteribus paribus, hoteliers should improve their ROS, as mentioned below.

$$
R O S = \text { Operating   Profit / Sales   Revenue } = [ (p - u v c) ^ {*} q - F C ] / (p * q) = 1 - u v c / p - F C / p * q,
$$

ROS in case hoteliers capture the economic value of online visibility:

$$

$$

Coherently, with the two stages of infomediation described above, we look at hotels’ online visibility in two terms. First, we take into consideration the multiplicity of OTAs through which hotels are listed and sell their services, given our focus on assessing a hotels’ capability to contrast OTA’s market power and their bargaining power in applying high intermediation fees.

The second variable of hotels’ online visibility is related to their visibility on review aggregator websites, given its importance in sustaining hotels’ differentiation strategies. In this direction, we consider TripAdvisor because it is the dominant platform in aggregating travellers’ reviews, and in accordance with recent studies [22], we look at multiple dimensions of hotels’ online visibility on this platform. Our aim is not to look at which of these dimensions counts more in driving sales growth, because this topic has been extensively theorised and empirically explored. Rather, our aim is to weigh the importance of these attributes in the overall hotel’s strategy of online presence. Based on the way (described above) the value chain in infomediation works, the crucial assumption of our analysis is that consumer reviews on TripAdvisor may sustain hotels in playing a game of differentiation and increasing customers’ willingness to pay, thereby helping hotels to improve their marginal profit margins. Instead, visibility of hotels on multiple OTAs may allow them to increase sales volume and to obtain more favourable commission fees.

To disentangle the mechanisms through which consumer reviews can generate economic value, we look at the following dimensions of hotels’ visibility on TripAdvisor:

1. The volume of reviews and their average ratings (valence), which are expected to reduce customers’ uncertainty in their purchasing decisions.

2. The dispersion of customers’ ratings, which is expected to capture the degree of horizontal differentiation pursued by a hotel [32] because travellers with different interests, preferences

#

and needs may disagree significantly about a hotel’s service compared with the ones supplied by hotels specialised in ‘good enough’ services for a mass market [38].

3. Whether hotels reply to their customer reviews. This practice may have multiple purposes for hotels. It can help them to defend and manage their reputation over the Internet. It can foster their learning in quality management and in the continuous improvement of the services [39]. It may be seen as a lead generation tool to engage prospect or actual customer in a long-term relationship [40] or as a mechanism for communicating their customer responsiveness to external stakeholders [39]. According to industry surveys [41], this practice was rather rare among hoteliers in the first decade years of diffusion of TripAdvisor.

From a theoretical standpoint, the market reputation built through customer reviews and the number of OTAs with which a hotel has signed distribution agreements represent variables over which hotels have a different degree of managerial control, given the prominent roles of TripAdvisor in being ‘quality guardian’ and of the ‘crowd’ in providing the data for quality certification. On the one hand, hotels have limited control in the short term on their reputation on TripAdvisor, apart from deciding whether to be listed or not on this platform. On the other hand, the number of OTAs on which a hotel is listed reflects its deliberate distribution strategy, whereas the number of rooms distributed on these platforms reflects the hotel’s tactical approach about web distribution as the capacity sold on the Internet can be adjusted dynamically depending on the condition of the demand. For example, in a given week, a hotel may decide to increase the number of rooms sold on a certain OTA if the expected occupancy rate is low. From a perspective of how profit is distributed along a value chain, comparing the effects on hotels’ profitability because of their visibility on OTAs and TripAdvisor, respectively, equates to understand which of these two intermediation stages count more in value creation. This is a relevant issue in response to the mounting concern over TripAdvisor’s capacity to bring an objective view about quality [16] and given the decreasing trend in profit and market value shown by this company after 2012.<sup>1</sup>

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1 Multiplicity of OTAs and value creation

Being on multiple OTAs can have several positive effects on the way hotels manage their online visibility and their sales. First, by increasing their presence and visibility on OTAs hotels can increase customers’ awareness on their products [7]. OTAs are channels used for promoting hotels’ facilities and for maintaining continuous relationships with travellers. Therefore, the larger the number of OTAs chosen by hotels, the larger is the number of promotional channels available for their online strategy.

Second, hotels can decide to cooperate with several OTAs [7] in the attempt to have a presence on multiple market segments because some of the more popular OTAs target a well-identified typology of travellers [4]. The majority of OTAs, such as Booking.com and Expedia.com, have a generalist positioning based on targeting all travellers around the world, whereas others such as Venere.com and Agoda.com are focussed on distinct geographical markets, such as Europe (in the case of Venere) and Asia (in the case of Agoda.com).

Third, by differentiating their online presence on multiple OTAs, hotels avoid over-reliance on one preferred distributor [42]. In addition, hotels that collaborate with several OTAs increase their bargaining power for two main reasons: they can decide to withdraw by the agreement with some OTAs without losing the totality of their online booking channel and they have more power in negotiating intermediation fees with each single OTA [43].

Finally, being on multiple OTAs suggests that a hotel may have a well-defined strategy of online presence that is based on having visibility on multiple channels other than OTAs, such as generalist search engines (Google, Yahoo or Bing) and their own web sites. In this vein, Anderson [44] shows that consumers who make reservations on hotels’ website also spend a great deal of time in performing both a search and an OTA visit. This implies that hotels may use OTAs as ‘billboards’ to give a broad visibility to their price and service features and to attract customers directly to their direct reservation channels.

Overall, we expect that hotels that choose to be listed on multiple OTAs have more advantages in terms of market coverage and room occupancy rates compared with those that choose to be on one or few of them. Therefore, we expect the following relationship.

H1. The number of OTAs on which a hotel is listed is positively correlated with its revenue growth.

## 3.2 Multiplicity of OTAs and value capture

From a market transaction cost perspective [18], a hotel’s presence on multiple OTAs may also have advantages on value capture and their profit margins because it reduces their overreliance and their bargaining vulnerability towards each single OTA. More specifically, hotels on multiple OTAs may face a lower hold-up risk [42]. Furthermore, as mentioned above, hotels on multiple OTAs, being more visible, are in more favourable position for driving customers towards reserving rooms over their direct channels [31]. Therefore, being on multiple OTAs puts hotels in a more favourable position to disintermediate OTAs. Similarly, hotels may shift part of their capacity of room available for sale over the Internet from a distributor to another one depending on the one who can promise a better sell-out at the lowest intermediation fee. The higher bargaining power of hotels may thus turn out into lower intermediation fee paid to OTAs. Therefore, we can hypothesise the following.

H2. The number of OTAs on which a hotel is listed is positively correlated with its operating profitability growth.

## 3.3 Visibility on user-generated review websites

Online consumer review has been demonstrated to exert a positive role in driving customers’ choices, in particular for the effect of review valence. In this regard, Wu et al. [37] offer an explanation demonstrating that a higher product review valence can have a positive impact on customers’ willingness to pay, regardless of their risk attitude towards product uncertainty, whereas higher review volume and variance do not increase a purchase likelihood for customers that are risk-neutral or riskaverse towards uncertainty. In a similar way, hotels’ responses to customer reviews may be not a decisive element for guiding customers’ purchasing decision because it may fail in mitigating information asymmetries and the uncertainty about quality levels in the relationship between hoteliers and customers. Based on these considerations, we may posit the following.

H3. Review valence has a more salient effect on revenue and operating profitability growth than other dimensions of review visibility (i.e. review volume, review variance, hotel responses).

#

Despite the centrality of review valence in guiding customers’ purchasing decisions, this variable may be losing part of its nature as a differentiating asset because over time, the ratings of online reviews are becoming concentrated around high scores [34] and they have thus a low discriminatory power in guiding customer decisions. From a strategic standpoint, resource-based view [19] may indicate that review volume and valence are losing its nature as a differentiating asset for hotels because of the large diffusion of hotels with high ratings and a high flow of reviews. This can be the consequence of the way the market ‘has adjusted’ its equilibrium in response to the greater transparency and increased customer power brought by TripAdvisor. In this market scenario, hotels response could have been an increase in their service levels and in their capability to manage their reputation on TripAdvisor, and customer’s response could have been to discard hotels with low rankings, thus opting for hotels with higher quality that tend to become more visible and attract more customers and more reviews. These dynamics could have brought more competition among a more concentrated tier of hoteliers that are capable to provide high quality in terms of cleanliness, room comfort and customer responsiveness in general. These dynamics of hypercompetition have been shown to be common in other information intensive sectors as a consequence of ICT-related innovation [13, 45]. In this scenario of hypercompetition, high review valence and volume are thus no longer rare and costly to imitate resources.

A second argument in favour of the decreasing discriminatory power of review valence stems from the increasing concern over TripAdvisor’ capability to detect and block fake and misleading reviews [16] has negative consequences on the economic value that reviews can generate by supporting travellers’ decisions about where to reserve a room.

For these reasons, TripAdvisor and, by reflection, recent studies are paying a lot of attention on the importance of finer-grained dimensions of reviews, such as their descriptive accuracy [46] and their readability [47], and even on reviewers’ attributes, such as whether their identity is disclosed or not [48].

These elements suggest that aggregate measures about how the crowd of travellers expresses their reviews are not becoming decisive to guide customers’ purchasing decisions online, and consequently hotels’ performance. In a resource-based perspective [19], a high review valence is not a rare and costly-to-imitate resource and may not denote some hotel’s rare capabilities in providing a unique customer experience. In addition, hotels cannot directly control these aggregate metrics in the short term as they can do for the number of rooms and the prices listed on OTAs. As such, to be effective, hotels’ managerial action may be more concentrated on decisions about the capacity of rooms distributed on OTAs every week.

Therefore, in light of these considerations, we can assert that the aspect of online visibility that may account more on the value creation opportunities is related to the multiplicity of OTAs chosen by hotels to distribute their rooms rather than to the visibility achieved through their review valence. Thus, we formulated the following hypothesis.

H4. The number of OTAs on which a hotel is listed has a more salient effect on revenue and operating profitability growth than each dimension of its review visibility.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1 Sample and data collection

Our data collection involved a random sample of 355 Italian hotels, extracted from a population of 2,862 small and medium Italian hotels. Hotels not listed on TripAdvisor were excluded from the sample frame. Similarly, we did not consider hotels that had more than 250 employees, given our intention to focus the analysis on small- and medium-sized firms (in accordance with the European Union’s definition) that are expected to have weak brands, limited direct online sales and greater need of the infomediation of digital platforms. All the hotels selected were listed on the AIDA public database (distributed by Bureau Van Dijk), which is the main compendium of financial information of firms in Italy. The median size of the hotels included in the sample is represented by 15 employees (in 2014) and a 1.67 million-euro turnover, thereby confirming the prevalence of small firms over medium-sized ones. More precisely, small hotels (i.e. below 50 employees) represented 89.85% of the sample.

We built a panel dataset that spanned the period between 2004 and 2014. The dataset includes gathered data about online customer reviews from TripAdvisor on an overall amount of 62,865 reviews. Data on reviews were retraced retrospectively during 2014. To do so, we processed the date on which reviews were posted on TripAdvisor. We adopted a similar approach for retracing hotels presence on OTAs because these platforms report reviews from travellers that used the platform to book rooms in the hotel. Such reviews are mainly collected by TripAdvisor and showed by OTAs. We base the analysis over hotels’ presence on OTAs on an array of 13 platforms that were covering the Italian market in 2014. Some of these platforms, such as Expedia, were already in the Italian market since 2004, the beginning of the time horizon covered by our analysis. Instead, between 2006 and 2011 other OTAs entered the market. This reflects well the evolution of the industry environment in which hotels made their strategic choice about online presence and visibility.

## 4.2 Measures

## 4.2.1 Independent variables

Each dimension of hotel visibility on infomediation platforms and performance was considered as a flow variable. This is consistent with the panel nature of our dataset and also with the importance that the dynamics of online consumer review have on sales [49]. In the same way, the decisions of hoteliers to adjust their capacity sold on OTAs to the demand conditions can have a crucial impact on their sales and profitability.

OTAs’ multiplicity. The variable represents the number of OTAs over which a hotel is listed and sells rooms out of a list of 13 players that cover Italian hotels.<sup>2</sup> This list covers both well-known brands such as Expedia or Booking that sells many travel-related contents and also players that are more focussed either on a geographical area of touristic inflows or on hotel reservation.

Review variance. It expresses the dispersion of customer ratings on a five-point scale. It expresses the level of disagreement or heterogeneity among customers’ evaluations over the quality of a hotel services. According to the previous studies, it captures the degree of horizontal differentiation in a hotel offer.

#

Review valence. This variable represents the average online rating for each year for each hotel written on TripAdvisor by travels. Travellers’ rate is given on a five-point scale, whose value anchors are ‘terrible’, ‘poor’, ‘average’, ‘very good’ and ‘excellent’.

Review volume. This variable represents the number of reviews (in logarithmic form) written on TripAdvisor by travellers in a given year.

Hotel responses. This variable considers the number of replies provided by hotelier to online customer reviews in a given year.

## 4.2.2 Dependent variables

Dependent variables refer to the value creation and capture capacities of hotels in managing their online visibility. As stated above, the value creation is measured with the revenue growth and the value capture with the operating profitability growth.

Revenue growth. This variable is computed by the annual logarithmic growth of the sales revenue for each hotel (i) and for each year (t) by the following equation: Revenue growth<sub>i</sub> = Log [Rev<sub>i</sub> (t)/Rev<sub>i</sub> (t-1)].

Operating profitability growth. We measured hotel operating profitability in terms of ROS, i.e. operating income on sales revenue. This variable is operationalised by considering the difference between the value recorded in the t-th year and the value registered in the former year by the following equation: Operating profitability growth = ROS (t) − ROS (t-1).

## 4.2.3 Control variables

Years. Nine dummy variables (from 2006 to 2014) are included in the models. We added these variables as controls for economic cycles (and thus the shift to a recession phase occurring in 2008).

ROS. The one-year-lagged value of the ROS is included in the models where the dependent variable is the ‘operating profitability growth’ for controlling the value of the dependent variable (in this case, the ROS) of the hotel in time t-1.

Revenues. The one-year-lagged value of the logarithmic form of sales revenue is included in every model for controlling for the size of the hotel (revenues is a proxy of the hotel size).

## 5 Findings

## 5.1 Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics (Table 1) show some interesting facts about how online visibility works for hotels. First, the number of OTAs is positively and significantly correlated with review valence, review volume and hotel responses. This somehow corroborates the idea that review aggregators and OTAs are intertwined stages of the infomediation value chain and that hotels tend to exploit a greater and better visibility on TripAdvisor through the presence on multiple OTAs. Second, review volume and valence are positively and significantly correlated. The reason might be that hotels with higher valence tend to attract more customers, who, in turn, by writing opinions about their experience, increase the volume of reviews. Review variance is positively correlated with valence, showing that hotels where travellers express higher ratings are also the ones where on average customers show a higher disagreement. This corroborates the idea that review variance captures the differentiation nature

#

of hospitality services. Finally, the time variable influences online visibility. The number of OTAs is significantly and positively correlated with the year variable, highlighting that over the years, the hotels increasingly decided to be present on multiple OTAs. Clearly, time has also a positive effect on the annual volume of reviews received by hotels, confirming the increasing popularity of TripAdvisor among travellers. Over time, review valence is increasing and variance is decreasing, as shown in Table 2. In addition, the percentage of reviews with ratings higher than 3 has been increasing over time. As stated above, this may be the consequence of the fact that hotels have been learning how to manage effectively online visibility and improve the services given the increased market transparency brought by TripAdvisor.

Lastly, there is also an important time effect over the number of OTAs (Table 3) on which hotels have been listed. This number shows an acceleration of this number after 2012. In fact, until 2010, the presence on multiple OTAs was a quite rare condition in the sample, with more that 25% of the hotels not listed on OTAs (first quartile of the OTAs’ number was 0) and about 50% of the sample listed on one or two platforms (third quartile of the OTAs’ number equal to three).

## 5.2 Hypotheses validation

To validate the hypotheses, we ran 11 fixed effects regression models with robust standard errors controlling for year fixed effects. In the model specifications, the variables measuring the number of OTAs, the variance, the valence, the volume of online customer reviews and hotel responses were lagged for 1 year to assess whether the value of the variable in a certain year (year t-1) leads to performance improvements in the subsequent year (year t).

For each model, a Hausman specification test established the appropriateness of a fixed effects model over a random effects model, because estimates from the random effects model were not consistent. Fixed effect models thus allowed to control unobservable variables of hotels such as star rating, position, brand and probably also managerial capabilities. Such capabilities are likely to be time invariant because small hotels usually experience a limited turnover in the management team. Although their learning on managing Internet visibility may have evolved over time, their learning capability over these technologies (which had radical improvements in the period observed) is expected to be essentially time invariant.

To deal with potential multicollinearity problems because of the high correlation between the variables of review visibility, we tested each of their effects first separately and then in a comprehensive model specification that included all the dimensions of hotels visibility on OTAs and TripAdvisor. The most comprehensive model specifications did not report issues of multicollinearity. In such models, each predictor had adequate VIF (between 1.078 and 2.835), well below the suggested threshold of 10 (Greene, 2003).

Table 4 shows the estimates of the models. In hypothesis H1, we posited that the number of OTAs over which a hotel is listed has a positive impact on its revenue growth. Model 5, which represents the complete specifications of effects that we want to estimate, provides evidence in support of this hypothesis, showing a positive and significant coefficient for the number of OTAs. The positive and significant effect of the number of OTAs on revenue growth emerges when review variance and valence are included as separate effects in the analysis (models 1 and 2, respectively).

In hypothesis H2, we postulated a positive impact of the presence of the hotel on multiple OTAs on operating profitability growth. Model 10 supports this hypothesis because the contribution of the variable that refers to the number of OTAs on the operating profitability growth is positive and statistically significant. This effect is also confirmed when the effects due to the dimensions of review visibility are tested separately (models 6, 7, 8 and 9).

In hypothesis H3, we claimed that review valence has the most salient effect on hotel revenue and operating profitability growth among the dimensions of review visibility considered. In model 2, review valence has a positive and significant effect over revenue growth. In addition, the other dimensions of review visibility have no significant effects over the dependant variables, apart from the negative effect of review variance on revenue growth (model 1) and the positive effect of review volume on operating profitability growth (model 8). To have a further validation of the hypothesis, in the two-model specifications (models 5 and 10), including all the dimensions of review visibility, we performed Wald tests to compare the magnitude of the coefficients of review valence with the ones related to the other dimensions of review visibility. We did not find systematic evidence of differences in the magnitude of these effects, apart from the most salient effect of review valence on review variance when the impacts on revenue growth are considered (model 5). Based on these results, we can conclude that hypothesis H3 is not confirmed. Because of the growth in review valence and the decrease in review variance shown in the sample and generally reported by hotels on TripAdvisor in the last years, we decided to restrict the time frame of the panel analysis to assess whether review valence lost importance in influencing hotel performance in the last years of the time horizon observed. Model 11 shows that such effect was positive and significant over operating profitability growth in the time interval between 2004 and 2012.

In hypothesis H4, we posited that the number of OTAs over which a hotel is listed has a more salient effect on revenue and operating profitability growth than the dimensions of review visibility. In reference to model M10, we found that the magnitude of the coefficient of the OTAs’ number is statistically and significantly higher than the magnitudes of the coefficients associated with review variance, review valence, review volume and hotel responses. This result supports hypothesis H4 for the effects of hotel online visibility on operating profitability growth. This result is also confirmed when the effects due to the dimensions of review visibility are tested separately with the exception for review volume (see models 6, 7, 8 and 9). Instead, when we consider the effects on revenue growth, Wald tests indicate a significant difference among the magnitude of the coefficients only for the OTAs’ number over the review variance (see models 1 and 5).

In sum, these results indicate that the number of OTAs through which hotels distribute their rooms has a greater importance than their review visibility and that also review valence, which is shown by previous studies of the most influential dimension of consumer review on performance, has no significant effect. Figure 2 reports a graphical interpretation of the effect of the OTAs’ number on changes in ROS based on the estimates produced by the regression models. Controlling for time and time-invariant effects, we can say that hotels are expected to see an increase on their operating profitability when they are listed at least on three OTAs. Descriptive statistics reported in Table 3 show that this was a quite rare condition involving a limited number of hotels in the period analysed.

## 6 Discussion and conclusions

In this article, we explored how hotels can drive value their way when they manage their visibility on infomediation platforms. This topic assumes relevance in response of the shift in market power and economic control from hotels towards travellers and infomediation platforms. For exploring these trends, we drew on transaction cost economics, resource-based view, the concept of changes in industry architecture, and data that correlate actual profit and revenue performance of hotels and their visibility on OTAs and TripAdvisor. Compared with previous studies, we were able to explore the relationship between online visibility and performance by adding two novel points. First, we disentangled the effects due to consumer reviews, which are expected to increase customers’ willingness to pay, from the ones due to the hotels’ presence on multiple online distributors, which are expected to drive sales volume and occupancy rates. Second, we explored how these visibility dimensions impact profitability, which is in general a relevant issue for producers (hoteliers in our case), given the higher market power that infomediaries have been able to exert in the new industry architecture produced by Internet brokerage.

Based on arguments from transaction cost economics, we demonstrated that just few hotels that choose to distribute their rooms on a higher number of OTAs can bolster their sales and their profitability and thus capture more economic value from their visibility on these distribution channels. Instead, visibility on TripAdvisor in the form of higher review ratings (valence), larger review volumes, and higher variance in ratings and hoteliers’ high number to traveller reviews has no salient effect on revenue and profit growth. Despite the importance shown by earlier studies on consumer reviews in driving customers’ purchasing decisions and in increasing their willingness to pay, these results reveal that because of the evolution of the industry architecture of hospitality services, TripAdvisor is weakening this ability to capture value as a ‘guardian of quality’ in the value chain [10]. We posit that it might be consistent with the difficulties shown by TripAdvisor in recent years in preventing misleading reviews and in preserving the discriminatory power of aggregate metrics such as the review valence. In this regard, our data confirm these difficulties, showing that for the hotels in the sample review, valence has increased and variance has decreased over time. This might be the consequence of how infomediation has altered competitive dynamics: hotels may have increased their service levels in response to the higher market transparency and customers may now tend to discard hotels with low rankings, thus opting for hotels for higher quality, which tends to reduce variance. Lastly, aggregate metrics such as review valence and volume (over which TripAdvisor popularity index is built) have lost part of their power in driving hotel sales and profit and supporting hotels to play a game of differentiation and apply a price premium. This insight is reflected in our results that show a more salient effect of review valence over profit before 2013 when ratings were still not particularly concentrated around a high score. This result is consistent with the increasing attention in recent studies and also from TripAdvisor on valuing some other attributes of reviews, such as their accuracy [46] and their readability [47]. These considerations suggest that in resource-based terms, to retain its role as ‘quality guardian’ in the hospitality industry, TripAdvisor should sustain hotels in building the attributes of consumer reviews that reflect rare and costly-to-imitate competitive resources. In addition, the concept of the value created by the quality guardian in the industry architecture suggests that TripAdvisor’s weakened role in quality assurance is related to the necessity to assume a higher share of legal liability if something goes wrong in the customer experience at a hotel [29]. This requires attention on the need for TripAdvisor for envisaging new mechanisms of risk sharing between hotels, travellers and TripAdvisor when something goes wrong in a hotel experience.

#

Taken from hotel perspective, our results have straightforward implications on where hoteliers should put resources for capturing value from their online visibility. Managing multiple agreements with OTAs allows them to reduce vulnerability to OTAs’ unilateral renegotiation of their commission fees or may also allow them to have a broader and more focussed market presence, considering the geographical focalisation that some of the OTAs have. For their review on TripAdvisor, aggregate metrics of visibility or of customer responsiveness (such as replies to customers) are no longer important in attracting customers or in increasing their willingness to pay. In this perspective, our arguments support the idea that OTAs are more effective than TripAdvisor on bringing volume growth and higher occupancy rates because of their market penetration and their dynamic pricing capabilities. In this way, hotels should probably invest their resources for adjusting their capacity on multiple OTAs depending on the demand conditions. This might be consistent with the evolution of the industry environment, which has become more competitive in the distribution phase because of the entry of new platforms and the removal (imposed by competition authorities) of clauses that limited hoteliers’ degree of control over the prices applied on OTAs.

In regard to the limited effects of travellers’ reviews on value capture, our results suggest that to use consumer review to drive higher profit margins, hotels should evolve their practices and strategies on review aggregator websites. Instead of merely encouraging their guests to write reviews, hoteliers should engage customers in writing readable and accurate stories of what they appreciated more of their stay or should direct word of mouth towards prospect customers that are more similar to the guests that expressed high satisfaction. In this regard, hotels, similar to what TripAdvisor is doing, should exploit social networking features to attract customers and reinforce these differentiation strategies.

We believe that these results are also relevant to other industries that like tourism face a global demand for experience goods and where distance (at the cultural, social and geographical level) and lack of skills or knowledge put the customer in a limiting condition for assessing quality. In this setting, producers must be able to exploit the Internet to penetrate new market segments and defend their share of value from specialised Internet distributors that can assume the role of quality guardian.

These arguments reveal that in the new scenario of Internet intermediation, hoteliers are required to develop new competencies that are far from the ones that are traditionally deemed to be ‘core’ for them: analysing web traffic on search engines, tailoring value proposition to more specific customer segments and setting up dynamic pricing procedures are just examples of the required change. This technological change offers Information Systems scholars many directions to investigate acceptance and assimilation of the new instruments of online visibility. In the same way, organisation behaviour scholars can grasp opportunities to investigate how the new infomediaries bring changes in the internal routines and practices of hotels.

In raising this issue, this paper has some limitations. For example, the time frame under analysis should be extended beyond 2014 to consider the changes in the competitive environment brought in Europe by the decisions taken by some national competition authorities towards OTAs and review aggregators in regard to their market transparency and their parity rate clauses in prices. These changes could have weakened the value capture capability of OTAs, and this could even augment the importance of being listed on different OTAs for hotels, given their increased possibilities to apply different prices on each OTA. A further limit could be survivorship bias due to the modality through which we collected data; our sample only includes hotels that were still present on infomediation platforms in 2014. The disruptive nature of the changes brought by the Internet could have caused an exit of some hotels. Another limitation stems, as we repeatedly stated, from the fact that we did not consider finer-grained attributes of reviews as recent studies have done. Lastly, our study has been conducted just on Italy, where local enterprises show a delay in managing Internet presence and ecommerce compared with other advanced nations. Thus, a further contribution could also come from extending our study to other countries.
