# Typing or messaging? Modality effect on deception detection in computer-mediated communication

Lina Zhou <sup>⁎</sup>, Dongsong Zhang

Department of Information Systems, University of Maryland, Baltimore County, 1000 Hilltop Circle, Baltimore, MD 21250, USA

Received 26 December 2005; received in revised form 5 January 2007; accepted 30 March 2007 Available online 6 April 2007

## Abstract

Motivated by the practical significance of deception detection to both organizations and individuals and the recent surge of research interests in deception in computer-mediated communication (CMC), this study examines whether and how the modality of communication media can influence deception detection in CMC. The results of empirical evaluations revealed that the messaging and the chatting modalities were more conducive to deception detection than the typing modality, and the chatting modality is the most preferred for the process of deception detection. Additionally, the effect of message veracity was also investigated. The findings raise broader issues about media properties for deception detection. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Deception detection; Modality; Veracity; Computer-mediated communication; Outcome; Process

## 1. Introduction

Deception, a common phenomenon in human communication, can mislead decision makers to use false information or to ignore useful information by knowingly transmitting information to create a false impression or conclusion [6]. High-stake deception can result in devastating consequences to not only the individual victims of the deception, but also the entire society at large. The inability of detecting deception is a major risk to the success of our society [7].

The proliferation of computer-mediated communication (CMC) in the past decade provides a new venue for deception [70], which drives a stream of recent studies that attempt to identify cues to deception and to explore automated approaches to detecting deception in CMC. CMC may offer more fertile grounds for deception than other types of communication media. Information seekers can “pull” deceptive messages from a Web site rather than have them “pushed” towards them, which not only increases the credibility of deceptive messages but also offers an extended reach to wider audience [32]. Moreover, people can easily falsify their identities in a CMC environment. Many young Internet users encounter unwanted sexual overtures [52]. Consequently, CMC may fundamentally alter the social, legal, and economic distribution of deceptive practices [33]. Therefore, deception detection, a process of differentiating deception from truth, is highly desired to improve the quality and trustworthiness of CMC.

Deception detection, however, is a complex task subject to the interpretation of a variety of contextual factors. Our literature review shows that previous studies on deception and deception detection (e.g., [19,25,65]) have mainly focused on traditional face-to-face communication. Different modalities (e.g., visual and audio) of face-to-face communication media could influence the performance of human deception detection [2,22,37,43,71]. The emergence of interest in deception in CMC (e.g., [12,70]) highlights the unique characteristics of CMC and their potential impact on deception behavior. As today's communication paradigm is shifting from face-to-face to CMC environments, both the effectiveness of cues to deception [70] and the performance of deception detection change accordingly [8,21,39,57]. To the best of our knowledge, there has not been any empirical investigation of the effect of the modality of CMC media on deception detection. In more general terms, structural properties of a communication medium and their impact on deception detection have been overlooked or understudied in the extant literature.

To fill this void in the existing literature, we examined possible effects of the modality of communication media on deception detection in CMC in this study. The differentiation of media modalities is grounded on theoretical models that explain their affordance of different types of behavioral cues, which in turn serve as the antecedents for determining whether a specific textual message is deceptive or not. For example, face-toface communication typically involves verbal, vocal, and visual modes. Accordingly, people can transmit multiple types of behavioral cues to their communication partners via spoken language, expressive tones, and body gestures, which all contribute to the detection of deception [19].

The focus of instant messaging in this study was motivated by its pervasiveness and provision of a rich set of interaction cues [35]. In addition, the timing of messages is associated with significant differences in participants' interpersonal perceptions [62]. Characterized by its spontaneity and instant feedback, instant messaging imitates traditional interpersonal interaction, making the comparison of findings of this study and those of previous studies in face-to-face communication feasible. Based on a behavioral model of cues to deception [68], we decomposed an instant messaging medium into two modalities: typing and messaging. The typing modality refers to the mode in which a message is displayed to the user while it is typed, while the messaging modality refers to the mode in which a sequence of messages is presented in the order of being received. The two modalities differ in their affordance of behavioral cues because the typing modality involves rich non-verbal behavior (e.g., keyboard behavior) but limited verbal behavior (i.e., message content) and interaction context, whereas the messaging modality enables rich verbal behavior and discourse and relational information (e.g., message exchanges), but is relatively poor at transmitting non-verbal behavior. The combination of both constitutes the third modality called chatting modality.

In this study, we investigated deception detection in the above three modalities and message veracity effect on the performance of deception detection. In particular, we examined possible impact of modality and message veracity on the process and outcome of deception detection. The process of deception detection was measured by user satisfaction and perceived usefulness, and the outcome was measured by the accuracy and confidence of detection. This study was carried out through controlled laboratory experiments. The findings of this research offer theoretical contributions and practical implications, as well as insights into structural properties of communication media in general and the impact of media modality on deception detection in specific.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the related work on deception detection, introduce different modalities of instant messaging, and propose hypotheses about the potential effects of modality and message veracity on deception detection. Then, we describe the experimental design and report findings of the study in Sections 3 and 4, respectively, followed by the discussion of the implications of those findings in Section 5. Finally, we conclude the paper with future research in Section 6.

## 2. Background and hypotheses development

Previous research has identified a number of challenges associated with detecting deception. Some are related to human and media factors. Human factors refer to the poor ability of human beings in detecting deception, which is partly caused by their natural truth bias [27,50]. It is one of the well-known biases that affect humans while making a judgment on deception. Media factors have impact on the amount of information being presented and the level of social presence. For example, face-to-face communication tends to accommodate the largest number of social cues, and is typically considered as being high in social presence [51], while CMC media such as instant messaging have fewer social cues and are low in social presence. Thus, the amount of information cues available in a CMC environment that can help people detect deception is much less than that in a face-to-face, non-CMC environment. Media factors can have not only direct influence, but also indirect influence from their interaction with human factors, on deception detection. For example, individuals' personal experience with communication media may make individuals' perception of media to be richer than they really are [12]. Extensive research on channel reliance has demonstrated differences in interpretations of communication encounters associated with utilization of or exposure to various communication modalities, such as text-only, audio-only, video-only, or audio–visual modalities. Contemporary research on CMC further highlights the importance of taking communication modality into account while detecting potentially deceptive messages [8]. Fig. 1 presents our research model. We describe each of the constructs and their relationships to deception detection in the following sections.

## 2.1. Media modality

We define a media modality as a form of a communication medium that transmits distinctive and selfcontained behavioral cues that can be isolated from the medium. Media modalities are particularly relevant to interpersonal perception such as deception detection. Research has shown that communication media have a moderating effect on cues to deception (e.g., [9,57]), as well as a direct [19,71] or moderating effect on human deception detection in face-to-face communication and video conferencing [2,22,37,43]. However, a simple comparison of the results of previous studies on deception detection that consider communication media as a whole will not be able to explain when, how, and why communicators favor one modality over another. To determine what is essential or useful for accomplishing certain communication goals [9], it is imperative to dissect a medium by examining its different modalities. This offers an indispensable dimension for understanding the impact of media properties on deception detection.

Communication media have fixed, structural properties that “afford” or enable people to interact with each other. These affordances include contingency, participation, synchronicity, proximity, and richness of contextual information available in the modality of a communication medium being used [9]. There are different assumptions about communication media modalities in the literature. Some confuse them with the media themselves or with communication channels [44]. Given that the identification of cues to deception, the focus of a large body of deception studies on interpersonal communication, is a precursor of deception detection, we propose that the categorization of modalities should be grounded on theoretical models that are able to explain the affordance of different types of behavioral cues, which may also imply differences in immediacy of feedback and the amount of context provided.

According to a model of deception behavior [68], cues to deception can be broken down into verbal and nonverbal behaviors. The former refers to the spoken or written content and language. The latter includes other non-content properties exhibited when people are speaking or writing, which is further classified into paralinguistic, proxemic (distance), and kinesic (body posture) behavior. Although CMC does not provide any true capabilities of reflecting meaning through kinesic and proxemic features, some paralinguistic cues such as keyboard, participatory, and sequential behavior are available (e.g., in instant messaging). On the one hand, communication via instant messaging shares many characteristics with informal face-to-face communication—being opportunistic, brief, and context-rich [67], making it possible to use previous findings about the effect of modalities of face-to-face communication on deception detection as the baseline in the current study. On the other hand, instant messaging differs from face-to-face communication in terms of proximity, reprocessibility (also called recordability), and the extent of context. For example, people in face-to-face communication have access to visual cues (e.g., facial expression) and high proximity. Those cues are not available in text-based instant messaging. During an instant messaging session, all messages previously exchanged are recorded and made available to all communication partners involved in the session, while in face-to-face communication, people have to memorize previous conversation. The above difference makes it interesting and worthwhile to study instant messaging.

We identified two basic modalities of text-based instant messaging: typing and messaging (see Fig. 2). The typing modality transmits users' real-time messageentering behavior, and the messaging modality transmits and displays all messages previously exchanged by all participants in a session in a sequential order. Additionally, a modality may not be atomic. One modality can contain or be a part of another one. For example, the audio–visual mode consists of audio and visual modes. Thus, we obtained the third modality of instant messaging, the chatting mode, by integrating the typing and messaging modes.

Different media modalities commonly lend themselves to different levels of social presence. Social presence theory predicts that media low in social presence may not be suitable for inter-subjective interpretation when interactivity and reciprocity are needed in communication [51,59]. Central to social presence theory is the assumption that “the presence of the information sender influences recipients' understanding of the message” [[51], p.89]. A communication medium that provides more social cues will generate a higher level of social presence, thus leading to enhanced ability to interpret and judge online communication. Thus, we propose that the level of social presence is a positive function of the accuracy of deception detection.

We compared the levels of social presence of the three modalities of instant messaging in terms of deception behavior [68], as shown in Table 1. Communication modalities intrinsically vary in their capacity to carry non-verbal messages [55]. In terms of non-verbal behavior, the typing mode is rich in keyboard behavior, while the messaging mode is rich in participation and sequential behavior. In terms of verbal behavior, content or memory is rich in the messaging mode but poor in the typing mode. The above differences allow us to predict and explain potential influences of media modality on deception detection.

## 2.2. Impact of media modality on deception detection outcome

Structural affordance may exert direct influence on the process and outcome of communication, such as social judgments that participants make about each other and task performance [9]. A low level of social presence that can be afforded by a communication medium may impede the detection abilities of information receivers for two reasons. First, non-verbal indicators of deception, even if they are leaked by deceivers, may be effectively filtered by the medium. Second, the lack of social presence may prevent people from engaging in a realistic dialogue to determine the veracity of received information [30]. Based on the discussion in Section 2.1, different communication modalities bear different types of behavioral cues. The messaging modality is dominated by verbal behavior (i.e., what is said), which is reflected by contextual and sequential behavior embedded in the recorded messages. In contrast, the typing modality is dominated by non-verbal behavior (i.e., how something is said), which is expressed in the typing prosody. The results of previous research on the relative advantage of particular modalities have been contradic tory. Some studies (e.g., [19]) show that people can detect deception better from non-verbal cues (e.g., facial expression and voice pitch) than from verbal cues (e.g., the content), while others (e.g., [71]) report opposite findings. Despite those mixed results, it seems clear that the presence/absence of verbal and non-verbal cues can affect the performance of deception detection. It is suggested that the effectiveness of non-verbal (i.e., visual) cues be likely to increase when some verbal cues (e.g., audio) are included [20], and there is an interaction between the use of non-verbal and verbal information when making veracity judgments [2]. Further, individuals' ability of deception detection deteriorates in those media featured with fewer social cues [24]. Therefore, deception detection is more difficult when messages are presented in a lean medium mostly involving non-verbal behavior. We expect that, compared with the typing mode, the accuracy of deception detection in the chatting mode would be higher because the additional verbal information offered by the embedded messaging mode complements the interpretation of nonverbal behavior delivered via the typing mode.

Mutual knowledge, maintenance of mutuality, and context of interaction are important to communication effectiveness [34,44]. Additionally, task-technology fit is found to improve decision quality [17]. The typing mode does not provide important contextual (i.e., message history) and relationship cues for interpreting messages. In contrast, the messaging and the chatting modes allow for more shared context and mutual knowledge, thereby making it easier for people to make detection decisions. Moreover, the semantic structure and message discourse available from the messaging and chatting modes could be another source of leakage cues to deception. Since the shared context and discourse are relevant and important to deception detection, we would expect more accurate detection in the messaging mode than in the typing mode. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1a. The accuracy of deception detection in the messaging modality will be higher than that in the typing modality.

Hypothesis 1b. The accuracy of deception detection in the chatting modality will be higher than that in the typing modality.

Despite repeated evidence showing that human beings detect deception with an accuracy rate of not much better than flipping a coin, they tend to be overconfident in their ability to detect deception [5]. However, there is no systematic relationship discovered between detectors' confidence and accuracy of deception detection [18,56]. In a meta-analysis [60], it was found that the overall correlation between confidence and accuracy in eyewitness identification was only 0.29. Thus, it is necessary to examine confidence separately. Confidence in a single deception detection activity can be modeled as a subjective probability [31]. The choice of a decision (i.e., truth or deception) and confidence judgment can be determined by cue validity [31], as shown in Formula (1):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {   If   } p (d | d C _ {i} t; R) > p (t | d C _ {i} t; R) \text {   then   choose   } d; \\ \text {   else   choose   } t. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where d and t represent deception and truth, respectively; R is a reference class (a set of cases known from a person's prior experience and knowledge that contain judgments of d and $t ) ; d C _ { i } t$ signifies the relation of d and t on cue set $C _ { i } ,$ or d and t differ in their values for $C _ { i } ( C i \in { \mathrm { P } } ( C )$ , where C is a universal set of behavioral cues). If d is chosen, the confidence that d is correct is given by the probability $p ( d | d C _ { i } t ; R )$ , and vice versa.

Choice and confidence are the results of a cue-testing and activation cycle [31]. Confidence in decision making can be subject to a variety of contextual factors. For example, the information retrieved from an online evidence system influenced clinicians' confidence in their answers to selected clinical scenarios [66]. A significant interaction effect of modality and culture on confidence measures was detected in the perception of interpersonal relationships and deception [38], showing that the difference in confidence between American participants and Japanese participants was larger in the audio–visual condition than in the visual-only condition. Certainty in verdicts generated in the mock juror task [22] was also found to be greater when the testimony was conducted based on the transcript than based on the video tape. The typing mode in instant messaging is similar to the video mode in face-to-face communication in that information is transient and behavior is displayed in real time. In contrast, both messaging and chatting modes provide a documentation of intermittent messages and contextual information. Further, suspiciousness can destroy perceivers' confidence in their own interpersonal skills [64]. Highly dynamic keyboard behavior (e.g., erasing actions and pauses) is likely to increase the suspiciousness of detectors and in turn lower their confidence in detection decisions. Thus, we expect that the confidence in deception detection in the messaging and the chatting modes is higher than that in the typing mode, which are described in the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 2a. The confidence in deception detection judgment in the messaging modality will be higher than that in the typing modality.

Hypothesis 2b. The confidence in deception detection judgment in the chatting modality will be higher than that in the typing modality.

## 2.3. Impact of media modality on deception detection process

User satisfaction is formed by the amount of positive disconfirmation between post hoc perceptions and prior expectations [15,53]. Many factors such as content, format, and ease of use can be antecedents of user satisfaction with information systems [1,3,49]. We extended the definition of satisfaction from [47] as an affective state representing an emotional reaction to the entire experience with deception detection. This definition focuses on the perception of deception detection process as opposed to the outcome-oriented approach. User satisfaction with the process has been extensively studied in group support systems research [17]. It has been found that users are “more satisfied with the process when there is a task-technology fit with appropriation support than when there is no fit ([17], p.180)”. Many people, especially college students, have some experience with instant messaging. Thus, appropriation support becomes less important but task-technology fit remains to be influential on user satisfaction. A modality that does not meet the requirements of a deception detection task is expected to have a negative impact on user satisfaction.

Deception detection involves cognitive information processing. People engage in different cognitive processing activities (e.g., heuristic and systematic processing) when facing different vividness effects [13]. In particular, more vivid effects require source-oriented cues to process, while less vivid effects require messageoriented cues to process. Thus, we infer that vivid information provided by the typing mode is primarily processed using heuristics, which needs more sourceoriented cues (e.g., credibility and attractiveness) than message-oriented cues (e.g., quality of arguments and emotional display) to make judgments. However, there is an absence of source-oriented information in the typing mode of instant messaging, which may result in users' frustration and dissatisfaction with deception detection. In contrast, people primarily engage in systematic information processing when dealing with a record of previous message exchanges in the messaging mode, where message-oriented cues become more salient and exert more influence on judgments than sourceoriented cues. The better fit between information availability and systematic information processing for deception detection in the messaging mode would lead to more positive affect toward deception detection than the typing mode. The positive disconfirmation of users' expectation would be even higher in the chatting mode due to the presence of both source-oriented and message-oriented cues. The keyboard behavior, which is present in the chatting mode but missing in the messaging mode, shortens the latency for users to see the next message and thus would further improve user satisfaction. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. User satisfaction with the process of deception detection will be the highest in the chatting modality, followed by the messaging modality, and finally the typing modality.

By extending the definition from [41,47], we define information usefulness as users' assessment of the quality and contribution of a particular information set to their deception detection decision. In CMC, people tend to rely on message-oriented cues while judging the veracity of incoming messages, which in turn lead to credibility judgment about the messenger (i.e., information source). As discussed earlier, given the availability of message and interaction history, the messaging mode can better support the systematic processing of messages than the typing mode. In contrast, there is little source-oriented information presented in the typing mode, which hampers the underlying heuristic processing. Even if source information is available, it may still be of little use because participants may not really know one another in an instant messaging environment. Thus, the usefulness of information available in the messaging and chatting modes is expected to be higher than that in the typing mode. Further, it is recommended that the use of media providing immediate feedback should improve the performance of converging on a decision [16]. According to McGrath's task circumplex [46], deception detection can be considered as a decision-making task. Among those three modalities, the chatting mode provides the highest level of social presence and quickest feedback, which is expected to be most useful for deception detection. Therefore, we propose the fourth hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 4. Information usefulness for deception detection will be the highest in the chatting modality, followed by the messaging modality, and finally the typing modality.

## 2.4. Impact of message veracity on deception detection

It is argued that the presumption of truth is fundamental and instrumental to all conversations [40]. People who are asked to distinguish deception from truth have consistently exhibited a strong truth bias despite of variations in the base rate of deception [42]. The basic assumption of truth bias is that when people attempt to decide if a person is telling the truth or lying, they tend to make more assessments of truth than deception [8]. Truth bias reflects a cognitive heuristic [61], a simple decision rule that arises from conventional beliefs and expectations that are used repeatedly in daily interaction [8]. Intuitive lie detectors often rely on stereotypical beliefs or global heuristics that are detached from authentic cues encoded during lie production [28]. By separating deception detection accuracy and truth detection accuracy, we are able to confirm the existence of truth biases in deception detection in CMC. In other words, we predict that the accuracy of making a truth judgment is higher than that of making a deception judgment, which leads to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5. The accuracy of detecting truth will be higher than that of detecting deception.

Decision-making satisfaction examines users' perception about the ability of a system to support decisionmaking and problem-solving activities [3]. Quality of information is one of the factors that directly and positively influence decision-making satisfaction [3] and computer user satisfaction [1]. Information quality has also been studied as a separate factor while evaluating online consumer satisfaction [47]. Existing measures of information quality include accuracy, completeness, relevance, and timeliness of information [1,3,49], as well as content needs [23]. According to information manipulation theory [45], deceivers have the propensity to violate the conversation maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and/or manner. In other words, deceptive messages are likely to lack content, completeness, relevance, and/or language convention, thus diminishing satisfaction. Conversely, quality information in truthful messages would foster detectors' satisfaction with the process of making detection decisions. Thus, we propose the last hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 6. User satisfaction with the process of detecting truth will be higher than that with detecting deception.

## 3. Methods

This study was carried out with a 3 × 2 repeated measure design varying media modality (typing, messaging, and chatting) and message veracity (deception and truth). We compared participants' actual performance and their perceived quality of deception detection process and outcome across different modalities. The manipulation of deceptive and truthful communication allowed us to evaluate the effect of message veracity.

## 3.1. Task and data preparation

In this study, each participant was asked to watch 12 video clips (i.e., 12 experimental instant messaging sessions), half deceivers' and half truth-tellers'. Those video clips were recorded during a previous deception study [68]. Each video clip captured the screen of an MSN Messenger session from the view of one participant. In that study, the participants were asked to perform a decision-making task, one of the most commonly used group tasks in research [46]. In particular, distributed dyads worked together as a group to find the best route among three alternatives for desert survival, given a hypothetical scenario that they were trapped in the middle of a desert in a hot summer day. In each dyad, one participant was randomly assigned as the deceiver and the other one as a naïve truth-teller. Neither of them was aware of the role of the other group member. Deceivers were instructed to recommend a route that was different from his or her own true belief and from the choice of the truth-teller. The members in each dyad discussed their route choices through the MSN Messenger only. The length of an experimental session was about 6 min on average. We operationalized three modalities of instant messaging by editing the recorded video clips with specialized software. All video clips were prepared in three versions corresponding to three modality formats (see Fig. 2). The video editor was not in the research group and was not aware of the objective of this study.

In this study, participants' task was to judge whether previous participants, whose instant messaging communication was replayed in video clips separately, were deceiving or telling the truth. Among the six deceptive (or truthful) clips that each participant watched, there were two clips for each communication modality. The video clips were randomly sorted for each participant. We asked participants to watch recorded video of previous communications instead of real time communication because of the following reasons. First, it is a common method used in previous studies of deception detection (e.g., [4,11,65]). Second, it allowed us to design the experimental conditions for different media modalities and message veracity by selecting and editing communication data beforehand. Third, the presence of the third-party observers may influence the behavior of participants involved in real-time communication. Fourth, it is practically infeasible to observe 12 live instant messaging sessions in a random order.

## 3.2. Participants

Our sample population in this study consisted of 40 volunteered undergraduate students at a public university on the east coast of the United States. Undergraduate students were used because of their homogeneity in the level of familiarity with instant messaging. Participants provided some information about their demographics and prior experience with desert survival via an online registration system. They were told that an experiment session would last about an hour and they would be given a monetary reward for their participation. All the participants had used instant messengers before and the average self-rated experience was 4.2 out of 5, but none of them had any prior experience with desert survival.

## 3.3. Experimental procedure

Upon arrival, participants were ushered to a desktop computer. They started with reading instructions about the objective and the procedure of this study, which were prepared in writing to ensure consistency across all participants. In the instruction, the participants were told that “Deception detection aims to detect information that is knowingly transmitted to create false conclusion. Successfully detecting deception can benefit both individuals and organizations in their decision making. The main objective of this study is to investigate human deception detection in instant messaging”. Participants were also provided with a hardcopy of the task scenario used by the participants in the previous deception study.

After reading the task scenario, they started to watch 12 video clips one by one through a Web-based system, which was developed in JavaScript and Cold Fusion to support online video streaming. Upon the completion of each video clip, participants were administrated a short questionnaire on their perceived process and outcome of deception detection. They could replay a particular video clip if they wanted to. However, they were not allowed to change their previous ratings. Since the video clips were assigned to the participants in a random sequence, the “training” effect could be ignored. In addition to video clips, the best routes argued for by the participants in the video were also provided to facilitate deception detection.

## 3.4. Dependent and independent variables

This study aimed to compare the outcome and process of deception detection across different modalities and message veracity. There were two independent variables, namely media modality (typing, messaging, and chatting) and message veracity (deception and truth).

All dependent variables except accuracy were measured based on participants' responses to survey questions using 7-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree).

• Accuracy: the accuracy of deception detection. It was measured by the percentage of video clips on which participants made correct judgment.

• Confidence: participants' response to the survey item “I was confident about the detection decision that I made about the communication partner from the video”.

• Satisfaction: participants' response to the survey item “I was satisfied with the overall experience of detecting deception”.

• Usefulness: participants' response to the survey item “the information presented was useful in helping me detect deception”.

## 5. Discussion

The wide adoption of CMC technology raises several new research questions: how does the communication modality affect deception detection? What gains or losses in deception detection are associated with adding or removing structural affordance (e.g., modality) of a communication medium? The findings of this study (Table 4) provide some insights on how communication modality and message veracity may affect the outcome and process of deception detection in synchronous CMC such as instant messaging. This study makes unique and valuable contributions to deception research by investigating various verbal and non-verbal behaviors conveyed via different modalities of CMC.

## 5.1. Findings and explanations

There are several major findings of this research. The first is that the modality of communication media has significant impact on the outcome of deception detection. Specifically, the accuracy of and confidence in deception detection in the messaging and chatting modes were significantly higher than those in the typing mode.

The second finding is that the media modality also has significant impact on the process of deception detection. Specifically, user satisfaction with deception detection process and perceived information usefulness were the highest in the chatting mode, followed by the messaging mode, and the lowest in the typing mode.

There are several alternative explanations for the above results regarding media modality. First, the messaging mode is able to provide information that better satisfies the information needs of the underlying systematic cognitive processing activities required for deception detection than the typing mode. On the one hand, people must encode information in the video cognitively at the rate it is presented [14]. In the typing mode featured with fast-paced keyboard behavior, contribution of an information source to the detection judgment outweighs the content associated with interpersonal modalities [55]. As a result, people tend to rely on heuristic processing by expecting more salient source-oriented cues such as identity-related cues and voice/visual behaviors. On the other hand, the communication setting used in the previous deception study was anonymous and distributed instant messaging, where participants did not see each other before, during, and after the experiment. Thus, there was very limited information about the information source (i.e., communication partners) that could be implied from the typing behavior and input messages. Therefore, the inability to express source-oriented information in the typing mode made it less desirable.

Second, people who read messages are more likely to be influenced by subtle message cues than others who process the same information in video [22]. Compared with the typing mode, the messaging mode and the chatting mode provide complete and contextual messages that allow people to read in detail, and thus lead to better performance in deception detection.

Third, when people attempt to memorize information, they are distracted by rehearsing and integrating processed information into an impression or evaluation [36,58]. This is especially true in the typing mode, which does not offer any memory assistance. In contrast, the messaging mode has built-in recordability, where the most recent messages are presented for later reference. So the cognitive load of remembering the preceding context is minimal. Therefore, the messaging and the chatting modes could support deception detection more effectively and were perceived as more satisfying and useful than the typing mode for deception detection.

Fourth, since the task of deception detection was based on the information accessible to one communication partner in this study, the typing mode was about only one party involved in the communication. On the contrary, both messaging and chatting modes involved interaction between two communication partners. Therefore, the lack of interaction context between two partners in the typing mode could be one of the reasons that resulted in the poor accuracy.

Finally, synthesizing information from more than one mode can improve observers' perception (e.g., satisfaction and efficiency) of the deception detection process. When people try to form a judgment, they tend to organize specific pieces of information into more general evaluative judgments spontaneously [36,58]. Additional valuable information can facilitate credibility judgments in fast-paced synchronous instant messaging interaction. Therefore, participants preferred the chatting mode to the messaging mode for deception detection.

The third finding is that message veracity has significant impact on the accuracy of and user satisfaction with deception detection. Specifically, truth detection was significantly more accurate but less satisfactory than deception detection. The higher level of satisfaction associated with detecting deception than detecting truth was unexpected, which can be explained from at least four perspectives. First, deception is essentially a goaldirected activity aiming to create a false impression [10]. When motivated to advocate their own choices, deceivers have to simultaneously maximize their credibility and avoid suspicion through image management [6]. While performing a group decision-making task via computer mediation, deceivers are likely to adopt a persuasive strategy and their messages tend to be longer [68,70]. The rich ‘facts’ and arguments provided by deceivers enhanced the ability of people to detect deception and increased their satisfaction with the process of deception detection. Second, the equity theory (cf. [48,54]) suggests that a person would feel satisfied if he or she perceives the outcome-to-input ratio to be sufficiently fair, and such satisfaction should increase as that person's inputs decrease in relation to others. Possible signs of violating conversation maxims in deceptive messages would help ease participants' effort (i.e., reduce input) in deception detection by suggesting a deception decision. Such an increased outcome-to-input ratio could improve participants' satisfaction with the process of deception detection. Third, message exchange during instant messaging is intense and fast-paced. Consequently, truth-tellers tend to reciprocate deceivers messages and thus potentially reduce the quality of their own messages. Possible suspicion expressed in truthtellers' messages may lower participant judges' confidence in their interpersonal skills and decrease their satisfaction with the deception detection process. Fourth, people are truth-biased and accordingly will expect little deception prior to detecting deception. Since the expectation level serves as a baseline around which subsequent disconfirmation judgments are made [54], the delight of a positive disconfirmation (i.e., deception identification) enhances the satisfaction with a judgment, while the disappointment of a negative disconfirmation (i.e., truth identification) decreases it. The above explanations need to be validated in future studies.

Some of our results are consistent with early research on the relationship between verbal and non-verbal communication. As already mentioned, proxemic, environmental, and vocalic non-verbal cues are “value-added” in relative to text-only interaction. Correspondingly, the results of this study demonstrate that combining the typing mode (dominated by non-verbal cues) with the messaging mode (dominated by verbal cues) could improve the process and outcome of deception detection, and the messaging mode is superior to the typing mode for detecting deception in instant messaging. Additionally, the better performance in detecting truth is consistent with the notion of cognitive truth bias in human deception detection.

In view of the lack of support for accuracy–confidence relationship in deception detection in the extant literature (e.g., [18]), we examined the correlation between accuracy and confidence. Surprisingly, accuracy and confidence were found to be strongly correlated (Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) = 0.301, p b 0.01). A further decomposition along the modality dimension yielded significant positive correlations between accuracy and confidence in both typing mode (PCC= 0.317, pb0.01) and chatting mode (PCC=0.235, pb0.05), but not in the messaging mode (PCC=0.215, p=n.s.). Those findings suggest that confidence is generally a good predictor of deception detection accuracy in instant messaging. The conflicting findings on the accuracy–confidence relationship between the current study and prior studies merit future investigation.

## 5.2. Implications of the findings

A better understanding of modality effect on deception detection can contribute to the design of systems for automatic deception detection and lay a foundation for further research on how media modality relates to deception detection and communication effectiveness. This is the first study that looks into the modality property of CMC media and investigates the effect of modality on both process and outcome of deception detection.

In terms of theoretical implications, this study helps explain the fundamental challenges of deception detection in CMC and presents an innovative approach to addressing the challenges by examining the modality of computer-based media. The proposed mapping between online behavior and media modalities (see Table 1) can guide future research on human deceptive behavior in CMC. The findings of this study indicate that complex interfaces of CMC tools that allow the transmission and interpretation of information in a variety of modalities add values to deception detection. Examining a communication medium in terms of modality allows us to isolate perceptual and demonstrable qualities that affect the process and outcome of deception detection.

In the typing mode, most of the cues that people rely on to detect deception are embedded in the keyboard behavior and the current message. The messaging mode, on the opposite, contains the context of previous interaction. This study suggests that the performance of deception detection could be improved by establishing mutual context between communication partners, and the process of deception detection could be further improved by integrating typing and messaging modalities. Such findings have research implications and advance our understanding of different media characteristics for deception detection in specific and communication effectiveness in general.

Compared with the messaging mode, there was slight improvement in both accuracy and confidence in deception detection in the chatting mode. However, a further inspection showed that the differences were not significant. The results suggest that the typing mode was not complementary to the messaging mode for improving deception detection outcome. This may be attributable to mixed behavioral signals transmitted via different channels in the chatting mode. The fact that some effective cues to deception in CMC are different from those in face-to-face communication could also be a reason. People also tend to rely on false clues or unreliable “rules of thumb” [26] to guide their credibility judgments. Without special training on cues to deception, the participants were likely to resort on heuristics gained from traditional face-to-face communication while making credibility judgments in instant messaging. Based on the analysis of our data, we speculate that message veracity may moderate the effect of media modality such as the chatting mode and the messaging mode on deception detection outcome, which requires further verification.

Practically, this study provides some insights on what may be done while choosing different communication modalities to support deception detection. Organizations and individuals can cautiously exploit structural affordance of communication media to facilitate proactive prevention of deception or reactive deception detection. First, a user of instant messenger should be encouraged to take advantage of the information provided by the messaging mode in deception detection. Second, in addition to message space shared by all participants of a CMC session, the individual typing mode that is currently private can be made visible to other participants in order to improve the process and possibly the outcome of deception detection.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of the present study. They also suggest several promising avenues for future research. The participants in this study were anonymous. It will be a natural extension to study deception detection in an identified CMC environment. By attaching contributors true identities to their messages, people would know who has said what. This generates source-oriented information in geographically dispersed settings, which can be used to reduce heuristic bias and in turn improve deception detection. Participants in the current study were thirdparty observers, who did not actively involved in the original communication. Freed from the demands of communication management and given limited access to the original interaction experience and context, participants in this study shared little communication reality with previous communication partners, thereby they may assign different meanings to certain behavioral cues [10] and achieve different levels of accuracy in deception detection [29]. Therefore, the effect of communication modality on the performance of deception detection by active participants in instant messaging should be explored. This study can also be extended to other types of CMC media for performing other tasks to evaluate the generality of the modality effects on the process and outcome of deception detection. Additionally, some preliminary results have shown that females are better at deception detection than males [29,63]. Potential effect of gender and/or other demographic factors on deception detection under varying modality conditions merits exploration. Further, we adopted single-item measurements for satisfaction and usefulness. Multi-item scales for the two constructs and a summary evaluation of the entire deception detection experience including both cognitive and affective components would be desirable. Finally, the number of participants involved in an instant messaging session could vary and its potential impact on deception detection should be examined. For example, there may be more than one deceiver and/or truth-teller in an instant messaging session. A study of group size in the context of deception has found that deceivers behave differently in a larger group than they do in a smaller group [69]. Such a change in deception behavior in multiperson interaction requires reassessment of the effect of modality on the process and/or outcome of deception detection accordingly.

## 6. Conclusion

This study aims to extend our understanding of deception detection in computer-mediated communication (CMC) by examining whether and how the modality of communication media can influence deception detection in CMC. In this research, we find that the modality of instant messaging and message veracity have impact on the process and outcome of deception detection. Messaging and typing modes can mutually reinforce each other to improve the process of online deception detection. The potential outcome gain from enriching the messaging mode with the typing mode requires future evaluation. Further studies are necessary to discover what specific cues are effective for detecting deception in instant messaging, how to present those cues, and how to augment the ability of instant messaging users to detect deception. The line of research that focuses explicitly on what non-verbal behavior can augment verbal cues and on how those cues are actually perceived and utilized in deception detection will tremendously contribute to understanding the impact of communication technologies on interpersonal perception.
