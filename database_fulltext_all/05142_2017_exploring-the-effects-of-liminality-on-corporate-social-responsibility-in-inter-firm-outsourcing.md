# Exploring the effects of liminality on corporate social responsibility in inter-firm outsourcing relationships

Brian Nicholson<sup>1</sup>, Ron Babin<sup>2</sup>, Steve Briggs<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Manchester, UK; <sup>2</sup>Ted Rogers School of Management, Ryerson University, Toronto, Canada; <sup>3</sup>Leadership Partner, Gartner Group, Manchester, UK

Correspondence:

B Nicholson, Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Booth Street West, Manchester M15 6PB, UK. E-mail: brian.nicholson@manchester.ac.uk

## Abstract

This article draws on the evidence gathered from a corporate social responsibility (CSR) research project in the area of global information technology (IT) outsourcing to examine the impact of liminality. IT outsourcing offers a novel context to study this phenomena, as it operates across the boundaries of both <sup>fi</sup>rm and country. The case study focuses on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c project of a school in India, as the liminal space found ‘betwixt and between’ the client and provider of IT outsourcing services. Three stages of liminality are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: separation (divestiture), transition (liminality) and incorporation (investiture); through the interpretive analysis of the empirical material. The construct of communitas is proposed for analysing the impact of liminality on the relationship between an outsourcing client and the provider. The understanding of liminality and communitas has both theoretical and practical implications, and contributes to the understanding of relationships and the wider role of CSR in global IT outsourcing.

## Introduction

his paper seeks to contribute to the corporate social responsibility (CSR) discourse of ‘doing well by doing good’ in the domain of Global Information Technology Outsourcing (GITO). Matten and Moon (2008) de<sup>fi</sup>ne CSR as Ta ‘clearly articulated and communicated set of policies and practices of corporations that re<sup>fl</sup>ect business responsibility for some of the wider societal good’ (p. 405). Authors including Bishop and Green (2008), Emerson (2003) and Porter and Kramer (2006, 2011) have all argued for a ‘doing well by doing good’ approach to CSR that utilises pro-market strategies to increase returns on philanthropic investment. They posit that corporations that embrace social concerns create a ‘win-win’ outcome for both parties (Falck and Heblich, 2007). However, Ahmad and Ramayah (2013) highlight the controversy that exists, questioning whether ventures that devote resources and effort in trying to improve society will suffer in terms of performance; or whether enterprises that ‘do good’ will also ‘do well’, and thus be successful both <sup>fi</sup>nancially and socially. This issue remains inconclusive, as prior studies have presented mixed results (Roper and Parker, 2013), highlighting the need for further empirical research. Furthermore, prior studies have largely been situated within a <sup>fi</sup>rm hierarchy or strategic alliance, and there is a paucity of literature exploring how the ‘doing well by doing good approach to CSR might prevail in market-based inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm outsourcing relationships. GITO presents an interesting context in which to study this phenomena, as it involves the subcontracting of IT services by transacting partners (client to a vendor) with some or all of the tasks undertaken in a different country (Sahay et al., 2003).

In response to the gap in the literature, this paper presents an empirical case study that explores the sponsorship of an Indian school by staff from both GITO client and provider. It draws upon anthropological concepts to analyse how the school creates a liminal space ‘betwixt and between’ the two organisations (Turner, 1969). In liminal spaces, the customary identities, routines and rules of the formal organisation are suspended, with new identities, routines and norms emerging (Turner, 1977). The liminal space may engender spontaneous and enduring communitas, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as community spirit and feelings of great social equality, solidarity and togetherness (Turner, 2012). These concepts are used as a lens to examine the research question: ‘How does the liminal space created by establishing a CSR project in<sup>fl</sup>uence the GITO relationship’?

This question is analysed through an intensive empirical analysis that draws upon semi-structured in-depth interviews undertaken with actors in the respective client and provider <sup>fi</sup>rms. Interviews also took place with others in the GITO industry, and this material is supplemented with different types of secondary data. Furthermore, the experience of one of the authors as an active participant in the case over several years helped to develop a rich contextual understanding of this phenomenon.

The article makes two contributions. First, we propose the extension of CSR to the Kern and Willcocks (2000) model of outsourcing relationships. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the concepts of liminality and communitas support the theorizing of mechanisms activated in the CSR-mediated client-vendor outsourcing relationship (Turner 1977, 2012; Hedström and Swedburg 1998). Second, this research contributes the concepts of liminality and communitas to the ‘doing well by doing good’ discourse in CSR and extends previous work (e.g. Porter and Kramer, 2006, 2011) to include inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm outsourcing relationships.

## Conceptual framework

The theoretical basis for the analysis rests on the foundational concepts of ‘doing well by doing good’, GITO relationships and the concept of liminality. Within the backdrop of these concepts, this section has three main subsections. Firstly, the doing well by doing good discourse in CSR is discussed. In the second section GITO relationships are explored, with the third section introducing the concept of liminality.

## Corporate social responsibility and ‘doing well by doing good

Over the last decade, the ‘doing well by doing good’ approach to CSR has gained momentum in both research and practice. The main proponents of this approach to CSR include Emerson’s (2003) concept of blended value; Hart’s (2005) ‘Capitalism at the Crossroads’, Bishop & Green’s ‘Philanthrocapitalism’ (2008) and Porter & Kramer’s ‘Shared Value’ (Porter and Kramer, 2006, 2011). A central tenet of this discourse is the belief that the application of business principles to CSR provides the ability to bene<sup>fi</sup>t both the business and good causes, thereby offering a ‘win-win’ scenario (e.g. P<sup>fi</sup>tzer et al., 2013; Polak and Warwick, 2013). However, critics of this approach argue that socially responsible <sup>fi</sup>rms experience lower pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and reduced share holder wealth. Margolis and Elfenbein (2008) are particularly pessimistic, suggesting that ‘Research over 35 years shows only a weak link between socially responsible corporate behaviour and good <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance’ (p. 20).

Nevertheless, GITO clients and providers have increasingly embraced CSR issues. Babin and Nicholson (2012) trace the evolution of CSR in outsourcing from 2009, when the industry association, the International Association of Outsourcing Professionals (IAOP) established a CSR committee, with the goal of de<sup>fi</sup>ning a CSR guide for the outsourcing industry, which in 2010 included an evaluation of the CSR of applicants to the Global Outsourcing Top 100. In 2010 an IAOP survey on social responsibility in outsourcing found that ‘CSR is an important and growing issue for outsourcing customers and providers – 71 per cent say that CSR will become more important or much more important in future outsourcing contracts’ (Babin and He<sup>fl</sup>ey, 2013). In 2012, IAOP unveiled an inaugural Global Outsourcing Social Responsibility Impact award and in 2013, the same organisation published the <sup>fi</sup>rst ‘Outsourcing Professionals’ Guide to Corporate Responsibility’ (Babin and He<sup>fl</sup>ey, 2013).

The importance of CSR in outsourcing has been bolstered by the Rockefeller Foundation’s Impact Sourcing program (Nyoro, 2011; Heeks, 2013). Impact Sourcing is part of the Rockefeller’s Digital Jobs Africa program, with the objective to ‘intentionally employ[s] people who have limited opportunity for sustainable employment – often in low-income areas (Rockefeller Foundation, 2015). The concept of Impact Sourcing has been broadened to include disadvantaged populations in North America such as prison inmates, aboriginals and armed services veterans (Carmel et al., 2014). Recent research has found that the Impact Sourcing market is growing slightly faster than the overall outsourcing market (11% for Impact Sourcing, 9% for outsourcing market) and labour costs can be 65–87% lower compared to traditional outsourcing (Everest Group, 2014). With this understanding of how CSR is increasingly important in outsourcing we now explore the literature regarding managing relationships between outsource buyers and providers.

## Information technology outsourcing relationships

There is a substantial literature that has enabled a greater understanding of the management of GITO relationships. For example, in this journal Rottman (2008) adopted a social capital framework in his case study presenting a US manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rm outsourcing to an Indian software provider. This work focused on the hitherto poorly understood value that is created through social relationships. Also in this journal, Kelly and Noonan (2008) examined the role of trust as a mechanism to reduce anxiety and improve psychological security between transacting partners. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings concur with Sabherwal’s (1999) conclusion that there is a need for trust in outsourcing projects, which frequently requires the cooperation of strangers in tough, high-stress situations (Sabherwal, 1999). Goo and Huang (2008) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the role of commitment in outsourcing relationships and described how commitment is linked to overcoming the adaptive limits of formal contracts. On a similar theme, Poppo and Zenger (2002) found that contracts and relational governance function as complements, not substitutes, determining that ‘customized contracts narrow the domain around which parties can be opportunistic’ and ‘that managers tend to employ greater levels of relational norms as their contracts become increasingly customized’ (p. 721). They further state that ‘relational governance may heighten the probability that trust and cooperation will safeguard against hazards poorly protected by the contract. Finally, relational governance may help overcome the adaptive limits of contracts: a bilateral commitment to ‘keep-on-with-it’ despite the unexpected complications and con<sup>fl</sup>icts’ (p. 708). The extant literature lacks any reference to the role of CSR in developing trust in the outsourcing relationship.

Figure 1 provides a conceptual model that depicts relational governance as an interaction between buyer and provider. This model is adapted from Kern and Willcocks (2000) theoretical model of the outsourcing relationship as comprised of the following dimensions:

● Context, contract and structure refers to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial, business, technical and political context that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the relationship. The contract prescribes dimensions of success in the form of the service level agreement together with structure of key personnel and management procedures.

● Interactions concerns formal and informal communication processes and cultural adaptation processes requiring investments of time, knowledge and resources from client and supplier. This dimension also embraces the importance of social and personal bonds in the relationship.

● Behavioural dimensions focuses on issues of commitment, trust, satisfaction, cooperation and con<sup>fl</sup>ict, power and dependency.

This model formed the theoretical starting point for our inquiry because of the wide scope of theoretical literature it brings together giving equal importance to contractual and social relations involved in relational governance. More recent research by the same author (Willcocks et al., 2011) reaf<sup>fi</sup>rms the value of this model in contemporary outsourcing relationships postulating that: ‘relationships that generate trust offer an important competitive advantage over those that do not’ and that outsourcing practitioners should actively build the relationship advantage to create ‘a long term investment designed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t both parties’ (p. 55). Similarly, Lacity and Willcocks (2012) in a review of 1356 research studies found that ‘the best outsourcing relationships are based on sound contractual governance and on strong relational governance’ (p. 23). The researchers identify communication and trust as key elements of relational governance. Furthermore, global supply chain research regarding informal socialisation (Cousins et al., 2006) came to similar conclusions, ‘informal socialization processes are important in the creation of relational capital, which in turn can lead to improved supplier relationship outcomes’ (p. 851). Oshri et al. (2007) define socialisation as the process by which individuals acquire the required behaviours, attitudes and knowledge. It is through socialisation that the norms, identity and cohesion develop enabling team members to effectively communicate and perform. Cousins et al. (2006) and Lawson et al. (2009) make the distinction between formal and informal socialisation and argue that formal mechanisms link to workplace structures and process for socialisation (e.g. meetings, events) whereas informal socialisation occurs outside. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the positive effect of informal socialisation on relational governance including goodwill and learning via regular social events, workshops, off-site meetings and communication guidelines. Returning to Figure 1, we posit that relational governance is enhanced by informal socialisation in a liminal space which creates communitas. These latter two concepts are discussed in the sections below.

## Liminality and liminal space

Anthropologist Victor Turner is largely credited with reintroducing the concept of ‘liminality’ into anthropological discourse. The word liminal, derived from limen – the Latin word for threshold – was <sup>fi</sup>rst employed by the French anthropolo gist, Arnold van Gennep (1909) in his work Les Rites de Passage. Liminality is a social space (liminal space) that is ‘betwixt and between the original positions arrayed by law, custom, convention and ceremony’ (Turner, 1977: 95). In anthropology, the concept is used to make sense of various rites of passage such as the rituals surrounding the ascendancy of a tribal chief (Turner, 1977) but may equally be applied to other rites of passage including Bar Mitzvahs, marriage rituals or fatherhood. Liminality is a component of what van Gennep distinguished as the second of three phases of a rite of passage: separation (divestiture), transition (liminality) and incorporation (investiture). Van Gennep describes the separation phase as the process that occurs when the individual or group subjected to the passage becomes separated from their previous social environment and way of life. During the transition phase, the person separated from their previous environment experiences the liminal space. During the <sup>fi</sup>nal incorporation phase they enter a new group and begin a new phase in their life. Therefore, during the transitory or liminal phase, those involved are said to be ‘betwixt and between the positions assigned and arrayed by law, custom, convention and ceremony’ (Turner, 1977: 95), living ‘at the limits of existing structures’ (Tempest and Starkey, 2004: 507).

In an organisational context, a liminal space is created where the regular routines of the formal organisation are suspended. While there is limited prior research drawing on liminal spaces in an organisational context, the concept of a space betwixt and between organisations has still proven useful in interpreting empirical data. Czarniawska and Mazza (2003) discovered that business consultants and their clients were neither part of one organisation nor another. Management consulting also formed the context for the study undertaken by Sturdy et al. (2006), who analysed shared dinners as the liminal space for political activity. Garsten (1999) and Zabusky and Barley (1997) both focused on temporary employees and professionals who identify neither with their organisation nor occupational group, and those engaged in inter-organisational networks and projects (Tempest and Starkey, 2004). Wagner et al. (2012) similarly focused on the liminal spaces for learning in teams implementing enterprise systems. Henfridsson and Yoo (2014) identify the underlying mechanisms of how entrepreneurs re<sup>fl</sup>ectively conceive and bring forth new innovation trajectories in liminality understood as a state of ambiguity and a ‘borderland between past and future’ (p. 933) when an innovation trajectory is not fully formed but co exists with established trajectories.

In different ways, these earlier studies each draw upon notions of liminality in order to examine the implications of liminal spaces where previously settled organisational identities, routines and rules disappear. Consequently, new blurred or transitional identities, routines and norms are created (Garsten, 1999). Activities occurring in the liminal space are separated from mainstream organisational activities, allowing individuals and groups to think, act and envision arrangements unconstrained by their existing work practices (Wagner et al., 2012). Participants are thus free from the existing institutional obligations (both technical and bureaucratic) speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the organisation, and are able to enter into new symbolic worlds and to transcend social structural limitations (Turner, 1977).

## Communitas

As a result of the rituals and symbols found in liminality, along with the suspension of normal institutional structures, an intense comradeship and egalitarianism develops where distinctions of rank and status disappear or are homogenised. This is indicative of what Turner (2012) refers to as communitas, based on af<sup>fi</sup>liations of equality and solidarity. Communitas as experienced in a liminal space is representative of a society comprised of a relatively unstructured communion of equal individuals. This is in sharp contrast to the ‘structure’ of society as secular and presenting a ‘differentiated and often hierarchical system of polito-legaleconomic positions with many types of evaluation, separating [individuals] in terms of “more” or “less”’ (Turner, 1977: 360). The crucial component of a liminal space is acquired during rites of passage, through the rituals, ceremonies and transient humility learned by the participants. Communitas may therefore be understood as the intense community spirit and solidarity created during the conditions of social equal ity, arising as people experience liminal spaces together.

Turner (2012) provides a number of familiar examples of how communitas has arisen in times of disaster; including the ‘Blitz spirit’ that arose in the UK during World War II, and the rise of oppressed peoples as led by Mahatma Gandhi and Nelson Mandela. Communitas may also occur at festivals, during religious rituals, at work, or in times of stress, revolution, and in nature. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of communitas, she writes, are the ‘quick understanding, easy mutual help and long term ties with others’ (Turner, 2012: 3). The communitas concept is also found in discourses of humanistic management, de<sup>fi</sup>ning business organisations as a community of persons (e.g. Melé, 2003).

For Turner (1969), communitas may emerge where social structure is absent. In essence, Turner asserts that the ‘ritual subject’ is released from institutional norms, and once released from such structures into communitas, they will return to the given structure revitalised by the experience of communitas, suggesting that communitas is both spontaneous and temporary. However, Turner argues that communitas may endure even after the return to structure, arguing that ‘Communitas itself soon develops into a structure, in which free relationships between individuals become converted into norm-governed relationships between social personae’ (p. 132). There are therefore two identi<sup>fi</sup>able forms of communitas: (i) existential or spontaneous communitas; and (ii) normative communitas ‘where under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of time, the need to organise and mobilise resources and the necessity for social control among members of the group in pursuance of these goals, the existential communitas is organised into a perduring social system’ (p. 132). ## Methodology

An interpretive case study methodology was adopted in this research (Walsham, 1995, 2006). The project started with 52 semi-structured interviews undertaken with GITO buyers, providers and advisors in order to examine the role of social responsibility in GITO. The sample included representatives from major banks, consumer product companies, a global mining company, an energy company, global outsourcing vendors and outsourcing advisors. Six interviews with vendors were completed in Bangalore and Mysore, India, including the vice chair of NASSCOM, India’s outsourcing trade association. Following this initial data collection, one of the authors enabled access facilitated by his position of Senior Manager in UK-based bank, Co-operative Financial Services (CFS), and their GITO provider Steria, which has centres across India. Subsequent to this case study, CFS has changed its name to the Co-operative Bank. This relationship was selected for scrutiny for a number of reasons. Firstly, ethical, environmental and community matters hold a high priority in CFS’ marketing literature and website, with its policies developed in consultation with their customers. The GITO provider concerned is described as a ‘strategic provider’ due to the importance of the outsourced project and the high level of contract spending, which amounts to £1 million annually. Thirdly, the provider (Steria) has recruited the client (CFS) into the collaborative sponsorship of two schools in India. Finally, the authors were granted access to employees at both CFS and Steria for interviews and other forms of data collection. The liminality framework was not selected a priori, but we have used the concepts to re-analyse the data to understand the potential for liminality to be an explanatory framework for understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of CSR on the outsourcing relationship.

## Data collection

One of the authors was Head of IT Strategic Partnerships at the client company until 2014 and managed introductions to the relevant client and provider staff. Twenty-three semistructured interviews were then completed, and each interview was recorded and transcribed. Most interviews were conducted on-site over a two-week period, with interviewees being questioned at some point during the course of their working day. A further two interviews were undertaken by telephone with the provider’s CSR specialist in India, and one telephone interview was conducted with the provider’s Marketing Director based in London. The interviews followed the guidelines provided by Myers and Newman (2007), engaging in historical reconstruction of the GITO relationship, and drawing on a set of semi-structured questions. Each interview lasted approximately 45 min and interviewees were asked to describe both their past experiences and future expectations regarding CSR in the outsourcing relationship, and how they believed it would be relevant to their future IT outsourcing business operations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions were asked regarding the current and future CSR challenges and associated provider capabilities. In addition, questions were asked about the collaborative school sponsorship and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts consequently accrued by the respective organisations. All the interviews were tape-recorded and professionally transcribed. Detailed notes were also taken and these were written up in full as soon as possible, along with any additional comments and interpretations.

Several of the interviewees had visited the school and re<sup>fl</sup>ected on their experiences. One of the authors had visited the sponsored schools on three occasions in his role of Head of IT Strategic Partnerships since 2007 and this experience provided a rich source of in-depth data on the events in India and also contributed to interpretation of the interviews and evolving theory as presented in this paper. He was able to use his familiarity with Steria and the schools to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the utility of the liminality lens to understand the impact on the outsourcing relationship. With guidance from the other two co-authors and working together on this and other academic papers, the visiting author was able to interpret his intense involvement and deeply engaging liminal experiences through in-depth retrospective re<sup>fl</sup>ection.

## Data analysis

The analysis of themes arising from the interviews began by the authors becoming immersed in the data gathered from the interviews followed by discussions during breaks between interviews. Subsequently, reading and re-reading of the tran scripts, listening to the recordings to ‘relive’ the experience and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on supplementary notes and observations led to the initial identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of themes, while in parallel, a more formalised and systematic approach was initiated. Each interview transcript was reviewed, and responses to questions that were not provided by more than one interviewee were discarded. Responses that were given in multiple interviews were clustered into categories representing the common themes. These themes were then coded to identify the frequency of each mention in the interviews, allowing the researchers to quantify and prioritise the key themes. This engagement with the research participants, collection of rich data and discussion between the researchers and participants formed the nucleus of the research approach and subsequent linkages to appropriate theories.

Our approach in this paper is best described as an intermediate position drawing on elements of grounded theory (Strauss and Corbin, 1998) and direct application of theory to data. As a result of the thematic analysis and coding work described above, liminality and communitas was deemed to have powerful explanatory value for making sense of the themes. Thus, data analysis for this paper included re reading of the raw data with these concepts in mind, coding and sorting text that corresponded to each concept. Secondly, inspired by Henfridsson and Yoo’s (2014) inductive approach to deriving mechanisms (Hedström and Swedburg, 1998), we focussed on the incorporation phase of liminality and recoded the data with the notion of mechanisms in mind.

Hedström, and Ylikoski (2010) explain that mechanismbased explanations should detail the ‘cogs and wheels’ of the causal process through which the outcome to be explained was brought about. A mechanism provides a continuous and contiguous chain of causal or intentional links between the explanans and the explanandum (Elster, 1989). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we draw on the notion of an action formation mechanism that ‘shows how a speci<sup>fi</sup>c combination of individual desires, beliefs, and action opportunities generate a speci<sup>fi</sup>c action (Hedström and Swedburg, 1998, p. 23).

We sorted relevant coded data into categories and worked through multiple iterations drawing on the logic of mechanisms to derive the three mechanisms presented herein. Intensive discussion between the authors took place both face to face and using Skype from our respective locations on the iterative theoretical analysis of the data. In addition, prior to this paper a report outlining preliminary <sup>fi</sup>ndings was presented to key stakeholders at CFS and Steria, providing the initial analysis of the practices of CSR and the India school sponsorship. This was not part of an explicit action research strategy, but was nevertheless essential in obtaining and maintaining access, and provided a further point of discussion between the researchers and the interviewees.

## Case description

The outsourcing provider (Steria) employs over 20,500 employees across 16 countries, with of<sup>fi</sup>ces located in Europe, India, North Africa and SE Asia. Revenues in 2012 were over €1.8 billion. The client (CFS) is part of the UK Co-operative Group, a group of businesses with revenues of approximately £10.5 billion and with some 87,000 employees (2013). The outsourcing partnership is enduring, beginning in the 1990s. Today, the providers’ work is primarily focused on the maintenance and support of legacy software applications.

The provider’s CSR strategy consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve categories, relating to its priorities in relation to the environment, marketplace, workplace and community, access to IT and education. The school programme, as part of the <sup>fi</sup>nal category, is offered to selected clients on a partnership basis as a mechanism for injecting vital resources into schools located in the Indian cities of Chennai, Pune and Noida, where the provider’s centres are located. A total of 15 schools are supported by the provider, with clients presented with a variety of engagement options and associated costs. Clients are encouraged to communicate with the children in the school through mentoring, which may include email, video conferencing and scheduled visits. When visiting India, client staff (usually but not exclusively high ranking executives) are encouraged to visit the school that they sponsor.

Two schools are sponsored by the client described in this case. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is Medavakkam High School located in the suburbs of Chennai, which has been sponsored since 2007. The second school is the Shri Ghanshyam Sharma Memorial High School in Dujana, Noida, which has been sponsored since May 2010. In both schools, the pupils are from lowincome families, such as farmers and labourers. The client budget in each school is £15,700 per annum, supporting the activities summarised in Table 2.

## Case study

We now describe the case study drawing on salient themes that were analysed from the empirical material using the three phases of a rite of passage (separation, liminality and incorporation) and the concept of communitas. Using the notion of an action formation mechanism understood as a description of ‘how a speci<sup>fi</sup>c combination of individual desires, beliefs, and action opportunities generate a speci<sup>fi</sup>c action’ (Hedström and Swedburg, 1998: 23), we explored the mechanisms in the incorporation (post liminal phase) and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of CSR on the relationship.

## Separation (divestiture)

For Turner (1977) the divestiture phase involves the separation and detachment from the norms of the social environment and the previous way of life. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the separation that arises during the occasion of travel and the arrival at the school forms the basis for this separation. The school visits are scheduled into the period when clients are invited to the provider’s offshore centre for formal classbased training and less formal knowledge transfer and social networking. These visits to India usually involve senior staff staying for a few days (up to approximately one week), based in hotels close to the Steria of<sup>fi</sup>ces in Chennai and Noida, but may also involve more junior staff staying for a longer term.

Client interviewees’ recollections describe how visits to the school involve ‘leaving the normal routine’ in the drive to the school, which is located some 20 km away from the Steria centre. This is formally timetabled as an event, with the visit notable for its relatively informal dress code and by staff travelling together to the school in shared cars. Interview participants revealed that while the journey to the school from the provider centre was relatively short in distance, it actually necessitated over 1 h of travel, due to the overcrowded and potholed nature of India’s roads. A client-side interview describes this journey, revealing aspects of the separation it presents to participants:

We emerged from air conditioned 5 star hotels and hightech air conditioned of<sup>fi</sup>ces and embarked on a journey which highlighted starkly the huge wealth gulf between the economically disadvantaged lower castes of Indian society. Great poverty co-exists literally next to great wealth, with plush air conditioned cars weaving between bicycles, often with 2 or 3 riders and noisy 3 wheel taxis, cows, dilapidated lorries, motorcycles and occasionally elephants. Traf<sup>fi</sup>c chaotically weaves across the road and sometimes onto the other side attacking all the senses. When turning into the school’s entrance it is, in contrast, relatively calm.

## Incorporation (investiture)

Using the notion of an action formation mechanism (Hedström and Swedburg, 1998), we explore three action formation mechanisms (community values synchronisation, deformalise and network and reflexive engagement) associated with the incorporation of the participants into the regular structures and routines of client and provider. Our analysis shows that CFS and Steria staff enacted the three contingent action formation mechanisms (see Figure 11) and in what follows we focus on these three mechanisms in detail.

## Community values synchronisation

Our data analysis showed that liminality experienced in the school mobilised participants into enacting an action formation mechanism related to community between the client and provider. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the mechanism involves participants institutionalising community values into the regularised practices of the client and provider and widening awareness of the school sponsorship to a joint collective beyond the limited group who experienced liminality in the school.

Client statements about the desired relationship with Steria illustrate the importance given to community:

We want a common set of values – to build more trust, [with] like-minded organisations, [where] we share something in common.

(Person B – CFS Commercial Manager)

… We are trying to involve each other in those types of activity (India school sponsorship) because it helps to embed the relationship in a way that just meeting around the table in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce doesn’t do … It helps to embed the relationship and just makes people feel like they are one community.

(Person C – CFS Procurement Manager)

Several episodes illustrate this mechanism: <sup>fi</sup>rstly, on return home to UK, a senior client manager enrols a wider community by giving a presentation in the staff work area focussed on the events and experiences during the visit to the school. This includes a slideshow of photographs with commentary that are also published on the provider’s website. Secondly, speci<sup>fi</sup>c voluntary practices are institutionalised into both client and provider organisations. Provider staff in India act as ‘mentors’ and visit the school to train teachers and children in the use of the donated computer equipment. Client staff on longer term assignment in India (two weeks or more) spend periods of time in the school computer lab and also participate in leading sports lessons for the children. The mechanism enrols a wider community than those who had visited the school via the Steria India Foundation Graduate Scholarship Scheme – SIFGSS. This programme funds a graduate degree college education for the brightest of children from the sponsored school known as ‘Steria Scholars’. Client staff, most of whom had never experienced liminality in the school, voluntarily organised fundraising events for the Steria Scholars from their sponsored school. For example, during our interviews we observed a cake sale held at the Co-op Manchester of<sup>fi</sup>ce where proceeds were donated to the SIFGSS scheme. The provider also organises an event titled ‘One Day Challenge’ that takes place on an annual basis to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the school and the SIFGSS, with photographs exchanged between the school teachers, children, Steria and Co-op staff. The principal sponsor of the scheme is the Cooperative Banking Group, and their logo is included alongside Steria on the materials prepared for the challenge. The One Day Challenge is otherwise known informally as the ‘group wide community day’ and during our interviews the staff restaurant at Co-Op had been transformed with bunting. Events during the day included a raf<sup>fl</sup>e and sponsorship events coupled with an air of joviality fomented by music and themed costumes. The deep commitment to the school activity by senior staff in Co-Op was mentioned in several interviews. The individual belief that underpins this action formation mechanism was illustrated by a story told to us with considerable reverence by several interviewees (client and provider) of a senior manager who personally <sup>fi</sup>nancially supports the salary of a teacher at the school. Interviewees were keen to point out that this manager continued to pay the salary of the teacher even after leaving Co-Op employment.

## Deformalise and network

Our research shows how the liminal space at the school provides the opportunity for interaction and cross-linkage between the actors in Steria and CFS. On incorporation, liminality enables the suspension of the formal designation of rank and communication rules within respective organisations and as stipulated by the outsourcing contract. We refer to this mechanism as deformalise and network, that is, the process by which formality is reduced between individuals and the network of contacts expanded. Wider dissemination of information about the school and experiences in liminality from the <sup>fi</sup>rst mechanism acts to mobilise wider support and provide a collective outcome enrolling others. A Steria manager said:

CSR initiatives have helped to diminish the formal communication hierarchy – it allows more junior staff to speak directly with their seniors on a CSR related activity.

(Person D – Steria Client Director)

The roles individuals assume in the school project enable a greater span of communication outside of the contractually speci<sup>fi</sup>ed governance routines, meetings and formalised reporting channels and procedures. Interviewees described how the personal bonding experienced in liminality facilitates subsequent communication without the ‘defences’ or any ‘sense of us and them’. Levelling of hierarchy in liminality affected knowledge sharing, with a CFS staff member revealing in interview:

(CFS staff) are closer to the action. They’re closer to the internal discussion. It’s not a standoff where ‘I'm protecting my IP mind-set’…

(Person E – CFS Vendor Relationship Manager)

Similarly, another Steria executive said:

These trips out to India (the school) are where you really get to know your customers. When you share different experiences with them, as you know, it puts your relationship on a very different plain. I think the closer that you work together, the more effective you are.

It’s about working with my main contacts; working with India enables me to understand what makes CFS tick, what makes the company tick.

I would just say it massively helps with our relationship and how we work together, you also bring in teamwork and there are so many other things that come into it, other skills such as communication. ## Reflexive engagement

Our data analysis showed the effect of the school sponsorship on commitment. We refer to this mechanism as reflexive engagement de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the process by which the liminal participants and the wider group in both provider and client identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with the values demonstrated by the school sponsorship. The Co-operative Group Annual Report (2013) states that their aim is ‘to build a better society… to be an ethical leader; to be an exemplary employer’. A senior manager at CFS explained how this vision, realised in the school sponsorship generates speci<sup>fi</sup>c action:

It’s the fact that we’ve got thousands of people sitting down at lunch with each other talking about this stuff … engagement is the issue for me. If I’ve got engaged staff, they’re going to stand up and step forward rather than sit back and be passive. I mean it’s not just in the textbook; it’s a fact you see. It exudes from the pores of the individuals working on a programme … engagement is the thing, everywhere you get more out of people and they get more out of you if you're engaged.

(Person K – CFS Programme Manager – Banking Transformation)

The engagement theme arising from the school project was mentioned by others:

I have people that come back happier, much more engaged, and feel they're actually doing something that <sup>fi</sup>ts in with the values of the companies. So I think the staff engagement, that’s just a great thing to do upfront … I see direct and immediate bene<sup>fi</sup>t there … they're coming back and they write blogs on it. So they write on blogs on what they saw the children that they met, what they are saying.

(Person K – CFS Programme Manager – Banking Transformation)

Employees at CFS and Steria working on the CFS account were re<sup>fl</sup>exive regarding the vision of an organisation offering more than simply increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Productivity is a measure of motivated and engaged employees and interviewees spoke of the energy and attentiveness of the employ ees who are engaged in the CSR projects shared between Steria and CFS. A Steria account manager explained:

People stay longer, sometimes they’re investing a lot of their time and it’s not paid, I won’t necessarily see it on my bottom line, but you will see it on the productiveness of that project, of hitting targets, etc.

(Person L – Steria Engagement Manager)

A CFS executive expounded on the motivation of Steria employees, who value the strength of the relationship with CFS:

When you are under pressure of getting the tests done against a tight deadline, do you know what the guys say? Well, time to go home now, or do they stay until midnight? These guys stay until midnight and beyond and all night if necessary… . the school [CSR] thing is just a little part of that – it just builds that.

(Person M – CFS Director – Integration and Change Management)

Another senior CFS executive con<sup>fi</sup>rmed an effect on staff turnover:

Turnover is the biggest thing for me. So we’re not constantly losing staff in their area and bringing new staff in and letting it just start off with new skills which means projects have more of a success of delivery in the time schedule set … that continuity with people on a programme from start to <sup>fi</sup>nish was really hard but the turnover in Steria is lowest I've seen of all the Indian third parties.

(Person H – CFS CIO)

While the Steria average attrition rate for 2010 was 16.5%, the attrition on the CFS project was 12%. In some areas, Steria attrition was as high as 25%, presenting a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost for the provider, who must hire and train replacement employees, which inevitably disrupts the service for buyers. Reducing attrition is therefore of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to both the outsource provider and the buyer, as the buyer gains continuous service from a knowledgeable outsource employee, while the provider keeps their training and recruitment costs low. Quotes from Steria executives regarding the challenge of keeping staff turnover low are provided below:

Attrition is a massive issue for the industry … attrition on the CFS account is really, really … nothing compared to others, I mean it stands out. … what it is certainly linked to is the strength of the relationship between the two organisations, which is really visible to the guys in India … you couldn’t say the reason for the low attrition on CFS is solely because of what we do on CSR. But it’s certainly part of the equation … de<sup>fi</sup>nitely a factor.

(Person G – Steria Head of Banking Industry)

## Discussion

The liminality concept has helped examine and explain participants’ experiences in the school CSR sponsorship and the outcomes. Adopting Turner’s (1969, 1977) conceptual lens reveals the school as a liminal space ‘betwixt and between buyer and provider where ceremonies and rituals act to detach participants from their normal structures, allowing them to enter into liminality. There is a distinction here between liminality as part of a rite of passage and liminality that is a ‘neutral space’ but not forming part of a rite of passage where participants are not giving up their previous state or role for a new one. In this paper, the rite of passage offers a useful classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the liminal practices encountered but there is no attempt to argue that participant’s status was altered as a result. However, we argue liminality did change the individuals who had collectively experienced it and subsequently in<sup>fl</sup>uenced interactions between the client and provider and enrolled others in their respective organisations.

Figure 11 pictorially depicts the process of liminality and mechanisms that impact outsourcing relational governance. Liminality in the school CSR project and the resultant communitas between participants triggers and foments the action formation mechanisms of values synchronisation, deformalise and network and reflexive commitment which impact in the various ways described above to improve relational governance between buyer and provider.

The school CSR project as described above provides the liminal space, the venue for communitas and subsequently the trigger or catalyst for the three action formation mechanisms. The remaining depiction in Figure 11 is the in<sup>fl</sup>uence on relational governance between buyer and provider.

The three action formation mechanisms are enacted simul taneously, are interdependent and mutually reinforcing depicted by the double-headed arrows in Figure 11. There is no a priori sequence to the initiation, development or conclusion of the mechanisms but all are present in the case study. The mechanisms are not deterministic causal forces with predetermined outcomes. Instead, following Henfridsson and Yoo (2014), the mechanisms are understood as causal powers (Sayer, 1992). Individuals are capable of re<sup>fl</sup>ection and may decide to act or not to act due to other priorities or mechanisms. The <sup>fi</sup>rst mechanism is Community Values Synchronisation that captures the process by which participants in liminality establish communitas engaging in the school sponsorship. This provides a convergence (‘synchronisation’) of common shared values related to the common interest and belief in the importance of education and poverty alleviation for the schoolchildren. This is reinforced by the shared sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce of the computer equipment. Subsequent wider dissemination of information about the visits, co-branded events and wider sponsorship projects enrol a wider group in the community.

The second mechanism is Deformalise and Network that refers to the process by which participants were detached from normal structures of rank and ‘normal’ behaviour and communitas forms the enduring personal bonds. The notion of networks resonates with Granovetter’s (1973) assertion that the ‘strength’ of an interpersonal tie is a linear combination of the amount of time, the emotional intensity, the intimacy (or mutual con<sup>fi</sup>ding), and the reciprocal services that characterise each tie. Communitas developed in liminality in the school CSR project enabled strong ties between the participants. Post liminality, their roles remained unchanged but formed a ‘multiplexity’ (Kapferer, 1969) of overlapping roles, exchanges and af<sup>fi</sup>liations of both a commercial and school CSR-related nature incorporated into the relationship between buyer and provider. In the case, participants’ communitas from the school CSR project was woven into multiplex relationships in both the commercial and CSR domain which became inseparable. Lawson et al. (2009) point out that socialisation between organisations results in increased knowledge sharing which was also demonstrated in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Furthermore, the value of strong ties had practical utility when problems and crises are encountered which are common in managing GITO.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal mechanism is Reflexive Engagement that denotes the process by which participants and the wider group in client and provider were engaged with the values demonstrated by the school sponsorship and demonstrated commitment to the Steria and Co-op relationship. The evidence concurs with prior research into employee engagement realised through CSR projects. For example, Mirvis (2012) identi<sup>fi</sup>es ‘[s]everal studies aimed directly on the linkage between CSR and employee engagement [that] have found a strong correlation between employee’s commitment to their organization and how they rate its social responsibility’ (p. 95). Similarly, Bhattacharya et al. (2008) describe that in the war for talent there is ‘growing evidence that a company’s corporate social responsibility activities comprise a legitimate, compelling and increasingly important way to attract and retain good employees’ (p. 37).

## Theoretical contribution

Our main starting point in theorising about relationships in outsourcing was provided by Kern and Willcocks’ (2000) analysis of the constellation of relationship challenges in outsourcing. Our analysis corroborates and goes beyond Kern and Willcocks (2000) in several distinctive ways. Firstly, in the context, contract and structure dimension of the conceptual model, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings concur with and illustrate the assertion that contracts are relational entailing non-contractual and voluntary dimensions. Our analysis adds a novel facet to the relational contract showing how jointly conducted CSR projects may comprise an element of the governance of an outsourcing relationship.

Moving to the interactions dimension, Kern and Willcocks (2000) stress the importance of informal communication which they stress is often the result of voluntary (noncontractual) interactions. They write: ‘voluntary interactions and hence exchanges were revealed as those factors that enable the parties to adapt to the idiosyncratic working practices … in short they sustained the relationship especially in situations of dispute, con<sup>fl</sup>ict and moving of key management personnel’ (p. 343). It is here that we signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly augment the model <sup>fi</sup>rstly by introducing key literature from supply chain management (Cousins et al., 2006; Lawson et al., 2009) on the role of informal socialisation (hospitality trips to sporting events, theatre, dinners, team building etc.) although the school sponsorship offers novel insight into a process of longer term duration to most informal socialisation events that tend to be short term or ‘one off’. Secondly, our analysis shows how participants engaged in CSR liminality were united in the cause of poverty alleviation thus aligning CSR objectives. Kern and Willcocks point out the importance of cultural adaptation; they write: ‘problems in ventures emerge when the parties involved do not share the same social and cultural traits and norms’ (p. 330). The three mechanisms illustrate how the shared values were important and how this was reinforced by the school sponsorship CSR project and liminality. Extending Kern and Willcocks’ model to embrace CSR liminality allows explanation of how the social and personal bonds built in liminality have positive effects on communication and information exchange. Furthermore, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings identify the sources of personal bonding and the role of CSR liminality, the implications that arise for the management of relationships and how these shape project-related outcomes.

Considering the behavioural dimension, our analysis corroborates the importance of commitment and extends the model with evidence of how that may have its source in shared CSR projects. The implications for trust are clearly relevant reinforcing Kern and Willcocks’ analysis and offer a route for future research related to CSR liminality. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our analysis of CSR, liminality and the three action formation mechanisms provides an extension and illustration to what Kern and Willcocks refer to as durability of the relationship and how a strong feeling of chemistry contributes to satisfaction in outsourcing relationships.

Our <sup>fi</sup>nal point of contribution is to the CSR literature where the concept of liminality is underutilised. Our research demonstrates how CSR projects may offer the liminal space where individuals become united in the act of ‘doing good’ offering ‘win-win’ outcomes. Thus, this research contributes the concept of liminality to the ‘doing well by doing good’ discourse in CSR. Secondly, our case study of outsourcing extends previous work (e.g. Porter and Kramer, 2006) that assumes CSR is undertaken within the <sup>fi</sup>rm hierarchy.

## Practical implications

For practicing professionals, our analysis suggests that the cultivation of relationships may be addressed by CSR and that there is an important role for liminality. In line with prior research (Poppo and Zenger, 2002, Goo and Huang, 2008), practitioners may use the examples of practices in this case study as the basis for starting points in designing relational governance and complementing the formal contractual governance structures of outsourcing. As Babin and Nicholson (2012) have described, thoughtful participation in the collaborative relationships described in this paper may offer a competitive advantage for the outsource provider by creating barriers to entry for rivals who bid on renewal outsourcing contracts (p. 102).

## Concluding remarks, limitations and future work

A limitation of this paper is the basis on a single case; however, single case research design is generally accepted with many precedents (e.g. Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007, Levina and Vaast, 2008) but sometimes criticised for general isation potential. Following Walsham’s (1995, 2006) guidance on generalisation of interpretive cases, this paper focuses on theoretical development and offering rich insight. This research offers evidence that a more humanistic approach to the management of GITO relationships may be relevant to GITO performance and future research may continue to explore this linkage. Another area of future research may be to further explore the role of liminality in differentiating the CSR of GITO, extend our understanding of the distinctive nature of the choices to be made in CSR projects and the preliminal, liminal and post-liminal preparation. Thirdly, our extension to Kern and Willcocks’ theoretical model to include CSR reinforces mainly the relational dimension but there is clearly potential for future work to contribute to the formal contract, building on Poppo and Zenger (2002) for example.

Finally, it is noteworthy that a CSR basis for GITO relationship building itself has limitations. Primarily, the school in this case may become dependent on Co Op and Steria for the computing provision and any deterioration of the relationship or contextual shock (for example see postscript below) may affect the longevity of the support for the ‘good cause’. This is a theme that is of some concern to development studies researchers who remain critical of the ‘doing well by doing good’ discourse in management (e.g. Edwards, 2008, 2010).

## Post script

This case study research was conducted between 2008 and 2011. Co-operative Financial Services was selected for the study because of the organisation’s strong commitment to social responsibility. The Co-op began as the Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers in 1844, whose goal was to improve the social and domestic conditions of its members. Today the Coop balances pro<sup>fi</sup>table operations with a ‘purpose beyond pro<sup>fi</sup>t and a commitment to corporate sustainable development.

Beginning in 2013, CFS faced two signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges to its reputation: <sup>fi</sup>rstly, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nancial loss after a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult merger with the Britannia Building Society resulting in a portfolio of bad commercial property loans, and secondly the resignation of its chairman over allegations of illegal drug abuse. As reported in the Telegraph (22 August 2014):

The Co-op Bank has endured a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult 12 months. Last year, a £1.5bn black hole was discovered in its accounts, forcing it to raise emergency funds that saw hedge funds and institutions take control. Along with a drugs scandal involving its former chairman Paul Flowers, this has tarnished the bank’s ethical reputation in the eyes of some consumers.

The results from the case study are unaffected by the recent CFS challenges. As the Co-op works to rebuild its <sup>fi</sup>nancial strength and public reputation, it remains committed to CSR and continues to work in a highly collaborative outsourcing relationship with its provider Steria.
