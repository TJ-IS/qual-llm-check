## a b s t r a c t

Information sharing between firms has been cited as a major means to improve firm performance. This paper develops a conceptual framework that relates information sharing to firm profitability in a duopoly market. The framework allows each firm to have its own private information about uncertain market demand. We utilize a game-theoretical model to examine this framework. Our results reveal that information sharing has no uniform effect on firm performance. Firms behaving in the Bertrand mode benefit from information sharing only under certain conditions. However, firms behaving in the Stackelberg mode always benefit from information sharing. Further, we examine how the value of information sharing is affected by the nature of firm, the product brand, and the degree of product differentiation. Our results show that when two firms have high level of customer satisfaction, offer products with reputable brands, or sell products that are strongly substituted in the market, information sharing becomes signi cantly important and imperative to both rms. Particularly if two rms behave in a Stackelberg mode, the value of information sharing further increases. Furthermore, we also address the issue of information distortion and propose EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) information system with a nonnegative matrix-factorization technique as an effective and valuable mechanism to eliminate any possible information distortion, so that both firms can share their forecast information truthfully.

## 1. Introduction

Nowadays producing accurate forecasts becomes more and more challenging because of uncertain and turbulent economy [22,23]. As an efficient tool in helping improve information precision, information sharing between competing firms is becoming increasingly prevalent [34]. From the firm's point of view, information sharing improves the precision of information available to itself and its rivals, which in turn leads to higher expected profits. Many firms have embarked on initiatives aimed at facilitating the sharing of information among themselves in order to improve information accuracy and pricing decisions [9,21,24,35]. For example, Sainsbury and Marks & Spencers in the U.K. and Super Valu store and Von's supermarket in the U.S. have created interorganizational systems for information exchange and sharing [24]. American Airlines and United Airlines in the airline industry exchange their cost information in order to improve profitability [1]. The sharing of consumer or market information among firms has been recognized as a strategic tool in improving performances [36]. Information sharing assists firms in making improved decisions, thus eliminating the costly trial and error process. Information sharing pools different signals together and thus yields more precise information about market demand. Given that information sharing effectively improves information accuracy and firm performance, it seems intuitive that information sharing may have a uniform effect on firm performance and always benefits the parties involved in such arrangements. This result, however, is not obvious and we need a careful study.

In this paper, we study the value of information sharing in a duopoly market and address why information sharing among competitors helps improve firm performance under some business environment, but not always. In order to reflect commonly discussed business situations in practice, we consider two horizontal pricing competition modes (i.e., Bertrand and Stackelberg modes). In the business market, some firms behave in Bertrand mode, and other firms behave in Stackelberg mode in the fragmented industries [6,12,27]. We first develop a conceptual framework associated with information sharing. Then we theoretically investigate the effect of information sharing on firm performances and derive the optimal strategies. Specifically, we utilize a game-theoretical model to study the following questions:

(i) Will firms always perform better through sharing information under any mode of conduct? If not, which mode of conduct is more efficient for information sharing?

(ii) When does information sharing become imperative as a function of product differentiation?

(iii) Is there an incentive for firms to distort the information they would like to share?

(iv) If there is an incentive, what effective mechanism can be utilized to eliminate any possible information distortion and guarantee that information can be shared truthfully?

A number of studies on information sharing have been analyzed in the literature of marketing, supply chain management, and management information systems. For example, Cachon and Fisher [5] found that information sharing can create reduced lead time and batch size to firms, and Lee et al. [17] showed that firms can benefit from inventory reduction and cost savings because of information sharing. Raghunathan and Yeh [25] showed that information sharing about inventory data is beneficial to both the manufacturer and its retailers in the continuous replenishment program (CRP) context. Aviv [2] revealed that supply chain members' replenishment process can significantly benefit from the sharing of forecast information. Li et al. [20] studied the inter-organizational information sharing and showed that complete information sharing with multiple types of information can help firms achieve higher performance in a volatile market. Guo and Iyer [15] found that the manufacturer has a motivation to acquire more information about consumer preferences and demand under a voluntary sharing mechanism. All of the aforementioned papers focused on the inventory-related and replenishmentrelated benefits of information sharing in a vertical structure. Our research diverges significantly from these papers in that we focus on the effect of information sharing on pricing decisions in a horizontal structure.

Li [18] and Zhang [44] addressed the effect of information sharing on pricing decisions in a supply chain consisting of one manufacturer and multiple retailers. Their results showed that a manufacturer would like to buy its retailer information if information sharing is found to be beneficial to him. Li and Lin [19] conducted an empirical study to show that supplier uncertainty, shared vision between supply chain partners, and commitment of supply chain partners significantly influence the levels of information sharing and information quality. Guo et al. [14] utilized analytical models to show that valuable and useful information about systematic demand risk can be utilized to achieve accurate forecast sharing and better channel coordination in the supply chain of one manufacturer and multiple retailers. Choi et al. [7] used analytical models to show that the manufacturer and the retailer can utilize a proper returns policy to achieve a win–win result while sharing information. Yan and Wang [43] showed that all franchising members can benefit from information sharing while profit split is implemented. However, the aforementioned studies focused solely on the vertical channel structure and assumed that all parties would share their information truthfully. They did not address the issue of information distortion. Our study focuses on the horizontal channel structure (a duopoly market) and addresses the moderating role played by product differentiation, product brand, and firm's customer satisfaction in the information industry. Furthermore, our study indicates that each firm in a duopoly market does have a motivation to distort its information while sharing information.

Vives [34] and Gal-Or [9] are some most notably pioneering works which examined the value of sharing information between competing firms. However, there are several major differences between our study and theirs. First, Vives [34] and Gal-Or [9] only examined a special case where symmetric information is assumed. They did not address the sharing of asymmetric information. We assume that different firms have different forecast information and evaluate the value of asymmetric information sharing. Second, Vives [34] and Gal-Or [9] did not address the issue of information distortion, while we do. We also propose effective mechanism to eliminate any possible information distortion, so that both firms can share their forecast information truthfully. Third, Vives [34] and Gal-Or [9] did not address price leader and follower behavior (Stackelberg) in the duopoly market, while we do. In the business market, while some firms behave in the Bertrand mode, other firms behave in the Stackelberg mode [6,12,27].

Our study also complements the study of Raju and Roy [26]. Raju and Roy [26] investigated the impact of information accuracy on the performances of competing firms in the non-information sharing Bertrand and Stackelberg modes. However, they did not address the issue of information sharing for both the Bertrand and Stackelberg modes. In contrast, we extend our model to study information sharing equilibrium for both the Bertrand and Stackelberg modes and compare all scenarios to derive the value of information sharing. Furthermore, Raju and Roy [26] focused on the effect of product substitutability on information accuracy. On the other hand, our research focuses on different set of issues. We focus on how the value of information sharing is affected by the nature of firm, the product brand, and the degree of product differentiation. In addition, Raju and Roy [26] did not address the issue of information distortion when firms engage in information sharing (because distorted information may make the shared information useless or even stop the firms from participating in information sharing), while we do and propose a valuable and effective mechanism (e.g., Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) system with a nonnegative matrix-factorization technique to communicate their demand data directly) to eliminate information distortion.

Our research contributes to the extant information sharing literature, especially for the horizontal information sharing literature, by studying the strategic roles of the asymmetric information sharing, the various modes of conducts, the nature of firms and products, and the degree of product differentiation in an uncertain duopoly market. Specifically, we develop a conceptual framework to investigate the relationship between the information-sharing modes and the duopoly firm performances and provide valuable and counter-intuitive results to business managers. In the business markets, firms can compete on price, quantity, quality, and others. As in Vives [34], Raju and Roy [25], and Gal-Or et al. [11], our research focuses solely on price competition and the findings and approaches also are only applicable to industries competing purely on prices. In our research, we assume that each firm has its own private data about the uncertain demand. We first present a profit maximization model to obtain optimal strategies under four information scenarios: (1) the non-information sharing Bertrand mode, (2) the information sharing Bertrand mode, (3) the non-information sharing Stackelberg mode, and (4) the information sharing Stackelberg mode. We then compare these scenarios to derive some counterintuitive and clear-cut results: 1) when firms behave in the Bertrand mode, information sharing is not always beneficial to each firm; 2) when firms behave in the non-information sharing, the Stackelberg mode provides no competitive advantage while comparing with the Bertrand mode; and 3) the information sharing Stackelberg mode can help firms achieve higher profit than other modes. Furthermore, we address the important issue of information distortion while sharing information, and propose an effective and valuable mechanism (i.e., Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) system with a nonnegative matrix-factorization technique to communicate their demand data directly) to eliminate any possible information distortion.

## 3. The uncertainty model

Due to changing markets, product availability, and continuing shifts in consumer preferences, the base level of market demand, a, is a random variable, and $a = \bar { a } + e ,$ e is normally distributed with mean zero and variance $\nu \left( \mathrm { i . e . , v a r } ( a - \bar { a } ) = \nu \right)$ . The normality assumption has been used extensively in the past literature [3,26,34]. We mitigate this negative effect by allowing a large ā value relative to v. As in Raju and Roy [26], we also confine the demand uncertainty to an additive intercept term. We also assume that the price coefficients are known and do not change over time. The example is in a market with brands in the mature stage of product life cycles [26]. Each firm uses its information-gathering skills to obtain a forecast about the unknown demand (e.g., market research, surveys, and syndicated consumer database). Then each firm makes a forecast about a. Firm 1's forecast of a is $f _ { 1 } ,$ , and firm 2's forecast is $f _ { 2 } .$ We assume that

$$

where $\varepsilon _ { i }$ is normally distributed, independent of the base level of market demand, a, with mean zero and variance σ . When two firms operate separately, they would compete to maximize their individual profits. Prior researchers in marketing have conjectured that the advantages of one mode of conduct over the other may help firms alleviate price wars [4,28,33]. For example, in the fragmented industries, some firms behave in the Bertrand mode, and other firms behave in the Stackelberg mode (e.g., [6,12,27]). Our interest in this paper is to compare the value of information sharing when firms behave in a Bertrand mode with the value of information sharing when firms behave in a Stackelberg mode. In what follows, we first assume a Bertrand mode where both firms make their decisions simultaneously, rather than sequentially, to maximize their respective profits. Then an analysis of the Stackelberg mode follows.

In the Bertrand mode, there is no price leader and both firms make pricing decisions independently of each other to maximize their individual profits. For the Bertrand mode, we derive the optimal prices and profits under two scenarios: non-information sharing and information sharing.

Proposition 3.3.1. When duopoly firms behave in the Bertrand mode, information sharing is not a dominant strategy to employ. That is, $E [ \pi _ { i } ^ { I B } ]$ could be equal to, larger or less than $E [ \pi _ { i } ^ { N B } ] , i = 1 , 2 $

Proposition 3.3.1 reveals some important results. When different firms have asymmetric forecasts about market demand, information sharing is not always beneficial to both firms under the Bertrand mode. If the forecast accuracy of firm 1 $( \sigma _ { 1 } \mathrm { o r } t _ { 1 } )$ is significantly different from the forecast accuracy of firm $2 \left( \sigma _ { 2 } \thinspace 0 \mathrm { r } \ : t _ { 2 } \right)$ , sharing firm 2's inaccurate information will lead to worse information for firm 1. In the meantime, firm 2 also cannot use a side-payment as incentive to induce firm 1 to share its information (i.e., side-payment is defined as a payment made by one or more parties in an agreement to other parties, to induce them to join the agreement), because even the firm $2 \mathrm { : }$ increased profit cannot offset the firm 1's lost profit due to information sharing. Thus when firms engage in information sharing, they need to plan their information sharing carefully. Only when both firms can benefit from information sharing, $\mathrm { i . e . , } E [ \bar { \pi _ { i } ^ { I B } } ] > \bar { E } [ \pi _ { i } ^ { N B } ] , i = 1 , 2$ , would they like to pursue information sharing voluntarily.

Our finding, which indicates that when firms behave in the Bertrand mode, information sharing is not a dominant strategy to employ, is different from prior study [34] which shows that information sharing always dominates non-information sharing for firms behaving in the Bertrand mode. The rationale is that while Vives [34] assumed that different firms have symmetric forecast information about market demand, we assumed that different firms have asymmetric forecast information about market demand, which reflects real business situation in practice. From a managerial perspective, our research provides important guidance to duopoly firms contemplating information sharing in the Bertrand mode, pointing out that engaging in information sharing under such a mode may not always be beneficial to each firm. Thus, potential partners should decide carefully whether to enter into such an information sharing mode and under what conditions they should enter when their individual forecasts are different. If their forecasts are significantly different and thus a Pareto situation (i.e., win– win result) cannot be achieved for both firms, information sharing equilibrium should not be reached.

In the fragmented industries, while Bertrand mode is a common behavior, many reported incidences also show that some firms do behave in the Stackelberg mode [6,12,27]. ## 4. Analysis

## 4.1. Profit comparison between the Stackelberg and Bertrand modes

As in Raju and Roy [26], different modes allow firms to withstand market uncertainty differently. If a mode (Stackelberg or Bertrand) can help firms withstand market uncertainty better, that mode may have long term survival and propagation in the business market. By comparing firms' expected profits between the Bertrand mode and the Stackelberg mode with the consideration of market information, we can obtain Proposition 4.1.1. Proofs are given in Appendix F.

Proposition 4.1.1. In the information sharing case, the expected profits of duopoly firms behaving in a Stackelberg mode are higher than the expected profits of duopoly firms behaving in a Bertrand mode. That is, $E [ \bar { \pi } _ { i } ^ { I S } ] > E [ \bar { \pi } _ { i } ^ { I \bar { B } } ] , i = 1 , \bar { 2 }$ . However, in the non-information sharing case, neither mode has a competitive advantage. That is, $E [ \pi _ { i } ^ { N S } ]$ could be equal to, larger or less than $E [ \tilde { \pi } _ { i } ^ { N B } ] , i = 1 , 2$

Proposition 4.1.1 reveals some novel and counter-intuitive findings. Prior research [10,40,41] shows that in a market with demand certainty, in response to a given price of a competitor, the Stackelberg mode always yields higher profits than the Bertrand mode. In this sense, the Stackelberg mode might be thought to be more effective in coordinating price competition than the Bertrand mode at any circumstances. However, our results show that only if firm's forecast accuracy in the Stackelberg mode is equal to or higher than its forecast accuracy in the Bertrand mode, then firm's profit in the Stackelberg mode is higher than its profit in the Bertrand mode. If firm's forecast accuracy in the Stackelberg mode is slightly less than its forecast accuracy in the Bertrand mode, firm still can achieve a higher profit in the Stackelberg mode because the advantage of more coordinated Stackelberg mode outweighs the disadvantage of less accurate forecast; however, if firm's forecast accuracy in the Stackelberg mode is significantly less than its forecast accuracy in the Bertrand mode, firm will achieve a lower profit in the Stackelberg mode because the advantage of more coordinated Stackelberg mode cannot offset the disadvantage of less accurate forecast. In general, although the Stackelberg mode is more coordinated than the Bertrand mode, the more coordinated mode solely cannot help firms achieve a competitive advantage in a market with demand uncertainty. Thus an additional coordination mechanism, information sharing, is needed to help firms achieve this goal. When both firms share their forecasts, firm 1 behaving as a leader can utilize the firm 2's forecast to make a better decision. Then firm 2 acting as a follower will follow the better decision of firm 1 to make a much better decision. As a result, both firms can achieve a higher performance in the Stackelberg mode with information sharing. In the non-information sharing setting, the Stackelberg mode provides no competitive advantage to firms. That is why different modes (Bertrand or Stackelberg) work in the real business markets since real markets are always full of demand uncertainty.

In the next proposition, we study the competitive advantage from the information sharing Stackelberg mode. From our comparisons, we obtain Proposition 4.1.2. Proofs are given in Appendix G.

Proposition 4.1.2. The information sharing Stackelberg mode can help firms achieve a higher profit than any other mode. That is, E[π<sup>IS</sup>] is larger than $E [ \pi _ { i } ^ { N B } ] , ~ E [ \pi _ { i } ^ { N S } ] ,$ , and $E [ \pi _ { i } ^ { I B } ] , i = 1 , 2$ , respectively.

Proposition 4.1.2 indicates that the information sharing Stackelberg mode provides a dominant competitive advantage to both firms. This result makes sense as the information sharing Stackelberg mode is a more effective mechanism to alleviate the price competition between two firms than other modes, allowing both firms to achieve higher profits. The important managerial implication is that when market information becomes transparent to all firms through information sharing and collaboration, the information sharing Stackelberg mode is the best strategy for the competing firms to employ.

## 4.2. Effect of product differentiation on the value of information sharing

We now study how product differentiation influences the value of information sharing for both firms. Based on our results, we come up with Proposition 4.2.1. Proofs are given in Appendix H.

Proposition 4.2.1. The value of information sharing for each firm $i ( i =$ 1,2) always increases as the products differentiation between the two firms decreases (increasing c).

Proposition 4.2.1 indicates that information sharing becomes much more valuable when the products between the two firms are less differentiated. This is because information sharing effectively improves information accuracy and alleviates market competition resulting from product competition. Thus information sharing becomes more valuable to each firm as market competition increases due to less differentiated products. However, information sharing becomes less valuable if the products between the two firms are highly differentiated. The rationale is that when the products are highly differentiated (i.e., two products are highly different), the shared information from the different product becomes less valuable to this product. Therefore, the important managerial implication is that when duopoly firms are selling an identical product or highly competitive products, acquiring more sophisticated and detailed information about the market demand through information sharing and collaboration becomes mandatory to them. For example, in the airline market, airlines actively implement the strategy of information sharing and collaboration in order to improve their decision-makings and performances.

## 4.3. Effect of price effect on the value of information sharing

We here study how price effect influences the value of information sharing for both firms. Based on our results, we come up with Proposition 4.3.1. Proofs are given in Appendix I.

Proposition 4.3.1. The value of information sharing for each firm $i ( i =$ $1 , 2 )$ always increases as price effect on consumers' purchases decreases (decreasing b)

Proposition 4.3.1 reveals that information sharing is more valuable to firm's profitability when price effect on consumers' purchases is lower. The rationale is that lower price effect contributes to the improvement of firm's profitability; in the meantime, information sharing also helps improve the firm's performance. Thus lower price effect helps improve the value of information sharing to firms. Proposition 4.3.1 leads to important managerial guidance. (1) Firm managers should use any operational means to lower the price effect on consumers' purchases, since lower price effect always is beneficial to firms. In the business market, this can be achieved by offering non-price differentiating values to the consumers, such as enhancing product quality, building strong brand, and improving customer assistance and relationship management. According to the customer equity and marketing-based asset theory, higher product quality, stronger brand, and improved customer relationship management (CRM) all play an important role in helping firms build long-term asset [30], add more values (e.g., brand equity, customer equity, customer satisfaction and relationship, customer loyalty, positive word-of-mouth effect, etc.) to firms, and improve firm performances [13,29,32]. (2) Firms offering strong brand products or having high level of customer satisfaction (which can be obtained from The American Customer Satisfaction Index — ACSI) should actively engage in information sharing and collaboration.

## 5. Information distortion

The analysis so far has assumed that if forecast information is shared, both firms would share their forecasts truthfully. Sharing truthful information is reasonable only if both firms have no incentive to distort their information or if one firm can authenticate the other firm's information, and vice versa. However, more often, as each firm acts independently, there is a good motivation for the firm to distort its forecast information to maximize its own profit. For instance, one firm can share false information with its rival and then exploit the information at the expense of the rival.

In this section, we suppose that both firms would like to share their forecasts. The following results show that both firms have a motivation to distort their respective forecasts while sharing information. Thus we come up with Proposition 5.1. Proofs are given in Appendix J.

Proposition 5.1. In the information sharing equilibrium, the expected profit of firm $\ ( i = 1 , 2 )$ is higher if it shares an overstated forecast $\tilde { f } _ { i } { > }$ ${ \it f } _ { i } ( i = 1 , 2 )$ and the rival firm j $( j = 3 - i )$ believes the overstated forecast $f _ { i }$ and shares its true forecast $f _ { j } ( j = 3 - i )$ , compared to the equilibrium where both firms share their forecasts truthfully. Additionally, firm i's expected profit is increasing in $\tilde { f } _ { i } ( i = 1 , 2 )$

Proposition 5.1 shows that both firms have a motivation to distort their individual forecasts while sharing information. The rationale is that when firm 1 overstates its forecast and firm 2 believes this overstated information and shares its true forecast, firm 2 will set a higher price. As a result, firm 2 will suffer a profit lost due to reduced demand. However, firm 1 will use the true forecasts (e.g., a combination of its own and firm 2's true forecasts) to set a lower price and benefit from it. As firm 2 believes the distorted forecast. firm 1 always benefits from the distorted information sharing. The profit function of firm 1 indicates that the greater the distorted forecast, the more firm 1 can make. As a result, both firms always attempt to distort their forecasts to reach a maximum in order to maximize their individual profits.

When firms are able to control their own prices, have their own private information about market demand, and have the ability to manipulate the information, both of them have a motivation to distort their respective forecast information in order to obtain extra profits. Information distortion decreases the value of information sharing and makes the shared information become useless. As a result, neither firm 1 nor firm 2 believes in the shared information and would like to engage in information sharing. However, as shown in the information sharing arrangements, sharing information can bring extra benefits to firms. Firms are recognizing that information sharing is becoming a significant part of senior managers' agendas for improving firm performance [16]. Thus the important question is how to ensure that any distorted information can be eliminated and thus information sharing equilibrium can be reached? Since each firm does have a motivation to distort its private information while sharing information.

## 5.1. Valuable mechanism for sharing information

Fortunately, the ability to manage and track the flow of shared information across supply chain members has been greatly enhanced by recent technological advances. Therefore, to ensure that true forecast information can be shared and any distorted information can be eliminated, we propose an effective and valuable mechanism for duopoly firms to employ: firms can develop an information sharing network and use the Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) system to communicate their demand data directly. Since these data sources provide the truthful information about the state of market demand [11], information distortion will be eliminated and truthful information can be shared. If there is any confidential and sensitive data, a nonnegative matrixfactorization technique can be employed to protect the original data and preserve its overall characteristics (please see details about matrix-factorization technique in Wu et al. [38] and Xu el at. [39]). Airline industries are using such an information sharing mechanism in their businesses [31].

## 6. Conclusions and managerial insights

Due to changing markets, product availability, and continuing shifts in consumer preferences, the management of information sharing is increasingly viewed as a source of strategic advantage for participating parties. This research focuses on the relationship between the information-sharing modes and firm performances in a duopoly market. We posit that firms engage in information sharing to ultimately increase profitability. It is therefore necessary to examine the effect of information-sharing on firm performance when firms behave in various modes. This research develops a conceptual framework for understand ing the effects of information sharing on the performances of participating firms on various scenarios. In this research, we consider a duopoly market where market demand is uncertain and each firm needs to make decisions with incomplete information. Under this setting, we examine the value of information sharing to the duopoly firms. Specifically, we first derive optimal strategies for each firm under four scenarios: (1) non-information sharing Bertrand mode, (2) information sharing Bertrand mode, (3) non-information sharing Stackelberg mode, and (4) information sharing Stackelberg mode. We then compare these scenarios to investigate the effect of information sharing on the performance of each firm. Our results reveal that information sharing has no uniform effect on firm performance and various information sharing modes are differentially associated with firm performance. For these firms behaving in the Bertrand mode. both firms can benefit from information sharing only under certain conditions. However, for these firms behaving in the Stackelberg mode, both firms always can benefit from information sharing. Further, we examine how the value of information sharing is moderated by the nature of firm, the product brand, and the degree of product differentiation. Our results show that when two firms have high level of customer satisfaction, offer products with reputable brand names, or sell products that are strongly substituted in the market, information sharing becomes significantly important and imperative to both firms. Particularly if firms behave in a Stackelberg mode, the value of information sharing further increases. However, when two firms share their information, both of them have an incentive to distort their respective information. The information distortion may make the shared information become useless. Therefore, we propose an effective and valuable mechanism for two firms to adopt, thus eliminating the possibility for information distortion and allowing both firms to share their information truthfully. In sum, our research shows that information sharing reflects a competitive advantage that, when implemented correctly, is a double-winning proposition and strategy for firms. From a practical standpoint, our research contributes to the substantial and growing research about the value of information sharing in the business markets. These businesses are able to use the managerial insights from our research to improve their decisions in order to achieve a higher profit. For instance, the duopoly firms can choose to play a beneficial mode while sharing information, thus a Pareto situation can be reached.

Our analysis also might have some limitations. We assumed a linear demand function in this paper. While this demand model has been used extensively in business, it is desirable to examine whether the derived results from our model can be generalized to other nonlinear demands. This research also can be extended in several other directions for future work. First, future research can consider including upstream suppliers/manufacturers in our model. Second, our proposed model was not examined empirically. Future research can empirically test the empirical model with data collected from industries.
