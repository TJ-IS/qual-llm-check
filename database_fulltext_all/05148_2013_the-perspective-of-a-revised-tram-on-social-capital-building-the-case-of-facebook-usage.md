# The perspective of a revised TRAM on social capital building: The case of Facebook usage

ChangHyun Jin

College of Business Administration, Kyonggi University, San 94-6, Iui-dong, Yeongtong-gu, Suwon-si, Gyeonggi-do 443-760, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 2 April 2012 Received in revised form 3 December 2012 Accepted 4 March 2013 Available online 15 March 2013

Keywords: TRAM PTR NTR Social networking sites Facebook User behavior Social capital

## A B S T R A C T

The author explored the factors that affect user’s acceptance of Facebook by analyzing data provided in a survey of Facebook users (n = 346) using hypotheses based on the Technology Readiness and Acceptance Model (TRAM). In addition, he investigated the role of a revised TRAM on social capital building.

Results showed statistically significant support for the hypothesized model, indicating that positive and negative technology readiness (PTR and NTR) play an important role in the formation of perceived ease of use (PEOU), perceived usefulness (PU), and perceived playfulness (PP) as well as in generating the intention to continue using (ICU) Facebook and social capital building. However, NTR did not significantly affect PP.

The study also showed that ICU Facebook was mediated by users’ PEOU, PU, and PP, and that social capital building was mediated by ICU Facebook. Theoretical and practical implications and limitations are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Social networking, which uses a range of social media through social networking sites (SNSs), has few constraints on its growth. The most popular SNS in the USA, Facebook, has attracted more than 900 million users. Its rapid growth has drawn increasing attention to its potential in marketing-oriented industries.

The Technology Readiness and Acceptance Model (TRAM) [11] has been used to explain how consumer readiness to use a new technology influences beliefs about it [4,8,16]. Although SNS have existed for only a few years, their popularity and potential require an effort to identify and measure correlations and relationships among the factors posited by several theoretical models intended to help explain them. Research focusing on the social and psychological impact of social media [13,15] has yet to explain the relationship between technology acceptance on the part of SNS users and the building of social capital. This study was therefore designed to analyze, empirically, the relation between behavioral intentions and the intended outcomes of SNS usage. In particular, using a revised TRAM, the study explored factors affecting the acceptance of SNSs when applying the revised model to social capital building.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. The social networking sites environment

New developments in the IT industry have allowed online networking services based on user-generated content to evolve into SNSs such as MySpace, YouTube, Twitter, and Facebook. SNSs serve as platforms on which people can share ideas, experiences, and opinions.

SNSs are Internet-based social interfaces, which facilitate dissemination of information through familiarity. The social media ecosystem includes blogs, social networking services, message boards, Podcasts, Wikis, etc.; they supplement traditional media by providing communication with transparency. SNSs now provide easy social relations through microblogging media, outlets for opinions, and reflections on personal experiences.

## 2.2. Social capital theory and social networking sites

Social capital consists of resources that are accessed through social interactions; the emphasis on social relations distinguishes social from economic and human capital. They are generated and distributed in social networks, focusing on the role of social capita as an influence on both the development of human capital and the economic performance of firms, geographic regions, and nations. Social organizations, such as networks, norms, and trust, facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit, and social capital involves a mutual sense of reciprocity, participation, and trust that enables groups of people to live and work together successfully. Concepts such as trust and reciprocity play important roles as psychological resources that help us understand how the effects of social capital generate an individual’s social capital. For example, reciprocity involves emotional support and a feeling of sharing and belonging.

Beaudoin and Thorson [1] studied the relationship between the use of mass media, social capital, and social participation, finding that social capital was closely related to social participation as a pro-social behavior. Many studies have focused on two crucial facets of social capital: how individuals perceive others in society and how they are reciprocally connected. A recent study, investigating the role of Facebook in building and maintaining social capital, found that it appeared to play an important role in this process among students [3]. Also, individuals seek to maintain and increase their social networks by using SNSs.

It therefore seemed reasonable to consider elements of social capital when investigating the relationship between the use of SNSs and social capital building; i.e., are SNSs involved in building social capital as a result of social trust, reciprocity, or social participation?

## 2.3. The revised TRAM

Much prior research used TRAM, focusing on analyzing relationships between personality traits and technology acceptance and how consumer readiness to use a new technology influenced user beliefs about it. The objective of IT systems is to help users understand and use them. SNS users’ motivation to use is a desire to communicate with others, share information and content about their lives, and build relationships with others. My study explored the value of the TRAM model by focusing on ITbased functionality (e.g., new methods and content, etc.), provided by an SNS. TRAM’s measurement tools were modified to fit the functionality provided by SNSs, and user perception of usefulness, ease of use, and playfulness served as mediated variables my study. Finally, a focus on social capital building as the intended outcome of SNS usage was followed by a focus on the intention to continue using the system.

Perhaps applying the model to the SNS environment had limited utility. Measuring users’ desire for social connections by the user’s intention to continue using (ICU) may be limited. However, the study provided new results by analyzing the relationship between behavioral intentions and its sub-factors (trust, reciprocity, and participation) associated with social capital building as intended outcomes of SNS usage.

It was important to account for how positive (PTR) and negative technology readiness (NTR) affected perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, and playfulness and enhanced users’ ability to influence the process in desirable directions by revising the TRAM to reflect the new conditions of the SNS environment.

The Technology Readiness Index (TRI) measures an individual’s readiness to use new technology based on four personality traits: optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity; it applies to technology in general. Scores for these four traits differ among individuals, indicating different feelings about various technologies. Of the four, optimism and innovativeness are positive drivers of TR (positive technology readiness or PTR), while discomfort and insecurity are negative (resulting in NTR). Optimism indicates a belief that technology offers increased control, flexibility, learning, and efficiency. Innovativeness indicates a tendency to be a technology pioneer and thought leader. Discomfort indicates a perceived lack of control over technology (a being overwhelmed by it). Insecurity indicates distrust of technology and skepticism about its ability to work properly.

TRAM was the result of combining TAM with TRI [10]; it had been noticed that the personality traits of TRI were closely related to cognitive dimensions, such as PEOU and PU. While explaining the process of IT acceptance, the model suggests that user intention regarding acceptance is determined by PEOU, PU, and PP. The system ETAM (extended TAM) has been suggested as a supplemental model but some have considered it too simple because it overemphasizes user decisions [2,7].

## 3. Conceptual model and hypothesis

## 3.1. The TRI and components of the TAM

Many studies have concentrated on IT-related devices or tools in exploring individual behavior; e.g. [12]. The TRI construct can be viewed as measuring an overall state of mind resulting from a gestalt of mental enablers and inhibitors that determine a person’s predisposition to use new technologies. But the original TRAM did not provide an adequate basis for studying the relationship between SNS users’ technology acceptance and the building of social capital. Therefore, I revised the TRAM to reflect the new conditions of the SNS environment.

Studies using the original TRAM examined the relationship between the components of the TRI and PEOU and PU. Optimism, and innovativeness, as positive drivers of TR, were seen to be closely related to the PEOU and PU of a technology. Discomfort and insecurity as positive drivers of TR are negatively related to the PEOU and PU of a technology. Moreover, some research has found that optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity play an important role in IT usage, and also that TR is a powerful factor with respect to satisfaction and behavioral intentions [9].

Many studies have explored PP as a cognitive factor [5]; it measures the pleasure and satisfaction derived from performing a behavior. It is reasonable to suppose that Facebook users participate in networking activity because the process yields fun, playfulness, and enjoyment. Perceived enjoyment has been shown to have a significant effect on Internet usage. Thus PP was added to the TRAM in my study.

Therefore, the components of TR and PP should have significant effects on consumer adoption and use of SNSs. According to general studies of positive (PTR) and negative technology readiness (NTR), other significant factors include PEOU, PU, and behavioral intention to use as indicators of acceptance. I assumed that the components of interactivity should also be added; therefore these were components of the revised TRAM that were the theoretical basis of my study. This resulted in the following hypotheses.

H1. Positive technology readiness (PTR) significantly influences Facebook users’ perceived ease of use (PEOU) [H1-1], Facebook users’ perceived usefulness (PU) [H1-2], and Facebook users’ perceived playfulness (PP) [H1-3].

H2. Negative technology readiness (NTR) is negatively associated with Facebook users’ perceived ease of use (PEOU) [H2-1], Facebook users’ perceived usefulness (PU) [H2-2], and Facebook users perceived playfulness (PP) [H2-3].

## 3.2. The TAM and the behavioral intention to use SNSs

Intention to continue using (ICU) a technology does not fully explain its use if external variables affect PEOU, PU, and even PP. Therefore, I hypothesized that PEOU affects PU, PP and behavioral intention to use at the same time.

H3. Facebook users’ perceived ease of use (PEOU) has a significantly positive effect on perceived usefulness (PU).

H4. Facebook users’ perceived ease of use (PEOU) has a significantly positive effect on perceived playfulness (PP).

H5. Facebook users’ perceived ease of use (PEOU) has a significantly positive effect on intention to continue using (ICU) Face book.

H6. Facebook users’ perceived usefulness (PU) has a significantly positive effect on intention to continue using (ICU) Facebook.

H7. Facebook users’ perceived playfulness (PP) has a significantly positive effect on intention to continue using (ICU) Facebook.

Social networking sites (SNSs) allow users to build online profiles, share information, join in on civic or community activities, and form relationships with other users. Users of an SNS are typically prompted to identify others in the system with whom they have relationships. Ellison, Heino, and Gibbs also examined the relationship between the use of Facebook and the formation and maintenance of social capital [6]. They found that SNSs help people strengthen their social capital and found that the more people use Facebook the more social capital they are able to build. Users are accustomed to joining Facebook and spending time on the website to keep in touch with old friends and to strengthen bonds with other people and colleagues. It seems reasonable then that ICU Facebook affects social capital building. Similarly, it can be hypothesized that social capital, which is characterized by trust, social participation, and reciprocity, is driven by ICU SNSs (Fig. 1):

H8. Facebook users’ intention to continue using Facebook significantly influences social capital building.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection and sampling

The population of this study was a sample of 346 students who use Facebook – 214 males and 132 females – found through convenience samples of Facebook users; almost every participant then worked in a business-related field. The survey was conducted from November through December 2011. Investigators directly contacted managers of the Korea Banking Institute (this organization provided programs for employees in many industries, including finance, construction, services, and manufacturing). A total of 402 questionnaires were collected. However, 56 questionnaires were deleted either due to a high number of missing values or because the respondents did not use Facebook. Across the sample, in terms of age, 131 respondents were in their 20 s, 103 were in their 30 s, 100 were in their 40 s, and 12 were in their 50 s. In terms of education level, 72% had earned at least a college degree (249) and 97 respondents had only finished high school. A large percentage of respondents (84%) used Facebook for more than one hour every day and 78% had used Facebook for six months to one year. Participants filled out the questionnaires before leaving their classrooms.

## 4.2. Operational definitions of major variables

## 4.2.1. Exogenous variables

TRAM measured the relationship between the personality trait dimensions of the TRI (optimism and innovativeness for PTR, discomfort and insecurity for NTR) and the cognitive dimensions of the TAM. The items are shown in Appendix. Most items were adapted from relevant research; e.g. [6]. Thirteen items were selected from the 36-item TRI posited by Lin and Hsieh and Parasuraman on the basis of factor loadings, item total correlation, and item difficulty, while maintaining a multifaceted scale. Reliability was measured at 0.90 (four items for optimism), 0.80 (three items for innovativeness), 0.86 (three items for discomfort), and 0.77 (three items for insecurity).

## 4.2.2. Endogenous variables

Scale items used in the study were adapted from previous studies to form the revised TRAM [17]; they were: PEOU (perceived ease of use), PU (perceived usefulness), PP (perceived playfulness), and ICU (intention to continue using) Facebook. Reliability was measured at 0.84 (three items for PEOU), 0.84 (three items for PU), 0.90 (three items for PP), and 0.83 (three items for ICU). The components of social capital building were analyzed into three subordinate concepts: trust, reciprocity, and social participation. Social capital building was measured by nine items eliciting respondents’ opinions about trust, reciprocity, and social participation: (1) believability, (2) trustworthiness, (3) fairness for trust, (4) strong solidarity, (5) fellowship, (6) a sense of comradeship, for reciprocity, (7) intention to participate in a non-profit organization, (8) intention to participate in a civic organization, and (9) intention to participate in a social organization or political party. Reliability was measured at 0.86 (nine items for social capital building). All items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale with 1 indicating ‘‘strongly disagree’’ and 5 indicating ‘‘strongly agree.’

It is normally suggested that the mean of skewness and kurtosis should be within the range ${ \sf o f \pm 1 . 9 6 } ,$ , which was satisfied in this study. I also assessed convergent validity using Cronbach’s alpha, as well as composite construct reliability and AVE. I assessed discriminant validity by comparing correlations of components with AVE. As seen in Tables 1 and 2, scale reliabilities were also estimated using Cronbach’s alpha. For all five constructs, Cronbach’s alpha exceeded the standard acceptance norm of 0.70. The AVE also satisfied the standard of greater than 0.5, implying that the measurement indexes exhibited convergent validity.

## 6. Conclusion

## 6.1. Discussion

My study found that some elements of the TRI and the TAM played a critical role in accounting for the formation and strength of social relationships. It also suggested that ICU Facebook is mediated by Facebook users’ PEOU, PU, and PP and that social capital building is mediated by Facebook users’ ICU.

When using an SNS such as Facebook, PTR seems to play a more important role with respect to PEOU than with respect to PU and PP. Users with a strong capacity for adopting new technology seek to initiate and develop SNS usage within their homogeneous groups. NTR negatively influenced PEOU and PU. Although the path coefficients of NTR on PEOU and PU were only marginally significant, the path coefficient of NTR on PEOU was slightly lower than the corresponding path coefficient of NTR on PU. NTR had no impact on PP. The results of my study were similar to those of Walczuch et al. [18]: SNS users did not perceive SNSs as being especially playful.

Consumers are quick to embrace social media as sources of general information that they can easily access and use, and they think doing so is fun. PEOU, PU, and PP proved to have positive effects also on the behavioral intention to use SNSs (for Facebook). It may be concluded, therefore, that social media that are considered to be easy to use and useful are more conducive to satisfying consumer demand. In addition, PU proved to be deeply involved in the behavioral intention to use SNSs, implying that consumers are likely to use SNSs when they think they provide a great deal of needed information.

In order to measure social capital building, the study considered social participation, and reciprocity. Although I did not examine the relationship between these three and the ICU SNSs, additional analysis indicated that reciprocity is a more powerful influence than social trust or social participation (b = 0.32, p < 0.001 for social trust, b = 0.44, p < 0.001 for reciprocity, and $\beta = 0 . 2 5$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for social participation).

ICU Facebook is mediated by Facebook users’ PEOU, PU, and PP, and social capital building is mediated by ICU Facebook.

Gamification is the process of adding video/computer game mechanics, esthetics, and game-style thinking to processes, programs, and platforms that traditionally do not employ such tools. It has become a popular topic across a wide range of industries and in academia in the game-based marketing area. SNSs, as opposed to other forms of Internet usage, are not affected by the absence of technology practices and therefore the discomfort and insecurity associated with NTR are balanced by the playfulness of the experience.

## 6.2. Practical implications

The TRAM integrates individual factors with system characteristics and substantially broadens the applicability and explanatory efficacy of prior models in marketing settings where adoption is not mandated by organizational objectives. In practical terms, the results of my study should be reflected in the development of marketing programs and content corresponding to consumer tastes. It should yield feasible strategies for undertaking successful marketing communication through social media, as it confirmed the relationships between PEOU, PU, PP, and ICU SNSs by measuring the components of the TRI (PTR vs. NTR). The results may be valuable to marketers seeking detailed strategies for capturing consumers who adopt and use SNSs.

Generally, social capital is generated through social interaction. SNSs are popular in contemporary society because they facilitate chain interactions. Thus, the results of the study should encourage SNS marketers to employ techniques through which to reveal SNS users’ characteristics and then to segment consumers into groups with similar characteristics. The results should also benefit social network marketing studies by suggesting crucial strategic directions for social marketers seeking to adopt effective social marketing strategies.

Formulating marketing strategy using SNSs should increase opportunities for SNS users to interact, promoting the development of deeper relationships between customers and the organization. The most important implication of the study is that SNS users become aware of how much needed information they are provided when using such media. In addition, the components of the TRI and the TAM are expected to become key components of SNS service in the future, and thus to play a key role in consumer adoption and use of such media.

## 6.3. Limitations

Despite the demonstrated reliability of its findings, this study suffers from several limitations. First, as argued in previous research, motivations to consume technology and media users may be too complex to be easily discerned. Many researchers have argued that users’ affective states (e.g., moods) are closely related to computer-related task and technology acceptance. Since people can easily become emotionally aroused when exposed to computers, mood has been incorporated into the model as an antecedent to the effort expended on computerrelated tasks. Individual cognitive styles as well as cognitive dimensions (e.g., control, curiosity, absorption) of technology acceptance positively influence user behavioral intentions. Second, there are gaps between SNS user expectations and actual formed relationships. Regarding user responses to new media, services, and IT, individual differences with respect to non-motivational variables (e.g., age as a demographic factor and cultural factors) must be understood to explore this similar topic, because these factors might lead to different responses to uses of specific media and IT.

Another possible limitation of the present study is that consumer segmentation might not be as representative as it could have been because the sample used comprises only one region of one Asian country. In addition, the data were collected in a convenience sampling at several companies, which may have resulted in sampling bias. Regarding the effectiveness of sampling, the study did not resolve the potential problem of sample size and sample segmentation. Indeed, there are several concerns related to sampling issues. The sample size of 346 was relatively small considering the nature of the study. In addition, the sample was limited to Korea Banking Institute managers only, who may exhibit SNS user behavior and motivations that differ from those associated with other consumer segments. Furthermore, with respect to marketing, this study has not specifically examined the use of social media as a marketing channel.
