# More than just noise? Examining the information content of stock microblogs on financial markets

Ting Li<sup>1</sup>, Jan van Dalen<sup>1</sup>, Pieter Jan van Rees<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University Rotterdam, P.O. Box 1738, 3000 DR Rotterdam, The Netherlands

## Abstract

Scholars and practitioners alike increasingly recognize the importance of stock microblogs as they capture the market discussion and have predictive value for financial markets. This paper examines the extent to which stock microblog messages are related to financial market indicators and the mechanism leading to efficient aggregation of information. In particular, this paper investigates the information content of stock microblogs with respect to individual stocks and explores the effects of social influences on an interday and intraday basis. We collected more than 1.2 million stock-related messages (i.e., tweets) related to S&P 100 companies over a period of 7 months. Using methods from computational linguistics, we went through an elaborate process of message feature reduction, spam detection, language detection, and slang removal, which has led to an increase in classification accuracy for sentiment analysis. We analyzed the data on both a daily and a 15-min basis and found that the sentiment of messages is positively affected with contemporaneous daily abnormal stock returns and that message volume predicts 15-min follow-up returns, trading volume, and volatility. Disagreement in microblog messages positively influences stock features, both in interday and intraday analysis. Notably, if we give a greater share of voice to microblog messages depending on the social influence of microbloggers, this amplifies the relationship between bullishness and abnormal returns, market volume, and volatility. Following knowledgeable investors advice results in more power in explaining changes in market features. This offers an explanation for the efficient aggregation of information on microblogging platforms. Furthermore, we simulated a set of trading strategies using microblog features and the results suggest that it is possible to exploit market inefficiencies even when transaction costs are included. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively examine the association between the information content of stock microblogs and intraday stock market features. The insights from the study permit scholars and professionals to reliably identify stock microblog features, which may serve as valuable proxies for market sentiment and permit individual investors to make better investment decisions. ## Introduction

ncreasingly, individual traders move to social media platforms and embrace them as an interactive platform, because they allow users to not only check out information but also express opinions and comments related to companies’ performance. With the abundance of information sources available to individuals, investors become more informed. Scholars and practitioners increasingly recognize the popularity of online investment communities among individual investors and other financial professionals (Antweiler and Frank, 2004; Zeledon, 2009). Stock microblogging, predominantly based on the microblogging service Twitter (www.twitter.com), has been at the forefront of this development. Twitter is a social network platform that allows users to broadcast short messages with up to 140 characters, so-called ‘tweets’. These tweets are visible on a user’s public message board or through third-party applications. Twitter’s public timeline has turned into an extensive real-time information stream with many messages about public companies and trading ideas. The investor community has labeled Twitter and its related third-party applications (e.g., www.stocktwits.com) as ‘a Bloomberg for the average guy’ (Zeledon, 2009).

The rise of stock microblogs signifies financialization, which refers to ‘the increasing dominance of financial actors, markets, practices, measurements and narratives’ (Aalbers, 2015). Lagoarde-Segot (2016) depicts financialization as ‘the joint product of the development of information technologies, the deregulation of economies, and the rise of the shareholder value paradigm.’ Nearly all of the financial innovations that have arisen in recent decades would not have been possible without the support of information technology. Stock microblogs in particular capture the market discussion and have a significant impact on financial markets. As early as 2009, popular media already predicted: ‘Communities of active investors and day traders who are sharing opinions and in some case sophisticated research about stocks, bonds and other financial instruments will actually have the power to move share prices making Twitter-based input as important as any other data to the stock’ (Time, 2009). Here is a lively illustration of how strongly stock markets could react to stock microblogs and the dominant role of information technology in the modern day financial system. On April 23, 2013, the Twitter account of the Associated Press was hacked. Hackers sent a faked tweet saying that President Barack Obama had been injured after explosions in the White House. Within a couple of seconds, this fake tweet sent stock markets down 1% (Standard and Poor’s 500 Index), wiping out \$136 billion in value (Lee, 2013).

What is unique about stock microblogs compared to other similar platforms? Stock microblogs capture the wisdom of the crowd in ways that are different from other stock-related platforms. First, stock microblogs provide a live conversation that updates quickly rather than an archival content as in Wikipedia (Xu and Zhang, 2013). Second, investors do not need to pull out the information by going to a platform to learn about the market information, such as the investor platform Seeking Alpha; instead, the information is pushed to them in a timely manner. Third, diverging from conventional online media (e.g., stock message boards), stock microblogs are unique in their ability to generate, share, and spread information virally. These distinguishing characteristics create a social contagion effect that drives the unparalleled speed of digital information diffusion (Aral and Walker, 2011; Luo et al., 2013).

This paper examines the extent to which stock microblogs contribute to the price discovery process and how they can be used to predict stock market performance and help individual investors make better investment decisions. The question of whether sentiment affects stock markets has long attracted a great deal of attention from academics. Previous literature has studied noise trader sentiment in financial markets and its influence on asset prices (e.g., De Long et al., 1990; Tetlock, 2011). An important question is not whether sentiment has an effect on stock prices, but rather how to measure investor sentiment more accurately and ascertain its effects on the stock market (Baker and Wurgler, 2007). This is largely because qualitative information has not been reflected fully and instantly in market prices (Tetlock, 2011).

With an increasing availability of social media data, there is an emerging stream of studies across Information Systems, Marketing, and Finance disciplines that tap into these data sources and explore the impact of social media on financial market (Chen et al., 2014; Luo et al., 2013; Tirunillai and Tellis, 2012; Xu and Zhang, 2013; Yu et al., 2013). In particular, the information content of stock microblogs with respect to financial markets is a recent research topic that has presented promising results (Oh and Sheng, 2011; Bollen et al., 2011; Sprenger et al., 2014; Zhang and Swanson, 2010). While these studies offer a first indication of the relationship between stock-related microblog messages and stock performance, they are limited in a number of ways. First, previous studies used daily analysis to explore the relationship between microblog features and stock market performance. This gives little answer to whether the information contained in messages is related to the intraday market performance of individual stocks. The real-time nature of microblogs warrants an intraday analysis. Second, previous studies did not explore the mechanism that leads to efficient aggregation of information in stock microblogs. Individual investors that are active on microblogs may have a strong incentive to publish valuable information to maintain or increase their publicly available number of mentions, retweets, and followers factors representing one’s opinion leadership in the community. Previous research found that the properties of users are stronger predictors of information propagation than the properties of the tweets (Yang and Counts, 2010). However, it remains unclear whether, on a large scale, stock microbloggers that produce valuable information would have stronger social influence and affect information dissemination differently, or they simply represent the online equivalent of uninformed noise traders.

In this paper, we study the effects of information content of stock microblogs on stock market performance and the role of social influence. We investigate the relationship between microblog message features (message bullishness, message volume, and message disagreement) and market performance (stock returns, trading volume, and volatility) on an interday basis and an intraday basis. Moreover, we examine the possible mechanisms of the efficient information aggregation on stock microblog platforms by studying the extent to which good investment advice receives greater attention. We find that bullishness affects contemporaneous daily abnormal returns. New information reflected in the microblog messages is incorporated in market prices quickly, as suggested by the intraday results. Message volume can predict 15-min follow-up returns, trading volume, and volatility. Disagreement in microblog messages positively influences stock features, both in interday and intraday analysis. In addition, the results offer an explanation for the efficient aggregation of information on microblogging platforms. Users who provide above-average investment advice are given a greater share of voice through higher levels of retweets and larger influence. Notably, following expert users amplifies the relationship between bullishness and daily abnormal returns. Further, we simulated a set of trading strategies based on the microblog features and the results suggest that it is possible to exploit market inefficiencies even with the inclusion of transaction costs.

Our paper makes a number of contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the current debate on the role of information technology on financialization by further examining how stock microblogs contributes to the stock market; in particular, we study the extent to which the information content of stock microblogs facilitates the price discovery process and can be used by investors to make better investment decisions. Second, we explore the information content of stock microblogs with respect to intraday analysis of individual stocks in a 15-min interval. We go beyond the daily granularity of analysis, and leverage the real-time nature of microblogs to warrant an intraday analysis. Third, our study extends prior research by providing an explanation for the underlying mechanism that leads to the efficient aggregation of information on stock microblog platforms. Fourth, we develop a trading strategy simulation to demonstrate how individual investors can make better investment decisions by using microblog features. The findings of this paper hint at the possibility that social media platforms specialized in financial markets such as stock microblogs may have changed how information is produced, evaluated, and disseminated (Chen et al., 2014). It has far-reaching implications for financial market participants and financialization as a whole.

Methodologically, this study leverages an innovative method from computational linguistics to evaluate the dense, real-time information stream that closely reflects the way investors need to process and interpret information. Specific attention is paid to the pre-processing of Twitter data, which involves the interpretation of the relatively unstructured data, the identification of spam, and the classification of messages by sentiment. To improve the quality of the data for sentiment analysis, we have gone through an extensive process that includes spam detection, language detection, slang removal, and message feature reduction, which has led to an increased accuracy of the classification output. The results permit scholars and financial professionals to reliably identify stock microblog features, which may serve as valuable proxies for investor behavior and social influence.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the theoretical foundations and related work. Second, we describe the data and the method used. Third, we report the results and the findings, and describe the trading strategy. Finally, we discuss the main findings, theoretical and practical implications, and limitations and future research.

## Theoretical background

In this section, we will review the theoretical foundations and related work to address the key question of this paper: To what extent do stock microblogs contribute to individual investors’ price discovery process? We will first discuss the role of individual investors, namely the uninformed noise traders, in stock market. We will then explain what is price discovery and the importance of looking into investor sentiment using qualitative information processing for price discovery. Further, we will discuss how stock microblogs uniquely contribute to the informedness of individual investors.

## The role of individual investors in stock market

The literature in finance often assumes that stock prices are driven by two types of investors: arbitrageurs and noise traders (De Long et al., 1990). Arbitrageurs trade on the basis of the fundamentals and strive to bring prices in line with true value. Noise traders, mostly individuals, on the other hand, trade on pseudo-signals, noise, and other popular trading models and leave their impressions on the Internet. In the finance literature, individual investors are considered as the least informed market participants (Easley and O’hara, 1987; Hirshleifer and Teoh, 2003) and empirical evidence confirms that individual investors pay a significant performance penalty for active trading (Barber and Odean, 2008).

While the survival of noise traders in the long run remains open for debate (e.g., Kogan et al., 2006), there is a growing consensus that noise traders can induce large price movements, and excess volatility in the short run. Keynes (1936) argued that markets can fluctuate wildly under the influence of investors’ ‘animal spirits’, which move prices in a way unrelated to fundamentals. This notion was formalized in a model by Black (1986), theorizing that the trading of biased noise traders can be correlated on either the buy or the sell side of a particular stock and lead to an increase in volatility, because the unpredictability of noise traders’ beliefs creates a risk that deters arbitrageurs from correcting market prices. Other studies found that noise trading induces volatility (Antweiler and Frank, 2004). Sometimes, aggressive selling by individual investors can induce institutional investors to trade. For example, Yuan (2015) found a negative impact on market prices, reducing market returns by 19 basis points on days following attention-grabbing events.

Moreover, De Long et al. (1990) formalized the role of investor sentiment in financial markets. They demonstrated that if uninformed noise traders base their trading decisions on sentiment and risk-averse arbitrageurs encounter limits to arbitrage, sentiment changes will lead to more noise trading, greater mispricing and excess volatility. In fact, prior studies revealed that arbitrageurs are not always successful in bringing prices back in line with fundamentals, which makes it important to study the value of individual investors sentiment.

## Price discovery process

Price discovery is one of the central functions of financial markets. There are a number of different interpretations of price discovery in the market microstructure literature: ‘the search for an equilibrium price’ (Schreiber and Schwartz, 1986), ‘gathering and interpreting news’ (Baillie et al., 2002), ‘the incorporation of the information implicit in investor trading into market prices’ (Lehmann, 2002). These interpretations suggest that price discovery is dynamic in nature, and that an efficient price discovery process is characterized by the fast adjustment of market prices from the old equilibrium to the new equilibrium with the arrival of new information.

Traditionally, the efficient market hypothesis (EMH) states that successive changes in stock prices are independent and conform to some probability distribution. The path of price levels of a stock has no memory, and thus, the past cannot be used to predict future price changes in stocks. Predictability of stock price movements would be evidence of market inefficiency, since it suggests that available information was not already reflected in share prices. Hence, the EMH refers to the notion that stock prices at any time fully reflect all available information in an efficient market (Fama, 1970). The theoretical foundation of the EMH is based on three arguments which rest on gradually weaker assumptions. First, investors are assumed to be rational and therefore to value each stock rationally for its fundamental value. Second, when some investors are not entirely rational, their trades are random and therefore cancel each other out when trading strategies are uncorrelated. Third, investors are irrational in comparable ways; however, they are met in the market by rational arbitrageurs who eliminate their influences on stock prices (Shleifer and Vishny, 1997). The implication of the EMH relates to ruling out the possibility of investors earning profits or returns in excess of equilibrium expected profits or returns, after an adjustment for risk, based on a relevant information subset (Fama, 1970, 1991).

Later, many financial economists and statisticians have begun to believe that stock prices are at least somewhat predictable. These scholars stressed psychological and behavioral elements of stock prices ascertainment. Many of them found that future stock prices are to some extent predictable and could enable investors to earn excess returns on the basis of past stock price patterns as well as certain fundamental valuation metrics (Malkiel, 2003). The emerging field of behavioral finance has challenged both the theoretical foundations and the empirical evidence of the EMH by arguing that financial markets are not efficient and investors are not fully rational. Behavioral finance, which includes concepts from psychology and sociology, stands in sharp inconsistency to much of the EMH (Shiller, 2003). Perfect informational efficient markets are impossible as there would be no incentive for investors to profit from gathering information and trading (Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980). The most enduring critique of the EMH is conditioned upon the behavior of market participants. Psychologists and economists have documented a number of behavioral biases that are ubiquitous to investor decision making under uncertainty, several of which lead to undesirable economic welfare outcomes. The critics of the EMH assert that market participants are often irrational and stock markets are inefficient, exhibiting predictable behavior and arbitrage opportunities.

However, there are several reasons that should prevent one from interpreting the empirical results as an indication that financial markets are inefficient. First, it is important to distinguish statistical significance from economic significance, as market activity includes transaction costs (Malkiel, 2003). Second, numerous predictable patterns that have been documented may simply be an artifact of data mining. The published literature is likely to be biased in favor of reporting such significant effects (Malkiel, 2003). The neoclassical view depicts the valuation of financial assets as a price discovery process in which equilibrium is achieved whenever prices reach a single point that clears out transactions. However, real financial markets are socially embedded in interpersonal networks, geographical territories, physical spaces, social institutions, political structures, and cultural forms (Muniesa, 2014). Muniesa (2014) stated that ‘It is when one attempts to discover, unveil and decipher the hidden position of the single point that one discovers that there are actually myriad possible points, and that their rationale and justification, their existence and their behavior, will depend exactly on the technological effort that one has to make to provoke them’ (p. 70).

## Market information environment for individual investors

Investors respond to different information sources differently. For example, a study found that investors react more slowly to bad news, examining monthly returns following public news headlines (Chan, 2003). Another study suggested that firms’ stock prices under-react to the textual information embedded in news stories, i.e., the fraction of negative words in firm-specific news, and found that individual investors overreact to stale instead of new information, leading to temporary movements in firms’ stock prices (Tetlock et al., 2008). Mizrach and Weerts (2009) found that 55% of the (individual) investors of a public stock-related chat room made profits even after controlling for the transaction costs.

Previous research has shown that investors are subject to sentiment (De Long et al., 1990) and that betting against sentimental market participants is costly and risky (Shleifer and Vishny, 1997). Rational investors, i.e., arbitrageurs, are not as aggressive in forcing prices to fundamental values as the standard finance model would suggest: There are limits to arbitrage (Baker and Wurgler, 2007). According to Shleifer and Vishny (1997) and De Long et al. (1990), an important reason why arbitrage is limited is that movements in investor sentiment are in part unpredictable. One approach to study investor sentiment uses psychological biases of individual market participants to explain investor under-reaction or overreaction to past returns or fundamentals, while another focuses on aggregate measures of sentiment in stock markets (Baker and Wurgler, 2007).

The sentiment of investors is traditionally determined by surveys, in which investment strategists, newsletter writers, or individual investors recommend their stock portfolio allocation. Surveys are, despite their popularity, resource intensive and therefore expensive to conduct. With the outburst of digital platforms, such as (micro) blogs, discussion forums, social network sites, and other types of social media, researchers have examined various applications to mine sentiment from large-scale online data (Chen et al., 2014; Luo et al., 2013; Tirunillai and Tellis, 2012; Xu and Zhang, 2013). This approach holds considerable promise. First, computational analysis of sentiment may be more costeffective, accurate, and rapid to conduct than large-scale surveys. Second, there is support for the claim that sentiment indicators based on these large-scale online data are indeed valid measures of investor sentiment and can predict a variety of socioeconomic phenomena (Bollen et al., 2011).

Recent studies have advocated that qualitative, i.e., textual, information is not reflected fully and instantly in market prices. Tetlock et al. (2008) found that firms’ stock prices under-react to the qualitative information embedded in news stories, i.e., the fraction of negative words in firm-specific news. Moreover, Tetlock (2011) examined stock market responses to public news stories that may contain stale information and found that individual investors overreact to old information, leading to temporary movements in asset prices.

Sources of qualitative data, such as those mentioned above, have been largely unrecognized and neglected by scholars in the financial literature. While quantitative information is easily processed, the complexity of textual information requires more costly and computational linguistic methods to process. One of the most recent and intriguing sources of qualitative information is the massive amount of online usergenerated content. In the context of the stock market, online communities dedicated to financial topics, such as Internet stock message boards, like Yahoo! Finance, provide a timestamped archive of the collective interpretation of information by individual investors.

Twitter, an online microblogging service created in 2006, allows users to publish messages, so-called tweets, with up to 140 characters. Users can post tweets in multiple ways – via the official Web site, SMS, or external (smartphone) applications. They can also subscribe to (i.e., follow) a selection of favorite authors and send tweets of other authors they find interesting to their followers, i.e., retweeting. Twitter is gaining increasing popularity since 2009, with 10% of Internet users visiting twitter.com on a regular basis. As of March 2016, Twitter had more than 310 million monthly active users. On the day of the 2016 US presidential election, Twitter proved to be the largest source of breaking news, with 40 million tweets sent by 10 p.m. that day.

Stock microblogs are different from other stock-related platforms that investors most often use (e.g., investor platform, stock message board). First, message board users who do not actively enter the forum for a particular stock may not become aware of breaking news for a particular company, whereas investors use stock microblogs are usually exposed to the most recent information for the stocks they follow because of the more ticker-like live conversation. Second, microbloggers, compared to message board users, may have stronger incentives to publish valuable information to maintain or increase their publicly available number of mentions, retweets, and followers – factors representing one’s opinion leadership in the community. As such, stock microblogs uniquely contribute to the price discovery process in stock market by better informing particularly the uninformed noise traders.

## Data and methods

## Data collection

The study focuses on the S&P 100 to adequately reflect the entire range of US equities, covering a broad range of industries, while limiting the research to well-known companies that generate a substantial amount of stock microblog messages. In total, 1,278,604 stock-related microblog messages were collected containing the dollar-tagged ticker symbol used for S&P 100 companies, between August 20, 2012, and March 25, 2013. Daily postings range from 287 to 19,375, averaging to 6,111 messages per day with a standard deviation of 3,480 tweets.

Microblog Data Microblogging platform Twitter was chosen as the data source for stock microblog messages as it has the broadest acceptance in the financial community and messages are accessible via the Twitter application programming interface (API).<sup>2</sup> The service was initially designed for short textual messages to be shared via SMS, limiting the length of a tweet to 140 characters. Twitter users added a well-defined mark-up vocabulary to reference other users (‘@user’ syntax), indicate topics (‘#keyword’ syntax), and formulate language to forward other users’ messages (‘RT @user message’ to retweet). Similarly, traders have adopted the convention of tagging stock-related messages by a dollar sign followed by the relevant ticker symbol (e.g., ‘\$GOOG’ for Google). We focus on this explicit market conversation, which allows one to examine the most relevant subset of stock microblog messages and avoid ‘noise.’

Financial Data We obtained daily financial data from Bloomberg for the 100 stocks of the S&P 100 companies and for the S&P index that serves as a proxy for the market.<sup>3</sup> Intraday data were acquired via QuantQuote. Using the same data source, we also obtained the data for the exchangetraded fund, which mimics the measure of the S&P 500 index (ticker symbol SPY) and was used as a benchmark for the market for the 15-min abnormal return analyses.

Analyses will be based on daily time intervals, labeled interday analysis, and 15-min time intervals, labeled intraday analysis.<sup>4</sup> Because of the sparseness of stock-related microblog message posted during 15-min time periods, only Apple Inc. is used in the intraday analyses.

## Feature selection and reduction

To improve the quality of the input data, we first deleted microblog messages with spam, removed non-English messages, and dealt with misspelling and slang language. Next, we sequentially performed 14 pre-processing steps to reduce the microblog message feature space. (1) Messages were identified as either an original tweet or a retweet. (2) Tweets were scanned against an acronym dictionary to translate acronyms into their initial words. (3) We tokenized a number of repeating elements: We replaced all usernames with the token ‘USER|ID’ to count the number of mentions during the sample frame. Next, we replaced all hyperlinks (4), stock tickers (5), number and percentage figures (6), and emoticons (7) with a token, respectively. (8) Diacritics were replaced with their basis letters. (9) Lengthening words by repeating a single character were replaced by three characters to differentiate between the regular usage and emphasized application of the word. (10) Hashtags, words, or phrases prefixed with the number sign ‘#’ were replaced with by a hash token. (11) All messages were lowercased and punctuation, apostrophes, excess spaces, and newlines were removed. (12) Messages were negated using two supplementary methods. (a) We performed a simple negation algorithm introduced by Das and Chen (2007), concatenating the negating word directly to the following word. (b) Messages were POStagged using the Brill tagger implementation by Mark Watson. (13) The Porter stemmer was applied to remove morphological endings from words. (14) We compiled a custom stop word list to remove noise words. ## Sentiment analysis

In order to compare the sentiment signals from stock microblogs to market movements, messages are classified as buy, hold, or sell recommendations; see Table 3 for a few examples. Following prior research, the naive Bayesian classification was employed, which is widely used for supervised text classification. Compared to more advanced methods in computational linguistics, this method is relatively simple (e.g., high replicatability and few arbitrary finetuning parameters) and has consistently shown robust results in the microblog domain while providing a high degree of transparency into the underlying data structure (Sprenger et al., 2014).

Training the Classifier Input for the naive Bayesian method comes from a training set of 2,144 microblog messages, which were manually classified as buy, hold, or sell signals. Roughly a quarter of these messages were considered to be hold recommendations (27.4%). Among the remainder, buy recommendations were more likely (43.0%) than sell signals (29.6%). This indicates that stock microblogs appear to be more balanced in terms of bullishness than Internet message boards where the ratio of buy versus sell signals ranges around 5:1 (Antweiler and Frank, 2004). However, to avoid biased classification, we used a uniformly distributed training set with 1,752 microblog messages annotated as buy, hold, or sell recommendations.

In line with most text classification methods using a manual training set (e.g., Antweiler and Frank, 2004), one primary judge was used. The manual classification was reviewed by uploading the training set to Crowdflower,<sup>5</sup> where a job was created to let every microblog message be manually classified by at least five different trusted contributors. The agreement (i.e., confidence) on individual message classification among the five trusted Crowdflower contributors was 73% on average. The agreed and annotated messages were used as training set. Message Volume Message volume is defined as the natural logarithm of the total number of messages for stock i at time interval $t .$ The log transformation ln $\left( 1 + M _ { i t } \right)$ is equivalent to the transformation of the trading volume for stock i at time interval $t ,$ which allows for computing elasticities and controls for scaling (Sprenger et al., 2014).

Agreement Consistent with Antweiler and Frank (2004), an agreement measure $A _ { i t }$ reflecting the prevailing level of consensus among microblog contributors, is defined for each stock i and time intervals t as:

$$
A _ {\mathrm{it}} = 1 - \sqrt {1 - \left(\frac {M _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {\text { Buy }} - M _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {\text { Sell }}}{M _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {\text { Buy }} + M _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {\text { Sell }}}\right)}\tag{2}
$$

If all messages at a given time interval t are either buy or sell signals, agreement equals 1. For silent periods, without tweet messages for particular stocks, all three tweet features were set equal to zero.

Social Influence In order to examine the influence of highquality investment advice, one measure of quality and four measures of social influence are defined. The sentiment s of buy, hold, and sell signals in individual messages are coded as þ1, 0 and -1, respectively. The quality $Q _ { i t }$ of a microblog message i is defined as the accuracy of this recommendation relative to same-day t returns of the stock in question as: 1 if $s _ { i t } / R _ { i t } > 0$ , 0 elsewhere. Hold messages are ignored in the calculation of quality scores as people searching for investment advice on the Internet will be primarily interested in buy or sell recommendations. Besides the quality of individual messages, the quality of a particular user’s investment advice is computed as the average quality of all sentiment embedded messages posted by this user.

We used four measures of user influence: retweets, mentions, followership, and Kred influence. Retweets can serve as a proxy for the weight given to an individual tweet. Microblogging users frequently forward $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ retweet) messages which they find noteworthy to their followers. To properly identify retweets, the number of forwarded messages is computed by filtering all retweets and matching the 40 characters following the retweet token (i.e., RT) and the name of the original author with all other tweets. Beside retweets, users can increase exposure, and gain credits by mentioning their name (e.g., @name). Therefore, for every username, the number of mentions is extracted. Users of microblogs subscribe to $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ follow) a selection of relevant and interesting authors whose messages appear in reverse chronological order on their public timeline. Thus, the amount of followers is a good indicator of a user’s social influence. The number of followers a particular user has is measured and updated at the time of a (new) message of that user in the sample period. A third-party measure (Kred) is used to determine a user’s social influence. Kred scores use transparent calculations (www.kred.com rules), which reflect trust (the ability to inspire action) and generosity (engaging with others and spread messages) among microblog users. The method uses retweets, replies, mentions, and the number of followers to determine the Kred score. The influence score of a particular user is measured and updated at the time of a (new) message of that user in the sample period.

$$
R _ {i t} = \ln \left(\frac {P _ {t}}{P _ {t - 1}}\right)\tag{3}

## Analysis and results

We first examined the relationship between stock microblog message features and market features using both contemporaneous and time-sequencing analyses. We then investigated the efficient information aggregation by studying whether quality investment advice receives greater attention on stock microblog platforms. The results are based on one-day time period for the interday analysis of S&P 100 firms and 15 min for the intraday analysis of Apple Inc. Finally, the potential of microblog bullishness for active trading are explored, under different cost structures.

The most evident question in time series analysis of microblogs and the market is whether message sentiment can help predict returns. Table 6 shows, on the one hand, that there is not much connection between bullishness and returns, nor between bullishness and trading volume or volatility. On the other hand, previous day returns have a positive and significant effect on bullishness $( \hat { \beta } = 0 . 0 3 0 $ p\0:01). Thus, returns affect bullishness in stock microblogs, but not vice versa. For the intraday analysis, the results in Table 7 exhibit no associations between bullishness and returns. Yet, trading volume is seen to have a positive effect on bullishness $( \ddot { \beta } = 0 . 0 4 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

In contrast to Antweiler and Frank (2004), message volume is not found to be related to daily stock returns, though it does affect future trading volume $( \ddot { \beta } = 0 . 4 3 6 , \ P < 0 . 0 1 )$ and volatility $( \hat { \beta } = 0 . 0 5 2 , \ P < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Reversely, trading volume and stock volatility are positively influenced by previous day message volume. The dynamic interplay between these measures suggests blogging intensity on the one hand and trading activity and volatility on the other hand enforce each other. Previous day volatility leads to increased message volume, confirming that uncertainty causes investors to exchange information and consult their peers.

Results for 15-min intervals in Table 7 show that lagged message volume is positively and significantly associated with trading volume $( \hat { \beta } = \overset { \cdot } { 0 . 4 } 5 2 , \quad \overset { \cdot } { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ and volatility ( <sup>^</sup>b ¼ 0:047, p\0:01). The effect of returns on message volume is statistically significant $( \ddot { \beta } = 0 . 0 2 8 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , but quite small in magnitude. One-period and four-period lagged trading volume seem to predict the current message volume to increase. Further, there is a strong and significant effect of volatility on trading volume that persists over an hour. The stronger estimates for both interval periods are typically from the market features to the number of messages, rather than the reverse direction.

Evidence from the interday analysis, furthermore, suggests that disagreement among investors does lead to higher trading volumes $( \hat { \beta } = - \bar { 0 . 1 0 2 } , \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The opposite relationship also holds for one and two trading days ago. The results further shows that disagreement also leads to lower volatility $( \hat { \beta } = - 0 . 0 1 9 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which is in line with the results of the contemporaneous regressions. Similar negative effects are observed in the reverse relations: previous day trading volume $( \ddot { \beta } = - 0 . 0 0 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and lagged volatility (b<sup>^</sup> ¼ -044, p\0:01) both contribute to disagreement. Results for the intraday analysis in Table 7 are consistent with these findings: lagged disagreement stimulates trading volume and volatility, while the latter in turn promote disagreement in online messages.

Dynamic relations In order to further support our timesequencing analysis, we estimated structural VAR models for both the mean interday and the intraday microblog and stock features. Specification tests indicated that a single lag is appropriate for the interday analysis, and three to six lags for the intraday data; by way of compromise, we selected four lags for the intraday analysis. Upon estimation of the models, impulse response functions were derived to illustrate the dynamic interactions in the system. As an example, Figure 3 shows the impulse response functions for the intraday model following a shock in message volume. The largest response to this impulse is from trading volume, which slowly tapers off throughout the day (the horizontal axis represents 15-min intervals). Stock volatility likewise shows a significant response, but the effect is small. As a result of the dynamic interrelations in the VAR model, bullishness reacts after a few quarters with a slightly negative effect. The same holds for abnormal returns, which show a negative delayed response, though insignificant at the 5% level. Message agreement does not respond to shocks in message volume. ## The effect of social influence

In this section, we explore whether good investment advice receives greater attention. First, we investigate whether one can identify above-average advisors (e.g., market mavens) and whether these users have more social influence in the online community through higher levels of retweets, followers, mentions or Kred influence. Second, we examine whether pieces of information (i.e., individual messages) by expert users may be weighted more heavily and accordingly strengthen the relationship between bullishness and market features.

The frequency distribution of users and messages shows that participation is highly skewed: 1.6% heavy users are responsible for more than 60% of all contributions, while two thirds of all users have posted only one stock-related message in the sample period. The higher-frequency users appear to be better investment advisors in terms of average quality.

Table 8 provides contemporaneous regressions for daily (interday) and 15-min (intraday) time intervals. The lefthand side of Table 8 shows that the effect of higher-qualityweighted bullishness messages on (abnormal) returns is more than four times as large as the effect of Kred influence and followership sentiment messages on these market features. However, the results illustrate that none of the effects of weighted bullishness messages and trading volume and volatility is significant. The results provide support for the statistically significant strengthening of the relationship between quality-weighted bullishness and market features, consistent with the data measured at daily frequency.

Turning to the right-hand side of Table 8, the findings show no evidence of statistically significant estimates for weighted bullishness relationships on abnormal returns, except in the case of quality-weighted bullishness $( \hat { \beta } = - 0 . 0 3 8 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ . Thus, on the one hand, none of the expert user proxies amplify the relationship between microblog message bullishness and (abnormal) returns. On the other hand, the associations with trading volume and volatility are strengthened through followers, mentions, and Kred influence. Therefore, it can be concluded that leveraging the information of expert users who have a larger followership, have higher levels of mentions, or have above-average Kred influence, can be used to amplify the relationship between bullishness and trading volume or volatility. Following knowledgeable investor’s advice results in more power in explaining changes in market features for 15-min interval data.

Table 9 provides time-sequencing regressions to explore whether weighted microblog message bullishness changes precede or follow stock price changes. The left-hand side of Table 9 shows that there is not much connection between unweighted bullishness and daily returns. Yet, previous day bullishness weighted for the level of retweets significantly amplifies the predictive (i.e., lagged) relationship with stock returns (b<sup>^</sup> ¼ 0:027, p\0:05). Sentiment weighted for retweets two days ago is associated with negative returns $( \ddot { \beta } = - 0 . 0 2 4 , \ p < 0 . 0 \dot { 5 } )$ . The predominantly nonsignificant findings are in line with contemporaneous regression estimates and suggest that weighted bullishness measures do not contain much predictive information. The movement of the stock returns are generally in the same direction and of the same size as the movement of the market index.

Data measured for 15-min intervals (right-hand side of Table 9) show that bullishness and weighted sentiment measures do not contain predictive information with respect to intraday returns. Although one-period lagged bullishness weighted with quality reports a statistically significant coefficient $( \hat { \beta } = - 0 . 0 4 3 , \ p < 0 . 1 0 )$ , the estimated sign is negative. The findings do not reveal statistically significant effects of the weighted expert user proxies on bullishness and intraday returns. In addition, the nonsignificant results in the right-hand side column suggest that the movements of the 15-min time period returns are uncorrelated with the movements of the market index.

## Trading strategy

Preceding analyses have explored the relationship between microblog features and stock performance from the aggregate and market perspective. The results provide general insights into the extent that stock microblogs carry information about future stock performance. These results may be considered less useful, however, by individual traders who seek financial returns through active portfolio management based on available information, including information from stock microblogs. This section evaluates the performance of trading strategies based on microblog message features. Trading strategies vary with respect to choice of microblog features considered, portfolio size, length of the feature history, and applicable cost structures. Below, we detail the trading strategies and discuss their outcomes.

Assuming a fixed initial budget of \$100,000, the trading strategies involve buying and selling stocks based on observed stock microblog features. Trading strategies differ with respect to the number of stocks held in portfolio $( n S t o c k s \dot { = } 1 , . . . , 5 )$ , the length of the feature history applied $( n L a g s = 1 , . . . , 7 { \mathrm { ~ d a y s ) } }$ , and the tariff structure (no trading costs, fixed transaction costs, and combined fixed and variable costs). The strategies are evaluated for microblog bullishness and agreement. Measures of stock microblog features are constructed as averages over the selected history. Each day at market opening (09:30), each strategy compares the stocks in portfolio with the best scoring stocks in terms of past microblog features. Stocks in portfolio and in the microblog feature top-nStocks are kept; stocks in portfolio but not in today’s microblog feature top-nStocks are sold; and stocks in today’s microblog feature top-nStock but not in portfolio are bought. When buying, the available budget is equally divided over the stocks and only whole stocks (no fractions) are bought. The number of shares to buy is determined as the floored value of the available budget per stock divided by the stock’s market price at opening, after correcting for transaction costs involved. Remaining budget is not invested, but added to next day’ available budget. When selling stocks, all shares are sold against their market price at opening. The revenues are added to the budget available for investment, again after correcting for transaction costs. Next day, the feature measurement is repeated and the implied stock transactions are again compared to the existing portfolio. The implemented time period is August 20, 2012, to March 24, 2013, the portfolio is liquidated on March 25.

We considered three cost structures: (1) zero cost; (2) fixed cost (i.e., a fixed \$4.95 per stock transaction); and (3) fixed and variable costs (i.e., a combined fixed \$6.00 per transaction with a variable 0.08% of total transaction value, capped at a maximum of \$100).<sup>7</sup> The performance of the trading strategies is compared to the result of investing in the S&P 100 market index at zero cost, amounting to þ5:05% over the same period. Table 10 summarizes the results of the trading strategies, reporting overall (percentage) returns on the invested amount for different strategies defined by portfolio size, microblog feature history, and the three different cost structures.

In the absence of transaction costs, almost all trading strategies based on message bullishness yield returns in excess of the market index. Exceptions occur when trading single stocks on a two- to four-day history, or when trading based on a two-day history. Larger portfolios with three or more stocks tend to show higher overall growth rates than smaller portfolios, with the þ24:45% return of a single-stock portfolio and a seven-day bullishness history as the exception. Similar excess returns are observed when trading on microblog agreement (e.g., a þ22:92% return on a portfolio of four stocks and a one-day feature history). Here, the returns are seen to decrease when the feature history increases, particularly for smaller portfolios.

The performance of our trading strategies strongly depends on the consideration of transaction costs. For the fixed costs scenario, most bullishness-based portfolios tend to yield positive excess returns when considering a single lag history (þ10:96%) or a history of more than four days (þ15:89%). Agreement shows even higher excess returns (þ18:72%), which gradually erode the longer the feature history. It is noted that these results are less advantageous when the initial investment is smaller, and the impact of trading costs is comparatively larger. However, assuming the relatively expensive intermediary, who combines fixed and variable costs, only two incidental bullishness strategies and no agreement-based strategies lead to returns in excess of the market. In addition, we explored the value of expert information by trading based on quality-corrected bullishness and agreement measures. The results of these trading strategies are largely consistent with those obtained for the unadjusted measures, with mild evidence of a shift in favor of strategies based on a shorter microblog feature history. Furthermore, we compared the performance of the trading strategies based on microblog features with the ones based on past abnormal stock returns. In the absence of trading costs, strategies based on microblog features appear to yield large overall growth rates. Interestingly, trading strategies based on one-day lagged bullishness or one- or two-day lagged agreement outperform strategies based on abnormal returns.

In all, the results suggest that stock microblog-based feature indicators are informative about future stock returns. Sentiment-based trading strategies yield higher returns than the market index in most of the cases. They yield higher returns than strategies based on abnormal market performance, when adopting a short sentiment history of one or two days, depending on the microblogging feature applied. The results further indicate that it may be challenging to monetize the excess value suggested by sentiment trading due to transaction costs, which particularly affects smaller investments.

## Discussion and conclusions

## Main findings and contribution

Stock microblogs have become a vibrant online platform to exchange and discuss trading ideas and other stock-related information. The purpose of this paper is to examine the extent to which the informational content of stock microblogs can contribute to the price discovery process in stock markets by exploring the relationships between three key stock-related microblog message features with stock market performance, and design a trading strategy to leverage the informational content of these messages, exploring the efficient aggregation of information on microblog platforms. We used computational linguistics to determine the key message features, namely message bullishness, message volume, and level of agreement, of nearly 1.3 million stockrelated microblog messages on a daily and 15-min basis. We focused on the S&P 100 companies that generate a substantial amount of stock microblog messages. We explored the relationship between the microblog message features and abnormal returns, trading volume, and volatility and examined the importance of social influence in stock microblogs.

First, we found a daily relationship between bullishness and contemporaneous (abnormal) returns. This result is in line with previous findings in the context of stock message boards (Antweiler and Frank, 2004; Das and Chen, 2007). We also found negative and nonsignificant relationships between lagged bullishness and returns for both time intervals, which suggests that these lagged relationships on microblogging platforms are similar to that in stock message board (Antweiler and Frank, 2004).

Second, the result shows that microblog message volume is consistently significant in explaining contemporaneous abnormal returns, both in the interday and the intraday analysis. The results provide support for a daily contemporaneous association between message volume and (abnormal) returns. We found that changes in previous trading day(s) message-posting volume have no predictive ability for explaining next day abnormal returns. The result also shows that contemporaneous and lagged message volume and intraday stock returns are found to be positively associated, contrary to Antweiler and Frank (2004).

Third, we also found support for the contemporaneous and lagged relationship between message volume and trading volume for both interday and intraday data. This result suggests that an increase in message volume may induce trading from side-lined investors who decide to participate as they learn that other traders share a similar signal. The result also shows that increased message volume is positively associated with contemporaneous volatility from both time intervals. This suggests that noise traders posting messages might induce market volatility, confirming the unpredictability of noise traders’ beliefs creates a risk that deters rational arbitrageurs from correcting stock market prices, leading to excess volatility (De Long et al., 1990).

Fourth, we found that disagreement induces trading and volatility in contemporaneous and time-sequencing regressions. This result is consistent with prior literature (Antweiler and Frank, 2004) and suggests that trading occurs when two market participants assign different values to an asset. We found that the level of disagreement among the messages helps predict subsequent trading volume for data measured at daily frequency as well as 15-min time periods.

Finally, it is beneficial to distinguish expert users from non-experts in a stock microblog setting as following expert investors’ advice results in more precise stock price movement predictions. Our analysis of information diffusion in the form of retweets, followers, mentions, and the Kred influence score shows that users who provide above-average investment advice have more social influence on stock microblogs through higher levels of retweets and Kred influence. Results of interday analysis provide support for the strengthening of the relationship between qualityweighted bullishness and abnormal returns. This finding implies that people could be influenced by individuals with greater stock market knowledge as their advice could be valued for its perceived quality. Intraday analysis shows that the associations of bullishness with trading volume and volatility are strengthened through followers, mentions, and Kred influence. This suggests that leveraging information about expert users who have a higher rate of mentions, or a larger followership, can be used to amplify the relationship between message bullishness and trading volume or volatility. The results, however, show no evidence of statistically significant relationship between weighted bullishness and contemporaneous intraday abnormal returns, except in the case of quality-weighted bullishness. Time-sequencing findings provide evidence that previous day bullishness, weighted for the level of retweets, significantly amplifies the predictive relationship with interday stock returns. Overall results for intraday (weighted) bullishness measures do not contain predictive information with respect to returns, with the exception of quality-weighted bullishness.

In conclusion, our results suggest that stock microblogs contain valuable information that is not yet fully incorporated in the current market indicators. The method proposed in this study can be used by social investing platforms, such as stocktwits.com or Twitter, to extract value from usergenerated content. The results permit scholars and financial professionals to use microblog message features as valuable proxies for investor behavior and belief formation. Organizations can leverage stock microblogs to manage buzz, which may ultimately influence investors and firm performance in the stock market. In addition, the extent that the properties of users are stronger predictors of information propagation, it may affect how firms manage the microblog sphere’s currency – user influence. Firms may re-evaluate the way they work with and leverage social influencers, as our findings suggest that the quality-weighted sentiment strongly impact firm performance in the stock market. Further, the findings suggest that stock microblogs capture key aspects of the market conversation and imply that ’’all that [stock microblog] talk is not just noise’’. In fact, we found that there is substantial relevant information for decision making.

## Limitation and further research

We acknowledge a number of limitations that create interesting avenues for future research. First, we used both daily and 15-min data granularity for the analysis. The downside of using daily intervals is that we compute message features based on messages posted throughout the day and are aggregated at the end of the day. The real-time nature of microblogs warrants an intraday analysis on smaller granularity as market features rapidly adjust to new information. As far as we know, this is the first study that examines the relationship between stock microblog messages and stock market performance using 15-min intraday data. Because of the limited message volume on a 15-min interval, we could only focus on one company for the intraday analysis. Future research could select a handful of stocks that attract sufficient message volume for longer timespan.

Second, to really understand if stock microblogs provide information value that goes above and beyond other information channels, future research needs to control for other news source that might influence the stock market performance. For example, one could collect news data from Lexis-Nexis database for news articles about each of the 100 companies in our sample and follow prior research to conduct a content analysis to quantify the language used in news stories following the literature on stock market responses to news content (Antweiler and Frank, 2004; Das and Chen, 2007). One could also collect editing history data from Wikipedia for each of the 100 companies and control for firm online visibility by collecting Google’s search volume index data (Xu and Zhang, 2013).

Third, large numbers of observations often lead to statistically significant results despite high variance among financial measures. Thus, it is possible that certain relationships may break down when fundamental economic conditions change or that the apparent patterns in the data may be due to pure chance. One needs to be careful when interpreting the results. Future research can benefit from replicating the study and examine whether the patterns would hold under different market conditions (e.g., bullish market versus bearish market) for each stock.

Finally, people that are active on stock microblogs are mostly day traders including individual and institutional investors. Most financial features that are considered, such as returns and volatility, are only available as aggregate market indicators. However, one could replace trading volume with the number of trades of different size categories to distinguish between small and institutional investors. In addition, further research can use market capitalization to study whether the contemporaneous and lagged effects are stronger in small versus large stocks, since the former are held more by local investors and their valuations are more likely to be affected by sentiment.
