## a b s t r a c t

To bring about intended use of a technology amongst a target user group, the technology must be a proper match with the task at hand. We learn from the <sup>fi</sup>t literature that users are motivated to use a technology because they perceive it to be a better <sup>fi</sup>t over and above alternative methods. This article re<sup>fl</sup>ects upon technological developments in information systems, particularly those that use location information as their central component for providing services. By drawing on locatability, we are able to recast the traditional <sup>fi</sup>t models by differentiating between under-, over-, and ideal <sup>fi</sup>t conditions. A wireless laboratory experiment with 112 subjects tests how differing fit levels impact individual performance across various tasks performed with locatable technology. For the experiment, a wireless environment is created that provides faster transmission rates and better localization accuracy than any currently existing cellular phone infrastructure. Findings conclude that subjects perceive differences between under- and ideal <sup>fi</sup>t conditions when exposed to tasks that include localization components.

## 1. Introduction

Task and technology are key factors in information systems success and research [10,38], and because these two key factors interact, new technologies have the ability to change the nature of tasks and potentially create new tasks. For example, the Web changed the nature of shopping and created new tasks such as Web design. Mobile technologies threaten to be just as disruptive as the Web because they free people from tethered systems (e.g., landline phones, desktop computers) and give them ubiquitous access to people and information. Critical everyday tasks, such locating resources, contacting a person, or accessing data are all potentially changed by mobile technologies. As these tasks are often the backbone of economic activity, it is important that we understand how task and technology interact in the new era of communication and information ubiquity.

Along with the emergence of mobile technology, the question arises whether the interplay between task and technology has remained the same or has changed. Finding places or people, for example, has always been a challenging task. With the advent of mobile technology, technology is able to expedite this task by using a new data component: location information. Navigational services are available that use location information to simplify our lives while traversing familiar as well as unfamiliar roads; mobile applications that <sup>fi</sup>nd the nearest gas station, garage, or restaurant, are gaining increasing popularity [48]; and applications, such as “friend <sup>fi</sup>nder” [12,16] or “Boost” [37], notify cellular phone users if friends are in their geographical vicinity. How well does the technology <sup>fi</sup>t the tasks in mobile environments? Studying these situations from a revised task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t angle is necessary. “The old computing was about what computers could do; the new computing is about what users can do. Successful technologies are those that are in harmony with users' needs. They must support relationships and activities that enrich the users' experiences” [[39], p. 2].

We pursue multiple objectives. First, we re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon technological developments in information systems, particularly those that use location information as their central component for providing services. These services, also called location-based services (LBS), are relatively new and–even though projected to be the “killer application” of m-commerce [34]–have not yet achieved the forecasted market penetration.

Second, we revisit traditional <sup>fi</sup>t models with regards to mobile locatable information systems. In nearly every case, the <sup>fi</sup>t between the task at hand and the support provided by the technology to solve it is a critical factor for systems' success [19]. Individuals using technologies that are seen as too complex or lacking in relevance to the individual's needs make them feel unsuccessful overall [39]. Nevertheless, software developers, seeking to meet the needs of a wide range of customers, typically provide more functionality than the majority of customers require. There is an inherent danger that this ‘over-<sup>fi</sup>t’ degrades task performance as users spend too much time bypassing unneeded functionality. Also, there can be tasks that lack appropriate supporting technology, which results in ‘under-<sup>fi</sup>t. In contrast, when task and technology form an ‘ideal <sup>fi</sup>t’, there should be (at least theoretically) task performance improvement. As past research has typically focused on <sup>fi</sup>t versus non-<sup>fi</sup>t conditions, we conceptualize <sup>fi</sup>t as a trichotomous variable, differentiating between under-, over-, and ideal <sup>fi</sup>t.

And third, we examine ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness gains with regards to differing levels of technology <sup>fi</sup>t and also with regards to various kinds of tasks. As past task taxonomies have failed to incorporate aspects that locatable information systems are able to facilitate, our objective is also to examine this technology applied to a series of location-based tasks. We conduct a wireless laboratory experiment with 117 subjects that is characterized by technical capabilities that cannot be found in current practice. It provides faster transmission rates and more accurate localization techniques than any currently available cellular phone infrastructure.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We elaborate on mobile and locatable technologies <sup>fi</sup>rst, followed by a re<sup>fl</sup>ection upon the <sup>fi</sup>t model, and how <sup>fi</sup>t has been measured within IS scholarship. Then, we discuss tasks that can be solved by this new form of technology and incorporate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings into a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>t model. In subsequent sections, we operationalize the proposed model and test it in an experimental setting.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Mobile locatable devices

Recent developments in information technology gave birth to the era of mobile devices that can range from wireless laptops and wireless personal digital assistants (PDA) on one end, to Blackberries, cellular and smart phones on the other. Devices of this category have in common the notion of mobility—one of the fundamental characteristics of mobile technologies in general (e.g., [25,28,31,40,55]). Mobility provides untethered communication and convenience [26]. In the past, information systems were considered tethered, i.e., they were bound to stationary locations. Even in the era of e-commerce, computers were still dependent upon the availability of wired outlets. Nowadays, in the era of m-commerce, information systems are portable and hence are not anymore geographically bound. They can easily be carried around, and–suf<sup>fi</sup>cient coverage assumed–can be used to access the network. Additionally, they can be reached through the network at any point in time, at any location. Highly mobile devices are compact, lightweight, have high bandwidth, and can be used anywhere around the globe (e.g., a 3G mobile phone). Worldwide, the number of mobile subscribers reached over two billion by the end of 2005 and is predicted to rise to 3.96 billion by 2011 [35]. In the same vein, mcommerce, i.e., business generated through the usage of mobile devices, is expected to grow from USD 22.2 billion in 2005 to USD 88 billion in 2009 [15]. Increasingly, people are online when riding in a car, eating at a restaurant, or waiting for a train.

One of the most cited characteristics of mobile devices, apart from mobility, is their ability of providing locatability [7,26,36,40,44]. Locatability, or the capability of an information processing device to determine its geographic position, is commercially available in the form of location-based services (LBS). Among the most popular cited location-based services examples are navigational services that have been available for several years now. It is conceivable that a future system could alarm you of an upcoming traf<sup>fi</sup>c jam and calculate the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient route around it, assist in <sup>fi</sup>nding the nearest and cheapest gas station along the way, or might receive discounted gas rates from the gas stations you are passing by.

For any localization request there are at least two entities involved: one of the entities is always the object of locatability (i.e., it is the entity about which location information is recorded) whereas the other one is the recipient of the location information. Therefore, LBS researchers have distinguished between location-tracking services and location-aware services [5]. Location-tracking services provide information about a user's whereabouts to entities other than the user, while locationaware services supply the user (i.e., information requester) with personal location data. In other words, in the case of locationaware services, the location information causing entity is the recipient; whereas in the case of location-tracking services, an external third party requests and receives location information about another entity. A car navigation system that is used by its driver in order to get real-time directions is an example of a location-aware service.

With mobile penetration rates surpassing 100% in more than 30 countries worldwide [49], it is imperative for researchers and practitioners alike to understand how people operate in these newly created ubiquitous environments. Until now, mobile technologies have been used mainly for voice communications. However, a transition to other “non-voice” services such as sur<sup>fi</sup>ng the Internet, checking email, and using locationbases services (LBS) is virtually status quo. In this context, questions such as “What are the consequences of providing a set of new functionalities to users that includes locatability? What type of task does this recently emerged technology try to solve? Does this technology obey a “one-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all” strategy? Or is it useful only under particular conditions?” Answering these questions will help organizations, for example, to determine what kind of technology is well suited to solve which set of tasks. Instead of having one uniform information system that includes all functionalities within an organization, tailored information systems may be more appropriate in achieving anticipated ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness gains. To our knowledge, no study exists that empirically examines the effects of locatability across a variety of differing tasks with regards to their performance—particularly in the case where the target market has extremely limited experience with similar information services. In order to elaborate on the questions raised previously, we turn our discussion to <sup>fi</sup>t models before elaborating on tasks that might be solved with the help of locatability.

## 2.2. Defining and measuring fit in IS research

Fit models that re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon the discrepancy between what a technology is able to provide and what the technology is expected to support, are important instruments in IS as they have been successfully used to explain and predict information systems utilization and performance [19,42]. In this context, <sup>fi</sup>t has been typically applied in two forms: “<sup>fi</sup>t as matching” and “<sup>fi</sup>t as deviation”. The perspective of “<sup>fi</sup>t as matching” views <sup>fi</sup>t as “a theoretically de<sup>fi</sup>ned match between two related variables […] Stated differently, a measure of <sup>fi</sup>t between two variables is developed independent of any performance anchor”, whereas “<sup>fi</sup>t as deviation” is viewed as “the degree of adherence to an externally speci<sup>fi</sup>ed pro<sup>fi</sup>le” [45].

An example of measuring <sup>fi</sup>t from a “<sup>fi</sup>t as matching” perspective can be found in the task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t model (TTF), in which task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which the functionality of a technology matches the task as well as the abilities of the individual who performs the task [19,21]. The model has been usefully applied to a variety of technologies and settings, such as in the context of group support systems (GSS) [57], software maintenance [13,14], or to measure performance impacts of an Integrated Information Center on end-users [18,20]. It states that an information system will have a positive impact on performance if, and only if, (a) it is used, and (b) the functionality provided by the information system <sup>fi</sup>ts the task (see Fig. 1).

Multiple conclusions can be drawn from the model: First, a rational user will always choose a tool that enables task completion with the greatest performance impact. Conversely, a user not having the appropriate tool will encounter a performance decrease. And second, <sup>fi</sup>t is contingent [11], i.e., depending on the task and the current technology con<sup>fi</sup>guration used, differing performance impacts may result. As pointed out in the strategic management literature, however, there has been a lack of distinction between the conceptual understanding of <sup>fi</sup>t and its testing schemes [45]. The original instrument developed to test the task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) construct contained eight dimensions, including, for instance, the quality of data, locatability of data (ease of determining what data are available and where), authorization to access data, etc. [19]—irrespective of any performance anchor. This instrument later was re<sup>fi</sup>ned and validated to a 12-dimensional construct [21]. Here, <sup>fi</sup>t is measured on a set of questions answered by the individual performing the task with the help of technology. Except for some recent studies (e.g., [32]), a review of the IS literature shows that the instrument, as originally proposed, has been applied rather infrequently. Researchers point out that new measurement items must be developed each time a new combination of task and technology occurs [13]. This makes the instrument too speci<sup>fi</sup>c in its applicability, and raises validity concerns across contexts.

As an alternative–and with a more generic instrument in mind–some researchers have taken the approach of focusing on the individual pieces of <sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rst, and then constructing a <sup>fi</sup>t measure out of these pieces, which is in line with Venkatraman's “<sup>fi</sup>t as deviation” approach. For instance, in a GSS study [57,58], a <sup>fi</sup>t measurement is developed based on a framework for each of the participating entities: task and technology. Tasks are evaluated based on an existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme by Campbell [8], and technology is rated on three GSS functionality dimensions, including communication support, process structuring, and information processing. Matching these evaluation schemes results in a dichotomous <sup>fi</sup>t measure (i.e., either the technology <sup>fi</sup>ts the task at hand ideally, or not). Another study that uses the same approach of assessing <sup>fi</sup>t is an investigation of software maintenance [13,14]. Here, task demands and technology functionalities are separately evaluated along two dimensions and <sup>fi</sup>nally matched into a dichotomous “<sup>fi</sup>t matrix.” Even though accounting for a more generic approach of assessing <sup>fi</sup>t, the aforementioned studies (as well as a variety of others, including [24,43,46,47]) commonly focus on the diagonal of the <sup>fi</sup>t matrix. They solely examine ideal <sup>fi</sup>t (versus non-ideal <sup>fi</sup>t) conditions and ignore the existence of different forms of non-<sup>fi</sup>t. Conceptually, we can distinguish between at least two deviations from ideal <sup>fi</sup>t: over- and under-<sup>fi</sup>t. In the case of over-<sup>fi</sup>t and under-<sup>fi</sup>t, the technology provides respectively more or less functionality than required.

![](/api/attachments/69RVTJ5F/fulltext/images/c53debad25bee22820d4e00c7d58f9c3c395b5741ab5dfab5bc8ca6234367591.jpg)

Whereas it seems intuitive that an under-<sup>fi</sup>t leads to a decrease in performance (as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by [19]), it is harder to anticipate what will happen in the case of an over-<sup>fi</sup>t. Some argue that over-<sup>fi</sup>t leads to better performance since people can explore and <sup>fi</sup>nd new ways of solving tasks and, thus, be more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient [22]. An alternative view is that over-<sup>fi</sup>t reduces performance because users are either overwhelmed with features and functionalities, or are distracted by the same so that the task at hand suffers negative repercussions [2,27]. Essentially, mobile devices are supposed to provide information when and where needed, yet the challenge remains to effectively design systems to preserve human attention and avoid information overload [41]. Humans have limited capacity to process information and deal with mounds of uncertainty [17]. Technologies enable people to allay problems of uncertainty, thereby reducing the need for information processing or increasing the capacity to process information [17]. The design of these systems requires determining the threshold at which additional technological capabilities over and beyond what is needed for task completion no longer add value [41]. Additionally, humans interact with systems differently and what may be distracting in terms of increased capabilities to one may not be distracting to another. Personalized services that enable users to opt out, or select and modify their selection of features displayed provide the user a sense of control over information content, thereby decreasing the likelihood of information overload as a result of over-<sup>fi</sup>t [29,30]. Undoubtedly, measuring when too much information is counterproductive is a challenging design implication [29].

In the context of mobile and locatable information systems, the question that arises is whether the technology provided is just right, too much, or too little to solve daily tasks in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective manner. But what types are these tasks? Facing the emergence of m-commerce, IS researchers have to ask themselves if the nature of tasks has changed. As task characteristics are the other underlying tenet of any <sup>fi</sup>t model, we thus examine tasks that can potentially be facilitated with the help of mobile technologies.

## 2.3. Task characteristics

IS experimental researchers, have typically based their conceptual understanding of tasks on the social sciences. In that discipline, task is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a stimulus complex and a set of instructions that specify what is to be done vis-à-vis the stimuli. The instructions indicate what operations are to be performed by the subject(s) with respect to the stimuli and/ or what goal is to be achieved” [[23], p. 113]. Within this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, IS researchers have typically relied upon the perspective of “task as a behavior requirement” [23] that views a task in terms of what response the subject should emit (opposed to what a subject actually does emit—which is in line with Hackman's “task as behavior description”). Because behavior requirements are a relatively stable property of any given task, they can be described independently of the characteristics of task performers [23,56], and are therefore most suited to be applied within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. For the purpose of this paper, we shall also apply this frame.

Any reasonable examination of the m-commerce phenomenon should–in a <sup>fi</sup>rst step and as proposed by Balasubramanian et al. [4]–differentiate on spatial characteristics. There is a need to distinguish between location sensitive and location insensitiveness. Location sensitive tasks entail a geographical component to the activity; whereas location insensitive tasks do not. Examples of location sensitive m-commerce application include information services such as wireless updates sought through traf<sup>fi</sup>c conditions on route to the airport, or coupon announcements broadcast by geographically proximate retail stores [4]; location insensitive examples comprise wireless stock quotes requests, or wireless access to digital music library <sup>fi</sup>les [4]. The two other dimensions proposed by Balasubramanian et al. [4], i.e., time criticality, initiating/ controlling role of user or provider, are not subject to the scope of this paper and are therefore omitted.

Using the classi<sup>fi</sup>cations introduced for task and technology, we propose a revised <sup>fi</sup>t model for the mobile context. In particular, we posit that locatability in combination with mobility will enhance our understanding of technology while location sensitivity (versus insensitity) will help us structure tasks (see Fig. 2).

Ideal <sup>fi</sup>t re<sup>fl</sup>ects the exact mapping of task requirements and technological functionality. Over- and under-<sup>fi</sup>t describe a digression from the ideal mapping. Exemplary, we can take two types of tasks, location sensitive versus location insensitive from Balasubramanian et al.'s [4] framework, and match them up with two types of technology: those that provide locatable applications and those that do not. Based on our prior discussion of <sup>fi</sup>t, we propose that out of the three <sup>fi</sup>t variations, ideal <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology will always result in the best (or highest) degree of performance as it represents the perfect match of task and technology. Under-<sup>fi</sup>t as well as over-<sup>fi</sup>t, in contrast, will always lead to worse performance compared with ideal <sup>fi</sup>t. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we pose the following propositions:

P1: An ideal fit between task and technology will lead to higher individual performance than over-fit.

P2: An ideal fit between task and technology will lead to higher individual performance than under-fit.

A laboratory experiment was chosen that manipulated a range of <sup>fi</sup>t conditions based on variations in tasks and technologies and examined their resulting differences with regards to an individual's performance. In other words, performance was chosen to be our dependent variable whose level was tested based on a set of manipulations. Fig. 3 depicts our underlying research model. ## 3.1. Experimental manipulations

Technology was manipulated in two ways: across locatability (high versus low) and across mobility (high and low). As locatability is most often–but not necessarily–embedded into a portable (i.e., wireless) system, applying mobility as an additional dimension in our technology manipulation seemed plausible. As a result, we distinguished between four treatment groups: group A whose subjects were equipped with high mobility by having ubiquitous access to a local wireless area network (WLAN) using a Personal Digital Assistant (PDA) and high locatability by being able to access location-based services (LBS) within the experimental zone; group B whose subjects were equipped with a mobile PDA that could only be used as a stationary device situated in a particular room (simulating a desktop environment) and high locatability, i.e. location information was available on their stationary device; group C whose subjects were equipped with mobility, i.e., subjects could use their PDA as a mobile device, however, it did not have any locatability functionality available; and group D whose members had neither mobility nor locatability, i.e., they were forced to use their PDA as a stationary device in a particular room (again, simulating a desktop environment) without the availability of location-based services (LBS). The resulting four different technology groups are displayed in Fig. 4.

The wireless experimental zone spanned two college buildings including a small courtyard in between, totaling a size of 80,525 ft<sup>2</sup> (=7481 m<sup>2</sup>). Within this experimental zone, wireless Internet accessibility at a transmission rate of 11 Mbps was guaranteed, so was the availability of location-based services (LBS) for those that were part of the respective treatment group. The level of localization accuracy at any point throughout the network averaged between 10 and 20 m using cell-based localization techniques. With these technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, the experimental zone provided faster transmission rates than any currently available cellular phone infrastructure, and higher localization accuracy than mandated by the FCC in their E911 mandate.

An ideal <sup>fi</sup>t was present when location sensitive tasks were matched with locatability capabilities, or when location insensitive tasks were matched with the absence of locatability capabilities, respectively. An under-<sup>fi</sup>t condition existed when location sensitive tasks required locatability capabilities that were not provided; and an over-<sup>fi</sup>t condition occurred when locatability was present even though not required for solving the task at hand.

Individual performance, our dependent variable, was measured by the time it took to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a task, also referred to as timeto-completion (TTC) henceforth. Time-to-completion (TTC)

represents the true time it took for a subject to complete a task, i.e., from the moment a subject read the task description to the moment when the subject entered the answer. In other words, the time-to-start (TTS), i.e., the time elapsed between the software issuing a new task by sending out an email message and a subject opening this email, was excluded (see Fig. 5).

Matching the time measures, we also recorded a set of location measures for each subject. We captured an individual's location when receiving an email, location when opening the email, and location when <sup>fi</sup>nalizing the task. In addition, a task that required a subject to <sup>fi</sup>nd a moving person, recorded not only the location of the subjects at event time, but also the location of the (moving) person to be found. This was necessary in order to adjust time measures based on a subject's distance in relation to another person or place when executing location sensitive tasks. As a consequence, time measures for location sensitive tasks were standardized by calculating the ratio between distance and time for the actual analysis. Also, time measures were corrected by the length of interruptions that had occurred during task ful<sup>fi</sup>llment. Location as well as time measures (as displayed in Fig. 5) were recorded by the experimental software for all subjects, irrespective of their experimental group assignment.

## 3.3. Experimental procedure

Every subject received a personal PDA and was randomly assigned to one of the four technology treatment groups. Due to the limited number of devices available, batch sizes ranged between <sup>fi</sup>ve and ten subjects at one time. Subjects underwent a 30 min training session that included the successful completion of various small exercises. Among other things, subjects were familiarized with the use of a stylus and the browsing capabilities of their PDA—which included accessing their Web-based email account that was individually created for the purposes of the experiment. The training session ended with <sup>fi</sup>lling out a survey on general topics, such as demographics, experiences, motivation, etc. Then, subjects were provided with an extensive initial scenario description that drew on a situation of an upcoming group project presentation in front of a potential future employer along with a situation in which the subject won a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious summer vacation (see Appendix A). This scenario represented the introductory vignette and was intended to set the stage for the experimental tasks to come. Moreover, subjects were told to expect a sequence of eight tasks total that would be sequentially emailed to them (using their experimental Webbased email accounts) within the next 2–3 hours. As our intention was to present subjects with a variety of tasks that would <sup>fi</sup>t the technology at hand in an ideal (versus not ideal) way, we assigned every subject, irrespective of his treatment group, to the same set of tasks.

In order to ensure participant motivation subjects were told that if they performed within the top 50% of a treatment group, they were eligible to participate in a drawing that provided 10 monetary prizes of USD 30 each. As mentioned before, all tasks were embedded into an overall descriptive, real-life scenario. Whereas Table 2 only brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarizes the tasks T1 through T8 used for the experiment, Appendix A provides a detailed description of the scenario and task descriptions.

Following the training session, subjects were issued their <sup>fi</sup>rst task via email after a random amount of time (ranging between one and ten minutes). After task ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, subjects were questioned about potential interruptions (such as meeting a person and getting drawn into a conversation, or technical issues, etc.) that may have had occurred meanwhile and that may have introduced a distorting factor into the experiment. Since an experiment administered in a mobile environment, by default, does not con<sup>fi</sup>ne subjects to the stipulations of a traditional laboratory setting, this measure was essential in order to rule out potential threats to internal validity.

Also, in order to avoid grouping or learning effects, the task order for each subject as well as the time until the next task was issued was randomized by the software developed for the experiment (see Fig. 6). As a result, every subject in a batch of <sup>fi</sup>ve to ten was part of a different treatment group and ful<sup>fi</sup>lling a different task at any point in time.

The experimental procedures were pre-tested with 26 subjects to discern and pre-empt administrative and technical hurdles as well as to make minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the wording of the task descriptions. For the actual experiment,125 students from a university in southeastern U.S. were recruited. Out of these, 13 data points were unusable due to a technical fault that occurred during administration. A remaining 112 students contributed to our final data set. Students received course credit for participation. Of students participating in the experiment, the majority (88.3%) was between 19 and 23 years old, and 83.7% were seniors in MIS. The majority of students (N88%) had either moderate or intensive experience with at least one of three technologies (Internet, computers, or cellular phones) and was highly motivated to partake in the experiment (mean=1.64, STD=0.88 on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from −3 to +3).

## 4. Results

For the analysis of the manipulation checks, a nonparametric approach is chosen because of violations of normality assumptions. The differences in technology treatments are perceived as intended. Variance tests and their post-hoc tests show that subjects perceived differences in mobility (F(1)= 40.04, p=.000) and locatability (F(1)=24.01, p=.0000). A twofaceted analysis is applied to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying forces at work. First, we analyze individual tasks across differing levels of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t. Second, we conduct an analysis across the four treatment groups.

As indicated, as a cross-check, individual task performance measures are also analyzed across the four different technology groups, neglecting any <sup>fi</sup>t conditions. ## 5. Discussion

We applied two separate frameworks, one for technology relying upon locatability in combination with mobility and one for tasks relying upon location sensitivity, to formulate a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t model (TTF). Contrary to traditional operationalizations of <sup>fi</sup>t as a dichotomous variable, a trichotomous <sup>fi</sup>t variable was used. Further, mobile (versus non-mobile) and locatable (versus non-locatable) devices were applied to a range of differing tasks and gauged against individual performance measures. The technological environment created for this experiment provided higher transmission rates than currently available in cellular networks and better localization accuracy than required by the E911 mandate issued by the FCC. As commercial applications with these speci<sup>fi</sup>cations will not be available until some time in the future, this study has taken an initial step in exploring locatability with regards to some fundamental constructs in IS that include <sup>fi</sup>t and performance measures.

Multiple conclusions can be drawn. First, neither locatability nor mobility made a difference when performing location-insensitive tasks. Irrespective of the level of <sup>fi</sup>t provided, these tasks were performed equally by every subject in terms of their time-to-completion (TTC). This <sup>fi</sup>nding supports the assumption that mobile devices are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better suited to perform tasks such as browsing for information, writing emails, etc., in a timelier manner than their (wired) desktop counterparts. In other words, for location-insensitive tasks, the availability of mobility does not constitute a performance increase or decrease, i.e., the availability of mobility is not pertinent to save time. For location sensitive tasks, in contrast, differences in performance measures occurred.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, and as a second <sup>fi</sup>nding, applying locatability to tasks that required subjects to <sup>fi</sup>nd a moving person led to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains. Subjects equipped with location-tracking capabilities (mobile or stationary) were able to hunt down moving individuals much faster than those without. Whereas subjects not equipped with locatability had to rely on their chances to <sup>fi</sup>nd the moving person as quickly as possible, subjects equipped with the electronic version of a “hide and seek application” clearly performed better.

In contrast, and as a third <sup>fi</sup>nding, location sensitive tasks that included <sup>fi</sup>nding a currently open of<sup>fi</sup>ce resulted only in insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains. Subjects that were assigned to the treatment of locatability in searching for an open of<sup>fi</sup>ce versus those equipped without performed equally. In retrospect, this <sup>fi</sup>nding might be attributed to the experimental setup as <sup>fi</sup>ve of<sup>fi</sup>ces with varying opening hours were scattered across 80,525 ft<sup>2</sup> (=7481 m<sup>2</sup>). Apparently the likelihood of <sup>fi</sup>nding an open of<sup>fi</sup>ce without having the technological support of locatability was comparable to that of the experimental group—albeit the pilot study advised otherwise.

As a fourth <sup>fi</sup>nding, we were able to demonstrate that ideal <sup>fi</sup>t conditions outperform under-<sup>fi</sup>t conditions, however, we were not able to reveal the same discrepancy between ideal and over-<sup>fi</sup>t. Whereas the lack of locatability was perceived as a de<sup>fi</sup>nite gap, the unnecessary availability of locatability did not impact individual performance. This may be due to variations in personal tolerance of what is considered to be too much functionality. Some users may actually not feel overwhelmed or distracted by the additional features or information regarding the tasks. Additionally, users may not have legitimized all of the information provided in the task completion sequences. For example, when users were provided with more information on location than needed, they may not have been able to discern the criticality of the location information required for solving the task at hand. Thus, we infer that these users do not cognitively make a distinction between ideal and over-<sup>fi</sup>t conditions, but rather determine that the technology “suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently” meets the needs of the task at hand. It is also possible that the context lacked a perceived urgency and that over-<sup>fi</sup>t conditions are detectable only when there is a critical situation to address. For example, Emergency Room medical personnel using mobile devices in patient care became much more agitated with super<sup>fl</sup>uous information and deemed the mobile technology to insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently <sup>fi</sup>t the information processing needs in life threatening situations [1]. However, when some of these same medical personnel used the mobile technology in non-critical or acute-care settings, they were much more tolerant of the design of the information system and regarded the additional medical data provided by the device as educational even if it were not directly related to task at hand [1]. As we were not able to demonstrate the danger of users spending too much time bypassing unneeded functionality, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can only support the notion of operationalizing task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t as a “deviation” approach [38]. Future studies however should consider the time-critical nature of tasks when studying over-<sup>fi</sup>t conditions.

## 5.1. Limitations

When conducting experimental research, there is a perpetual con<sup>fl</sup>ict between internal and external validity [33]. Since experiments should be primarily designed for high internal validity, establishing external validity, by default, is limited [6]. Even though we also acknowledge that our study population might have been on average more tech-savvy (as they part of an IS class) than a randomly selected user group, this characteristic was of great importance for the experimental setup of the study. Considering that location-based services were (and still are) commercially limited and the fact that our “wireless lab” was entirely developed from scratch, it was pertinent to acquire users that were not opposed to using novel technologies. In other words, as experimental researchers we had to ensure as part of internal validity that no other factors, such as computer anxiety or computer self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, would distort our causality observations and show an impact on the relationship between <sup>fi</sup>t and performance. Also, in addition to ensure internal validity, the experimental set-up was chosen to provide a realistic environment so that subjects could easily identify with the experimental tasks. The tasks were drawn from personal and professional life scenarios of a senior IS student.

## 5.2. Implications and future considerations

In this <sup>fi</sup>nal section, we want to look beyond this particular experiment to consider the wider area of IS research that it addresses, because we believe that mobile technologies will force us to rethink some fundamental ideas about IS. For example, historically, an information system has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “an integrated, user-machine system for providing information to support operations, management, and decisionmaking functions in an organization” [[9], p. 5]. Facing mobility and locatability in the form of mobile devices, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition requires recasting. For decades, we associated an organizational setting with physical boundaries. Mobile technologies disrupt these task boundaries in that the mapping between activities and places breaks down [3]: we <sup>fi</sup>nd people working even though they are driving a car, eating at a restaurant, or waiting for a train. Thus, it is necessary to revisit some of the cornerstones that frame information systems. Particularly, we raise the question of whether mobility and locatability are simply characteristics of yet another type of technology? Or do they steer us towards an overarching scheme that has yet to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in IS?

Until now, only a few researchers have tackled the question of what comes next in IS. Some suggest it is “ubiquitous computing” [52,53], envisioned as, “… a physical world richly and invisibly interwoven with sensors, actuators, displays, and computational elements, embedded seamlessly in the everyday objects of our lives and connected through a continuous network” [[54], p. 694]. Another thought, termed “nomadic computing,” envisions a “heterogeneous assemblage of interconnected technological and organizational elements, which enables the physical and social mobility of computing and communication services between organizational actors both within and across organizational borders” [[31], p. 378]. Yet, another idea, termed “ucommerce,” points towards four underlying constructs, i.e., ubiquity, uniqueness, universality, and unison, as fundamental dimensions in organizing current as well as emerging technologies [50]. All three of these realms of thought have in common that–even though initiated or driven by technology–they abstract from technology at the same time. Technology is portrayed as a steady companion that is embedded in everyday life in a non-intrusive and transparent manner, aimed at supporting us in our immediate surroundings in performing a variety of tasks in the most effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient manner.

Of these frameworks, the “u-commerce” concept matches our current study quite well. In particular, two constructs seem to encompass mobility and locatability: ubiquity and uniqueness. According to its de<sup>fi</sup>nition, with ubiquity, people are able to access information at any time from anywhere, and in turn, are reachable at any time and any place [51]. Ubiquity envisions that, though limited at present, future information systems will provide ubiquitous functionality. This prediction is based on the assumption that: (1) widespread wireless networks will mean many can access and be reached through the network; (2) there will be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient bandwidth, and (3) information systems will become independent of particular wireless networks. In other words, ubiquity extends current technological boundaries to environments that build upon WiFi, 3G, Bluetooth, WiMax, ZigBees, and other wireless technologies that will surely emerge. In this context, current mobility can be viewed as a stage in the development of a technological infrastructure that will eventually support ubiquity.

On the other hand, uniqueness depicts the idea of complete identi<sup>fi</sup>cation—not only in terms of identity and associated preferences, but also in terms of geographic position [51]. As many individuals own portable information appliances, they can customize them based on their preferences ranging from selecting personal colors, layout schemes and ring tones, to storing personal contacts. As a result, (mobile) information systems will more and more take over the role of a personal assistant capturing preferences, habits, and the identity of the owner. Combining knowledge about the identity of a personwith geographical data leads to the de<sup>fi</sup>nitive level of uniqueness: we know who this person is, where he is, and what he prefers or needs. In this context, locatability de<sup>fi</sup>nes only one aspect of uniqueness. With location-based services becoming more and more available, we therefore envision that future information systems will provide an even higher level of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

In sum, the current study can, besides adding to the body of knowledge about task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t pertaining to mobile technology, also stimulate further IS research questions on not just mobile technology but also our fundamental conceptualization of IS. Thus, we believe, IS researchers need to think about an overarching IS conceptual model that incorporates traditional as well as emerging technologies and that considers organizational as well as personal perspectives. A <sup>fi</sup>rst step in this direction could entail the derivation of a parsimonious set of underlying IS constructs that explain what drives consumers demand for, and their use, of information systems. This study aims to contribute to this development by showing how TTF provides a potential foundation for understanding the consumption of information.
