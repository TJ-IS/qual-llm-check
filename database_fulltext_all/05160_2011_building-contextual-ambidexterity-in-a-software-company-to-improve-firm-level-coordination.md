# Building contextual ambidexterity in a software company to improve firm-level coordination

## Abstract

Software organizations increasingly face contradictory strategic choices as they develop customized and packaged solutions for the market. They need to improve efficiency of development processes while at the same time adapting to emerging customer needs; they need to exploit software products in relation to existing customers while simultaneously exploring new technology and market opportunities; and, they need to consider both incremental and radical innovations. While the integration of such opposing strategies requires software organizations to become ambidextrous, there is limited actionable advice on how managers can develop such capability. Against this backdrop, we report from a two-year action research study into a small software firm, TelSoft. Based on Pettigrew’s contextualist inquiry, we develop a framework that integrates existing theory on contextual ambidexterity with a generic process for improving software organizations, and we apply this framework to analyze how TelSoft improved its coordination of products, projects, and innovation efforts. As a result, we offer principles for how software managers can build ambidextrous capability to improve firm-level coordination. Keywords: software organization; contextual ambidexterity; firm-level coordination; action research

## Introduction

Managers face a diverse set of challenges as software organizations develop customized and packaged solutions for the market (Carmel & Becker, 1995; Sawyer, 2000; Xu & Brinkkemper, 2007). As a reflection of how product development is organized, managers are confronted with important projectlevel challenges, such as software project management (e.g., Boehm & Ross, 1989; Barki et al, 1993; Keil, 1995) and software team management (e.g., Carmel & Sawyer, 1998; Dube, 1998; Krishnan, 1998). At the same time, however, managers face complex firm-level challenges, including turning software into industrial products (Carmel & Becker, 1995), responding to dynamic environments (Mathiassen & Vainio, 2007), designing performance measures that reflect time to market pressures (Sawyer, 2000), synchronizing and stabilizing development across multiple teams (Cusumano & Selby, 1997), and improving software processes (Humphrey, 1989; Mu¨ller et al, 2010). Although project-level considerations are critically important in software organizations and project outcomes shape customer satisfaction and strategic options, our focus is here on firm-level coordination, the capability of software managers to effectively coordinate multiple products, projects, and innovation efforts across the organization.

Firm-level coordination in software organizations involves contradictory choices between options such as efficiency vs flexibility (Adler et al, 1999), exploitation vs exploration (March, 1991), and evolutionary vs revolutionary change (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). Software managers must optimize the efficiency of existing development processes while simultaneously adapting them to individual customer needs; they therefore need to emphasize repeat-ability of development processes on the one hand and response-ability to dynamic market conditions on the other (Boehm, 2002; Napier et al, 2006). Managers must also exploit the organization’s current capabilities in relation to existing customers while at the same time exploring new technology and market opportunities; they must therefore make sure that the product and project portfolios satisfy existing customers while also allowing for market expansion (Markowitz, 1952; McFarlan, 1981; De Reyck et al, 2005). Moreover, software managers need to engage in both evolutionary and revolutionary change (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996); they have for many years implemented incremental innovations like software process improvements (Humphrey, 1989; CMMI Product Team, 2006) and new project management techniques (Fonstad & Robertson, 2006; Woolridge et al, 2007); but, increasingly they need to also consider radical innovations like open source and globally distributed software development (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003, 2006).

Instead of choosing between these options, software organizations could become ambidextrous. Ambidextrous organizations compete by optimizing efficiency, cost, and incremental innovation while simultaneously exhibiting flexibility, speed, and radical innovation (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996; Raisch et al, 2009). One option is to implement structural ambidexterity by developing opposing capabilities in different organizational units; another is to build contextual ambidexterity by developing capabilities for both alignment and adaptability within existing organizational units (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Within the software management literature, there is some focus on adoption of structural ambidexterity (Boehm & Turner, 2004; Vinekar et al, 2006), but we found no contributions that investigate how software organizations could benefit from contextual ambidexterity.

Against this backdrop, this research was designed to address the following overarching research question: How can software organizations improve firm-level coordination by building contextual ambidexterity? We have investigated this question through a two-year action research project (Susman & Evered, 1978; McKay & Marshall, 2001; Mathiassen, 2002) into TelSoft (pseudonym), a small software organization. We focused on firm-level coordination with the following practical goals: developing and communicating TelSoft’s product strategy; establishing policies and improving processes for software projects; and implementing new practices for resource allocation across products and projects. This allowed us to provide a detailed account of how TelSoft improved firm-level coordination by building contextual ambidexterity. As a sensitizing device, we use contextualist inquiry (Pettigrew, 1990) to integrate existing theory on contextual ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004) with a generic process for improving software organizations (McFeeley, 1996). We combine this framework with the empirical findings from TelSoft to develop principles for how software organizations can build contextual ambidexterity to improve firm-level coordination.

## Theoretical background

## Ambidextrous software organization

There is an emerging literature exploring the application of ambidextrous principles to software organizations. With primary focus on the project-level, Boehm and Turner suggest that project characteristics such as developer skill set, customer availability, and requirements predictability be evaluated and used to guide selection of a development process that best fits the situation (Boehm, 2002; Boehm & Turner, 2004). If the main goals are speed and customer satisfaction, agile processes may be more appropriate. However, if the main goal is a quality product and requirements are stable, then plandriven processes may be more suitable (Boehm, 2002). Managers are advised to use risk analysis to determine the appropriate mixture of discipline and agility for specific projects. This approach focuses on applying ambidexterity principles on the project-level and places the burden of coordination on a few managers. Hence, one limitation is that it does not consider how ambidexterity principles may impact the day-to-day behavior of project managers, developers, and others throughout the organization.

Considering the firm-level, Vinekar et al suggest that software organizations create a traditional subunit focused on exploitation and an agile subunit focusing on exploration (Vinekar et al, 2006). Each unit would differ with respect to management, desired skills, processes, and technology. In the traditional subunit, managers would use plan-driven processes in which developers would be tasked and rewarded as individuals for their conformance to standard development processes and technologies. In the agile subunit, managers would work as facilitators, developers would be tasked and rewarded within collaborative teams, and technology would support iterative development and adaptive processes. The perceived benefits of this separation of concerns include allowing the management team to learn and apply best practices from each subunit, permitting individuals within the organization to work in the culture that best matches their predispositions, and providing a straightforward means of adding ambidexterity to an organization already proficient at either discipline or agility (Vinekar et al, 2006). While this approach to firm-level structural ambidexterity may be effective, organizations may lack the resources or stability required to create different subunits dedicated to plan-driven and agile processes (Lubatkin et al, 2006).

Although the emerging literature demonstrates the usefulness of applying ambidexterity principles on the project-level and structural ambidexterity on the firm-level, there are limitations related to each of these approaches. Moreover, software organizations face additional challenges that are not addressed by either of these approaches. More specifically, managers face complex firm-level challenges, including managing a product portfolio (Carmel & Becker, 1995), responding to market pressures (Sawyer, 2000), coordinating across their project portfolio (Cusumano & Selby, 1997), and continuously innovating and improving both processes and products (Humphrey, 1989; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003). There are therefore good reasons to further explore alternative approaches such as contextual ambidexterity.

## Contextual ambidexterity

Contextual ambidexterity requires the combination of opposing capabilities within a firm (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Thus, the firm or one of its units must have both the capacity to work toward common goals (alignment), and the capacity to change quickly in response to dynamic market conditions (adaptability) (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Hence, ambidexterity is seen as a shared responsibility and emphasizes the dayto-day activities of individual employees within the firm. As a result, the role of the management team is to create contexts that enable and encourage individuals to make their own judgments about how to balance the conflicting demands for alignment and adaptability.

Prior research demonstrates that contextual ambidexterity emerges when leaders in a business unit develop an organizational context that includes appropriate degrees of performance management and social support (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). The performance management context represents systems, processes, and beliefs related to meeting performance objectives set by management (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Discipline is an attribute that encourages people to meet objectives voluntarily whereas stretch is an attribute that encourages people to strive for even more ambitious goals (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994). The social support context represents systems, processes, and beliefs associated with member relationships (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Trust is an attribute of the organizational context that encourages people to rely on one another, whereas support is an attribute that empowers people to lend assistance to others (Ghoshal & Bartlett, 1994).

In software management, discipline can be exhibited by consistently completing projects that meet stakeholder requirements on time and within budget and by ensuring that the existing project portfolio is well managed, resources are appropriately distributed, and under-performing activities are reined in or terminated. Stretch, by contrast, encourages software organizations to work hard to add value to their customers and to alter investments based on new technology or market options. However, where stretch is not balanced with discipline, software organizations can experience problems, for example by adding unnecessary functionality to products (i.e., feature creep) or by allowing the scope of projects to expand to the point where they become unprofitable (i.e., scope creep). Successful software management also requires a context with strong social support. For instance, software development depends heavily on the level of trust that designers and managers build with customers and future users (Sabherwal, 1999). Also, ensuring appropriate quality in software products requires managers to provide expert support, for example in the form of peer reviews (Weinberg & Freedman, 1982). In fact, the essence of managing software organizations is arguably to create an environment in which designers and engineers become empowered to develop customized and packaged solutions for the market (Weinberg, 1986).

However, software organizations seeking actionable advice on the process of building contexts that facilitate and enable ambidextrous capability presently find limited guidance from the management literature. Thus far, most research investigates the antecedents of ambidexterity and the impact of ambidexterity on performance without considering in detail how contextual ambidexterity is developed (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; He & Wong, 2004; Jansen et al, 2005; Lubatkin et al, 2006). Gibson and Birkinshaw describe the process of building contextual ambidexterity as ‘complex, causally ambiguous, widely dispersed, and quite time-consuming (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004, pp. 209–210) and provide general lessons on where and how organizations can start: diagnose the organizational context; change key aspects of the context; ensure communication about ambidexterity throughout the organization; consider contextual and structural ambidexterity; and, empower employees throughout the organization to participate. Although these lessons serve as a starting point, much more is needed to understand how context and organizational practices interact over time and shape each other as organizations strive to build contextual ambidexterity.

As a framework for building contextual ambidexterity (see Figure 1), we therefore use Pettigrew’s contextualist inquiry (Pettigrew, 1985, 1987) to integrate existing theory on contextual ambidexterity with a generic process for improving software organizations (McFeeley, 1996).

Contextualist inquiry focuses on how content, context, and process interact to transform organizations; it addresses multiple levels of analysis; and, it emphasizes the interconnectedness of phenomena in historical, present, and future time. Content refers to the areas being transformed, in this case, the capability of a software organization to combine alignment and adaptability in its activities (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Context refers to the systems, processes, and beliefs within the organization through which ideas for change proceed, in this case, changes to the performance management and social support context (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Finally, process refers to the actions and interactions between interested parties as they attempt to improve practices through the IDEAL model’s four main phases of Diagnosing, Establishing, Acting, and Learning (McFeeley, 1996).

## Research approach

## Action research

In October 2004, we initiated a two-year research collaboration with TelSoft. The first author was a Ph.D. student and had previously been employed at TelSoft. The collaboration was executed by the researchers, a steering committee of senior management from TelSoft, and several problem-solving teams. The different teams focused on specific innovation areas such as software process reengineering (Napier et al, 2008), requirements management (Napier et al, 2006, 2009), and firm-level coordination (the focus of this paper). As elaborated in detail below, improvement of TelSoft’s firm-level coordination led to establishment of a software coordination group (SCG) comprised of the Division President, the Vice President (VP) of Software, the Development Manager, and the Product Manager. SCG meetings were facilitated by two of the authors.

Our study was based on collaborative practice research (Mathiassen, 2002; Iversen et al, 2004), a form of action research emphasizing strong collaboration between practitioners and researchers to effect change. The dual goals (Rapoport, 1970; McKay & Marshall, 2001) were to stimulate more innovative practices at TelSoft and to contribute to scientific knowledge on managing software organizations. Following Chiasson et al (2009), we adopted an interactive approach in which software management theory informed the problem-solving cycle at TelSoft while empirical insights from TelSoft helped us develop principles for improving firm-level coordination through contextual ambidexterity.

## Research site

TelSoft, a privately held company founded in 1971, provides geographic information systems services for the telecommunications and utility industries. Those services include the development of custom software tailored to the specifications of two major customers as well as generic packaged software geared toward a mass-market (Sawyer, 2000; Xu & Brinkkemper, 2007). Our study focused on a permanent business unit engaged in developing software solutions. At any point in time during the study, this unit managed a portfolio of five major software products with a multiplicity of customized solutions, 8–15 software projects, and, 2–5 improvement projects.

TelSoft is representative of small software organizations because it is oriented toward known customers in a niche market, relies upon dedicated employees who perform many roles within the organization, and has few resources devoted to innovation (Horvat et al, 2000). Struggling to survive in a competitive environment, TelSoft frequently neglected innovation and adaptation, and instead emphasized known customers and existing products. In fact, despite awareness of technological changes in the marketplace, TelSoft invested few resources to upgrade its software. For instance, even as Microsoft products became the standard for developing Windows-based software applications, software engineers at TelSoft used an obsolete application framework no longer supported by its vendor.

TelSoft’s reluctance to innovate was due in part to a history of failed innovation projects, including an unprofitable attempt to introduce spatial databases into their packaged mapping software. After years of missed deadlines, inadequate functionality, and limited market success, the CEO halted what he called ‘speculative development’ until further notice. From that point on, management was wary of developing new practices and pursuing new markets. However, despite this resistance to change, TelSoft was prompted to act in response to new issues with their main customers: software releases were frequently shipped late, projects ran over budget, and products contained deviations from agreed upon requirements. Our research collaboration was an opportunity for TelSoft to gain assistance in innovation.

## Data collection and analysis

After initiation (I) of the research collaboration, data collection occurred throughout the four main phases (DEAL) of the IDEAL model as summarized in Table 1 and elaborated below (McFeeley, 1996). Detailed notes from all interviews, workshops, and meetings were taken by the research team. In addition, all SCG meetings and assessment interviews were taped and transcribed. The research team met during each phase to analyze patterns emerging from the interview data and to reflect upon what was learned. To validate our understandings, we created interim reports and held status meetings with members of TelSoft’s software development group.

To examine the process of improving firm-level coordination at TelSoft, we applied contextual ambidexterity as analytical lens (see Figure 1). We applied three analysis strategies: temporal bracketing, narrative strategy, and qualitative coding (Langley, 1999). We first bracketed the data into the temporal phases of diagnosing, establishing, acting, and learning. Next, we applied the narrative strategy to create a detailed story of events at TelSoft. We then coded data according to the four concepts of performance management, social support, alignment, and adaptability. The initial coding scheme was created by direct application of earlier theoretical work (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Using Atlas.ti qualitative data analysis software, the first author applied these predetermined codes to indicate how the systems, processes, and beliefs at TelSoft either encouraged or discouraged performance management, social support, alignment, and adaptability.

## Improving firm-level coordination at TelSoft

In the following, we analyze the process of improving firm-level coordination at TelSoft. Using the lens of contextual ambidexterity, the narrative describes changes in performance management, social support, alignment, and adaptability through the four phases of diagnosing, establishing, acting, and learning (see Figure 1).

## Phase 1: diagnosing

In October 2004, we began conducting interviews with the software development group, internal customers, and external customers to better understand the practices involved in coordination at TelSoft.

Performance management (low) TelSoft’s systems, processes, and beliefs did not support people working in a disciplined fashion to meet or exceed business objectives. Instead, each project manager autonomously executed projects and managed budgets based upon personal preferences rather than professional standards. As a result, project outcomes varied considerably depending upon the project manager and resources used. For instance, one TelSoft project manager frequently exceeded budgets and missed deadlines because he emphasized quality over and beyond what was required by the Quality Assurance team. This practice continued, in part, because there were no rewards for either project failure or success. Employees admitted that there were few incentives for meeting or exceeding project objectives. Long-time project managers faced no threat of being replaced, and non-management employees had limited opportunities for promotions or increased responsibilities. Incentives were not given for exemplary performance; in fact, employees had not received a raise in three years, resulting in low morale.

Social support (mixed) Social support and trust within the management team was high. The VP of Software had worked with several of his direct reports for over 15 years in a friendly, comfortable relationship. When cost overruns and blown schedules occurred, the VP’s displeasure was tempered by a belief that managers were committed to doing the best job they could under difficult circumstances. Despite cordial relations, the VP of Software acknowledged that the Division President operated on hunches, reacting to events emotionally or intuitively. As a result, company-sponsored innovations were often not aligned with the market and were, therefore, unsuccessful. Haunted by prior failed product and process innovation attempts, TelSoft’s employees were hesitant to move forward and take risks. A Development Manager confessed:

I did have some skepticism about it initially. I was involved in CMM initially and that was a total flop. It was all about defining the process – not how to implement or follow them. Then all that stuff got forgotten. It wasn’t easy to get me fired up about this.

Social support and trust with some customers and nonmanagement employees were also strained. Overall, most customers were pleased with product quality and customer service; one external client praised TelSoft project managers for understanding their needs and successfully delivering software solutions. However, representatives for another major client spoke bitterly about recent product releases and accused TelSoft of having a hidden agenda to sneak code through: ‘We don’t have confidence level in what we receive in a software package. We don’t even have confidence that it’s ours’. TelSoft did not facilitate or encourage employee development. Task assignments were based on existing expertise rather than seen as opportunities for employees to expand expertise through professional development.

Alignment (mixed) Within TelSoft, the strength of alignment with overall business objectives varied across projects. On some projects, managers made heroic efforts to ensure project success, taking on extra responsibilities and verifying product quality before submitting to clients. Employees rallied behind these project managers to ensure the completion of assigned work. On other projects, clients were clearly dissatisfied with the performance of the project manager and resulting product quality; in turn, employee efforts on these projects were less than satisfactory.

Beyond trying to make individual projects successful, TelSoft lacked a systematic process for portfolio management that would effectively manage resources across products and projects to ensure the company’s profitability. For example, TelSoft’s Product Manager identified a limited pool of four qualified engineers, who had to be spread across three projects. Rather than allocating resources to maximize profit, TelSoft privileged requests from major clients over requests from internal customers, which jeopardized the productivity of the company as a whole.

Adaptability (low) TelSoft initiated projects reactively and lacked shared visions of a long-term product strategy and an optimal project portfolio. In this way, TelSoft lacked adaptability. TelSoft employees focused on existing products and were reluctant to invest in changes. Existing customers were a major impetus for product and process innovation. For example, in July 2000, TelSoft was prompted into process innovation by a major client’s requirement for outside certification of its software capability by achieving level 2 on the Capability Maturity Model (CMM). However, after only one year, all TelSoft resources associated with this initiative were abruptly reassigned when the client removed the certification requirement. Subsequently, no organized activity focused on innovative management of individual projects or the project portfolio.

Although the major customers appreciated TelSoft’s responsiveness, they also wanted TelSoft to be more proactive in investing in its products. One customer representative commented:

TelSoft has a tendency to wait until their major clients tell them they want something before they do something that may make their software better. TelSoft should have been working on things on their own for the core product and we shouldn’t have to ask for them and pay for them.

There were no systems in place for assessing products and processes or improving them. Although TelSoft responded quickly to its customers’ needs, its track record for responding to the market at large was dismal.

## Phase 2: establishing

This phase was triggered by the arrival of a new Division President in June 2005 who approved investment for improving firm-level coordination at TelSoft. To spearhead the innovation initiative, the research team and VP of Software created the SCG and developed a detailed plan for its efforts. The group would meet monthly and follow a fixed agenda covering software strategy, current software projects, new business opportunities, and process innovation initiatives.

Performance management (some improvement) The establishing phase saw some improvements to performance management, specifically in the desire to become more disciplined about monitoring the company’s performance. The SCG committed to use objective information to facilitate project portfolio management. The SCG agreed to use key performance indicators (KPIs) for assessing current projects, and the VP of Software reinstituted the practice of weekly status reports from all project managers.

Still, the group struggled to identify KPIs that would be meaningful for software projects. The managers preferred quantitative measures of software quality, such as number of defects reported for a particular product that could make comparisons across projects easier. At these early SCG meetings, the President tried to push for a broader definition of software quality to include items such as customer satisfaction and long-term quality; however, the other SCG members were more focused on the project level. Reluctantly, they acknowledged that existing measures did not really give a comprehensive picture of software quality, and they needed to rely on the subjective judgments of those most familiar with the project. The group did not consider consulting the existing knowledge base on software metrics and trying to apply industry best practices to their current situation.

Despite useful meetings and managers’ positive intentions, outcomes were not adequately recorded or followed up. The Development Manager arrived at the second meeting completely unprepared, claiming to have forgotten the meeting until the day before so he did not have metrics from the project managers. There was no apparent consequence for his irresponsibility.

Social support (no change) The Development Manager assumed responsibility for collecting information and distributing it to SCG members before each meeting. This process initially did not go smoothly. The information provided by the project managers was not in a suitable format, did not include the information requested, and was late. This prevented the group from being able to use the information to make decisions. The VP of Software

Development indicated these problems also occurred with the status reports: ‘One project manager has a half a page because he doesn’t fill out a lot of the sections and another might have five pages’. The Development Manager also expressed some concerns over having enough time to process the information provided to him by the project managers:

I need to update the spreadsheet, look at the KPI’s, potentially go research what’s going on with different projects, uh, so that I can come up with an assessment and if we’re going to get current data, if we’re going to get KPI’s on Monday from the project managers, I don’t see how to do all that and give everybody the stuff to review well before this meeting on Wednesday morning. (SCG Meeting #1)

In sum, although beliefs were changing among members of the SCG, it was too early to tell whether others outside the SCG would adapt their behavior based upon these systems.

Alignment (some improvement) During the establishing phase, alignment increased among SCG members through the creation of systems for defining, debating, and modifying performance against business objectives. The SCG collaborated with others in the organization to develop TelSoft’s reason for being, software strategy, and operating policies (Haeckel, 1995, 1999). The reason for being and software strategy served as explicit, shared understandings of the criteria that would be used for assessing product innovations. Operating policies guided process innovations and included requiring approval from the quality assurance department before delivering official releases, and managing each development project with a two-phase approach that separated requirements from development activities. Collectively, the reason for being, software strategy, and operating policies became known as TelSoft’s software charter.

Adaptability (some improvement) TelSoft’s openness in allowing outside researchers to challenge existing practices and their commitment to monthly meetings were promising signs that changes to adaptability could occur. The SCG members were open to direction, criticism, and new ways of thinking from the researchers. For instance, the following comment from a researcher challenged TelSoft management to think more deliberately about the level of discipline on internally funded projects:

Do you treat yourself as a customer on equal footing with other customers or do you give yourself bigger freedom in being flexible and democratic in the way that you deal with yourself as a customer? You know, [a major client] would never accept all that jockeying back and forth.

Two positive initiatives were designed to increase TelSoft’s ability to sense new markets for their products. First, the Division President invited anyone within the organization to make suggestions for new business possibilities. However, potential suggestions had to pass through the Product Manager, whom the Division President described as the ‘gatekeeper of opportunities’:

He might think it’s the craziest damn idea he ever heard. But I think, to be open to that person that’s come with the idea, [he should] at least give it the credibility of being recorded. (SCG Meeting #1)

Second, the SCG began reviewing new business opportunities at the monthly SCG meetings. The Product Manager prepared a template for justifying investments based on cost-benefit analysis. During the first two meetings, he used this template to present two proposals for enhancing TelSoft’s existing product line. These initial proposals represented uncontroversial ideas, aligned with old modes of doing business, and targeted existing markets. The SCG failed to think radically about new markets for their products.

## Phase 3: acting

The acting phase began in January 2006, the first meeting in which the Development Manager provided data about current software projects using the KPIs. SCG members prepared documents in advance of the meetings and these became important data sources. For the current project review, the Development Manager prepared a spreadsheet listing cost, schedule, and quality assessments for each project. For the review of new opportunities, the Product Manager provided a prioritized list of possible business opportunities, business cases, and maintained a list of future product releases.

Performance management (major improvement) Project managers began reporting KPIs on time. However, data used to track performance were not always accurate, so the VP of Software developed a tool to automatically retrieve data from the time-tracking system. The SCG also introduced periodic customer account reviews as an important new practice. In these reviews, the project manager responsible for a specific account reflected on the performance of recent releases, identified open issues, and talked about future business opportunities.

During the acting phase, project managers were held more accountable for project performance, and feedback was used to improve performance. The VP of Software enforced the discipline of weekly written status reports and instituted periodic oral customer account reviews. One noticeable feature during this phase was that the SCG members began to use objective data, despite their limitations, to identify troubled projects. Previously, project managers typically reported that their projects were ‘going smoothly’ even as the objective evidence suggested otherwise. In response to such discrepancies, the VP of Software followed up with project managers:

I’m going to invite [project manager] to do a [major account] project review at the next meeting and we’ll rake him over because it ain’t going smoothly. (SCG Meeting #6)

Although the monthly reporting of KPIs increased awareness of problems, monthly KPI reports continued to show that most projects missed deadlines and went over budget – even projects that the SCG thought were going to be successful. The VP of Software urged TelSoft’s project managers to stretch themselves more to meet project goals:

I don’t see any corrective action plans coming from the projects when schedules slip. What I see is, you know, ‘this took longer than we thought or we had this issue come up’ y and then there’s no attempt to make a corrective action plan to get back on track. (SCG Meeting #8)

Social support (some setbacks) The SCG meetings continued to be a useful mechanism for the managers involved to communicate and get a better understanding of the big picture for current and future projects. The software charter was communicated to employees through workshops and to external customers through a letter from the Division President. However, as project managers received more pressure to provide objective project information, problems with the social support context surfaced. The drive to complete projects on time and history of poor project performance caused the VP of Software to threaten mandating extra work from employees until the project got back on track. Rather than trusting others, one project manager insisted on personally completing certain aspects of the project, causing the project to be delayed unnecessarily:

I haven’t had a chance to read three of the five documents and I typically don’t like to ship documents that I haven’t had a chance to read, review, and edit.

Alignment (no change) The system of gathering project information required people throughout the organization to work together: the project managers created the overall plans; the development coordinator scheduled developers for specific tasks; developers provided status against those plans; and the project manager adjusted the project plan. However, the project managers complained that the developers did not provide appropriate estimates. For their part, the project managers did not always adjust plans to reflect what was learned as tasks solidified. Some project managers reported projects as being on track each week, despite objective evidence indicating otherwise. Overall, this lack of coordination and communication among the project managers, development coordinator, and developers caused confusion and prevented progress.

From the standpoint of the Division President, managers and developers did not always keep in mind the business objective of making money. The desire to win contracts initially caused the organization to bid low on the project. With small profit margins, it was even more important for everyone to be mindful of finances. Speaking about the developers:

They can make it look great, they can make the code perfect, but it took us four times the budgeted money to get that perfect. (SCG Meeting #3)

Adaptability (some improvement) TelSoft was more successful with adaptability as they tried new techniques to attract potential customers. They purchased a new contact management system and began to track sales leads, pursuing customers outside of their traditional markets. After initial success in winning a contract in a new market, the SCG began discussing the ways of attracting additional customers and partnering with others to get further business in this non-traditional market. For example, the VP of Software suggested talking to lost customers about what functionality would have been important for them to remain.

Breaking with the tradition of responding to customer requests, TelSoft managers proactively planned to revive the failed spatial database software. This product vision was shared with one of the major clients and favorably received. TelSoft requested feedback regarding the most attractive product features. Although the potential for financial sponsorship was uncertain, the TelSoft managers felt this exercise provided useful insights.

The VP of Software also began pushing for more systematic planning of the features that would be added to each software release. The discussion of business opportunities and assessment of markets would provide valuable input into planning software releases.

## Phase 4: learning

The learning phase began in August 2006 as changes that had been envisioned and tested earlier became institutionalized at TelSoft. The group began to base decisions more confidently on presented data, and there were more critical discussions and questioning during the review of current projects. The research collaboration ended February 2007 with an evaluation of the initiative’s impact. Each of the four members of the SCG was independently interviewed for approximately 1 h regarding the importance and effectiveness of the group (see Appendix B: SCG Evaluation Interview Guide). Several positive comments centered on the areas of stakeholder involvement and product and project portfolio management.

Performance management (no change) Discipline with respect to project portfolio oversight had increased due to the regular review of projects and discussions to prioritize resources across projects. During this phase, the VP of Software decided to assign a project manager to plan and track the funds received by customers as part of their software maintenance fee. According to the VP of Software:

So what I’ve done there is asking major project managers [for maior clientsl to watch the numbers ... try to take some responsibility for what time is being charged to their space. (SCG Meeting #10)

The SCG members valued having a historical record of the project data, but they also realized that their KPI reports were not the early warning system they had imagined.

Social support (some improvement) Through the SCG process and meetings, multiple stakeholders at all levels of the organization participated in creating polices, discussing software strategy, and evaluating business opportunities. In fact, the VP of Software began using the Business Opportunity list in his biweekly managers meeting. The Division President and CEO agreed to continue working with the research team for another year to develop the project management capabilities of selected employees. However, some issues remained with people at lower levels of the organization not sharing information. For instance, in discussing reasons for a project slipping, the Development Manager indicated that a developer had wasted 15 h trying to figure something out alone instead of asking his immediate supervisor for assistance.

SCG members informally spoke about lessons learned from each project and also looked forward to incorporating knowledge learned from more formal post-project reviews. The VP of Software said:

Four months ago, we thought we were going to do a whole lot better with the project, so when we do a post-project review on this, one of things we’ll be looking at is what kind of things happened.

TelSoft managers continued to show interest in sharing product strategy information with external clients. The software charter was posted to the company’s website and shared face-to-face with management representatives from major clients. Face-to-face interactions with major customers increased so that TelSoft could better understand their needs and customers could better understand software capabilities.

Alignment (no change) During this phase, alignment among SCG members remained fairly strong. Group members continued to share the software charter internally and externally.

Adaptability (some improvement) During this time period, several actions were taken to help the SCG focus on understanding and responding to market needs. First, a temporary worker was hired to contact potential customers and qualify leads. Second, TelSoft’s Product Manager increased the number of product demonstrations and webinars. Finally, the SCG discussed expanding the sales force to follow-up on leads. As a consequence, the business opportunity review list continued to grow and supported a much richer discussion over a larger percentage of the meeting time.

In addition, TelSoft made strides in challenging outmoded practices. For example, the Division President took a more proactive role in educating other senior managers across TelSoft about product capabilities at the annual manager’s meeting. In another example, the development team adopted new strategies in testing and algorithm development that resulted in greater efficiencies. The Development Manager reported:

We’re way ahead of schedule and I think that’s related to a new approach to the span correction algorithm that we’re doing y [This] turns out to be a lot easier.

While these gains did provide some level of improvement in adaptability, TelSoft’s progress was hampered by insufficient resources. Follow-up became particularly challenging when one of TelSoft’s two sales people resigned.

## Summary of analysis

Figure 2 summarizes changes in context throughout the four phases. Birkinshaw & Gibson (2004) have categorized context based upon low and high amounts of social support and performance management, with the high performance context being the most effective long term. TelSoft started in the country club context (i.e., strong social support, weak performance management). During the establishing and acting phases, performance management improved significantly as the SCG increasingly held project managers accountable, used objective data to make decisions, and measured performance against goals. Many positive actions took place, including holding customer account reviews during SCG meetings and more systematically evaluating KPIs for current projects. However, it remained problematic that staff throughout the organization had difficulty sharing accurate and timely information. For a time, this negatively impacted the social support context, taking TelSoft temporarily into the burnout context (i.e., low social support, high performance management). During the learning phase, the social support context improved somewhat as the SCG members deliberately tried to involve other managers in decisions about business opportunities, learn from failure through post-project reviews, and develop project management capabilities of select employees.

Throughout each phase of the initiative, adaptability improved as evidenced by the growing business opportunity list and the willingness of TelSoft to approach non-traditional customers.

Although evidence supports our claim that the intervention helped TelSoft to transform from a reactive organization within a country club context toward an ambidextrous organization with a high performance context, challenges remained related to both performance management and social support. Overall, however, TelSoft’s management team was pleased with our research collaboration as demonstrated in this email message from TelSoft’s VP of Software:

It has been a good education experience for most of the individuals in the software group, and by involving a large number of the software employees in the innovation initiatives it has demonstrated to the entire group the importance of following a few key policies and processes to ensure that we have an appropriate level of control and repeatability to maintain a successful software business.

## Discussion

We have addressed the contradictory strategic choices that software organizations face as they develop customized and packaged solutions for the market (Sawyer, 2000; Xu & Brinkkemper, 2007). In particular, we have focused on firm-level challenges of managing complex portfolios of products and projects (Carmel & Becker, 1995; Cusumano & Selby, 1997), and we have explored how building contexts that facilitate and enable ambidextrous capability can help address these challenges. Considering our integrated model for building contextual ambidexterity (Figure 1) and the empirical results from TelSoft (Figures 2 and 3), we offer principles for how software organizations can improve firm-level coordination through contextual ambidexterity.

Principle 1 (Process): Establish a software coordination group with an agenda focusing on product portfolio management, project portfolio management, market and technology research, and software process improvement. Software organizations may establish a coordination group (the SCG at TelSoft) as a vehicle for improving firm-level coordination. The coordination group involves senior managers representing software development (e.g., quality assurance, product development) as well as external relations (e.g., sales, marketing, business intelligence). The coordination group promotes the value of building contextual ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004) and commits to meet on a regular basis to discuss product portfolio management, project portfolio management, market and technology research, and software process improvement (see Figure 4). The establishment of the coordination group and the details of its unfolding agenda are provided in Tables 2 and 3.

Principle 2 (Process): Guided by a software charter, develop a context that facilitates and enables ambidextrous capability through an iterative process of diagnosing, establishing, acting, and learning. Ambidextrous capability in support of firm-level coordination is developed through an iterative process consisting of four phases (McFeeley, 1996): (1) diagnosing the software organization’s alignment, adaptability, performance management, and social support (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004); (2) establishing an approach to build contextual ambidexterity; (3) acting to transform firm-level coordination practices; and (4) learning and institutionalizing new practices. To guide this process, the coordination group creates and maintains a software charter specifying the organization’s reason for being, software strategy, and operating policies. The software charter establishes which products, projects, and processes are aligned with the organization’s goals. If there is ambiguity on these items, the coordination group debates, reaches consensus, and publicizes the result. The specific activities of each phase and the elements of the software charter are detailed in Table 2.

Principle 3 (Content): Increase alignment by focusing on project portfolio management and software process improvement. Alignment is present when the organization’s projects, people, and processes perform consistently toward achieving business goals and objectives (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). To assess alignment, the software organization begins by evaluating its existing project portfolio, staff capabilities, development processes and infrastructure. Information about staff deficiencies can be used to identify professional development needs. The coordination group can initiate, assess, and improve processes related to project portfolio management and software process improvement. At periodic coordination group meetings, the project portfolio can be managed through reviewing the status of development projects and process improvement initiatives and adjusting them as needed. Suggested actions for attending to alignment over time are provided in the first row of Table 2.

Principle 4 (Content): Increase adaptability by focusing on product portfolio management and market & technology research. Adaptability occurs when the organization is able to scan the external environment and adjust its product portfolio appropriately (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). To assess adaptability, the organization characterizes the competitiveness of its product portfolio with respect to customer needs, market demands, and emerging technologies. The coordination group agenda covers adaptability by including customer account reviews and analysis of market and technology opportunities. Hence, the coordination group initiates, assesses, and improves processes for analyzing market and technology opportunities and customer account reviews. Suggested actions for attending to adaptability over time are provided in the second row of Table 2.

Principle 5 (Context): Apply established principles for contextual ambidexterity to improve performance management and social support. Performance management (i.e., discipline and stretch) and social support (i.e., trust and support) can be benchmarked against Gibson & Birkinshaw’s (2004) principles. Assessment results can inform plans for reinforcing strengths and overcoming weaknesses. To improve discipline by holding managers and developers accountable, the coordination group can initiate, assess, and improve the process for collecting and reviewing KPIs. To cultivate stretch by challenging managers and developers, the coordination group can explore business opportunities within new accounts and reward timely project completion. A positive social support context facilitates performance management activities. For example, supportive and trusting relationships can help managers and developers collaborate in resolving discrepancies in reported data and assessing business options. The coordination group is charged with continuously improving the context by collecting data, identifying improvement areas, reflecting on lessons learned, and sharing best practices across the organization. ## Conclusion

Future research could overcome several limitations of this study: the single-case design that does not allow for comparisons across contrasting contexts; the focus on firm-level coordination that does not consider insights from other types of innovation initiatives; and, our restricted conceptualization of contextual ambidexterity relying on Gibson & Birkinshaw (2004). Future research could enrich theory by further investigating the aspects of organizational context that influence the process of becoming ambidextrous. For example, we found that TelSoft was particularly influenced by historical events such as unsuccessful attempts at innovation of software products and processes. There may be additional contextual factors in the context of software organizations that can be identified and discussed. Future research could also extend our understanding of how and when contextual characteristics translate into ambidextrous capability. Although our study demonstrates that managers at TelSoft developed a high performance context and increased the organization’s ability to align and adapt, our application of Gibson & Birkinshaw’s (2004) framework did not provide detailed insights into how individuals at TelSoft negotiated conflicting demands for alignment and adaptability. While current theory emphasizes that ambidextrous capability emerges as organizations move towards high performance contexts, there might be additional value in investigating the threshold or the critical point they need to cross before we consider them ambidextrous.

Prior to this research, ambidextrous principles have been applied to software organizations on the projectlevel (Boehm & Turner, 2004) and on the firm-level in terms of structural solutions (Vinekar et al, 2006). We have explored contextual ambidexterity as an alternative suited to support firm-level coordination. Future research could provide more specific comparisons between the applicability of contextual and structural ambidexterity and the different processes through which these capabilities are developed. Future research could also validate our framework (Figure 1) and principles (see above) under different antecedent conditions. Given the relative absence of theories of the process of becoming ambidextrous in both management and software research, more efforts should be expended to understand the unfolding of these complex change processes over time.

Our findings also have implications for practicing software managers seeking to apply ambidextrous principles. One key implication is that the contextual ambidexterity lens may be used to help shape intervention into firm-level coordination of software organizations. Guided by our principles, managers could explicitly assess the organizational context across each phase, develop improvement goals, and design effective interventions. Our analysis of the change process indicates the importance of addressing contextual issues early so that the proper conditions (heightened social support and performance management) for improving other capabilities are established. For organizations like TelSoft that are diagnosed as weak in performance management but stronger at social support, Gibson & Birkinshaw (2004) recommend to focus first on performance management. By contrast, organizations with weak social support are recommended to work first at increasing trust and support. Over time, managers should anticipate such shifts between improvements in contextual issues and attention to alignment and adaptability.

## SCG evaluation interview guide

The following questions served as the basis for the interviews regarding the SCG. All questions were not always asked of each person.

1. What do you see as the underlying reason for having this team?

2. What is the main impact of this team?

3. How effective has this team been in managing its effort?

4. Specifically ask about each item on fixed agenda: current projects, business opportunities, improvement initiatives, account review, and strategy.

5. What changes could improve this team’s effectiveness?

6. What is your long-term vision for this team?

7. What goals should this team focus on next year?

8. What role do the policies play in business decisions and everyday actions?

9. What have you shared with your customers about policies and SPI? How do you think this has been received?
