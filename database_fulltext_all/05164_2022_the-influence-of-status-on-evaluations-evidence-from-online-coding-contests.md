# THE INFLUENCE OF STATUS ON EVALUATIONS: EVIDENCE FROM ONLINE CODING CONTESTS<sup>1</sup>

Swanand J. Deodhar Information Systems Area, Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad, Gujarat, INDIA, {swanandd@iima.ac.in}

Yash Babar Operations and Information Management, Wisconsin School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Madison, Madison, WI, U.S.A. {ybabar@wisc.edu}

Gordon Burtch Information Systems, Questrom School of Business, Boston University, MA, U.S.A.{gburtch@bu.edu}

In many instances, online contest platforms rely on contestants to ensure submission quality. This scalable evaluation mechanism offers a collective benefit. However, contestants may also leverage it to achieve personal, competitive benefits. Our study examines this tension from a status-theoretic perspective, suggesting that the conflict between competitive and collective benefits, and the net implication for evaluation efficacy, is influenced by contestants’ status. On the one hand, contestants of lower status may be viewed as less skilled and hence more likely to make mistakes. Therefore, low-status contestants may attract more evaluations if said evaluations are driven predominantly by an interest in collective benefits. On the other hand, if evaluations are driven largely by an interest in personal, competitive benefits, a low-status contestant makes for a less attractive target and hence may attract fewer evaluations. We empirically test these competing possibilities using a dataset of coding contests from Codeforces. The platform allows contestants to assess others’ submissions and improve evaluations (a collective benefit) by devising test cases (hacks) in addition to those defined by the contest organizer. If a submission is successfully hacked, the hacker earns additional points, and the target submission is eliminated from the contest (a competitive benefit). We begin by providing qualitative evidence based on semi-structured interviews conducted with contestants spanning the status spectrum at Codeforces. Next, we present quantitative evidence exploiting a structural change at Codeforces wherein many contestants experienced an arbitrary status reduction unrelated to their performance because of sudden changes to the platform’s color-coding system around contestant ratings. We show that status-loser contestants received systematically more evaluations from other contestants, absent changes in their short-run submission quality. Finally, we show that the excess evaluations allocated toward affected contestants were less effective, indicating status-driven evaluations as potentially less efficacious. We discuss the implications of our findings for managing evaluation processes in online contests.

Keywords: Evaluation, arbitrary status change, competitive coding, online contests

## Introduction

Online contest platforms are now an established avenue for organizing distributed problem solving (Hutter et al., 2011). Evaluating the large volume of contributions is, however, a perennial problem. The scale of submissions implies that evaluations require considerable time and effort to process. To overcome this challenge, platforms often turn to users (henceforth, contestants) to evaluate each other’s submissions (Chen et al., 2020). For instance, Threadless (an online apparel design platform) allows designer contestants to vote on each other’s designs and uses these evaluations to decide whether a design moves forward toward production (Riedl et al., 2020). An important benefit of such evaluation schemes is that it makes the process highly transparent, e.g., votes received by a given design are visible on Threadless and, in cases of subjective opinions, this process is effectively democratic (Hoornaert et al., 2017).

The design of these evaluation schemes is important because their fairness and transparency (or lack thereof) can have significant implications for contestants’ behavior on the platform. A potentially problematic aspect, which we focus on here, is that evaluations may be influenced by the evaluating contestant’s competitive interests, as well as the perceived status of the contribution’s author. Relying on contestants to evaluate each other’s submissions thus implies that the platform walks a tight rope, balancing the collective benefit of scalable evaluations with a potential lack of objectivity, both because evaluators may respond to status cues may face conflicts of interest (e.g., evaluators may shade their evaluations to undermine competition). We explore this potential tension here, seeking to better understand the dynamics of contestant-driven evaluations in online contests. We explore the potential trade-off in such evaluation schemes, employing a status-theoretic perspective, suggesting that based on their perceived status, a contestant may be subjected to a differing degree of evaluation from others. The direction of that difference will speak to the relative dominance of personal, competitive benefits as a driver of evaluation behavior versus community, collective benefits.

Our emphasis on status is driven by prior work in multiple streams of literature, which we draw upon jointly to inform our understanding of evaluation in this context. We examine the tension between status and evaluation highlighted in prior work. A large body of literature suggests that a high-status actor may escape evaluation simply due to their status and the resulting assumption of competence it fosters (Sharkey, 2014). For example, in scientific, academic communities, it has been argued that a “social bias” in the evaluation of publications weakens the credibility and efficacy of the review process because publication is ideally based on merit and not the identity of the author (Lee et al., 2013). To reduce this notion to an aphorism, to the extent that “who says it” outweighs “what is said,” the quality of evaluations is degraded.

At the same time, other streams of literature have argued that high-status actors may actually attract greater attention in evaluations as a result of their prominence (Graffin et al.,

2013; Rustichini & Vostroknutov, 2008). Prior studies have documented evidence that gaming and manipulation arise in online contest voting (Xu et al., 2021) and that contestants systematically target high-skilled contributors (Riedl et al., 2020) as evaluators seek to further their own relative performance in a contest. Relatedly, in organizational settings, recent evidence has similarly suggested that a company exhibiting strong social performance (indicative of higher status) may find its IT systems disproportionately targeted by hackers (D’Arcy et al., 2020) who are seeking to “make a name for themselves.” Given these competing mechanisms, the dominant role of status in driving evaluations in online contests is unclear; addressing this uncertainty is one of the key objectives of our study.

We argue that the role that status plays in driving evaluations likely depends on whether evaluators are motivated more by competitive or cooperative concerns, given that evaluation decisions in online contests may combine elements of both. Specifically, the collective benefit of potentially fairer and more robust evaluations may be compromised, at least partially, if contestants use evaluations as an opportunity to further their individual, competitive interests, wherein evaluators may recognize that the poor performance (as signaled by the lowering of status) of a fellow contestant may be to their own personal benefit. This tension raises questions as to whether and in what way a given contestant’s status may influence the evaluations they attract. Our study provides an empirical examination of these relationships, addressing the following research question:

RQ: In online contests, how does a contestant’s status influence the extent to which they are subjected to evaluations by other contestants?

We undertake our study in the context of an online platform for software coding contests, Codeforces.com, which relies on voluntary participation from its contestants in the form of solutions to its contest problems. This context is well-suited to studying our research question for multiple reasons. First, the platform incorporates a defined evaluation mechanism, known as “hacking,”<sup>2</sup> wherein individual contestants can formally challenge/evaluate others’ submissions. If a hack is successful, the hacker is awarded points. Simultaneously, a successful hack invalidates the original submission, damaging the respective contestant’s chances of winning the contest. Thus, a successful hack offers the hacker a strong competitive benefit. At the same time, the hacking feature is aimed at improving evaluations by spotting defective submissions that could bypass the contest’s limited, pre-coded set of test cases.

By encouraging hacks, the platform crowdsources the discovery and implementation of niche test cases that can lead to potentially broader and effective evaluations to which competitive submissions are subjected. Second, the platform provides an explicit status hierarchy. It ranks contestants into color-coded tiers, which associate coarsely with their performance in past contests. Contestants situated in higher color categories enjoy greater prestige and admiration from fellow contestants, signal their expertise to potential recruiters, and even earn a nominal monetary reward directly from the platform. Thus, higher status on Codeforces is associated with several benefits.

We begin by offering qualitative evidence about the role of status in contest evaluations based on semi-structured interviews with seven contestants from Codeforces who span the status hierarchy. The results of these interviews provide initial evidence that contestants do indeed incorporate competitors’ status markers (color codes) into their decisions about whom to “hack.” Subsequently, we present additional, large-scale quantitative evidence that status affects evaluation decisions. Our analyses exploit an exogenous event in which the platform operator arbitrarily changed the status hierarchy, redefining the relationship between performance ratings and color tiers.<sup>3</sup> This structural change resulted in many contestants being reassigned to a lower position in the status hierarchy, despite exhibiting no changes in their performance.

We leverage this arbitrary shock to identify the effect of status on evaluation decisions (“hacking attempts”) as distinct from performance ratings. Our estimates indicate that submissions from contestants assigned to a lower status experienced an approximately two-fold increase in the number of hacking attempts received. Importantly, that we observe an inverse relationship between status and evaluation implies that cooperative, community benefits tend to dominate personal, competitive benefits as a motivator for evaluation decisions.

We provide several additional analyses that establish the robustness of the estimated effects, demonstrating that they are not driven by a reduction in affected contestants’ quality or timing of submission.<sup>4</sup> Further, we show that the observed effects persist beyond the status change event for several subsequent contests. Importantly, we also find that statusinduced evaluations were less effective, as the increase in hacking attempts received by affected contestants was paired with a rather large decline (\~70%) in the rate of successful hacking attempts.

Our study makes several contributions. In demonstrating the role of status in online contest evaluations, our work builds on the literature dealing with evaluation mechanisms in contests and tournaments. Specifically, while the literature has provided an indirect connection between evaluation and status, indicating that contestants’ status drives their participation in the presence of contestant-driven evaluations (Chen et al., 2020), our work provides a more direct link, showing that status has an inverse relationship with the intensity of evaluation that a contestant attracts. Further, in showing that status-driven evaluations were less effective, our study implies that platforms should consider masking contestant status markers from potential evaluators to encourage more objective evaluation decisions.

## Related Work

## Evaluation in Online Contests

A trustworthy, meritocratic evaluation regime is crucial to motivating voluntary contributions on online contest platforms. A codified, centralized quality evaluation mechanism presents a challenge, given the scale and openended nature of many contests. Accordingly, contest platforms often rely on contestants not only to solve contest problems but also to evaluate each other’s submissions. Contest evaluations typically occur through a dedicated feedback mechanism (e.g., voting) that allows contestants to record their evaluation of others’ submissions (Chen et al., 2020). As the contest concludes, these evaluations inform performance evaluations and the selection of winners.

Of course, feedback and evaluation mechanisms of this sort are not unique to online contests. For example, in open-source software projects, “mutual cross-examination” of users’ code contributions is a long-standing practice (Johnson, 2006). The members of OSS projects spend a great deal of their active time and effort evaluating each other’s contributions and communicating their feedback to other users (Rigby & Storey, 2011). On Wikipedia, users similarly evaluate and refine each other’s article contributions (Kane & Ransbotham, 2016). And, on ideation platforms, community members collectively vote on each other’s ideas and suggestions (Bayus, 2013; Huang et al., 2014). However, the particularly competitive nature of online contests raises important tensions around the evaluation mechanism.

While the evaluation scheme offers potentially fairer and more robust assessment, the prospect also exists that the contestants may leverage the evaluation mechanism to gain personal, competitive benefits over others. That is, contestants might leverage these evaluation mechanisms to undermine their competition (Konrad, 2007). An interesting question is thus the extent to which cooperative or competitive motivations may serve as dominant drivers of contestant evaluations in online contests. The uncertainty, we argue, is tied to platforms practice of saliently revealing contestants’ social cues, specifically status, which acts as a heuristic of ability and quality (Levina & Arriaga, 2014). That is, we suggest that status, given its presumed linkage with quality, could significantly drive how contestants evaluate each other in online contests and contests in general (Flynn & Amantullah, 2012).

## Role of Status in Evaluations

Status, known to be a powerful social attribute, is defined as “one’s relative standing in a social hierarchy as determined by respect, deference, and social influence” (Ridgeway, 2000; p. 281). In extending status to evaluations in online contests, our central argument is that letting contestants evaluate each other may lead to problems because “hierarchies of attention, status, and influence” emerge among contestants (Shaw & Hill, 2014; p. 218). The pool of contestants in an online contest is unlikely to be homogenous (Boudreau et al., 2016), as the status hierarchies create clear demarcations between contestants. In general, online platforms explicitly encourage status hierarchies, often through salient cues (Levina & Arriaga, 2014). Consequently, we argue that contestants’ status appears as a highly visible social property of a focal contestant, which could influence others’ behavior, including evaluations, towards them. Especially given the scale of most online platforms and the congregation of veritable strangers virtually with a low chance of extensive interpersonal relationships, on-platform status is perhaps the most visible distinguisher between constituents.

In prior work, there are two competing arguments about the precise manifestation of the effects of status on evaluation. On the one hand, contestants might evaluate those with lower status more stringently. Indeed, some evidence consistent with this possibility has been reported in past work, pointing to contributors’ social characteristics as determinants of the evaluations they receive (Lee et al., 2013). Specifically, a lower-status contestant could be viewed as one with subpar expertise and skills, who would be hence more likely to make mistakes. In contrast, a high-status contestant might be viewed as a likely expert and hence presumably less susceptible to making errors. This perception could lead to a lower-status contestant attracting more evaluation, while a higher-status contestant might be seen as intimidating.

This argument is in line with the view that those placed higher in the status hierarchy accrue significant and even disproportionate benefits, relative to those placed lower (Piazza & Castellucci, 2013). For instance, as Kim and King (2014) found in their study of umpiring decisions within a prominent sports league, “the high expectations set by a social actor’s greater status allows them room for error that lowerstatus actors do not have” (Kim & King, 2014, p. 2636). In their recent work on voting-based evaluations, Chen et al. (2020) found that higher-status contestants prefer online contests that rely on such evaluations, anticipating a favorable assessment from others on account of their higher status (Chen et al., 2020). Thus, the lower status of a contestant could project them as low on skills, thereby attracting significantly more evaluation from other contestants.

Were this relationship to manifest, it would not be in the platform’s best interest, as it would presumably lead to a richget-richer effect, with high-status contestants avoiding critique. Further, such a relationship, if present, would be consistent with a collective, community benefit serving as the primary driver of evaluation choices. In targeting lower-status individuals, evaluators would presumably be relying on status markers as a heuristic, seeking to identify and weed out lowerquality submissions.

In contrast, there is also the possibility that a lower-status contestant may make for a less attractive target and hence face lesser evaluation from rivals. Some prior work reports evidence consistent with this narrative as well. In their laboratory experiments, Rustichini and Vostroknutov (2008) showed that in contests of skill, contestants holding higher status attract greater evaluation and scrutiny than contestants of lower status because evaluators perceive that they have more to gain from the dethronement of a successful competitor. Several field studies report similar findings in other competitive contexts (Balafoutas et al., 2012; Deutscher et al., 2013). Recent evidence in the online contest literature has even suggested that contestants of high skill tend to be targeted by other high-skill competitors while lower-skill contestants fly under the radar (Riedl et al., 2020). These findings are in line with the broader claim that “victims of mobbing are sometimes found to be overachieving” (Münster, 2007; p. 943).

Should evaluation and scrutiny concentrate at the top of the status hierarchy, it would not be in the platform’s best interest because it would imply that the longer tail of lower-status contestants would be deprived of feedback, hindering their learning and advancement, in addition to allowing their presumably lower-quality submissions to achieve undue success. Moreover, if this relationship were to manifest empirically, it would be consistent with a personal, competitive benefit serving as the primary driver of evaluation choices. In systematically targeting higher-status individuals, evaluators would presumably be relying on status markers as a heuristic to identify opportunities to undermine the competition and advance their interests.

In sum, while prior work has suggested that status is likely to influence the evaluation choices made by rival contestants, the extent to which this is true remains unclear, as does the directionality of the effect. By examining whether a lowerstatus actor will systematically attract more or less evaluation from peers, we can gain a better understanding of the role that status plays, as well as the motivations that underlie contestants’ use and participation in contest evaluation mechanisms.

## Study Context

## Codeforces.com

Our study is situated at a coding contest platform named Codeforces (http://www.codeforces.com). The platform hosts regular time-bound contests in which contestants can submit multiple solutions to improve their contest score against a predefined software scoring algorithm. A contest is typically a collection of coding-related problems. Unlike other contest platforms (Boudreau et al., 2011), Codeforces does not solicit contests from client organizations. Instead, it allows its contestants to develop such contests. To that end, the platform is self-governing and hence akin to other prominent platforms such as OSS projects and Wikipedia. Moreover, it is in the platform’s interest to ensure that contest winners have submitted a solution that is rigorously evaluated for quality. If solutions of inferior quality win contests simply because they have not been thoroughly scrutinized, contestants may, over time, lose interest in competing. Ensuring that winners are indeed entering high-quality solutions is further necessitated by the real-world implications of contest wins, such as job hunting or signaling to recruiters. If winning a contest is not directly connected to the quality of the submitted code, the signaling value of succeeding on the platform diminishes for recruiters as well. Therefore, the platform has a clear motivation to ensure that strict quality norms are enforced.

As the first step in the workflow of a typical contest, the contest creator(s) on Codeforces provide input for each problem, along with the expected output in a predefined format. Predetermined, automated tests, which measure performance based on output accuracy and on time and memory consumed by the code, are used to evaluate submissions. Submissions are rejected on upload if they do not meet minimum contest-specific requirements for each test (https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/4088). Moreover, contests impose a penalty against a contestant’s final score calculation for each rejected submission. This constraint acts as a simple, quantitative filter to ensure the baseline quality of entered solutions. The accepted submissions for each problem count toward a contestant’s final score in the contest. Finally, each contestant is ranked (in descending order) by their total score (across problems). This ranking informs a platformwide rating based on the well-established Elo rating system, which is used in chess and other competitive sports (Neumann et al., 2011). Elo ratings consider the expected ranking of a contestant in a particular contest relative to other contestants, such that rating changes based on contest performance are a product of both comparisons between contestants and comparisons within the contestant’s current as well as past performance. This approach ensures that contests remain a challenging activity in the long run.

To participate in a contest, the contestant must first register for the contest online, indicating a willingness to be available at the designated time. Once the contest begins, the contestant typically makes multiple submissions. Multiple submissions occur because contestants may submit their solutions to different problems in the contest problem set, or they may repeatedly revise prior submissions to the same problem to improve. Typically, a contestant makes repeated submissions for a single problem until the solution meets the baseline evaluation requirements and the platform accepts it. Submissions to a contest may be one of two types: Competitive or Practice. Competitive submissions take place while a contest is live, and they have the potential to impact the contestant’s Elo rating (and thus, in turn, their status). Practice submissions, on the other hand, may be made any time during or after a contest has ended, and they have no rating implications. Our focus here is obviously on competitive submissions.

## Contestant Status Hierarchy

Codeforces is highly transparent in its assignment of contestants to distinct status categories. First, based on the contestant’s current rating, they are allocated to one of two divisions.<sup>5</sup> Contestants can submit solutions to contests hosted either in their division or in contests that are explicitly made open to both divisions. Second, contestants belong to a color-coded grouping in the status hierarchy, based on their Elo rating, such that a contestant’s username is displayed consistently in the associated color across the platform. For other details of the contestant’s past performance or participation, one must navigate to their individual profile pages.

Because color assignments derive from past performance, a contestant’s peers infer expertise and skill levels based on the color codes. The monikers associated with the categories, e.g., Newbie, Expert, and Grandmaster, reinforce these interpretations. Because the color scheme has existed since November 2011, there has been ample opportunity for these colors to develop meaning in the community to serve as a heuristic of contestants’ expertise on Codeforces.com. Anecdotal evidence from discussion thread postings at Codeforces.com also suggests that contestants ascribe importance and value to this color-coding. Further, as discussed in the Qualitative Evidence section below, we conducted several long-form interviews with various contestants on Codeforces across the status spectrum to understand how they perceive the color codes and how the colors factor into hacking decisions. There, we report additional consistent evidence.

In contrast to some other settings, the significance of the color codes as status signals are quite high at Codeforces because the platform provides little in the way of alternative rewards, such as money. Thus, belonging to a higher-status category presents a robust, extrinsic driver for contestants. Simply put, because of their highly salient presence and the importance that contestants attach to them, these categories are arguably a vital feature of the platform. Contestants quickly notice any changes made to these categories. Further, the design of these categorizations implies they are valid markers of status. This approach of demarcating status positions through non-overlapping categories is consistent with past work on arbitrary changes in status (Bowers & Prato, 2018) and work considering the role of status in contests (Moldovanu et al., 2007).

## Evaluations on Codeforces: Hacking

On Codeforces, the feature that enables contestants’ evaluation of one another’s submissions is known as hacking.<sup>6</sup> The process of hacking works as follows: during a contest, the platform randomly allocates each contestant to a virtual room with a maximum of 19 other contestants, i.e., 20 contestants per room.<sup>7</sup> Once assigned to a room, contestants can enter their solutions in the form of software code. Once a contestant submits a solution, they can view competing solutions entered by other contestants in the same room. The objective of revealing competing submissions is to enable any contestant to evaluate others’ solutions, potentially devising test cases they believe would break other contestants’ code. If contestant j formulates a test case that reveals a flaw in the code created by contestant i, then contestant i’s solution is considered to have been successfully hacked by j, and j is awarded additional points in turn.

Each solution on Codeforces is also subjected to codified procedural quality checks, as noted above, independent of any hacking. Contest creators develop a series of test cases to which each solution submitted in a contest is subjected. A solution is considered valid, provided it passes these predefined test cases. However, the baseline set of cases may not be exhaustive, as these require foresight on the part of the contest designer. The crowdsourcing of problem formulation, while delivering variety and novelty, requires that the problem author also devise extensive tests to check submitted solutions. A solution may be categorized as valid, although the submission would break when exposed to additional test cases that did not occur to the contest designer. The hacking mechanism addresses this possibility because it empowers individual contestants to evaluate their peers’ submissions, providing them with the ability to devise additional test cases. As more test cases are devised, and more solutions are evaluated against them, the body of Codeforces contest submissions improves in quality, and the Elo ratings become more reflective of true quality and ability. Such dynamic improvement is likely to be seen within each contest over the time since commencement.

The process by which a contestant comes up with additional test cases is akin to a key mechanism underlying similar evaluation schemes in traditional software development, namely, shared mental models (Kude et al., 2019; Mohammed & Dumville, 2001). Shared mental models, as noted earlier, are defined as systematized understanding and mental representation of environment’s principal facets (Kude et al., 2019; Mohammed & Dumville, 2001). A contestant develops solutions under strict timelines on Codeforces (each contest lasts for only 2-3 hours) and, along the way, discovers pitfalls and traps.<sup>8</sup> The contestant can expect other contestants to encounter such pitfalls, given that (1) all contestants provide solutions to the same set of problems, (2) they have an identical view of the problem statement, and (3) they undergo the same stress and time constraints when competing. With this anticipation, a given contestant is likely to come up with ideas for additional test cases and can assess whether competing solutions address the nonobvious pitfalls involved.

However, not all solutions submitted in a room may be subjected to hacking. First, it may be infeasible to hack all the competing submissions because of strict time constraints. With each contest running for 2-3 hours, contestants may have limited time to complete their solutions and then engage in hacking. Second, the platform penalizes the indiscriminate use of hacking. Specifically, while the platform rewards a successful hack with 100 points, it also deducts approximately 50 points for an unsuccessful hacking attempt. This rule is designed to avoid potentially frivolous evaluation activity, which may not provide any meaningful improvement in solution quality. As a result, individual contestants must be selective in identifying which solutions to evaluate and hack.

To this end, the status of the submitting contestant is likely to be a highly salient heuristic that others rely upon to identify submissions they wish to evaluate. Specifically, our prior arguments suggest that a hacker contestant may either overlook submissions from a higher-status contestant, presuming the submission to be of inherently better quality, or evaluate such submissions more stringently. As a result, the status of a contestant can be expected to influence their receipt of hacking attempts (i.e., evaluations).

## Methods

## Qualitative Evidence

Before proceeding to quantitative analyses, we present some insights from preliminary, semi-structured interviews that we conducted with seven Codeforces contestants who spanned the status spectrum; the interviewees represented most of the status (color) tiers on Codeforces. We conducted these interviews with a specific focus on the process of hacking on Codeforces. Broadly, we sought to assess whether our arguments, especially the role played by status in evaluations, were congruent with how contestants engaged in the hacking activity in live contests. Thus, we employed this qualitative data collection in a confirmatory manner as a complement to our quantitative analyses (Bryman, 2006; Jick, 1979; Maxwell, 2004). As Jick (1979) states, when it comes to the use of multiple methods to build a more holistic picture, “qualitative methods, in particular, can play an especially prominent role by eliciting data and suggesting conclusions to which other methods would be blind. Elements of the context are illuminated” (Jick, 1979; p. 603).<sup>9</sup>

First, interviewees’ statements generally corroborated our view that hacking serves as an added evaluation mechanism on Codeforces, one offered with meritocracy in mind. As Interviewee A stated, “in a way, we are improving testing. The hack cases that we develop get incorporated in the testing”. In a related quote, the contestant stated that “[hacking] judges solutions correctly. Otherwise, invalid solutions may also get accepted.” Second, interviewees’ statements also supported our claim that evaluation (hacking) is a resource-intensive activity that is subject to budget constraints. That is, contestants have to prioritize which solutions to hack because testing all competitor solutions would be infeasible. As Interviewee B stated, “if there are 20 submissions, then checking each submission in a row [i.e., in sequence] doesn’t make sense. You try to find strategies for which code to check first.”

Third, interviewees made statements indicating that the status of other contestants plays a role in their decisions about whether to attempt to hack a submission. As Interviewee C explained, “in a room, we first start with those people who have solved the problem, but their color or rating is not that good. There is a chance because their standing is not so good, they made some mistakes.” Interviewee B also stated that “if a person is higher, then there is a high probability that he may have written a bug-free code.” Such statements were a consistent theme among our interviewees. Accordingly, it appears that the status of a contestant does affect their propensity to receive hacking attempts (i.e., evaluations).

These interviewee statements provide some initial evidence that evaluation is undertaken to some extent with community benefits in mind, i.e., a desire to identify lower-quality submissions. Further, the interviews made clear that status plays a role in the choice of whom to evaluate. However, the extent to which these qualitative statements from seven interviewees generalize to the platform more broadly remains unclear. Moreover, interviewees’ awareness of the meritocratic intent of the evaluation mechanism may also have fostered a social desirability bias in their responses. That is, interviewees may have been unwilling to admit to using the evaluation mechanism to undermine competitors. Accordingly, we next undertook a systematic evaluation, at scale, employing quantitative analyses of archival data related to contestant evaluation activity.

## Quantitative Evidence

The relationship between status and evaluation can be difficult to disentangle because hacking attempts may focus on certain contestants due to factors that confound status. For instance, to the extent that status correlates with actual performance, a high-status user may attract fewer hacking attempts because their submissions are of higher quality. Accordingly, to isolate the effect of status on contestant evaluation decisions, we considered a policy change that induced arbitrary status changes among a group of contestants, independent of any variation in their actual performance. The policy change in question resulted in the sudden reassignment of many contestants to a lower status, independent of any changes in their actual performance.

On October 1, 2015, Codeforces issued a blog post announcing a sudden modification to its color grouping system (https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/20638). Although there were some indications of an impending change, the platform operator did not disclose the specific details or timing ahead of time. Specifically, the color status indicators were changed to incorporate a new color group, leading to a change in the composition of all groups situated above it in the hierarchy. Figure 1a provides the preexisting set of categories, while Figure 1b depicts the status categories after the exogenous change. Comparing Figures 1a and 1b, we can see the change in the rating ranges associated with different color tiers, along with the introduction of the new cyan color tier. We exploit this event as an identification strategy that locates the sets of contestants who did versus did not arbitrarily acquire lower status because of the modification to the status hierarchy.

## Data & Measures

Our estimation sample is a panel of contestant-contest pairs, based on all contestants registered at the Codeforces platform (89,590 contestants) and their interaction with the four contests immediately surrounding the event in question, i.e., the platform operator’s change to the status hierarchy. More specifically, our sample draws on the pair of contests (one contest each for Division 1 and Division 2) hosted before the change and the pair immediately following.<sup>10</sup> By adopting this data structure, our focus is implicitly on the immediate, shortrun effect of arbitrarily acquired lower status upon contestants’ receipt of hacking attempts. Each observation in the estimation sample reflects a contestant-contest pair and includes contestant-contest specific measures of the contestant’s submissions to the contest (i.e., whether they registered for the contest, submitted any solutions, the total volume of solutions that were submitted, the total number of problems the user attempted to solve, the number of incoming or outgoing hacks, etc.).

Each observation includes a static measure of the contestant’s initial performance (Elo) rating and tenure on the platform, just before the change event, as well as the contestant’s ranking relative to other contestants from the same country, as well as a measure of the extent of the contestant’s social interaction on the platform (i.e., based on blog posts, and posting activity in the discussion forums). Lastly, each observation includes dynamic, binary indicators of whether the contestant acquired lower or higher status because of the change event—these values were initially 0 for all contestants and, for those who experienced a status color reassignment because of the event, one of the indicators changed to a value of 1. We report the descriptive statistics for this sample in Table 1a and a correlation matrix in Table 2a.

The sample used in our primary analyses of hacking, however, is smaller because to be eligible for hacking, a contestant had to successfully submit to a contest. This constraint reduced our observations significantly, and the subsample statistics and correlation matrix are reported in Tables 1b and 2b, respectively.

Of the 89,590 registered contestants at the time of the platform-initiated change, 20,135 were assigned to a lower status (moved to a lower-ranked color group). It is this group of “treated” individuals that we focus on in our analyses.

## Effects of Evaluation by Competitors

In Table 3, we report the effect of a contestant’s reassignment to lower status on the number of hacks they experience (Model 1), as well as the number of successful incoming hacks (Model 2). We see that the number of incoming hacks increased by nearly 247% $( \beta = 0 . 9 0 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ for those that lost status. We find that the proportion of successful hacks decreased for those with lower status. Although the total number of successful hacks went up, on average, the additional hacking activity induced by the arbitrarily acquired lower status was systematically less likely to be successful than the hacking activity experienced by nonaffected contestants. ## Are the Evaluations Net New or a Redistribution?

While the analyses speak to a rise in the evaluation of lowerstatus contestants, they do not reveal if that rise reflected a simple redistribution of evaluation attention among contestants or if, instead, the rise was to some extent driven by an increase in overall evaluation effort. To discern this, we performed an additional set of analyses. In our context, the hacking activity is restricted to the group of contestants assigned together in the same contest “room.” These room assignments are random, and each room typically comprises 20 contestants. We exploited the random contestant composition of rooms to understand whether rooms randomly constructed with more contestants who arbitrarily lost status would exhibit systematically larger increases in hacking attempts. If we observe a systematic rise in total hacking attempts at the room level as a function of the number of lower-status contestants in the room, this would be consistent with a growth in overall evaluation activity. A null result might suggest a simple redistribution of evaluation effort across contestants.

We performed these regressions with contest fixed effects, again employing Poisson regression. Our key independent variable was the count of contestants who possessed lower status, as a result of the status hierarchy change, in a given room, and we incorporated the number of room participants who submitted at least one solution (and who were thus eligible to be hacked) as an exposure term. In some sense, we thus estimated how the random assignment of more arbitrary lower-status contestants to a contest room impacts the incidence of hacking attempts in that room, with active contestants serving as the denominator.

The results of these analyses are presented in Table 6. We observe there that rooms hosting a larger number or fraction of contestants with an arbitrarily assigned lower status exhibit a significant increase in the incidence of hacking. These findings suggest that the observed shift in the evaluation activity is, to some extent, a net new evaluation effort that is not enacted by the contestants who lost status.

## Does Status Change Differentially Attract Evaluation from Contestants of Higher or Lower Relative Status?

While the focus of the analyses and the discussion so far has largely been on an affected contestant’s chances of receiving evaluations after status loss, it is also important to consider the relative status differential between an evaluator and their target. This assessment speaks to the fact that, inherently, status is a relative phenomenon and that, for the hacker, hacking decisions could be based on their status relative to that of the target. To test this possibility more formally, we examine whether the status gap between the hacker and the target is instrumental in mitigating the effects of the status on hacking for either the hacker or the target.

While the prior models mask any heterogeneity in the status gap, a dyadic level analysis would uncover the existence of such differences. Accordingly, we construct a panel of all possible dyadic combinations of contestants in a given hacking room. Each contestant had made at least one submission and was therefore eligible to initiate a hack as well as be a target of a hack. We built dyads for all the rooms across the four contests in our consideration set, resulting in 82,984 hacker-target pairs.

We modeled the number of incoming hacks from one contestant in each dyad toward the other (in a directed fashion) (InHacks<sub>i j,t</sub>) as a function of whether the target had their status lowered (LowerStatus<sub>j,t</sub>), the hacker had their status lowered (LowerStatus<sub>i,t</sub>), and the interaction between the two. We also included the relative status gap between the hacked individual and the hacker before the policy change (StatusGap<sub>j,i</sub>). The gap between a contestant’s and hacker’s status is based on the number of colors between their respective status categories before the policy change (Fig. 1a). Our results appear in Table 7. We observe that a lower status target is associated with a higher hacking propensity $( \beta = 0 . 6 6 6 ,$ $p < 0 . 1 )$ . However, we did not find any significant effect of lowered status on the part of the evaluator, on average. Thus, while arbitrarily acquired lower status leads a contestant to receive more hacks, it does not significantly change the contestant’s propensity to hack others. This result further confirms that personal, competitive benefits are not a primary driver of evaluations.

We also find that the status gap (i.e., the extent to which the target contestant is placed above the hacker in the pre-shock status hierarchy) reduces the instances of hacking (β = -0.289, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This again indicates that a contestant receives fewer evaluations from others who were below them in the status hierarchy before the policy change. In sum, we did not find evidence that the assignment to lower status affects contestants’ outbound evaluation decisions; rather, it seems only that the reassignment of others affects where the body of contestants directs its evaluation attention. Once again, our results are collectively consistent with the idea that the evaluation mechanisms are predominantly leveraged with the collective, community benefits in mind.

## Alternative Explanations

## Accounting for Shifts in Contestants’ Submission Timing or Quality

Contestants who experienced the status change event may have been driven to put in more effort and may thus have begun to submit their solutions systematically earlier in the contest window. Had this taken place, it would imply that contestants would have more opportunity to consider their submissions and to attempt to hack them. At the same time, their suddenly acquired lower status may have demotivated affected contestants, causing them to invest less effort in their submissions, resulting in lower quality. If either of these mechanisms is at play, it would confound the perception of quality from the status as the explanation for the observed change in the evaluation activity. Accordingly, we seek to rule both explanations out with our next analyses. To account for both possibilities, we modified Equation (1) to include the relative time (minutes) to the earliest contest submission by contestant i for contest t (EarliestContestSub<sub>i,t</sub>) and the logged compilation time consumed by the earliest submission in milliseconds (Log(TimeConsumed)). The higher the time consumed, the poorer the solution quality. The results of the resultant models are presented in Table 8.

We find in Model 1 that losing status still has a positive effect on the number of subsequent incoming hacks $( \beta = 0 . 7 6 5 , p <$ 0.01). The effect of cyan, notably, becomes insignificant. We also observe, as expected, that later contest submissions are less likely to be hacked $( \beta = - 0 . 0 1 3 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ , presumably, because there is less opportunity. In Model 2, the number of successful hacks was again found to increase for contestants assigned to lower status $( \beta = 0 . 8 6 5 , p \mathrm { - v a l } < 0 . 0 5 )$ .

Although the compilation time of the first submission a contestant makes is a reasonable proxy for quality, it does not wholly capture the net quality of submissions a contestant makes to contests. To further assess the role of possible shifts in contestant effort and submission quality,<sup>15</sup> we explored replacing the outcome variable in Equation (1) with the total points earned by contestant ?? in contest ??, as well as the number of total accepted submissions (i.e., the ones that passed the minimum per contest-problem level tests of memory and time performance).

The results appear in Table 9, in Models 1 and 2, where we observe that, upon losing status, contestants both earned more points per contest $( \beta = 4 6 4 . 3 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and experienced a higher number of accepted submissions $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ These two findings imply that, if anything, affected contestants began to generate higher quality solutions that were more likely to pass minimum compilation time and memory requirements (and thus be eligible to be hacked as well). Further, their submissions began to garner more points.

As a final analysis, we consider another proxy for contestant effort in response to the policy change event. In Appendix C, we present an analysis of the relationship between contestants assignment to lower status and their propensity to submit to contests. There, we again demonstrate a positive relationship, which suggests that, if anything, affected contestants increased their effort in contest participation. Overall, our results indicate that effort and quality declines cannot explain our main findings.

## Accounting for the Role of Division Change

Although status on Codeforces comes primarily from the color-coded status categories, it may also derive to some extent from membership in divisions. Note that contestants on Codeforces reside in one of two brackets, based on their rating, termed Division 1 and Division 2. Up to this point, all analyses presented have considered the average effect of lower status across both divisions. However, since the change to the status hierarchy also moved the threshold defining divisions, our results may be driven to some extent by contestants who were reassigned downward, from Division 1 to Division 2, because of the change. A change in a contestant’s division would be especially prominent given the fact that the contests are often division-specific, so a divisional change can drive a contestant to face an entirely new pool of rivals and an entirely different level of difficulty in their contest problems.

To ensure that such division changes did not drive our results, we first repeated our initial estimation, excluding all contestants who changed divisions. Then, we modified Equation (1) to separately model the effect of losing one’s division, above and beyond the impact of lower status in terms of color code changes. These two sets of results appear as Models 1-4, 5, and 6, in Table 10. We find that the effects of arbitrarily acquired lower status persist, even for those who did not lose their division. This result is valid for the number of incoming hacks (β = 0.798, p < 0.01, Model 2) as well as the number of successful hacks (β = 0.995, p < 0.01, Model 4). In Models 5 and 6, we find no additional impact of losing division on the number of incoming hacks.

## Discussion

## Implications

We have reported qualitative, interview-based evidence, as well as quantitative evidence from an event study, wherein we examined the effects of status on evaluation activity in online contests. We provide comprehensive evidence that a contestant’s status indeed influences the evaluations they receive from others, such that a lower status causally attracts more evaluations. Further, this rise in evaluations appears to be less effective and thus may be undesirable for the platform operator, given that we observe a significant decline in the detection of errors in the evaluated contestant’s submissions. These findings have several important implications.

First, our study contributes directly to the literature on evaluations (see Appendix B). Whereas related prior work in online settings has examined the importance of contestants status as a predictor of their participation in contests that employ evaluation (Chen et al., 2020), we show that status has a much more direct connection with the use of evaluation, such that a focal contestant’s status drives their scrutiny and evaluations. Moreover, such evaluations lead to a significant decrease in spotting defective submissions, suggesting that status perceptions induced by a salient IT artifact (a contestant status hierarchy on Codeforces) can influence discretionary evaluation decisions between contestants, setting the stage for potentially less effective effort allocations.

Second, our work addresses the inherent ambiguity in evaluations, which offer a collective benefit of a more accurate assessment, as well as a competitive benefit from influencing contest outcomes. The positive association between low status and evaluation indicates that contestants’ primary motivation for evaluations is a desire for collective benefit (i.e., hacking solutions of lower-status contestants, who may be viewed as providers of subpar solutions) rather than competitive benefit (i.e., targeting high-status contestants to hurt their chances of winning). Of course, it is likely that this result depends in part on the relative strength of the collective and competitive benefits at play in any given setting. Our finding that lowerstatus contestants attract more evaluations also contributes to the broader tension between status and evaluation (D’Arcy et al., 2020; Sharkey, 2014). Specifically, we suggest that the nature of the evaluation mechanism (i.e., whether it offers a collective benefit) could be an important contingency driving the effect of status on evaluation.

Lastly, our study implies two important practical considerations for platform operators. Given that status can result in less effective evaluations, platform owners should consider methods of mitigating status effects in evaluation decisions. One option to consider is to conceal status-related information when contributions are presented for possible evaluation. This approach is a somewhat effective means of mitigating the effect of users’ status evaluation contexts, such as online investment, i.e., crowdfunding (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). In our context, this would amount to concealing the status category of the contestants and only presenting their code for possible evaluation.

Alternatively, platform operators might consider instituting various interventions that reduce users’ reliance on status (Heilman, 1984; Heilman et al., 1988). Such interventions are generally predicated on the assumption that stereotypes play a role only in the absence of evidence to the contrary. Accordingly, a platform operator might seek to mitigate the implications of status on evaluations by providing evidence or examples that run counter to the stereotypical associations they wish to overcome. For example, in our context, contestants may potentially be provided with an example of a lower-status coder who produced particularly high-quality submissions. Conversely, an example might be highlighted of a high-status contestant whose recent submission was hacked. This must be done keeping in mind potential reactance by contestants if they feel singled out.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

Of course, our work has some limitations, over and above the questions of generalizability. First and foremost, our research design is an event study. Although we provide some arguments and statistical tests that speak to the validity of causal inferences and the presence (or lack thereof) of confounders, future work might seek to assess these relationships with alternative data or designs, e.g., through experimentation or regression discontinuity. Second, additional research is needed to evaluate the effect of higher status, achieved through an arbitrary change, on evaluations. In the current study, the status coefficient for the cyan color is hard to interpret because the “higher status” through arbitrary assignment to the newly created cyan color was limited to the single category, which lacks clear social meaning as a status marker (given status markers take time to acquire social meaning). As a result, our estimates associated with “gaining” the cyan color could be driven by the novelty of the category rather than any status aspect. Alternative restructurings of the contestant hierarchy could, in theory, yield arbitrarily assigned, higher-status markers with clear status interpretations. For example, if the number of status categories or the lower bound on existing categories is reduced, some contestants would find themselves exogenously granted higher status with a meaningful marker. As of now, we know very little about the response to such changes, which are equally likely to occur.
