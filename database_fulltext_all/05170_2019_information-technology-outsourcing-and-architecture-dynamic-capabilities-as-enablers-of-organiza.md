# Information technology outsourcing and architecture dynamic capabilities as enablers of organizational agility

Forough Karimi-Alaghehband and Suzanne Rivard

Journal of Information Technology 1–31 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2019 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621881627DOI: 10.1177/0268396218816271 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

Grounded in the dynamic capabilities perspective, our study addresses the question of how information technology outsourcing capabilities can interact with other IT strategic capabilities to enable organizational agility through the ongoing reconfiguration of IT solutions. To answer our question, we built on the notion of microfoundations that undergird the high-level dynamic capabilities of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring. Adopting a theory elaboration approach, we studied the case of a firm evolving in a turbulent environment, which had outsourced the quasi-totality of its IT services and had a mature IT architecture. From the case data, we specify two types of microfoundations: repeatability-related microfoundations (i.e. processes) and ability-related microfoundations (i.e. IT department structure, skills, simple rules, and communications) that undergird either information technology outsourcing dynamic capabilities or IT architecture dynamic capabilities. We propose a model that outlines how the interaction between repeatabilityrelated microfoundations, supported by ability-related microfoundations, enables the reconfiguration of IT solutions. Our study also elucidates how a firm can follow a logic of opportunity enabled by their IT outsourcing and IT architecture dynamic capabilities.

## Keywords

Dynamic capabilities perspective, IT outsourcing dynamic capabilities, IT architecture dynamic capabilities, microfoundations, case study, theory elaboration

## Introduction

The strategic nature of information technology outsourcing (ITO) is widely acknowledged in the literature. First and foremost, as it pertains to the boundary of the firm, the decision of whether to outsource IT services and assets is deemed eminently strategic (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; Valorinta, 2011). Because ITO allows firms to concentrate on core competencies and to rely on external partners for activities for which they do not have the required capabilities, it has been portrayed as a means of leveraging organizational skills and resources (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994). Furthermore, ITO can help firms reach their strategic goals, depending on the strategic motivation underlying an outsourcing initiative—whether improvement of IS services, productivity impacts (Chang & Gurbaxani, 2012), innovation (Oshri, Arkhipova, & Vaia, 2018; Oshri, Kotlarsky, & Gerbasi, 2015), or scalability (Lacity, Khan, & Yan, 2016).

Our study pertains to an important aspect of the strategic role of ITO, that of enabler of organizational agility through the ongoing reconfiguration of IT solutions. Indeed, the environment of many firms is hyperturbulent, with highly competitive markets, shifting customer demands, regulatory changes, and technological innovations (El Sawy, Malhotra, Park, & Pavlou, 2010; Nan & Tanriverdi, 2017; Teece, Peteraf, & Leih, 2016). This uncertain environment calls for agility—that is, “the capacity of an organization to efficiently and effectively redeploy/redirect its resources to value creating and value protecting (and capturing)

higher-yield activities as internal and external circumstances warrant” (Teece et al., 2016, p. 17)—if firms are to gain and sustain competitive advantage.

Researchers have suggested that ITO can directly contribute to a firm’s ability to reach strategic agility, for instance, by allowing the adjustment of volumes in response to demand variations or by providing business continuity in times of crisis (Willcocks, 2010, p. 65), thus freeing firms from the effects of large fixed investments by preserving flexibility (Teece et al., 2016). Researchers have also illustrated how, in the long run, ITO capabilities can interact with IT architecture capabilities to support and facilitate the transition of a firm’s enterprise architecture across maturity levels (Ross & Beath, 2006).

Notwithstanding this recognition, it is still unclear how ITO capabilities can, on an ongoing basis, contribute to IT becoming a “platform for agility,” integrated in key processes to nurture innovation, with digitalized platforms and specialized knowledge deployed to adapt quickly to changing environments or penetrate market niches (Sambamurthy, Bharadwaj, & Grover, 2003, p. 243). Indeed, although extant research shows that ITO can enhance a firm’s ability to create new capabilities in response to radical environmental changes (e.g. Sia, Koh, & Tan, 2008) by taking a variance approach, it focuses on ITO’s effect itself, not on how this effect occurs. Our study contributes to enriching current knowledge by examining the ongoing interaction between strategic-level ITO and other capabilities of the IT strategic set. More precisely, our study addresses the question: How can ITO capabilities interact with other IT strategic capabilities to contribute to the continuous reconfiguration of IT solutions in view of supporting the firm’s strategic objectives?

Because our research question pertains to the interaction of ITO and other capabilities for the continuous reconfiguration of IT solutions, we theoretically anchored our work in the dynamic capabilities perspective (DCP) (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997). Based on this perspective, ITO dynamic capabilities refer to a firm’s ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure its IT solutions through outsourcing arrangements. We focused on the microfoundations of dynamic capabilities, that is, the processes, skills, structures, and rules that undergird high-level sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities (Teece, 2007). Building on Helfat et al.’s (2007) conceptualization, we distinguished between microfoundations that relate to the repeatability dimension of dynamic capabilities (i.e. processes) and those that relate to their ability dimension (e.g. skills and structure). Using this theoretical foundation as a basis for our research, we followed a theory elaboration approach (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017) and engaged in specifying the microfoundations and their relationships in the context of ITO to develop new theoretical insights. To specify the microfoundations, we adopted an inductive approach (Eisenhardt, 1989) and analyzed a case of extensive ITO. In addition to microfoundations associated with ITO capabilities, our data analysis revealed microfoundations associated with another important type of IT capability, IT architecture capabilities, referring to the ability to “create a mutually reinforcing pattern of evolving, tightly aligned business strategy and IT capabilities” (Ross, 2003, p. 32). Based on our analysis of the case data, we developed a model that outlines how the interaction between ITO and IT architecture repeatability-related microfoundations (i.e. pro cesses), as supported by ability-related microfoundations (i.e. IT department structure, skills, simple rules, and communications), enables the ongoing reconfiguration of IT solutions, thus furthering organizational agility.

## Dynamic capabilities and their microfoundations

The notion of dynamic capability initially appeared in the work of Teece et al. (1997) and is defined as “the firm’s ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competencies to address rapidly changing environments” (p. 516). In line with this, Helfat et al. (2007) define a dynamic capability as “the capacity of an organization to purposefully extend, create, or modify [i.e. reconfigure] its resource base” (p. 1). It has been suggested that the value of dynamic capabilities for competitive advantage lies not in the capabilities themselves but in the resource configurations they create (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000).

The literature suggests three high-level dynamic capabilities: sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring (Martin, 2011; Teece, 2007; Teece et al., 2016). Sensing refers to the ability to scan the environment to spot and/or shape opportunities and/or threats. Seizing is conceptualized as the ability to address those opportunities/threats. Reconfiguring includes enhancing, combining, and protecting tangible and intangible assets as well as rearranging them in view of maintaining competitiveness.

In this context, authors have emphasized the role of microfoundations of dynamic capabilities. For instance, Eisenhardt, Furr, and Bingham (2010) conceptualize microfoundations as “the underlying individual-level and grouplevel actions that shape strategy, organization, and, more broadly, dynamic capabilities” (p. 1263). Teece (2007) offers a more encompassing definition, which includes “the distinct skills, processes, procedures, organizational structures, decision rules, and disciplines which undergird enter prise-level sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capacities” (Teece, 2007, p. 1319). We further specify the concept of microfoundations, illustrated in Figure 1, by suggesting that those microfoundations belong to either one of the two dimensions of dynamic capabilities put forth by Helfat et al. (2007). The first dimension is that of ability, which refers to “the ability to perform a task in at least a minimally acceptable manner” (p. 6) and corresponds to skills, rules, structures, and disciplines microfoundations. For instance, researchers have studied the role of microfoundations such as structure—for example, a central R&D department (Schneckenberg, Truong, & Mazloomi, 2015), in-house R&D (Li-Ying, Wang, & Ning, 2016), or a dedicated function (Bianchi, Chiesa, & Frattini, 2009)—and the role of skills and individuals (Felin, Foss, Heimeriks, & Madsen, 2012)—for example, cognitive abilities (Helfat & Peteraf, 2015; Hodgkinson & Healey, 2011), people’s attitudes and competencies (Bianchi et al., 2009), or perceptions and behavior (Fallon-Byrne & Harney, 2017; Strauss, Lepoutre, & Wood, 2017). The second dimension is repeatability and refers to whether “the function that a capability performs is repeatable and can be reliably executed to some extent” (p. 6); it pertains to processes, procedures, and routines (Bianchi et al., 2009; Dixon, Meyer, & Day, 2014; Kindström, Kowalkowski, & Sandberg, 2013; Mahringer & Renzl, 2018; Salvato, 2009; Schneckenberg et al., 2015).<sup>1</sup> As Figure 1 depicts, Teece (2007) conceptualizes microfoundations as supporting the three high-level dynamic capabilities of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring.

## Research method

Theory elaboration aims at extending existing concepts, and sometimes a preliminary conceptual model, by providing new theoretical insights through the execution of empirical research (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017). We adopted an inductive research design for the empirical portion of our work, and we conducted an in-depth case study (Yin, 2003). Before entering the field, we specified our main existing concepts (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017) by identifying three high-level themes related to dynamic capabilities— that is, sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring. We also established a list of firm-level microfoundations (Eisenhardt et al., 2010; Teece, 2007) with a priori concepts of structure, skills, communications, and rules (Teece, 2007) as ability-related microfoundations and processes as repeatability-related microfoundations. Furthermore, the conceptual framework of dynamic capabilities and their ability-related and repeatability-related microfoundations based on Teece (2007)—Figure 1—served as our preliminary conceptual model.

Our selected case had to meet three selection criteria. First, given our focus on dynamic capabilities, the firm had to operate in a turbulent environment and a highly competitive market. Second, given our focus on ITO-related dynamics capabilities, the firm had to have extensive ITO experience. Third, we sought a firm with mature IT architecture, standard technology and platforms, shared databases, and with identified, integrated, and optimized core processes (Ross, 2003). We set the third criterion because it is more likely that a firm with mature enterprise IT architecture would possess IT-related dynamic capabilities (Ross, 2003). In addition to ensuring that our terrain would allow us to explore dynamic capabilities, the three criteria led us to selecting a case that we deemed representative of large firms evolving in a turbulent, global environment and having to constantly reconfigure their IT.

Our chosen site, Air Canada—the main Canadian airline—met these criteria. First, the firm operates in a turbulent environment, with dramatic changes in fuel price, unpredictable natural disasters, epidemic and/or terrorism threats, and wars. Environmental turbulence is amplified by the highly competitive nature of the market, domestic or international. Second, Air Canada has had extensive experience with ITO, having signed its first contract in 1994, and with the majority of its IT activities being outsourced to multiple suppliers at the time of data collection. Third, the firm had multiple IT-enabled processes, had established corporate IT standards and policies, and had maintained a solid IT infrastructure upon which applications were built.

## Data collection

Our data collection was guided by dynamic capabilities theory and relied on multiple data sources: interviews, internal and public documents, and observation. Data collection started in August 2010 and continued until November 2011. To identify and gain access to key informants, we contacted the CIO who referred us to the senior director of IT sourcing as our main interlocutor, and who would oversee our access to key informants. During a first interview, this director (Senior Director IT Sourcing) provided general information about Air Canada, how IT supported the business, and the current structure of the IT department. In addition to attending an in-site observation session, we conducted interviews with 11 informants in 12 interview sessions (we had more than one interview with one of the informants). In addition to the senior IT directors, we interviewed managers in charge of the main ITO contracts, and a senior executive from one of Air Canada’s service suppliers.

Following a general interview guide (Appendix A), we customized several questions according to the responsibilities of each informant (Appendix B) while remaining open to emergent questions and concepts and adjusting our data collection instruments accordingly (Eisenhardt, 1989). Taking a retrospective approach (Taris, 2000), we invited our informants to narrate episodes of the reconfiguration of IT solutions. Most episodes had occurred during the 18-month period before the interviews took place. Each interview lasted between 1 and 1.5 h. We recorded all interviews except one and had them transcribed. This yielded transcripts with about 80,000 words. We completed the interview data with annual reports and industry magazines, using a total of more than 300 pages of public documents (Appendix C). We also consulted internal documents (e.g. one ITO contract) on site. Furthermore, one of the authors attended a meeting of the senior IT directors. Although this session was not recorded, the observation notes helped refine the interview guide and provided additional insights that were used during data analysis. To keep all data in a reusable format, we created a case study database.

## Data analysis

The first step of data analysis was triangulation, which involved comparing key data elements acquired from our different sources. As an initial coding scheme, we then coded the interview transcripts using our high-level themes of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring and our a priori concepts of structure, skills, communications, and rules (Teece, 2007) as ability-related microfoundations and processes as repeatability-related microfoundations. Following Eisenhardt (1989), these themes and concepts were high level in the sense that they were not precisely defined, and their definition was susceptible to change. Reading and analyzing the transcripts, we looked for instances of each concept. These instances later became “emergent codes” that were used to code the rest of the transcripts. For example, “Group of Seven” emerged as an instance of the a priori concept of communications and “technology watch” as an instance of the sensing theme. The codes were then aggregated to form new concepts—for example, environment scanning—that were closer to the substantive nature of our case. Appendix D lists the emergent codes, and the new concepts and their definitions along with their illustrative quotes.

We continued our data analysis using two strategies for sense-making (Langley, 1999). We used a bracketing strategy, decomposing the data into several episodes of reconfiguration of IT solutions. For each episode, we documented opportunities/threats that initiated the episode, the processes that were involved in the episode, and the ability related microfoundations that played a salient role in supporting the processes. We considered each episode a separate unit of analysis, a case (Langley, 1999), and conducted within-episode and cross-episode analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989), comparing the elements from one episode to those of the other episodes. The second sense-mak ing strategy of our theory elaboration effort was visual mapping, which consisted in enriching the initial conceptual model presented in Figure 1 with the case data. We applied these sense making strategies iteratively, in parallel with engaging with the literature, hence ensuring that insights from new literature informed our data analysis and helped shape our theoretical explanation.

## Findings

Founded in 1937, Air Canada is Canada’s largest airline, serving over 32 million customers annually. Canada’s national airline since its creation, Air Canada was privatized in 1988. In 2011, it was offering more than 170 destinations and was the world’s 15th largest commercial airline. Air Canada was a founding member of Star Alliance, a strategic partnership that included 27 partners, making it the world’s most inclusive air transportation network.

Air Canada’s mission was “connecting Canada and the world.”<sup>2</sup> To accomplish this, and because the country’s ethnic diversity contributed to a high and growing demand for international travel, the firm pursued an international growth strategy. In 2010, it entered major partnerships with Lufthansa and United/Continental, which significantly supported its growth strategy and connecting mission. Competitors were strong and growing airlines—domestic (e.g. WestJet), United States (e.g. Delta), or international (e.g. Air France-KLM). In pursuit of its vision to build loyalty through passion and innovation,<sup>3</sup> Air Canada followed a differentiation strategy that involved “engaging with customers with a focus on premium passengers and premium products.”<sup>4</sup> Cost reduction, however, remained critical. Indeed, since the firm did not have control of its main cost, that of fuel, reducing costs elsewhere in the business was crucial. To this end, the Cost Transformation Program (CTP) was launched in 2010 with the objective of allowing Air Canada “to compete more effectively on multiple levels against the low-pricing structures offered by low-cost carriers.”<sup>5</sup> CTP focused on three main areas: operational process improvement, supplier contract renegotiations, and revenue productivity gains.<sup>6</sup>

## IT at Air Canada

Among the first airlines to outsource its IT services, Air Canada signed its first contract with IBM in 1994. Since then, Air Canada has entered into new contracts and modified its IT sourcing strategy; the majority of its IT activities were outsourced to multiple service suppliers at the end of 2011.

Air Canada pursued a multi-supplier strategy in order to have access to the most specialized and best airline products on the market, quickly and less expensively. In 2003, Air Canada signed an agreement with Operation SYS, a European service supplier offering several applications that suited its needs. Having multiple service suppliers brought unique challenges. Therefore, Air Canada mandated IBM to act as an integrator, responsible for integrating new applications (provided by existing or new service suppliers) with existing applications. Learning from years of ITO experience and operating in a multi-sourced environment, Air Canada followed the policies of not granting exclusivity to any single service supplier as well as partnering only with service suppliers that were familiar with a 24/7 type of business.

In early 2010, the structure of the IT department was modified to be hybrid. As shown on the organizational chart of the IT department (Appendix E), the hybrid structure entailed four IT units as well as three hybrid units. The three hybrid units mirrored the three main business branches: Customer Service, Commercial, and Operations. For instance, Customer Service (business branch) would have a hybrid unit, IT Customer Service, with a single representative, the senior director of IT Customer Service who would supervise the work of several business analysts. The business analysts and their senior director resided in their respective business branch and reported to both the CIO and their branch’s vice-president. The hybrid structure was perceived by all IT senior managers as the mechanism that allowed for smooth communication among IT departments and business branches that supported timely coordination activities with minimum conflict. In addition to the hybrid structure in place, constant meetings among the seven IT and hybrid senior managers—monthly formal meetings of what they called the Group of Seven, and impromptu informal meetings— played a crucial role in facilitating communications among managers. Furthermore, both the IT units and the hybrid units would hire individuals who not only were innovative and IT savvy but also spoke the language of the business. These individuals would ensure bridging the two sides—business and IT—and facilitating communications and coordination activities.

## Emergent microfoundations

As synthesized in Table 1, our analysis led us to identify 11 processes—the repeatability-related microfoundations— that were either executed on an ongoing basis or set in motion by different events (i.e. opportunities/threats) to bring about the reconfiguration of IT solutions.

Some of the processes listed in Table 1 are directly related to outsourcing (e.g. external scanning of IT suppliers and orchestration of IT suppliers). These processes target either current/potential suppliers or ITO contracts/ arrangements. In contrast, other processes aim at changing, modifying, or reconfiguring extant IT solutions/services. For example, “external scanning of IT opportunities” examines the possibility of adopting new technologies. These processes aim at modifying the IT landscape (e.g. IT solutions, services, and offerings), that is, reconfiguring IT solutions. These processes are closely related to the concept of architecture competency, introduced by Ross (2003) as the ability to “create a mutually reinforcing pattern of evolving, tightly aligned business strategy and IT capabilities” (p. 32). For this reason, we labeled them “IT architecture dynamic capabilities.”

Some of these processes emerged through using a priori codes and emergent codes to analyze the data. For example, “search and selection” was used as an a priori code and led to the emergence of the codes “technology watch” and “sources of inspiration.” These emergent codes then were used to analyze the data further and were abstracted into the process of “external scanning of IT opportunities.” Other processes emerged through using only emergent codes. For example, analyzing the first few interviews, the codes “coordinated needs” and “holistic view of requirements” emerged and led to abstracting all their instances into the process of “consolidation of requirements.”

Emergent processes were further related to the highlevel themes of sensing capabilities, seizing capabilities, and reconfiguring capabilities. Figure 2, which results from a visual mapping of our data, shows the relationships among these levels. For example, “external scanning of IT opportunities” enables sensing dynamic capabilities because it implies looking into the market—without immediate intention to select—for new technologies, technology trends, and technologies that can be potentially adopted from other industries. Therefore, it is an instance of “IT architecture sensing” capabilities.

It is worth noting, as suggested by the items referred to from Table 1, that the processes pertaining to sensing capabilities—that is, environment scanning, internal scanning of ITO arrangements, external scanning of ITO opportunities, external scanning of IT suppliers, and internal scanning of IT solutions—were taking place on an ongoing basis and were almost generic in nature rather than being initiated by a trigger or pertaining to a specific opportunity or threat. The other processes pertaining to seizing and reconfiguring capabilities were executed every time a specific opportunity or threat was filtered through the sensing processes. As the examples below illustrate, however, some of the processes were more salient than others, depending on the nature of the opportunity or threat that triggered them.

## IT solution reconfiguration triggered by events

The 2010s were marked by numerous events, some of which were considered opportunities, others threats. The nature of these events varied but included technological innovations, such as the arrival of the iPad; regulatory changes, such as government requests for advance passenger lists; environmental issues, such as the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull (Icelandic) volcano eruption; or new service offerings, such as allowing customers to upgrade their seat selection. These events initiated the reconfiguration of IT solutions that sometimes resulted in minor adjustments and at other times resulted in a major reconfiguration. In 2010, the arrival of the iPhone and the iPad, the approaching end of a contract, the arrival of a new contract, and internal revenue generation initiatives were perceived as opportunities. On the contrary, fluctuating fuel prices were perceived as a threat.

While in each example provided below the salient microfoundations are shown within brackets, it should be noted that many of the other microfoundations not mentioned in the example are present on an ongoing basis, providing constant support and facilitation for the salient processes. For example, in “Technological innovations: iPhone and iPad,” the salient ability-related microfoundation is “skills.” However, at the same time, “structure” is playing an ongoing role to facilitate “communications” among business units and IT to crystalize their requirements from these new technologies. In another example, “Arrival of the new aircraft,” the salient microfoundation is “assessment of opportunities and threats,” while “environment scanning” is still in play to provide insight into how other players in the market are adopting the new aircraft.

## A model of the interaction between microfoundations of ITO and IT architecture dynamic capabilities

We propose a model that advances our understanding of how ITO capabilities can interact with IT architecture capabilities to provide, on an ongoing basis, IT solutions that contribute to the firm’s strategic objectives. The model portrays how the interaction between repeatability-related microfoundations (i.e. processes), supported by abilityrelated microfoundations (i.e. IT department structure, skills, simple rules, and communications), results in the reconfiguration of IT solutions.

## Assumptions

Anchored in the DCP, our model is based on two key conceptual assumptions of this theory. First, our model assumes that dynamic capabilities may exhibit commonalities across firms; that is, they “have greater equifinality, homogeneity, and substitutability across firms than traditional RBV [resource-based view] thinking implies” (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000, p. 1106). This suggests that dynamic capabilities are “fungible” and thus, in their abstract and general form, could be generalized to other firms in the same industry (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). Thus, although our model was developed based on the experience of one firm, it could be used to explain other similar settings. The second conceptual assumption is that the difference in firm performance or competitiveness does not lie in the capabilities themselves but results from the configuration (new or enhanced) of resources these capabilities create (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). In the context of our study, this translates into the assumption that the attainment of strategic objectives does not result from high-level dynamic capabilities themselves or from their microfoundations but from the configurations of the IT solutions they help create.

Two contextual assumptions shape the boundary of our model. Given that dynamic capabilities are assumed to be relevant and potential sources of competitiveness in environments with medium to high velocity (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000), our model pertains to firms that evolve in turbulent environments. This first assumption is based on the argument that firms operating in stable environments could gain and sustain competitive advantage through the efficiency of their routines and the uniqueness of their resources rather than through changes to their resources (Zollo & Winter, 2002). The second contextual assumption sets the boundary of the explanatory power of our model to firms that have mature IT architecture, are experienced in ITO, and deal with several IT suppliers.

## The model

The model, illustrated in Figure 3, portrays the ongoing reconfiguration of IT solutions as a succession of iterative ITO and IT architecture processes—repeatability-related microfoundations—facilitated by ability-related microfoundations: IT department structure, skills, communications, and simple rules.

Sensing. As shown in the figure, five scanning processes constitute the repeatability-related microfoundations of the sensing dynamic capability. These scanning processes aim at identifying promising opportunities and/or important threats and take place on a continuous basis. They are environment scanning, external scanning of IT opportunities, internal scanning of IT solutions, external scanning of IT suppliers, and internal scanning of ITO arrangements. These processes are supported by several ability-related microfoundations, for instance, the hybrid structure of the IT department, the formal and informal communications among IT directors, and the skills (e.g. an innovative frame of mind and IT savviness) that are sought when appointing or hiring managers and employees. The hybrid structure of the IT department supports these scanning processes through the hybrid units and therefore provides the mechanisms to monitor how the solutions and arrangements in place support the respective business branch, hence supporting internal scanning of IT solutions and internal scanning of ITO arrangements. Skills such as an innovative frame of mind and the IT savviness of directors and employees not only support the internal scanning processes by being critical of the systems in place but also support external scanning of IT opportunities, external scanning of IT suppliers, and environment scanning by being vigilant for new technologies and suppliers in the market, general industry trends, and environmental events.

Other skills, such as the monitoring and analyzing capacities of both directors and employees, support the processes that aim at revealing customer demands and preferences through being attentive to the general public as well as scanning and analyzing the feedback provided by customers on Air Canada’s website. Communication mechanisms— formal weekly and bi-weekly meetings and informal communications of the Group of Seven—enable directors to be aware of ideas and discoveries and whether or not they are all on the same page regarding what is happening in both the external environment and the internal domain.

Seizing. Once opportunities and threats have been sensed and filtered, the seizing capability comes into play, supported by four processes. The first process is the assessment of opportunities, which starts by determining the consequences of addressing or not addressing these opportunities and/or threats. If the decision is to take action, assessment then involves determining the implications for the business and for the existing configuration of IT solutions (e.g. Are modifications/enhancements to current solutions needed? Are new solutions/services needed? Is a combination of both needed?). This is followed by the consolidation of requirements process, which provides a coordinated and global view of business requirements from IT.

The execution of these two processes is supported by ability-related microfoundations, mainly the hybrid structure of the IT department, ongoing communications among the members of the Group of Seven, and their skills, for which a collaborative frame of mind and the ability to understand the language of both business and IT are essential. For instance, the presence of an IT representative in each of the three main business branches and the existence of a communication mechanism (i.e. the Group of Seven) facilitates the assessment—at both the corporate and unit levels—of opportunities/threats from different origins (e.g. a business unit, the external environment, or the corporate level). Similarly, during consolidation of requirements, those from the unit/department level are scaled up and are coordinated at the branch and corporate levels.

As shown in Figure 3, these two processes are iterative: an opportunity or threat first initiates a preliminary assessment, which is followed by a preliminary consolidation of requirements. The preliminary assessment occurs both in the business units/departments (e.g. realizing that a change in business processes is needed and the IT solutions supporting those processes need to be modified accordingly) and in the IT department (e.g. which IT solutions require amendment). This preliminary assessment is followed by a preliminary consolidation of requirements wherein requirements are identified and prioritized within each branch and scaled up to the IT department level and discussed for further assessment and evaluation. The result of this assessment is communicated back to the business units/departments for a new iteration of assessment of opportunities/threats and consolidation of requirements. This process continues until IT and business agree on a set of defined and coordinated requirements.

Two interwoven processes follow: search for and selection of IT solutions and search for and selection of IT suppliers.<sup>7</sup> Two typical situations occur: (1) the requirements call for an enhancement/modification to an existing IT solution and (2) the requirements call for acquiring a new IT solution. In the first situation, the search for and selection of IT suppliers is unnecessary. The current supplier of the IT solution to be modified is thus invited to participate in the search for and selection of IT solutions. In the second situation, all current IT suppliers are invited to an interactive session during which both the search for and selection of IT solutions and search for and selection of IT suppliers take place, as suppliers present potential solutions. If offers are deemed acceptable, both search and selection processes stop. These processes depend on intense communications between senior IT directors and service suppliers’ representatives. Among the skills that come into play, a collaborative frame of mind is crucial.

If no offer is satisfactory, new iterations of each process start. In terms of the search for and selection of IT solutions, this implies an in-house design of a more precise solution. In terms of the search for and selection of IT suppliers, an RFP is prepared and tendered to the market, offers are evaluated, and suppliers are selected. Here, simple rules are important microfoundations. One such rule is to send RFPs only to tier-one suppliers that are familiar with the 24/7 operations of a large business. Another rule is that no supplier is guaranteed exclusivity.

Reconfiguring. Once IT solutions and their service suppliers are selected, orchestration of IT suppliers and IT solutions takes place that supports the reconfiguring capability. No matter whether the current solutions are modified or new solutions are offered, the right pieces of work need to be assigned to the right service supplier and interdependencies among systems and applications need to be considered and verified. Indeed, due to the complexities of many current IT solutions, even slight changes in these solutions call for the orchestration of service suppliers and coordination of their work in order to achieve a smooth, seamless, and end-to-end solution. Here again, the hybrid IT department structure supports these processes occurring at the corporate IT level, in a centralized manner, with the main responsibilities belonging to Transformation IT, Innovation and Customer Solutions, and IT Operations units in collaboration with the IT Sourcing unit and the service supplier in charge of the integration of IT solutions. Orchestration processes also heavily depend on the skills of the directors in the Transformation and Sourcing units, such as their coordination and relationship management skills. Coordination skills enable managers to assign the right pieces of work to the right service suppliers and cascade the right SLAs across the board so that a seamless solution can be achieved. Relationship management skills (e.g. leadership) in the IT department as well as formal and informal communications with service suppliers, the CIO, and the business enable achieving agreement among different parties, which is paramount to achieving an orchestrated effort.

## Discussion

We propose a model of interactions among the microfoundations of ITO and IT architecture dynamic capabilities as enablers of the ongoing reconfiguration of a firm’s IT solutions. Our theorizing yielded an assemblage of microfoundations that interact in an intricate and interwoven way.

## An intricate assemblage of microfoundations

Although our model depicts an ordered succession of processes and their supporting structures, skills, communications and rules, in practice these microfoundations are interwoven, intricate, and iterative. The line between the end of a process and the beginning of the next, or where a specific skill plays its most important role, is blurry at best. According to Teece (2007), this should be expected since “the identification of the microfoundations of dynamic capabilities must be necessarily incomplete, inchoate, and somewhat opaque and/or their implementation must be rather difficult. Otherwise sustainable competitive advantage would erode with the effective communication and application of dynamic capability concepts” (p. 1321).

For instance, based on the above data analysis, we posit that although the five processes in support of the sensing capability aim at identifying promising opportunities and/ or important threats for the firm, their role is also to feed, facilitate, and quicken the other processes that follow. External scanning of IT opportunities performs a technology watch and monitors the market continuously to learn about available IT solutions, technology trends, and tech nologies that could potentially be adopted. This process later facilitates the search for and selection of IT solutions. External scanning of IT suppliers, which identifies the main IT suppliers, facilitates and quickens RFP preparation during the search for and selection of IT suppliers. Internal scanning of IT solutions, which evaluates current infrastructure and IT solutions, facilitates the search for and selection of IT solutions and the consolidation of requirements. Finally, the internal scanning of ITO arrangements, which is an ongoing assessment of current IT suppliers and their sourcing models, later facilitates and quickens the search for and selection of IT suppliers and the orchestra tion of IT suppliers.

We suggest that the processes supporting high-level dynamic capabilities—here, sensing—are in constant motion, occurring on a continuous basis rather than at a point in time, and that they are continually and continuously supported by ability-related microfoundations such as the hybrid structure of the IT department and the innovativeness and savviness of directors and employees. However, although innovativeness and savviness support these scanning processes, the collaborative frame of mind or the awareness and understanding of business strategies and objectives, which are in support of seizing, do not cease to exist. These microfoundations are constantly there, constantly interplaying, and are in confluence with one another.

In the extant ITO literature, although several capabilities have been studied, in many instances they are operational in nature such as vendor management processes (Han, Lee, Chun, & Seo, 2013; Han, Lee, & Seo, 2008), relational processes (Mani, Barua, & Whinston, 2010), and contract facilitation routines (Ranganathan & Balaji, 2007). The extant literature also has studied general IT and managerial capabilities as skills: for example, IT management skills (Goles, 2006), business functional skills and interpersonal skills (Park, Im, & Kim, 2011), and leadership skills (Willcocks & Feeny, 2006; Willcocks & Griffiths, 2010). In such instances, although these skills could serve as microfoundations of dynamic capabilities, they are not studied as such. The same observation would apply to capabilities such as structure: for example, governance structure (level of hierarchical control, contract length) (Mani et al., 2010), and the existence of a project management office and sourcing office (Ranganathan & Balaji, 2007).

As such, our study takes into account the “holistic confluence among environmental turbulence, dynamic capabilities, and IT systems—and their fused interactions unfolding as an ecosystem” (El Sawy et al., 2010, p. 837). In our model, these elements correspond to microfounda tions that support high-level dynamic capabilities, the configuration of IT solutions, services that correspond to IT systems, and the opportunities and/or threats from the turbulent environment. Therefore, our model can be consid ered an example of “middle-range, context-sensitive (rather than universal) theories—which are appropriate for the fields of IS strategy, strategic management, and social sciences in general” (El Sawy et al., 2010, p. 838).

## Following a logic of opportunity

The literature on dynamic capabilities suggests that firms can use dynamic capabilities following two types of logic. Under the first type, that of leverage, firms use dynamic capabilities “to strengthen the position of their existing resources” (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000, p. 1106), that is, make “small incremental adjustments” (Martin, 2011, p. 122) to their resources. This logic is based on the assumption of a moderately dynamic market where, if the rarity and value of resources is likely to change, this change will occur over a period of time sufficiently long for firms to modify their portfolio of core resources (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2008). With the second type, that of opportunity, dynamic capabilities are used “to build new resource configurations” (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000, p. 1106). This logic is based on the assumption of high-velocity markets characterized by unpredictability and ambiguity (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2008). Here, dynamic capabilities are used to “integrate, reconfigure, gain and release resources” (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000, p. 1107). It has been suggested that the effective use of dynamic capabilities under the logic of opportunity requires a number of conditions (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2008) related to the microfoundations of the capabilities. Bingham and Eisenhardt (2008) posit that the underlying processes have to be semi-structured, that is, be flexible enough to allow for seizing opportunities and leaving room for improvisation and spontaneity. The processes have to be supported by simple rules that play a number of roles which enable coping with a lack of information, improving the speed of decision making and selecting opportunities. Furthermore, in terms of skills, the logic of opportunity requires that the executives responsible be “alert to changes in the market” (Bingham & Eisenhardt, 2008, p. 250). These conditions correspond to microfoundations of dynamic capabilities, some of which being repeatability-related (i.e. semi-structured processes) and others being ability-related (i.e. skills and simple rules).

In our case, the existence of simple rules for selecting IT solutions (e.g. quick and dirty return on investment (ROI)) and for selecting IT suppliers (e.g. sending RFPs to tier-one service suppliers) provided the ground for managers to effectively and quickly prioritize and address opportunities as they happened. Regarding skills, the ability of senior managers to monitor and scan the market on an ongoing basis as well as their analytical abilities to make sense of available market information and its implications for business enabled those managers to enact the logic of opportunity. In addition, the innovativeness and IT savviness of both managers and employees provided the basis for identifying promising opportunities at Air Canada.

Our study, however, shows that two more microfoundations—that is, structure and communications—are also important conditions that are needed to enable the enactment of a logic of opportunity. As illustrated here, a hybrid IT department structure provided the flexibility required for managers to execute semi-structured processes (e.g. search for and selection of IT solutions) that are necessary to address opportunities. Without the proper structure in place, execution of these recursive and intricate processes would have been impossible or very challenging. Also, when it comes to sensing and seizing opportunities, communication becomes critical for improvisation and spontaneity. In our case, the existence of different communication mechanisms such as the Group of Seven, SWOT teams, and “everybody talking to everybody” (CIO) made it possible for senior IT directors to discuss and capture opportunities on the fly.

At Air Canada, not only ability-related microfoundations provided ground for the execution of semi-structured processes that allowed managers to quickly and effectively sense and seize opportunities when they happened; almost half of all processes (five scanning processes) also supported spotting and identifying opportunities. These five scanning processes were being performed differently by different units and different individuals, and they overlapped. For example, a group of analysts in the Customer Solutions and Innovation unit were performing technology watch to identify new potential IT solutions; the CIO also assigned a person who would actively look for new potential solutions and would share them with the rest of the IT department. Moreover, many ideas were originating from IBM, Air Canada’s innovation partner. Some other service suppliers that did not have innovation obligations in their contracts nevertheless began to present new ideas because they were aware of the innovation spirit at Air Canada. Similarly, the processes in support of seizing were semistructured and spontaneous. For example, many ideas were discussed in the hallway because senior directors constantly made preliminary assessments of opportunities while they were walking in the corridors. The search for and selection of IT solutions process was also semi-structured, supported by mock-up and prototyping sessions as opposed to a formal selection process.

Therefore, as per our findings, we suggest that the microfoundations that supported dynamic capabilities at Air Canada, whether ability-related or repeatability-related, were mainly put into use to enable the enactment of a logic of opportunity.

## Implications and conclusion

In this study, we investigated the strategic role of ITO as an intrinsic element of IT strategy. Anchored in the DCP, our investigation was guided by the high-level dynamic capabilities of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring. Our study further explored the microfoundations of these capabilities and offered a model that theorizes about the relationships among these microfoundations. However, our study has the following limitations: first, being based on a single case, the generalizability of our model might be limited. We consider, however, that our case selection criteria led us to select a case that was representative of large global firms evolving in a turbulent environment and relying heavily on IT to gain and maintain agility. This confers a higher degree of generalizability to our findings. Second, our informant base did not include all potential sources. For example, although we interviewed one account manager from the main supplier, all our other informants were from Air Canada. Therefore, our model is based on the client perspective. We acknowledge that this might have resulted in reporting successful episodes only. Future research could address this limitation by exploring the effects of unsuccessful episodes.

Notwithstanding its limitations, our research makes important contributions to the DCP as well as to the ITO literature. At the time of our review, no prior studies had examined the relationship among microfoundations of dynamic capabilities in the context of IT in general and ITO in particular. Our study addresses this gap by theorizing about the interaction between ITO and IT architecture processes (repeatability-microfoundations) and the enabling role of ability-related microfoundations in supporting and facilitating this interaction. As such, our study offers contributions to DCP by expanding its domain of application and therefore increasing its degree of generalizability. It also provides empirical evidence for the proposition that dynamic capabilities could be used following “a logic of opportunity” (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Martin, 2011). It contributes to the ITO literature by presenting a model that offers insight into the strategic role played by ITO through unpacking its interaction with another element of a firm’s IT strategy, that of IT architecture capability. While previous literature has mainly focused on the strategic intent of ITO (e.g. DiRomualdo & Gurbaxani, 1998; Kishore, Agrawal, & Rao, 2004) and its strategic impact on firms, the actual realization of this strategic role remains underinvestigated (Lacity, Khan, Yan, & Willcocks, 2010). Therefore, our study makes a contribution by unpacking the black box of ITO’s strategic role and illustrating and explaining how IT can become a “platform for agility” (Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

We explored and identified the microfoundations of strategic IT dynamic capabilities—ITO and IT architecture—and offered insight into their ongoing interaction. Thus, our theorizing complements the literature on the long-term interactions of ITO with other firm capabilities (e.g. Dedrick, Carmel, & Kraemer, 2011; Willcocks, Reynolds, & Feeny, 2007). In enfolding the extant literature, we made the observation that our theorizing about microfoundations is in line with capability-based ITO studies from the client’s perspective (e.g. Ranganathan & Balaji, 2007; Willcocks et al., 2007). While these studies did not label the identified processes, structure or skills as microfoundations, nor did they conceptualize them theoretically and explore their interactions, they found them to be important elements that influence the success of ITO relationships. We therefore contribute to this literature by theoretically conceptualizing salient microfoundations, by theorizing about how the repeatability-related microfoundations interact, how they are enabled by ability-related microfoundations, and how the microfoundations support high-level dynamic capabilities that contribute to the firm’s agility. Future research could extend our findings by adopting a complementary conceptualization of microfoundations. Indeed, this study is anchored in Teece’s (2007) definition that pertains to “the distinct skills, processes, procedures, organizational structures, decision rules, and disciplines [. . .] which undergird enterpriselevel sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring of capacities that are difficult to develop and deploy” (p. 1319). Another conceptualization of microfoundations, however, puts individuals at the center of microfoundations in order “to decompose macro-level constructs in terms of the actions and interactions of lower-level organizational members, understand how firm-level performance emerges from the interaction of these members, and how relations between macro variables are mediated by micro actions and interactions” (Foss & Pedersen, 2016). Future research that explicitly acknowledges the role played by individual actors in a context similar to that of our study would enrich our explanation.

Our study offers practical implications. In particular, it illustrates various ways through which managers could reconfigure their IT solutions and services in an assemblage of processes, structures, skills, decision rules, and communication mechanisms. In other words, our study emphasizes the role that ability-related microfoundations play for processes to be successfully executed. For example, the findings of our study remind managers that, in order to ensure that innovative ideas resulting from innovation-related processes are disseminated and shared in a timely and effective manner, the right communication mechanisms/channels should be in place. Our study also draws managers’ attention to the complexity of this assemblage and the constant focus needed to manage and maintain it. Also, the logic of opportunity discussed in this case illustrates that, with the right supporting microfoundations in place, ITO could indeed play a strategic role and could serve as a means of reaching and maintaining agility.
