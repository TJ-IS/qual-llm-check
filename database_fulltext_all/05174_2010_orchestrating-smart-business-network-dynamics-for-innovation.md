## Abstract

This paper proposes the concept of Orchestrating Smart Business Networks (SBN) as a managerial function that shapes structural dynamics for innovation. On the basis of commitment, as one way to exert power, and dynamic capacities, the suggested managerial function can develop a path of efficiency towards innovation by managing the SBN’s structural dynamics, the network boundaries and digital platforms. The network’s centripetal and centrifugal forces are used as the units of analysis, and this managerial function is then tested with empirical results obtained from a case study. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 481–493. doi:10.1057/ejis.2010.19; published online 13 April 2010

Keywords: Smart Network Dynamics; power; innovation; Business Network Operating Systems

## Introduction

Orchestrating is a network capacity to dynamically organize innovation to ensure future value creation (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Ritala et al., 2009). Innovation is recognized as a required activity to guarantee organizational long-term survival and overcome organizational suboptimal regimes (Elster, 2000). However, it is also a source of conflict and tension since it can upset the forces of equilibrium in organizations, industry structures and markets (Gresov & Drazin, 1997). In this paper, I propose looking at innovation as a purposive process (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) that starts by holding some resources for short-term consumption and investing them to build and co-create something new (see Elster, 2000), recognizing that the potential results and rents of these shared investments require some network interactions and time (Rai et al., 2003). The challenge is that novelty unfolds in a context of radical uncertainty through steps into the unknown, not a pre-determined path, and it does not necessarily end with performance improvements (Gersick, 1991). In this context, Orchestrating becomes a critical network managerial capacity to set a purposive set of actions to build a path that ensures value for all the actors in the network.

The attractiveness of Smart Business Networks (SBN) resides in that they include a focal firm or Orchestrator in their configurations (Vervest et al., 2008). In addition, SBNs emphasize the capacity to unfold novelty (Van Heck & Vervest, 2007), sharing costs and risks (Penrose, 2008) and facilitating fast access to and the coordination of new sources of innovation, complementary resources and actors who work together (Teece et al., 1997; Dyer & Singh, 1998). Finally, they offer a Business Network Operating System (BNOS) as the foundation for information and communications technologies (ICTs) (Van Heck & Vervest, 2007; Gawer & Cusumano, 2008). A BNOS enables inter-organizational processes and fast information exchanges (Powell et al., 2005; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2008) to facilitate the recombination of existing knowledge resources and the creation of new ones (Pedersen & Larsen, 2006).

In this paper, I use power as unit of analysis, since it allows us a better look at decision making to confront uncertainty (Pffefer, 1994) and its influence on structural change dynamics (Emerson, 1987). In addition, Power and Orchestration present a gap that has been recognized in previous literature (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Ritala et al., 2009). I propose a process-based view of Orchestrating, focusing on structural dynamics, an area with few empirical studies and in which more research is needed (Poole & Van de Ven, 2004; Powell & Grodal, 2007). Since I argue about the important role digital platforms play in SBNs, this approach will also allow us to examine the role of a BNOS on structural changes (Subramani, 2004; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008).

The aim of this paper, then, is to provoke ideas and reflection and contribute to fill this gap in the existing theory on Orchestrating Smart Business Networks of Innovation. To do so, I first explore the existing literature on orchestration and its relationship with power. Second, I propose a specific definition of orchestration as a managerial function that is then tested with empirical research based on a real SBN case. Finally, I conclude by providing some insights and propositions on orchestrating and its implications for theory-building.

## Background and theoretical review: orchestration and power

## Power definition and functions

Power is the capacity to influence others by the definition of limits of action (Emerson, 1987). In organizational studies, power has the specific function of set direction, reducing uncertainty and holding conflict (Pffefer, 1994). As such power is linked with choices and managerial action towards equilibrium, a notion that can be defined, paraphrasing Barnard (1938/1968), as the situation where all actors in the network obtain more value than the costs and risks they take, which is, at the same time, a principle for efficiency. Power studies start at relational level (Emerson, 1987; A<sup>´</sup> lvarez & Svejenova, 2005) and expand to structures embracing relational dynamics, roles and network boundaries, variables that are interrelated (Scho¨n, 1971; Abbott, 1995).

Conflict theories assume that power leads to equilibrium and cohesion (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978; Gersick, 1991). This is in opposition to ‘politics’ or the resistance to managerial action (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978; Pffefer, 1994), stressing that decision making and action-setting are always a process of balancing different forces, since all the actors in the network may exert a certain degree of power and influence. Indeed, networks may present what Thorelli (1986) metaphorically called ‘network entropy’, that is, (1) conflicting relationships to control resources (Gresov & Drazin, 1997); (2) relational tensions between autonomy and cohesion (Nohria & Ghoshal, 1997); (3) loose goal-setting and direction; and/or (4) other variables that may conflict with managerial purposive action (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978; Benkler, 2006). In the next sections, I review power and orchestrating theories, normally crystallized in the dimensions of position and the control over resources, but also dynamic capacities as processes of becoming or unfolding potential network capacities into explicit courses of action.

## Centrality

Focal firms and centrality are interchangeable notions in much of the network literature (Krackhardt, 1990; Ahuja, 2000). The assumption is that the more central the actor, the greater the influence that node has on overall network behavior (Thorelli, 1986). Centrality is grounded on position or pre-eminence (Wasserman & Galaskiewicz, 1994), legitimacy and signals of status (Molm, 2003), as well as access to critical resources or knowledge sources (Kogut et al., 1992; Kogut, 2000) that may generate attachment and dependency on other actors to follow the focal firm (see Powell et al., 2005).

## Resources, relationships and roles

Orchestrators can exert power by controlling resources or exerting a specific role. First, this can be achieved by (1) centralizing decision making and becoming a strategic apex in the network (Lorenzoni & Baden-Fuller, 1995); (2) controlling some strategic assets that may provide competitive advantage (Wernerfelt, 1984); (3) bridging structural holes (Burt, 1992); (4) building trust (Granovetter, 1985; Molm et al., 2000); (5) applying sanctions when needed (Jarrillo, 1993); and (6) managing network processes such as resource selection and mobilization, facilitating cooperative strategies (Ha¨cki & Lighton, 2001; Brown et al., 2002; Brown & Hagel, 2005) and managing global operations (Fung et al., 2007).

Second, power theory considers these ‘roles’ to be the managerial, individual capacities to influence others behavior, exploring leading capacities (Mintzberg, 1973) or ‘socially constructed’ behaviors (see A<sup>´</sup> lvarez & Svejenova, 2005, pp. 72–73). Orchestrating literature has metaphorically associated focal firms to managerial roles such as leaders, architects, builders and nurturers (Hinterhuber, 2002) in coherence with Thorelli’s arguments (1986) in which positions and roles are interrelated.

## Dynamic capacities and orchestration

Strategic management and dynamic managerial capacities underscore the importance of managerial action and power to purposefully create, extend and modify the firm’s resource base as well as unfold potentialities (Teece et al., 1997; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). The concept of ‘Asset Orchestration’ proposed by Teece (2007, 2009) stresses (1) the coordination of disparate actors, something which is voluntary in nature, without ‘central planners’ and (2) recognizes that innovation is a combination of emergent processes and managerial design (Doz et al., 2000; Tuomi, 2002). Similarly, Pedersen & Larsen (2006) propose a framework for network models of innovation, precisely emphasizing the actors’ autonomy and self-control and the network’s capacity to recombine its resource base grounded on knowledge dynamics among actors (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Dhanaraj & Parkhe (2006) hone in on the focal firm and specifically define Orchestration for Innovation as a function of value creation and absorption. Orchestration is about generating network externalities and increasing returns; this is how network dynamics ‘expand the pie’. The focus here is on the Orchestrator’s central position and its capacity to extract more value from the network, controlling some resources to obtain a bigger piece of the pie by managing knowledge mobility, trust, socialization and shared identity to ensure the stability of the network’s dynamics.

## Combining power and orchestration

Orchestration literature is almost silent about network structural dynamics for innovation in terms of how roles, relationships and boundaries can change. Pioneers such as Thompson (1967) proposed managerial power as the resource to maneuver between flexibility and loose alignment and, on the other hand, efficiency, certainty and equilibrium. This approach focuses on the managerial capacity to make decisions and maintain organizational dynamics, setting paths for efficiency and stressing appropriate resource mobilization in the design of strategic choices and structures for future action (Thompson, 1967; Scho¨n, 1971). Empirical evidence suggests that Networks of Innovation reflect ‘multiconnectivity’, indicating that networks may present a space for choices where actors connect to one to another, increasing the diversity of the network and the possibility of setting multiple independent paths (Powell et al., 2005). To that end, Orchestrating can be conceived as an activity that can ‘change the rules of the game’. Even recognizing that all organizations’ decision making can be shaped by some path dependencies, the Orchestrating capacity to engage in diverse and multiple relationships and conversations can lead to changes in the actors’ relational dynamics, roles and boundaries to choose (not pre-defined) a new path.

## Orchestrating SBN dynamics for innovation

I define Orchestrating SBN Dynamics for Innovation as a function to build a path of efficiency towards innovation. I assume that innovation can change the equilibrium or it can be the managerial function to set an equilibrium, requiring the management of network structures. The hypothesis is that this function has a positive influence on the SBN’s innovative capacity, especially through digitally enabled processes (see Figure 1) by balancing centripetal and centrifugal forces. This function presents some innovation outcomes such as products, services and business models, thus ensuring value for network participants.

In the next sections, I discuss the relation between strategic management, power and the dynamic equilibrium (Section ‘Power, strategic management and dynamic equilibrium’), the notion of structural dynamics and network boundaries (Section ‘Dynamic boundary management: centripetal vs centrifugal forces’) and, finally, the role of a BNOS (Section ‘Business Network Operating Systems’) in more detail.

Power, strategic management and dynamic equilibrium Power in this function underscores purposive action, rational choices and managerial processes (Augier & Teece, 2008) that stand for managing optimality and equilibrium (Eisenhardt & Schoonhoven, 1996; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). As discussed, networks are in equilibrium when all the actors perceive that the benefits they obtain are greater than the risks and costs they take. Optimums are not a teleological ‘final objective’ (Elster, 1994, 2000); rather, Orchestrating is a process to set the path where different optimal situations (‘A’ and ‘C’ in Figure 2) can be achieved along the way, followed by sub-optimal ones (‘B’ and ‘D’) and the transitions in between (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997) (see Figure 2).

Specifically, I propose that the Orchestrator exerts power through commitments (Teece et al., 1997; Elster, 2000). Commitments are based on power and trust (Granovetter, 1985; Ghemawat, 1991) to make rational choices in order to reduce uncertainty and shape relational dynamics. Therefore, I propose that Orchestrating is about commitments for decision making, coordinating actions, mobilizing resources and holding conflict, all of which represent steps into the unknown and outcomes that cannot be predicted with certainty.

In this function, I assume that trust is dynamic or timedependent. This also occurs with relationships that imply different phases: deterrent and calculative during the first stages and knowledge and identification in more evolved relationships (Shapiro, 1987; Clemmensen et al., 2008). I assume, then, that commitments shape relationship dynamics for innovation, highlighting co-creative activities to invest and create something new.

## Dynamic boundary management: centripetal vs centrifugal forces

In this paper, I define network boundaries as a set of fundamental commitments among network actors that shape an SBN’s structural traits: the different roles, relationship dynamics, complementarities, the technology deployed (e.g., a BNOS) and coordination mechanisms (Thompson, 1967; Malone et al., 1987; Montgomery & Oliver, 2007). I assume that, even considering that commitments may be tenacious in terms of path dependencies, SBNs also reflect that their evolution is dependent on different choices and commitments the Orchestrator attains from among the different network actors.

In order to study this phenomenon, I use the metaphor of centrifugal and centripetal forces as the units of analysis and examine how they shape the network’s structure, that is, its boundaries, relationship dynamics and roles.

Centripetal forces dominate the SBN when actors decide to be a part of the network. Relationship dynamics will tend to be cohesive but they are the result of a combination of factors, including economic aspects and social opportunities (Eisenhardt & Schoonhoven, 1996). Relationships can have many functions and dynamics, which may not necessarily be intended for investments. For this reason, this research takes into account that cohesive forces in social action may hide a conflictive nature such as many forms of opportunism that can be defined as the asymmetry between intentions and what is given and what is received (see Elster, 1994; Child et al., 2006). As I argue in this paper, innovation is a different game, one where relationships require commitments to invest and engage in co-creative activities. Centrifugal forces, by contrast, can encourage actors to weaken the relationship and their commitments, or leave the SBN altogether if any of the strategic or resource complementarities offer a poor fit and lead to competitive relations more than cooperative ones.

Centripetal and centrifugal forces may vary along the way depending on the capacity to reach commitments. This can affect (1) the actors’ strategic position; (2) their access to new or complementary resources or new markets; (3) new sources of knowledge (Jarrillo, 1993; Kogut, 2000); (4) coordination costs and risks (Williamson, 1994); (5) their capacity to search for complementarities (Teece, 2009); and (6) their social value and trust (Shapiro, 1987).

From a dynamic view, we can also see that centripetal forces lead to cohesion when there are positive economic returns and cumulative advantage (Teece et al., 1997; Powell et al., 2005), which may lead to network externalities and lock-in (Shapiro & Varian, 1999). Other social dynamics can add centripetal forces such as when actors’ decision making is influenced by what others decide; when they identify with others; when ‘follow the trend’ dynamics are present (Powell et al., 2005); and/or actors have a simple need to be attached to or a part of a social system. The reasons for all these can be opportunism, as argued, along with the need to reduce uncertainty (Elster, 1994; Child et al., 2006).

Centripetal and centrifugal forces may vary along the way, recognizing path dependencies. In other words, the direction a network can go (its future structure) is a function of its current structure (Teece et al., 1997) that is in turn a function of its capacity to change or set new paths vs the forces of resistance to confront change due to structural rigidity or over-embeddedness (Granovetter, 1985; Sambamurthy, 2000). Precisely for this reason, the actors’ main motivation in networks of innovation is access to new sources of innovation or ‘sources of change’ such as the capacity to establish multiple relationship (Powell et al., 2005), multiple conversations and sources of knowledge (Kogut, 2000). For this, the function of Orchestrating for knowledge and resource mobilization and information exchanges is required (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Pedersen & Larsen, 2006) to set new equilibriums that manage the cycles of optimal and suboptimal regimes.

## Business Network Operating Systems

Since innovation is a process stepping into the unknown, dependencies arise in terms of information exchanges and knowledge generation, thus highlighting the importance of a BNOS in SBN dynamics for innovation. A BNOS is a set of modular components that, on the one hand, offers a standard framework and, on the other, facilitates plug-and-play options (Van Heck & Vervest, 2007). In addition, a BNOS offers shared processes, interoperability and compatibility (Alstyne, 1997). The first obvious effect is noise reduction and lower transaction costs among firms (Gurbaxari & Whang, 1991).

Second, I suggest considering the BNOS as a proxy in an SBN’s shared investments for innovation. Indeed, a BNOS may consist of a platform based on digital assets (such as web services) that can be co-created and co-produced, thus underscoring the role of technology as a space for creativity and co-creation. This idea underscores the BNOS’ role as a resource to contextualize and make sense of the ongoing network activity (Orlikowski, 1996), but it is also a means to propose new functionalities and compose them with the existing set of digital assets (Gawer & Cusumano, 2008).

Third, a BNOS is a communications system that can foster togetherness and trust, allowing information to become knowledge (Kogut et al., 1992; Kogut, 2000), enabling actors to absorb and recombine this knowledge base (Teece, 1987; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) and, thus, permitting the recombination of the SBN’s digital resource base. This stresses that the SBN’s working systems shape and are shaped by information systems, thus assuming that network working systems are based on information-related work (Orlikowski, 1996). In this context, actors’ choices are not the result of agency, teleology or blind adaptation (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). They are the means through which the actors’ interactions can create new paths.

## Methodology and research method

Given the exploratory nature of the research question, I have opted to apply the Abduction Methodology (Peirce, 1992). The latter is based on critical views regarding existing theory, methods of inquiry and the observation of empirical data. It combines rational deduction from some hypotheses and tests them with empirical data. At the same time, however, observation leads to an inductive process of theory-building. Its objective is to expand existing knowledge (Peirce, 1992) and present theoretical constructs that are conclusions of the research and, at the same time, hypotheses for further research (Habermas, 1971).

In the following sections, I present empirical data and observations based on my deductions. The empirical research was carried out using an exploratory case study research method (Yin, 2003) over a 5-year data collection period (2004–2009) and as part of a qualitative/ ethnographic approach. The following sources of information were used: (1) field notes taken during visits to the company; (2) press notes and clippings from 1998 to 2008; (3) the firm’s magazine from 2004 to 2008; (4) internal company documents and the company’s financial reports; (5) personal interviews with: (a) the company’s top executives (each interview lasted an hour and a half and was carried out between 2005 and 2008) and (b) employees in different areas; (6) interviews with executives from three of the firm’s biggest corporate clients (each interview took approximately 2 h, and I repeated some interviews in 2005, 2006 and 2007); and (7) interviews with managers of the firm’s franchised suppliers, all of which are presented in Table 1.

I also held focus groups with the company’s Board of Directors in 2005, 2006, 2007 and 2008 and attended client meetings in 2006 and 2007, which took 3 days. Furthermore, I had numerous informal meetings and phone conversations with these sources.

## Case description and analysis

In this section, I describe and analyze Multiasistencia, a focal firm within an SBN that provides different services for banks and insurers. This case was selected to study different stages within the company to examine the equilibrium, innovation and change throughout its 25-year history (from 1984 to 2009). This period even encompasses a disruptive change from 2000 to 2003 when Multiasistencia successfully implemented a BNOS. Its evolution reveals optimal and sub-optimal stages affecting the various network relationships and the different actors’ roles. For analytical purposes, I propose a dynamic, four-stage process spanning (1) the network’s initial conditions (1984–1995); (2) the boom and expansion of the network (1995–2000); (2) a change in the network (2000–2004); (3) network behavior promoting innovation as an equilibrium to innovate with information through a BNOS (2004–2008); and (4) from 2008 on when the focal firm introduced some changes in network governance. The main unit of analysis is centrifugal vs centripetal forces. The levels of analysis are the focal firm and the strategic relationships with the other actors in the SBN.

## Network creation (1984–1995)

This phase is defined by an entrepreneur who started Multiasistencia after identifying a structural hole between small businesses (hotels and restaurants) and repair services. According to the founder, ‘what we proposed was to establish criteria for these repairs, guaranteeing a set price and response, service and quality standards and guaranteeing repairs for three months’. This idea was expressed in the company’s slogan and promise in the 1990s, ‘Yes, we can’. It was a promise of trust that filled the structural hole and generated a new business and even a new ‘repair market’.

Multiasistencia provided its repair service through its International Control and Co-ordination Center. The Customer Service Representatives (CSRs) working there received the initial calls from end-users to provide the final solution, in many cases home repairs. Once the claim was accepted, the CSR deployed jobs to a network of self-employed trade professionals coordinated by the focal firm. Actors were attracted to the network due to its trustworthy, convenience and efficiency (reduction in search and implementation costs), all of which started to generate cumulative advantages.

## The network boom (1995–2000)

Multiasistencia’s boom generated increasing returns that acted as a centripetal force, attracting new actors to the SBN and expanding boundaries through externalities. These included banks that (1) wanted fast access to the new insurance market, where time-to-market was crucial and working with Multiasistencia offered this possibility; (2) wanted to lower transaction costs and take advantage of increasing economies of scale; (3) gain access to a very specialized service; and (4) focus on their core activities. This allowed banks to generate more value by crossselling insurance to their home mortgage clients or credit card holders. Trade professionals, by contrast, had access to larger markets, ensuring a constant flow of jobs. Meanwhile, end customers appreciated the 24/7 availability, time guarantees (3 h for emergencies and 48 h for the rest) and warranties (3 months). By the end of 2000, Multiasistencia reached 9 million end customers in Europe through 100 major corporations such as banks and insurers that outsourced this service. From 1995 to 2000 Multiasistencia grew from h40 million in turnover to h90 million. Its growth generated important economies of scale, increasing returns and network externalities that increased lock-in.

However, there was a latent and increasingly important asymmetry with the trade professionals. In 2000, the network encompassed approximately 11,000 trade professionals. This group was only 20% dependent upon delivering the service. There was a ‘culture of freedom’ that hid latent opportunistic relationships during the first phase and served as a centripetal force so long as the business grew, in other words, as long as the business was attractive. The focal firm, however, assumed full responsibility for coordination (pushing the actors in the network), hiding opportunistic dynamics as a result. There were no clear rules of engagement or commitment and, therefore, no clear boundaries were set.

In 2000, the Control Center needed 5.5 calls on average to find an available trade professional. As such, the firm’s economic boom also hide an important ramp of increasing coordination and transaction costs that drained productivity and finally led to a sub-optimal situation, making it more risky and costly to be a part of the SBN.

For example, productivity declined from 1,900 claims/ customer service representative (CSR)/year in 1995 to 1,200 in 2000. While the Control Center grew from 100 CSRs in 1995 to 450 in 2000, the CSRs’ activity focused on searching for available trade professionals and responding to complaints from end customers and banks, dedicating less time to developing more business.

In parallel, Multiasistencia focused on creating new services, becoming the network’s ‘locus of innovation’ (see Powell et al., 2005). For example, the company succeeded in (1) reducing the average claim cycle completion from 30 to 5 days; (2) reducing the process for a bank customer to change accounts from 2 weeks to 2 h, bridging banks with utilities and invoicing providers through an automatic branch office billing service; and (3) linking banks/insurers with trade professionals to carry out repairs through a comprehensive claim management service.

One manager explained, ‘Multiasistencia was very creative, but faced what can be described as an invisible glass ceiling: we did not understand very well how to control a whole lot of fragmented operations in a manner that was more professional, more automatic’. We can even see Multiasistencia as a focal firm in the center of a network but powerless, since the relational dynamics made Multiasistencia much more dependent on the surrounding relationships based on opportunism. This led to an increasingly costly structure that jeopardized the business. This was ‘the invisible glass ceiling’.

## Network change (2000–2004)

New management joined the focal firm in 2000 and decided to change the rules of the game, defining a new set of commitments and boundaries with the trade professionals and banks.

The focal firm’s management team decided to commit to trade professionals by asking them to do more by themselves while coaching them in the process of: (1) changing their role of self-employed workers to managers of small firms consisting of 10–15 professionals; (2) managing a much larger amount of repairs; (3) co-investing in software modules (web services) to manage these new businesses (financials and job assignments) that were developed initially by the focal firm; and (4) accepting a new coordination schema to (a) pull jobs from the BNOS, as well as digital billing and Service Level Agreements (SLA) and (b) leave the sales process exclusively to the focal firm.

On the one hand, this new schema represented that these newly created repair firms had to implicitly accept to share coordination costs, and, on the other, they had to accept greater dependency on Multiasistencia. However, this new power equilibrium was based on mutual commitment and trust. As one manager added, ‘the communication policy of sharing our objectives with them was, then, an absolutely key factor. There has been a relationship of sharing the objectives and of growing together’. However, not all trade professionals accepted these new rules of the game, and, from 2000 to 2003, some of the trade professionals left the professional network, ending their relationship.

The focal firm’s management team also changed relational dynamics with banks by balancing the centrifugal force of conflict due to dissatisfaction. Multiasistencia’s management wagered on ‘openness’ to define a new set of commitments. It established agreements with its client banks to co-create the Internet solution, the Business Network Operating System (BNOS), among all the network actors.

The BNOS was designed to manage claims and process repairs in a fully automated manner, linking up banks and trade professionals in real time, and it was governed by 100 variables. Multiasistencia management asked for a new commitment from banks by sharing the scalability problem, presenting the solution of leveraging digital platforms and asking them to co-create the new BNOS to run network processes. This led to a peer-to-peer type of relationship, creating autonomy on both sides and allowing the co-creation of software modules that combined existing co-specific software applications to build the BNOS. According to one manager, ‘80% of the processes developed was through co-creative processes’. However, in a few cases, Multiasistencia had to create lock-in policies by increasing greater dependency and raising exit costs to keep some corporate customers from leaving the network and, thus, losing business revenues.

I interpret their action as exerting autonomy, fostering a centrifugal force to change relationship dynamics: they asked for greater self-control and commitment among network actors, fought against opportunism and created peer-to-peer relationships. In addition, Multiasistencia enhanced CSRs’ empowerment and autonomy by transforming their role from that of ‘information chasers’ to ‘process owners’. They began by assessing the repair according to the firm’s schema programmed on the BNOS. The latter was intentionally designed as a boundary management system, establishing a framework or pattern of expected behavior acting as a deterrent (boundary guardian). According to one executive, ‘we have substituted all human communicative elements in the repair service, except incoming calls, by machines. We have developed a sophisticated process supported by ICTs, allowing people to manage exceptions’. Productivity at the Control Center increased 25% a year from 2000 to 2003, contrasting with the previous years’ decline (see Figure 3).

Multiasistencia changed its role to that of reference in the SBN. One manager commented, ‘the BNOS had been conceived as a framework of reference for network behavior, freeing up resources to focus on developing new business and innovation’. According to another executive, ‘after the change process, the business was autonomous: we did not need to push it any more’. The change process led to a new equilibrium, giving the network a totally different structure.

## Network behavior for innovation (2004–2008)

At this stage, the focal firm managed network boundaries by setting commitments that balanced the centrifugal force of autonomy with centripetal forces of increasing returns and lock-in. Multiasistencia’s role was to serve as the reference in a network of innovation, opening up spaces for growth by leveraging the BNOS as a space for co-creation. According to one executive, ‘working with mobile and Internet-based systems is like doing a puzzle in which the pieces are different technological tools which in turn evolve and allow us to modify the puzzle and make it more creative, more complex and, at the same time, more simple’.

The BNOS also enabled trust as a deterrent, controlling network boundaries through SLAs and through the BNOS, which automated and kept track of all the processes but also acted as a space for communication and co-creation. According to one executive, ‘For us the BNOS does not just contribute value; it has also been the facilitator for the entire new operating model that we have developed.

The main perspective, greatly simplifying Multiasistencia, was that the new process came about with the idea: ‘We’re going to put it on the Internet’. On the other hand, the BNOS allowed us to redefine all sets of relationships. I would go so far as to say that, ‘without a doubt, 80 per cent of the processes that are currently working in Multiasistencia were created at the request of our clients, with a joint design and joint teams’. Multiasistencia changed its role from being ‘the innovator’ and perceiving the network as an instrument to diffuse innovation to becoming the reference (a trustee to identify and share knowledge). It saw the network as ‘the locus of innovation’ and became a facilitator to co-create innovation by orchestrating the interaction of the different sources and voices, understanding proposals, composing them and combining them as new services. One trade professional commented, ‘in this process, we learned to adapt to what they (Multiasistencia) asked of us and, in a way, well, we also learned to trust them, to be more transparent and share information to be more agile and do better business’. One bank executive added, ‘Multiasistencia is very innovative and always proposed new ways to develop new business’.

Another bank executive commented that ‘the current technology is going to help us a lot to reduce the times and to better communicate with the client’. First, CSR interaction with all the network actors served as a new source of solutions. New ideas appeared regarding conflict resolution due to gaps in service provision and the problem-solving process. Moreover, the BNOS provided a history of contacts and data analysis that fed predictive models to mobilize the right resources at the right time. Examples of this were better workload distribution in the Control Center, process improvements and increased availability by managing a network of three other Contact Centers belonging to insurance companies in Spain.

The latter acted as back-up centers to ensure access during peak periods in telephone traffic or during telecommunication network failures. Second, corporate customers (banks and insurers) and trade professionals were also important sources of innovation. New software components (web services) were developed by co-creating new services. The BNOS was a space for service innovation and shared investments and network resources although Multiasistencia continued to be the technology’s formal owner. Examples of this co-creation were, first, digital signatures and electronic invoicing, ‘desktop audits’ over the Internet allowing loss adjusters to assess claims over the BNOS, and premium services to inform customers through SMS messaging. Finally, Multiasistencia also continued innovating by replicating this model in other markets such as healthcare and public services (see Table 2).

Orchestrating the SBN at this stage implied managing a new equilibrium. We can identify the following centripetal forces: trust, higher business value and the generation of economies of scale and scope. By contrast, centrifugal forces at this point may have been misfits at the strategic or resource levels, representing a new cost base for firms that may have redefined the logic of transactional costs. Autonomy may have also worked as a centrifugal force in terms of the search for new opportunities.

Here the balance was the increasing dependency on Multiasistencia due to increasing specialization and different roles: banks focused on their core business and trade professionals only on repairs, leaving business management to the focal firm. The BNOS was also an important instrument to generate system lock-in and served to generate trust as a deterrent, keeping track of SLAs.

## Discussion and propositions

By conceiving and proposing the function presented in this paper, I have tried to contribute to the discussion on dynamic capabilities and power by defining and testing Orchestrating SBN Dynamics for Innovation empirically. Grounded on the foregoing analysis, this paper presents the propositions described in the following sections.

## Orchestrating SBNs: the dual nature of power

Proposition 1: Orchestrating SBN dynamics for innovation balances autonomy and cohesion through commitments as a power expression of managerial action.

With this proposition I argue that Orchestration, by nature, exerts power dually. First, it is about setting direction and opening new spaces. In the case studied, this occurs by exerting autonomy on network actors. More autonomous actors may make new choices to search for complementarities that the Orchestrator can then mobilize, making the SBN the ‘locus’ of innovation. This enables the SBN to work as a multiconnected space of new relational dynamics, which can combine and recombine new sources for innovation such as (1) autonomous staff focused on problem-solving, stemming from the interaction with other network actors and with information systems such as the BNOS; (2) the co-creation and development of a BNOS based on peer-to-peer dynamics; and (3) the redesign of network structures through the capacity to engage in new communication schemas.

The second source of power is setting commitments. Innovation requires trust since actors need to expose themselves and unfold new sources, propose ideas and combine them in co-creative activities. In addition, this process emphasizes that Orchestrating is about holding conflict at bay, since autonomy stresses doing more by oneself, having self-control and less dependency on other’s power but also assuming the risk of having different objectives and perspectives. To that end, commitments among actors are about taking care of each other and maintaining an ongoing equilibrium to not break up SBN structures (even during deep structural changes), and, therefore, creating an efficient path for innovation.

Orchestrating through commitments is about changing the rules of the game and keeping the game alive. I think that this approach presents SBN as a more natural way of organizing; it also challenges the neo-classical perspective of relationships as transactions, which presents many limitations for innovation studies (Teece, 2009). In the case studied, Orchestrating through commitments also contrasts with opportunism that, paradoxically, served as a centripetal force. This confirms that some social dynamics can be very cohesive but, since they may hide asymmetries, they are incompatible with co-creative processes of innovation. As a result, Orchestrating in the case also implied taking actions that led to some relationships being dropped while new ones were built, recognizing that not all actors in a network can have the interest or the capacity to set commitments. Moreover, in the Multiasistencia case, commitments were set precisely in sub-optimal regimes, emphasizing how trust is a needed resource that Orchestrators have to foster to manage investments in situations where, for some actors, costs and risks may be greater than the short-term benefits.

## The dynamics of co-creation

## Proposition 2: Orchestrating innovation in SBNs has a positive influence on maximizing outcomes when commitments lead to peer-to-peer relationships among autonomous players.

This proposition emphasizes that Orchestrating is a different way of exerting power, leading to different network structures based on peer-to-peer relations. Being peers seems to be the pre-requisite for co-creative activities according to empirical results. This requires the existence of complementary assets to exchange and create something new. This dynamic affects both actors’ roles and the network boundaries. Actors become more specialized, gaining greater differentiation and establishing a new division of labor. In the case study, the new network structure stressed complementarities but also relational co-dependencies since trade professionals needed to focus on their repair work, being more dependent on the sales process and market development carried out by the focal firm. The result was that tradesmen and the focal company balanced their relationship. By the same token, banks needed Multiasistencia’s service quality to expand their customer business value. This is also consistent with the proposition that, in order to establish a new equilibrium, firms can create greater specialization and differentiated resources (Wernerfelt, 1984). As the case study shows, orchestrating innovation, as a co-creative activity, maximized results and outcomes from 2003 to 2008 by opening up a space for new voices and novel sources of ideas that complemented and recombined, thus creating novel results with an important economic impact. ## The Orchestrator as the SBN reference

Proposition 3: A focal firm can act as the network reference, influencing dynamic boundary control and trust in the network.

In this proposition, I want to stress the structural attributes of the focal firm that acts as a vardstick for innovation, being able to exert power and strategic control on a peer-to-peer basis while maintaining the rules of the game. Although I underscore the role of peerto-peer interactions in innovation, in the case analyzed, the focal firm strengthened its central role and maintained ownership of the digital platform, thus controlling a strategic resource to keep an asymmetrical power balance with respect to the other actors though it also maintained its capacity to generate commitments. It is important to note that the focal firm changed its role as well as the network structure and its function in the different periods analyzed. In the first phases (1984–2000) the focal firm’s power was based on concentrating innovation and trying to control the network as a tool to spread its innovations. During the suboptimal regime (2000–2004) the focal firm exerted power by exerting autonomy, opening new spaces and establishing new commitments. In this transition, the focal firm proposed a new game and changed the rules of the game, balancing relational dynamics. From 2004 to 2008 the emphasis was on co-creative activities to design and implement new services. Here the focal firm exerted power by increasing its centrality as the reference for innovation, controlling resources and opening new spaces, thus unfolding dynamic capacities.

The new role of the BNOS

Proposition 4: The BNOS plays the role of proxy for commitments among network actors.

I argue that the BNOS plays this critical role, serving as a proxy for trust in the network by providing transparency and being built as the result of shared investments and cocreative activities. Indeed, a BNOS is a space for information-sharing that allows for the transformation of the network’s knowledge base. The BNOS plays a critical role in network domains by (1) exerting boundary control and surveillance, allowing the creation of network externalities and increased returns, and network scale and lock-in and (2) expanding network domains of knowledge as a result of co-creative activities, continuous improvements and the generation of new markets, that is, a digital infrastructure of innovation. This emphasizes the role played by technology in fostering the dynamic interplay of capabilities. This interplay leads to proposals for the creation of new services through the assimilation of new knowledge and reworking the existing knowledge base in order to expand value options and increase returns.

## Conclusions

Orchestrating SBN for innovation is a managerial function to set a new path of efficiency, managing an ongoing equilibrium between the actors’ benefits and costs that can vary along the way. This notion stresses that setting a path implies ‘changing the rules’ while keeping the game alive. I synthesize this discussion and findings through the power dimensions of centrality, control over resources and dynamic capabilities.

First, I propose that focal firms exert power as a reference (or central position) for generating novelty by opening new sources of innovation among network actors to facilitate co-creative activites.

Second, focal firms control (1) network boundaries, defined as network commitments and (2) the BNOS as the digital innovation infrastructure. Creativity in social settings may be part inspirational, but it also requires perception and communication in order to become a structural process. The BNOS is a SBN structural resource through which actors can exchange ideas, projects and new services to shape the SBN’s dynamics. This underscores the importance of two variables to understand an SBN’s smartness: autonomy and the predominant relational model among network actors (whether hierarchical or peer-to-peer).
