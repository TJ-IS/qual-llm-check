# Collaboration risk management in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships: Evidence from telestroke networks

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships Hub-spoke dyads Collaboration risks Telestroke

## A B S T R A C T

Information technology (IT)-enabled partnerships can unlock previously unattainable value propositions between organizations that have resource, capability, and other asymmetries by allowing larger organizations access to niche and local resources while providing smaller organizations access to enhanced resources and capabilities. However, recent studies have shown that many IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships often fail because the organizations do not e<sup>f</sup>ectively manage the involved collaboration risks. Most organizations focus on the strategic aspect of whether partners behave cooperatively or competitively, while paying scant attention to the operational aspect of bringing together partner contributions through coordination. To examine this problem, we report on a case study of two hub-spoke networks that used telemedicine to facilitate expertise sharing and decision making about stroke treatment at emergency depart ments in rural hospitals (spokes) based on information exchanges with remote neurologists at academic medical centers (hubs). As a result. we contribute to the inter-organizational in: formation systems literature by explaining how organizations in IT-enabled asymmetric part nerships manage collaboration risks. We demonstrate how partners in such relationships perceive and approach strategic cooperation and operational coordination risks di<sup>f</sup>erently. We also explain how collaboration interactions change over time as the partners manage risks based on needs and resource endowments. Drawing on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we provide guidance to organizations on how to manage sustainable IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships in general, and tele stroke networks in particular.

## 1. Introduction

Prior research has shown that information technology (IT) can enable collaboration between a larger organization and one or more smaller organizations by facilitating information exchanges and allowing the larger organization to access niche and loca resources (including assets and capabilities) and the smaller organizations to access an enhanced scope and scale of resources (e.g., Ceccagnoli, Forman, Huang, & Wu, 2012; Sarker, Sarker, Sahaym, & Bjørn-Andersen, 2012). These partnerships allow the involved organizations to take advantage of mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial symmetries (e.g., the technology platforms upon which their products and services are built) as well as asymmetries (e.g., size, resource endowments, and other attributes relevant to the partnership, such as market access, information availability, geographical location, and business relationships). IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships are typical in strategic inter-organizational network con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, such as hub-spoke networks, where one partner (hub) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger, is generally centrally positioned, and has more resources and expertise than the smaller and more dispersed partners (spokes) (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Jarillo, 1988). Such partnerships are quite common in emergency information systems, distribution networks, service provisioning, government agencies, and franchise arrangements (Constantinides & Barrett, 2012; Van De Walle, Turo<sup>f</sup>, & Hiltz, 2014).

Nowhere are IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships more evident—or critical—than in the context of health care, in which provider organizations use hub-spoke and other con<sup>fi</sup>gurations to o<sup>f</sup>er a variety of life-saving health services to geographically dispersed populations (Porter & Lee, 2013). Consider, for example, a 60-year old man waking up with severe headache and feeling of weakness in his left arm. His wife calls 911 for emergency medical services (EMS), which transports him by ambulance to the emergency department (ED) of the nearest hospital. The ED sta<sup>f</sup> must quickly determine whether he has a stroke, and if so, begin treatment. However, determining whether a patient is indeed su<sup>f</sup>ering from a stroke—and, importantly, what type of stroke—is vital and timecritical. Incorrect diagnosis, or treatment beyond a narrow time window after the onset of stroke symptoms, can lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant disabilities or loss of life. Most small hospitals do not have immediate access to a neurologist to assist in diagnosing and treating such emergency stroke cases. Therefore, some small hospitals have begun to address this capability and resource gap by connecting to larger hub hospitals, where a neurologist can virtually “view” the patient via stroke-speci<sup>fi</sup>c telemedicine technology (telestroke). Telestroke allows the remote neurologist to collaborate with the small hospital's ED physicians and take into account the patient's computed tomography (CT) scans and other clinical information to make an appropriate treatment recommendation. This life-saving and time-critical process would not be possible without telestroke, integrated electronic medical records (EMR), laboratory information systems, picture archival and communication systems (to share CT scans), and—equally importantly—intensive collaboration between the two hospitals. However, such asymmetric partnerships can have collaboration risks resulting from concerns such as unstable commitments, di<sup>f</sup>erences in motivations of one or both partners, opportunistic behaviors, and inability to coordinate across organizational boundaries (Gulati, Wohlgezogen, & Zhelyazkov, 2012). Drawing on Gulati et al. (2012), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne collaboration risks as challenges perceived by one or more partners in an inter-organizational con<sup>fi</sup>guration that may a<sup>f</sup>ect at tainment of organizational and alliance goals. Unfortunately, we know little about how to manage such risks in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships.

The inter-organizational information systems (IS) literature has considered asymmetric partnerships in contexts such as software development, technology innovation, and enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems (e.g., Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Prince, Barrett, & Oborn, 2014; Robey, Im, & Wareham, 2008; Sarker et al., 2012; Teo, Wei, & Benbasat, 2003). However, most studies have examined the strategic aspect of whether partners behave cooperatively or competitively, while paying very little attention to the operational aspect of bringing together partner contributions through coordination (Gulati, 1998; Gulati et al., 2012; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009) Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the literature has yet to fully examine both the cooperative and coordinating aspects of collaboration risk management in IT-enabled hub-spoke networks, which is unfortunate since such networks are especially vulnerable to collaboration risks due to inherent asymmetries.<sup>1</sup> It is quite conceivable that the hub organization—that most likely initiated the collaboration, has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater levels of technological and process expertise, or has committed more resources to the collaboration than the spoke—may seek more in<sup>fl</sup>uence in governing the partnership or claim greater share of resulting bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, thereby potentially jeopardizing the relationship (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006). As such, a large body of strategic alliance and inter-organizational relationship research has shown that partnerships often fail (Dyer, Kale, & Singh, 2001; Madhok & Tallman, 1998; Park & Ungson, 2001) and successful management of these risks can help sustain the collaboration (Kumar & van Dissel, 1996).

Against this backdrop, we ask - How do organizations manage collaboration risks in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships? To answer this question, we examine two telestroke networks, each organized in a hub-spoke con<sup>fi</sup>guration, where a large academic medical center (hub) with advanced neurological capabilities collaborated with nearby small and rural hospitals (spokes) with limited loca neurological capabilities. Each hub-spoke dyad connected via the same telestroke technology and jointly enacted emergency care processes whenever patients with stroke symptoms arrived at the spoke ED. Further, each dyad relied not only on telestroke, but also on integrated IT applications and complementary resources and processes. Based on our case <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we contribute to the interorganizational IS literature by explaining how organizations in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships manage collaboration risks, how they perceive strategic cooperation and operational coordination risks di<sup>f</sup>erently, and how collaboration interactions change over time as the partners manage risks based on needs and resource endowments. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we provide practical guidance on how to manage such partnerships in general, and telestroke networks in particular.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we present prior research on IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships. Next, we develop the analytical framework with cooperation and coordination as two complementary perspectives on collaboration risk management. Then, after providing details of our research design, methodological approach, and investigative context, we report the results of our qualitative analyses. Finally, we discuss theoretical contributions and implications for research and practice.

## 2. IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships

Organizations are increasingly leveraging IT to enable partnerships (e.g., Jonsson, Holmström, & Lyytinen, 2009; Kohli & Grover, 2008; Robey et al., 2008) that facilitate information exchanges about shared processes and allow them to access a wider range of valuable resources, including complementary assets and capabilities (Clemons & Row, 1992; Dyer & Singh, 1998; Gulati, Lavie, & Singh, 2009; Saraf, Langdon, & Gosain, 2007). Research within this domain has established that such strategic IT investments can enable previously unattainable value propositions by combining competencies, capabilities, and knowledge in various contexts, including supply chains (Subramani, 2004), logistics (Rai, Pavlou, Im, & Du, 2012), ERP technology platforms (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Sarker et al., 2012), and innovation alliances (Hagedoorn, 1993; Han et al., 2012).

Many IT-enabled partnerships are between organizations with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant asymmetries, particularly with respect to size, resource endowments, service delivery context, and network position. For example, in hub-spoke networks in software, the hub typically provides the systems' architecture and generic core functionalities, whereas the spokes build their solutions upon these platforms (Gawer & Cusumano, 2002; Huber, Kude, & Dibbern, 2017; Iansiti & Levien, 2004; Prencipe, 2003). Such partnerships are more likely to be successful when there are enough similarities between organizations to facilitate knowledge transfer across a shared knowledge base as well as enough di<sup>f</sup>erences to bring something new to the table rather than duplicating assets and capabilities (Dyer & Singh, 1998; Ireland, Hitt, & Vaidyanath, 2002; Lane & Lubatkin, 1998; Zahra & George, 2002). For example, global platform vendors such as Microsoft, IBM, and SAP frequently develop partnerships with smaller independent software vendors who co-develop applications and solutions for the product platforms as part of larger ecosystems (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). Sarker et al. (2012) examined the case of a leading ERP vendor that developed a standard packaged software for the global market while collaborating with small, loca system integration vendors who provided sales support, consulting, customization, and enhancement services for individual clients. In these partnerships, value creation depends on factors such as shared IT functionalities and communication routines (Rai et al., 2012), information <sup>fl</sup>ow integration (Patnayakuni, Rai, & Seth, 2006), process integration capabilities (Rai, Patnayakuni, & Seth, 2006), inter-organizational business process standards (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012) and governance mechanisms that counter opportunistic behavior and safeguard partner interests (Sarker et al., 2012).

Despite the increasing attention to IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships in software and other industries, few studies have examined such partnerships within health care. This research context is especially important, as asymmetric partnerships among healthcare organizations are quite common; they have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for care delivery to patients; and, they enable value cocreation between the participating organizations. However, the current IS literature often assumes that asymmetric partnerships in any organizational context—regardless of di<sup>f</sup>erences in size, assets, technological and process capabilities, network position, or other attributes—face similar collaboration risks and, therefore, have similar perspectives on managing such risks (Gallivan & Depledge, 2003; Mohtashami, Marlowe, Kirova, & Deek, 2006). In this paper, we argue that such partnerships face risks that are often dissimilar for the participating organizations, especially in the health care context. As such, there can be di<sup>f</sup>erences in risks for hub versus spokes, but also between spokes, depending on their circumstances, resources, and strategy. A few studies have indeed pointed to such divergent perspectives. For example, in the case of enterprise software applications, the independent software vendors may face competition from other vendors in the network (Shapiro & Varian, 1998) and from the dominant organization (Farrell & Klemperer, 2007; Kude & Dibbern, 2009). In emergency information systems, although a central hub may deploy signi<sup>fi</sup>cant resources to establish and maintain the IT network, the spokes may have limited resources or lack incentive to contribute to the overall network (Hiltz, Van de Walle, & Turo<sup>f</sup>, 2009). In the case of telemedicine networks, some spokes may become hubs themselves by acquiring requisite capabilities that enable them to o<sup>f</sup>er telemedicine services to nearby hospitals, thus potentially competing with the original hub (Singh, Mathiassen, & Mishra, 2015). Yet, we do not fully understand how each partner manages such collaboration risks.

The typical con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships in healthcare are illustrated and further described in Table 1. Like in the enterprise software context, healthcare partnerships are often between a large centrally positioned organization having signi<sup>fi</sup>cant advantage in assets and capabilities, and smaller peripherally located organizations with limited assets and capabilities. The simplest, dyadic con<sup>fi</sup>guration leverages IT to enable communication and collaboration, as often occurs when hospitals collaborate with imaging centers, laboratories, and specialty ambulatory care practices directly without being part of a larger network. A hub-spoke con<sup>fi</sup>guration represents a more complex relationship between asymmetric partners, where a focal service is o<sup>f</sup>ered by the hub, such as telemedicine (Constantinides & Barrett, 2006) or group purchasing for hospital supply chains (Markus & Bui, 2012). In this con<sup>fi</sup>guration, multiple spokes connect to the hub—and generally not to each other—to take advantage of the hub's assets and capabilities. In telemedicine networks, the hub's advantages may also relate to geography (it may be centrally located) or specialization (it may have the specialized equipment and expertise related to, for example, neurology). The hub bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by gaining access to a larger population of patients and by providing tertiary (i.e., highly specialized) care. The spokes bene<sup>fi</sup>t by gaining access to the hub's resources and expertise, among others. Finally, a fully connected network con<sup>fi</sup>guration occurs when the spokes connect to each other and to the hub. For example, hospitals routinely share patient-related health care information with other hospitals within a region, community, or hospital system using the IT infrastructure provided by health information exchanges (Yaraghi, Du, Sharman, Gopal, & Ramesh, 2014). In this study, we focus on hub-spoke con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, particularly in arrangements where expertise sharing occurs between collaborating organizations.

Examination of these risks is particularly important, as some studies have reported extremely high failure rates for collaborative endeavors, often exceeding 50% (Kale, Dyer, & Singh, 2002; Kale & Singh, 2009; Lunnan & Haugland, 2008). To better understand and mitigate collaboration risks in strategic alliances, we draw upon the conceptualization by Gulati et al. (2012) of two facets of collaboration: cooperation (i.e., partners' commitment and alignment of interests) and coordination (i.e., alignment and adjustment of partners' actions to accomplish shared activities). Although much of the early collaboration risk literature emphasized strategic aspects of partnerships and therefore examined the risks due to lack of strategic cooperation by one or more partners (Das & Teng, 1996; Das & Teng, 2001; Nooteboom, 1999), recent research has acknowledged the importance of addressing operational aspects of partnerships as well (Albers, Wohlgezogen, & Zajac, 2016). As such, researchers have begun to examine coordination risks, especially because partnership failures have been observed even when cooperation risks have been e<sup>f</sup>ectively mitigated (Gulati et al., 2012). However, to our knowledge, no study has examined both cooperation and coordination risks in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships. To address this gap and to provide a foundation for our empirical analysis, we <sup>fi</sup>rst draw on notable cooperation risk and coordination risk literature to develop an integrated collaboration risk management framework that includes various risk drivers and resolution mechanisms (Table 2).

Following Gulati et al. (2012), inter-organizational cooperation refers to the “joint pursuit of agreed on goal(s) in a manner corresponding to a shared understanding about contributions and payo<sup>f</sup>s” (p. 3). Within the consideration of cooperation risks, contribution failures can occur when partners avoid honoring their resource and time commitments. Each organization must agree on what it is willing to contribute (e.g., time, assets, capabilities, and market access) in exchange for what it expects (e.g., access to intellectual property, new technology, gains in e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and enhanced legitimacy) and these agreements determine the intended scope of the partnership (Gulati et al., 2012). A key concern is that some partners may not deliver on the agreed upon contributions (e.g., by shirking responsibilities or reneging on resource commitments). Hence, organizations may view the cooperation of a partner negatively or question the value of the collaboration itself (Gulati et al., 1994), and, as a result, may lose interest or reduce their investments in the partnership, leading to its deterioration and potentially to its dissolution (Gulati et al., 2012).

Appropriation failures stem from “the <sup>fi</sup>rm's concern about its ability to capture a fair share of the rents from the alliance in which it is engaged” (Gulati & Singh, 1998, pg. 788). Accordingly, appropriation failures occur when partners display opportunistic behavior by prioritizing individual protective or exploitive practices (Kale, Singh, & Perlmutter, 2000). Such behaviors are likely to occur when partners disregard unenforceable commitments or moral obligations (Williamson, 1985). If not managed e<sup>f</sup>ectively, such behaviors can strain the partnership, cause mistrust and reversion to protective behaviors, and may ultimately result in the partners' failure to cooperate (Gulati et al., 2012; Wathne & Heide, 2000).

Alignment failures can occur when incentives are misaligned, competitive behaviors replace cooperative behaviors, and when mutual understandings are not actively developed, resulting in confusion or di<sup>f</sup>ering views of how the partnership should be enacted (Gulati et al., 2012). Research has also shown that e<sup>f</sup>ective cooperation requires organizations to align interests, to make mutually beneficial contributions to the partnership, and to limit opportunistic behavior (Das & Teng. 1996. 1999: Gulati et al., 2012). The involved organizations in any partnership essentially remain independent economic actors with control over their own resourceallocation decisions and with di<sup>f</sup>erent—and possibly con<sup>fl</sup>icting—strategic objectives (Deeds & Hill, 1999; Park & Ungson, 2001). Therefore, their internal choices (e.g., focus on a particular market or a service) or external pressures (e.g., competition from an alliance partner or from new entrants in the market) may lead to different levels of interest in the joint effort or even direct conflict of interest and rivalry in the relationship (Gulati et al., 2012).

The current literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es various resolution mechanisms—including contractual speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, partnership governance, and resource investments (Table 2)—that organizations can leverage to resolve cooperation risks. Unfortunately, research has not fully applied these mechanisms to IT-enabled asymmetric inter-organizational relationships, which is the focus of this paper. Based on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context, a partnering organization may rely on one or more mechanism to counter cooperation failure. Collaborating organizations often rely on contractual specifications to generate value from their investments, to protect relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, and to safeguard from potentially competing interests. Such contractual speci<sup>fi</sup>cations outline the “mutual exchange of rights among the parties involved in a relationship” (Sobrero & Schrader, 1998, p. 586) and can manifest as obligations, exchange processes, and expected outputs (Kale & Singh, 2009). Contractual speci<sup>fi</sup>cations may also include enforcement and adjudication provisions (Kale & Singh, 2009) as well as task descriptions and contingency planning (Argyres et al., 2007).

Some organizations may see the collaboration purely in transactional terms, failing to see the value from developing a long-term relationship (Zajac & Olsen, 1993). As such, collaborating organizations must develop partnership governance mechanisms to prevent or minimize opportunistic behavior of any individual organization. Such mechanisms can be formal, such as leadership support or managers assigned directly to the partnership (Dyer et al., 2001), or informal, such as the presence or establishment of incentives that encourage self-enforcement, for example, norms, social pressures, and reputational mechanisms (Gulati et al., 2012).

The relational view literature (Dyer & Singh, 1998) and research on sustainable IT-enabled collaborations (Kumar & van Dissel, 1996) suggest that partners often rely on mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial resource investments to strengthen their relationship. Organizations have long addressed cooperation risks through equity agreements on partner investments (Das & Teng, 2001; Kale & Singh, 2009). For example, partners may commit technology and provide startup capital and complementary resources to facilitate and sustain the relationship.

## 3.2. Coordination risk management

Organizations may cooperate and implement cooperation risk management mechanisms, yet the collaboration may still fail if the partners do not adequately address the “mechanics of bringing together partners' contributions” (Gulati et al., 2012, p. 2). Studies have established that these “mechanics”—broadly referred to as coordination—play a critical role in successful collaborative e<sup>f</sup>orts (Gulati & Singh, 1998; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). Hence, the coordination risk perspective focuses on the operational speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of partnership, and refers to the “deliberate and orderly alignment or adjustment of partners' actions to achieve jointly determined goals” and includes the “speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and operation of information-sharing, decision-making, and feedback mechanisms” (Gulati et al., 2012). The need for such coordination results from inherent interdependencies in collaborations (Dekker, 2004).

The current literature on intra- and inter-organizational coordination suggests that partners face risks driven by di<sup>f</sup>erent types of coordination failures, including structural, institutional and adaptability failures. Structural failures result from a lack of forma structures—such as hierarchies and decision-making structures—to clarify the division of labor and formalization of activities (e.g., standard operating procedures). In addition, they may result from inappropriate consideration of strategy, technology and information processing needs, and internal as well as external context (Gulati et al., 2012). The basic assumption is that if shared understanding of activity sequences, decision making authority, and task responsibilities is not clearly established and then enforced, interdependencies and uncertainties will increase and more likely cause operational failures—especially in fast-response

environments (Faraj & Xiao, 2006).

In contrast, institutional failures stem from di<sup>fi</sup>culties in merging cultures and lack of standards in informal behaviors based on industry-, profession-, or organization-speci<sup>fi</sup>c norms, values, meanings, and rituals (Bechky, 2003; DiMaggio, 1997; Tilcsik, 2010). Such failures can lead to breakdowns in interdependent routines and activities (Gulati et al., 2012). For example, lack of understanding of culture of a partnering organization can in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals' perspectives on interdependencies (Steensma, Marino, Weaver, & Dickson, 2000) and their interpretation of the level and impact of uncertainties (Daft & Weick, 1984). Similarly, lack of shared perspectives can a<sup>f</sup>ect coordination across organizational boundaries as there may not be a basis for performance monitoring (e.g., metrics and measuring systems), implicitly agreed-on meanings (e.g., medical jargon), and mutually accepted values (e.g., importance of reciprocity, information sharing, and constructive feedback) (Chwe, 2013).

Finally, adaptability failures re<sup>fl</sup>ect shortcomings in individual partners' ability to change prescribed routines to address ad-hoc task issues (e.g., assignments and goal selection), dynamic constraints (e.g., scheduling and synchronization of activities), knowledge transfer, and information usability (Malone & Crowston, 1994). They also re<sup>fl</sup>ect limitations in managers' ability to function as boundary crossing facilitators, to interact with partners to reduce interdependencies and uncertainties, and to respond appropriately (Follett, 1949; Pauleen & Yoong, 2001). In e<sup>f</sup>ect, adaptability failures make it di<sup>fi</sup>cult for individuals and teams to deviate from plans, prescribed routines, or formalized hierarchies as emergent situations may require. This is especially critical in the ED as it is impossible to plan for every medical emergency.

The current coordination research identi<sup>fi</sup>es various resolution mechanisms that organizations can leverage to resolve coordination risks. For example, Gulati et al. (2012) suggest that e<sup>f</sup>ective coordination relies on mutual adjustment among those enacting interdependent routines. Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) suggest that (e<sup>f</sup>ective) coordination results from the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of exchanges between areas of work through roles, rules, and structures, Gittell (2002) emphasize the role of boundary spanners and liaisons to overcome coordination failures, whereas Thomas and Trevino (1993) examine the opportunities for boundary-spanning actors to share experiences and practices, thereby improving inter-organizational coordination. We draw upon these and other notable coordination research (e.g., Kumar & van Dissel, 1996) to develop three types of resolution mechanisms: roles and skills, information technology, and routines and improvisations (Table 2). These mechanisms can help coordinate collective work, especially as previously independent organizations become interdependent (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).

Roles (i.e., expected set of behaviors typically associated with speci<sup>fi</sup>c jobs) can provide an agreement on who is responsible for what tasks, how such roles are monitored, and how relationships between roles are established (Biddle, 2013; Ilgen & Hollenbeck, 1991). By mobilizing action between partners, roles can support coordination of shared tasks (Henderson, 1998). This is important in contexts where individual roles and responsibilities span organizational boundaries, especially when tasks, authority structures, or decision making processes di<sup>f</sup>er between organizations (Gulati et al., 2012; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). However, individuals holding the same job may enact their roles differently. leading to different outcomes (Biddle. 2013: Graen. 1976: Ilgen & Hollenbeck 1991). The outcome variation results from, among other things, di<sup>f</sup>erences in skills of the involved actors. Skills represent the set of abilities of individual actors required to perform a particular task, and include both behavioral and technological abilities (Green, 1989; Vitalari, 1985). In dynamic and complex environments, such as in hospital EDs, skills are important considerations when matching individuals to role assignments (Graen, 1976). In most EDs, physicians and nurses have specialized skills and to treat patients with variety of emergency medical conditions, they must coordinate e<sup>f</sup>ectively within the hospital and with external partners, such as emergency medical services (EMS), specialists, and tertiary hospitals.

Information technology can facilitate coordination within and between <sup>fi</sup>rms by making information processing less costly, by enabling rich communication, and by facilitating decentralized decision-making (Argyres, 1999; Meier & Sprague, 1991). In particular, IT can improve coordination by establishing standardized rules for exchanging complex information among actors (Argyres, 1999; Melville, Kraemer, & Gurbaxani, 2004) and by o<sup>f</sup>ering a medium through which a common understanding around how people interact and align their work can develop (Kumar & van Dissel. 1996: Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009: Wade & Hulland., 2004). For example, EMRs a<sup>f</sup>ord sharing of standardized descriptions of drug interactions for patients across organizational boundaries, thereby improving decision making related to medication prescription and dispensing. As such, advanced IT may uncover new opportunities for competitive advantage in asymmetric partnerships and other strategic alliances by improving inter-organizational coordination capabilities (Argyres, 1999).

Finally, routines, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as repeated patterns of behavior that are bound by rules and customs (Feldman, 2000, p. 611), establish understanding of who will complete which task and when, and de<sup>fi</sup>ne how transitions between roles will occur. Routines have also been described as e<sup>f</sup>ortful accomplishments in which participants enact prede<sup>fi</sup>ned patterns of actions selected from a repertoire of possibilities (Pentland & Rueter, 1994). Whether by following rules and customs or by selecting from a repertoire of possibilities, routines facilitate how organizations accomplish work and improve the stability of inter-organizational processes (Gulati et al., 2012; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). Importantly, while routines assume a predictable environment and establish a baseline of activities that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the norm or average approach, they must allow necessary <sup>fl</sup>exibility to address contingencies resulting from temporal practices (Constantinides & Barrett, 2012). Therefore, actors cannot rely solely on repeated patterns of behavior but must incorporate improvisations into routine planning (Constantinides & Barrett, 2012; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). Improvisations include individual and collective choices during routine execution that address coordination issues (e.g., Becker, 2004; Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Pentland & Rueter, 1994). Together, routines and improvisations allow organizations to respond to day-to-day events and adapt to changing conditions over time.

## 4. Research design

We applied the integrated collaboration risk management framework to analyze 32 hub-spoke dyads across two statewide U.S telestroke networks with hubs at the Medical College of Georgia (MCG) in Augusta and at the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) in Charleston, respectively. We selected the networks based on purposive sampling (Miles & Huberman, 1994) since both networks were organized in a hub-spoke con<sup>fi</sup>guration and used the same technology (a stroke-speci<sup>fi</sup>c telemedicine application called REACH—remote evaluation for acute ischemic stroke) for telestroke collaborations. A prior research relationship with the neurologists at the two networks helped us gain access to rich data in both networks. We followed the case study approach (Yin, 2003) to gain an in-depth understanding of how the spoke hospital in each dyad used telestroke and other IT applications to collaborate with the hub hospital for emergency stroke care. We collected cumulative quantitative data related to telestroke con sultations from all the spokes as well as qualitative data from eight selected spokes (four in each network). Our qualitative data analysis approach (Miles & Huberman, 1994) provided in-depth understanding of the stroke care process and rich description of how each dyad perceived and managed collaboration risks. Considering that we relied on retrospective identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of dyadic practices and arrangements, we triangulated across respondents, investigators, data collection methods, and continually validated our <sup>fi</sup>ndings through respondent feedback (Denzin, 1970; Singh et al., 2015) to improve the validity and credibility of the study (Creswell & Miller, 2000).

The <sup>fi</sup>rst and third author conducted <sup>fi</sup>eld visits between March and August 2012 to gather historical information about how each hub developed its telestroke network and collaborated with spokes. To get a closer look at telestroke activities at the spokes, we visited four spokes in each network (two spokes with high telestroke usage and two spokes with low telestroke usage, based on average REACH consultations/year). During these visits, we conducted semi-structured interviews with ED physicians, nurses, neurologists, hospital administrators, and EMS representatives. We asked open-ended questions about typical interactions with the telestroke system, and the history and status of its implementation. To understand how the system evolved over time, we interviewed the chief executive o<sup>fi</sup>cer, chief technology o<sup>fi</sup>cer, and other personnel at REACH Health Inc., a for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t company set up in 2005 by the MCG neurologists. In fall 2014, we conducted follow-up email conversations and phone interviews with two neurologists (one each at MUSC and MCG) to determine how strategies, tactics, and operations had evolved. In all, we conducted 52 in-person and telephone interviews with 40 respondents, with each interview lasting between 30 and 60 min. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author also had multiple email interactions (most recently in spring 2017) with hub neurologists for clari<sup>fi</sup>cations. The last column in Appendix B shows telestroke usage data from all 32 spokes. Our secondary data sources include presentations, technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, stroke protocols, and published papers, as summarized in Table 3. The second author joined later and added independent analytical capability to the research team.

To analyze the data, we followed recommended procedures for qualitative research (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994), using related literature to guide our coding process. Initially, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author reviewed all case data, including interview transcripts, observations, meeting notes, and other material (Table 3) to identify key events and phrases in the case evidence that suggested collaboration risks perceived by the hub and spokes. For other steps of the data analysis, all three authors were actively involved. Drawing on the collaboration risk management framework (Table 2), we categorized the risks further as cooperation and coordination risks. We also included evidence related to drivers of cooperation risk (i.e., contribution, appropriation, and alignment failures) as well as coordination risk (i.e., structural, institutional, and adaptability failures). This coding served as a basis for discussion to re<sup>fi</sup>ne our understanding of the concepts and agree upon a common understanding of the case. Finally, we coded case evidence that pointed to resolution of the various collaboration risks. The coding process was iterative, as initial codes were re<sup>fi</sup>ned and merged. We continued re<sup>fi</sup>ning the codes until all three researchers reached agreement. Although our data analysis progressed primarily from empirical toward conceptual analyses, there were repeated interactions: the empirical material led to re<sup>fi</sup>nement of the conceptual categories and the iterative re<sup>fl</sup>ections of these categories prompted further analysis of the data. Tables 5 and 6 summarize the analysis results.

## 5. Investigative context

Stroke—or “brain attack”—is a leading cause of death and serious, long-term disability in the U.S. and around the world. In this study, we examine a particular type of stroke, ischemic (i.e., non-hemorrhagic) stroke, which constitutes about 87% of all stroke cases and results from a blood clot that forms inside one of the arteries supplying blood to the brain (Go et al., 2014). The introduction of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) as a treatment in mid-1990s has helped reduce disability in patients su<sup>f</sup>ering from ischemic stroke. Still, the treatment of ischemic stroke presents several challenges. The main challenge is the time-to-treatment after onset of symptoms. According to recommendations of the American Stroke Association, the time elapsed from a patient arrival at the ED to intravenous tPA treatment (“drip”) should be less than 60 min (Saver et al., 2015). Each minute a stroke goes untreated and blood <sup>fl</sup>ow to the brain continues to be blocked, a person loses about 1.9 million neurons, which can lead to impairment of a person's speech, movement, memory, and other functions (Saver, 2006). As such, patients must receive tPA within 4.5 h after onset of symptoms, beyond which the treatment bene<sup>fi</sup>t diminishes rapidly (Switzer, Levine, & Hess, 2009). Emergency stroke treatment is further complicated as it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate ischemic stroke from other neurological conditions with similar symptoms and to determine which patients would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from tPA (after considering factors such as age and prior stroke history). It is vitally important to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate ischemic from hemorrhagic strokes because administering tPA in latter cases could be fatal (as tPA is a potent blood thinner). To provide timely stroke diagnosis and treatment, some academic medical centers (hubs) have begun using telestroke to remotely assess patients arriving in EDs of small and rural hospitals (spokes) that lack neurological expertise (Levine & Gorman, 1999). The resulting information exchange between the hub and the spokes reduces temporal and geographic barriers to emergency stroke care, thereby improving outcomes (Switzer et al., 2009)

In our study, each spoke linked to the hub via the REACH system and none of the spokes connected to each other. Both networks had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant asymmetries between partners in each hub-spoke dyad in terms of size, neurological resources, coordination capacity, strategic focus, and technological capability (Table 4). Both hubs had tertiary stroke center certi<sup>fi</sup>cation, signifying an advanced leve of neurological capabilities, while some spokes had primary stroke center certi<sup>fi</sup>cation, signifying they had necessary facilities (e.g., stroke unit), services (e.g., CT scans), personnel (e.g., stroke teams), and practices (e.g., stroke protocols). Further, the MUSC hub and some spokes had a stroke coordinator position to standardize processes for stroke care; to collect, analyze, and use telestroke performance data to improve care delivery; and, to become a stroke champion in the community. While the MCG and MUSC hubs focused on creating a statewide telestroke network, the spokes focused on their ED operations and on delivering stroke care in their communities. In terms of technological capability, four neurologists at MCG (assisted by a software developer) developed the <sup>fi</sup>rst REACH prototype in 2000. Later, they developed additional functionalities, such as two-way video streaming. In 2006, one of the founding MCG neurologists joined MUSC and established a telestroke network in South Carolina based on REACH. The technolog capability at the spokes was limited as they relied on the two hubs (and later on REACH Health Inc.) for support.

The REACH system consisted of a mobile, internet-ready cart (Fig. 1) that a nurse could wheel into an ED room at the spoke. The cart had an on-board computer, a high-resolution monitor, a phone, and a mounted remotely adjustable video camera. The supporting software included a web-based interface to view and share CT scans and other patient-related information stored within the hospital's EMR and laboratory information system. A stroke specialist at the hub evaluated patients using real-time visual assessment through two-way video conferencing. Further details about the design and operation of REACH system are published elsewhere (Cho et al., 2007; Cho et al., 2008; Singh et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2003).

The emergency stroke care process is activated when a stroke event occurs (i.e., a patient with stroke symptoms is brought to the hospital ED). An ED nurse conducts triage to determine whether to initiate a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed protocol (“stroke code”) or whether the patient should receive non-stroke care. If the triage indicates stroke, the nurse may use a paging system to contact a local neurologist (if available) or initiate a REACH consultation by contacting the hub's Emergency Communication Center, where an operator connects with an on-call stroke specialist. In the latter case, the stroke specialist at the hub logs onto the REACH website to “view” the patient virtually and consults with the spoke ED physician. After evaluating the patient's CT scans, lab results, and general condition, the specialist determines whether to recommend tPA administration and transmits the decision using the REACH system. Appendix A provides more details about the telestroke process, including information exchanged at each stage and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT involved. A key characteristic of emergency stroke care is that the ED sta<sup>f</sup> must be ready to implement the stroke code at a few minutes' notice, while a patient with stroke symptoms may arrive hours, days, or even weeks apart. The short notice and sporadic arrival of stroke patients makes coordination within the spoke and with the hub very challenging. Further, the telestroke process not only requires a high leve of hub-spoke coordination, but also pre-existing cooperative arrangements that allow the spokes to access the hub's resources.

## 6. Findings

Drawing on the integrated collaboration risk management framework (Table 2), we <sup>fi</sup>rst o<sup>f</sup>er an account of the cooperation risks that impacted emergency stroke care delivery and the corresponding resolution mechanisms implemented by the hubs and spokes. Next, we describe the coordination risks and the corresponding resolution mechanisms. Tables 5 and 6 summarize the management of cooperation and coordination risks respectively.

## 6.1. Cooperation risk management

## 6.1.1. Contribution risks

When the Georgia telestroke network was established, the MCG hub o<sup>f</sup>ered free REACH carts to the early adopting spokes (e.g., MCG-1 through MCG-9, Appendix B) and provided free on-demand consultations with a stroke specialist. The hub also subsidized spoke participation by paying for the monthly maintenance fees. The hub's rationale was that increased acceptance of telestroke would allow it to provide stroke services to a wider population and to focus on tertiary stroke care while assisting spokes in the initial tPA decisions. The spokes bene<sup>fi</sup>tted from the hub's investment in telestroke as it allowed them to provide emergency stroke care. As a result, however, the MCG hub faced an ongoing contribution risk, because nearly half of the spokes made no <sup>fi</sup>nancial contributions and therefore showed little commitment to sustain the partnership. To resolve this risk, the MCG hub adopted a business contract that included speci<sup>fi</sup>c hub and spoke obligations as well as payment requirements by the spokes for the services provided to them. As such, most spokes that joined the MCG network after January 2008 received no subsidies and had to pay a per-diem fee for telestroke coverage and a monthly maintenance fee to REACH Health Inc. Drawing on this, the MUSC hub never o<sup>f</sup>ered subsidies to spokes for participation but established formal processes from the start of the telestroke program to monitor and reinforce the contractual agreement with the spokes. In contrast, the MCG hub never formalized a process to monitor spoke contracts, which may explain some spokes' low commitment to telestroke, as suggested by their low usage of the REACH system (e.g., MCG-2, MCG-7, and MCG-9, Appendix B).

## 6.1.2. Appropriation risks

Following telestroke consultation, if recommended by the neurologist, the spokes administered tPA ( drip ) to the patient and subsequently arranged patient transfer (“ship”) to the hub for further care. This arrangement—called “drip-and-ship”—allowed the spokes to provide emergency stroke care and the hub to become the preferred regional destination of patients requiring post-tPA care and neurosurgical intervention. However, the arrangement mainly served the <sup>fi</sup>nancial interests of the hub and resulted in loss of revenue for the spokes (since, in the current fee-for-service model, hospitals receive more reimbursement if they provide more services to patients). This was especially the case if a spoke lacked the ability to provide advanced stroke care and rehabilitation. As a result, some spokes faced an appropriation risk as they failed to get satisfactory <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>t from their investments in telestroke. This risk threatened to jeopardize the hub-spoke collaboration. In fact, dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the inequitable value appropriation from the partnership, one spoke (MUSC-4) left the telestroke network altogether in November 2010. To resolve this risk, some spokes (e.g.. MCG-10, MCG-13, MCG-16, MUSC-1, MUSC-2, and MUSC-3 in Appendix B) hired local neurologists so they could provide local stroke assessment to patients in the ED. In addition, some spokes (e.g., MCG-11, MCG-13, MCG-14, MUSC-7, and MUSC-8 in Appendix B) established a neurological intensive care unit (neuro-ICU). Assisted by local neurologists, these spokes were then able to keep patients at the hospital for post-tPA observation (“drip-and-keep” arrangement), while transferring patients requiring neurosurgical intervention to the hub. These resource investments allowed the spokes to receive additional reimbursement for the medical services provided, thereby mitigating inequitable value appropriation from the partnership.

## 6.1.3. Alignment risks

As spoke ambition to enhance stroke capabilities increased over time, it introduced competition between the hub and the spoke. This competition had the potential to jeopardize the collaboration. As an example, MCG-14 (Appendix B) invested in local neurological capabilities, received primary stroke center certification, and eventually became a sub-hub that offered advanced stroke care to nearby spokes. We later found that another spoke that joined the MCG network in 2013 (and therefore not included in this study) also developed as a sub-hub. By 2014, two spokes in the MUSC network (MUSC-7 and MUSC-8) that had achieved primary stroke center certi<sup>fi</sup>cation also became sub-hubs. The sub-hubs began promoting themselves in the local community as a reliable alternative to the hub. These initiatives could create an alignment risk, in which some spokes begin to compete with the hub for the same patients (since the spokes could now offer telestroke consultation services similar to those previously offered by the hub to nearby smaller hospital). To resolve this risk, the sub-hubs and the respective hub made deliberate e<sup>f</sup>orts to maintain their relationship so they could provide emergency stroke care to the underserved community. The sub-hubs continued to send patients requiring neurosurgical interventions to the hubs, while the hub neurologists shared their stroke expertise and treatment innovations through training and frequent visits to the sub-hubs.

In another example of alignment risk, the increasing stroke capabilities of drip-and-keep spokes (e.g., MCG10, MCG11, MCG-13, MUSC-4, MUSC-7, MUSC-8, and MUSC-9 in Appendix B) could lead to reduced hub revenue (since these spokes could now provide post-tPA care to patients who would previously have been transferred to the hub hospital). This could potentially develop competition between the hub and spokes. To resolve this risk, in 2014, the MUSC hub implemented a “drip-ship-ship” arrangement (also called “boomerang” system), in which the hub transferred patients whose condition had stabilized and who were therefore not candidates for neurosurgical intervention back to spokes for continued care. This arrangement eased the <sup>fi</sup>nancial concerns of the drip-and-keep spokes as they could now receive additional reimbursement for providing post-tPA care. The hub also bene<sup>fi</sup>tted from this arrangement as it could focus on providing tertiary stroke care while continuing the spoke partnerships.

## 6.2. Coordination risk management

While the resolution of cooperation risks helped maintain the relationships, they did not fully address the collaboration challenges, especially those associated with day-to-day execution of telestroke-enabled emergency stroke care at the spoke ED. We describe these coordination risks and corresponding resolutions in the following.

## 6.2.1. Structural risks

As most spokes had limited IT capabilities (particularly during the early years of the telestroke program), they depended on the hub to resolve even minor problems during telestroke consultations. These problems included installation issues, poor quality of audio or video, and lag in transfer of CT scans from the spoke ED to the hub over low bandwidth internet connections. The resolution and follow-up of such technical problems were inconsistent, causing coordination failures (such as delays) during consultations. Lacking a formal arrangement—such as a dedicated entity to address IT-related issues—the spokes faced a structural risk, which could threaten the collaboration. To resolve this risk, in 2005, the MCG neurologists created REACH Health Inc. to provide $2 4 \times 7$ technology support during and after implementation of the telestroke system at spokes. When the MUSC hub established its telestroke network in 2008, it also engaged REACH Health Inc. for technology support to its spokes. Over time, REACH Health Inc. reduced spoke dependence on the hubs for IT support.

In another example of structural risk, the hub's lack of reliable or updated information about spoke telestroke activities reduced the hub's ability to address emergent problems during consultations, such as long wait times to connect to on-call neurologists. As there was no formal arrangement such as a dedicated person or role to help address such issues the resulting structural risk threatened the hub-spoke collaboration. To resolve this risk, the MUSC hub created a telestroke coordinator role (Table 3) that became the primary point of contact for the spokes. The coordinator identi<sup>fi</sup>ed issues that a<sup>f</sup>ected coordination with spoke ED, managed emergent issues, developed spoke performance measures, provided spoke-speci<sup>fi</sup>c recommendations, and facilitated a systematic annual review of the dyad's telestroke performance. Although the MCG hub recognized the need for such a role, its senior management did not provide the needed resources. As a result, many MCG spokes continued to face coordination failures and showed less commitment to telestroke, suggested by their low usage of the REACH system (Appendix B).

Moreover, since most spokes were small hospitals, they lacked appropriate decision-making structures to help coordinate emergency stroke-related activities. To resolve this structural risk, several spokes (e.g., MCG-14, MCG-17, MUSC-3, MUSC-7, MUSC-8, and MUSC-9, Appendix B) formed a permanent stroke committee with participation from the stroke coordinator, ED physicians, nurses, neurologists, and radiology sta<sup>f</sup>. The committee helped improve emergency stroke care capability at the spoke, for example, by standardizing and reinforcing stroke delivery processes, reviewing stroke performance, and advocating for stroke education within the hospital and the community. The committee met monthly to discuss any issues and suggest corrective actions. For example, under the guidance of its chief <sup>fi</sup>nancial o<sup>fi</sup>cer (who was a Six Sigma Master Black Belt, and therefore an expert in process improvement e<sup>f</sup>orts), the stroke committee at MCG-17 reviewed each stroke case and discussed any de<sup>fi</sup>ciency, for example, a missed diagnosis or delay in CT scan. In cases requiring further attention, the telestroke coordinator conducted a Root Cause Analysis (a problem-solving technique used for identifying the root causes of faults or problems) to identify reasons for the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed de<sup>fi</sup>ciency during emergency stroke delivery. Another example of the value of the stroke committee relates to MCG-14 where, prior to 2004, the local EMS sta<sup>f</sup> “dreaded bringing stroke patients to the hospital because they were not sure that it could handle emergency stroke cases” and instead took them directly to the MCG hub, even though it was nearly 2 h away. In 2004, the committee invited an EMS representative to become a member. This improved coordination with EMS and, as a result, EMS started to bring stroke patients to MCG-14. In addition, some spokes established a brain attack team (BAT) consisting of ED physicians and nurses, telestroke coordinator, radiology sta<sup>f</sup>, and laboratory technicians—to improve cross-functional coordination. The BAT facilitated proper action whenever a stroke patient arrived at the spoke ED.

## 6.2.2. Institutional risks

Lack of understanding among hub neurologists and spoke ED sta<sup>f</sup> about each other's organization culture, norms, values, meanings, rituals, and expectations could diminish their ability to coordinate interdependent activities e<sup>f</sup>ectively. For instance, some spokes (e.g. MCG-2, MCG-7, and MUSC-5, Appendix B) did not develop or share stroke care best practices across shifts within the spoke, with the hub, or with other spokes in the network. In another example, at MCG-8, the ED physicians—who were mostly locum tenens, i.e., they were on short-term contracts through a physician-sta<sup>fi</sup>ng <sup>fi</sup>rm were reluctant to interact with the hub neurologists for telestroke consultations In fact, the entire ED staff at MCG-8 showed a detached approach toward emergency stroke care in general, and using telestroke in particular. “Currently, all we do is triage,” was the general response. As a result, despite telestroke availability, the spoke ED sta<sup>f</sup> typically sent patients with stroke symptoms directly to the hub hospital that was located about 60 miles away. Unsurprisingly, the EMS sta<sup>f</sup> also began to transport such patients directly to the hub or to another hospital, bypassing the spoke hospital. These spokes had no mechanisms to develop shared norms and values (e.g., information sharing and constructive feedback about speci<sup>fi</sup>c cases), healthy rituals (e.g., “when in doubt, call REACH”), agreed-on perspectives (e.g., how to handle transient ischemic attack, which can mimic stroke-like symptoms) and mutual expectations (e.g., giving stroke cases top priority in the ED since they are very time-sensitive). As such, these spokes faced an institutional risk, which could threaten the hubspoke collaboration (these spokes also likely faced a structural risk as none of them had a telestroke coordinator or a permanent stroke committee (Appendix B)). To address the institutional risk, the hub and spoke telestroke coordinators at some spokes facilitated regular interactions of spoke ED sta<sup>f</sup> and hub neurologists. For example, MCG-17 organized regular visits of its ED sta<sup>f</sup> to the MCG hub, conducted lunch-and-learn sessions with neurologists, and facilitated joint participation in stroke conferences and industry-sponsored workshops. Some spokes (e.g., MCG-14, MUSC-3, MUSC-7, MUSC-8, and MUSC-9, Appendix B) arranged ongoing role-based training for their ED sta<sup>f</sup> (e.g., improving stroke triage skills of nurses) and involved them in decision making via participation in stroke committees. These e<sup>f</sup>orts improved sharing of information and culture across the hub and spoke, and the increased their commitment to telestroke.

## 6.2.3. Adaptability risks

Emergency stroke treatment is time-critical and decision-making in stroke cases is inherently complex. However, unlike the hubs which typically receive patients with neurological issues on a daily basis and are therefore always ready to engage in stroke care, the spoke EDs are general-purpose facilities with weeks or months between emergency stroke incidents. As Appendix B shows, the spokes had between 5.88 (MCG-7) and 123.48 (MUSC-8) average REACH consultations per year. The sporadic arrival of patients with stroke symptoms at spoke EDs treated by a di<sup>f</sup>erent set of ED sta<sup>f</sup> with di<sup>f</sup>erent emergency stroke expertise across shifts, triggered an adaptability risk that could threaten collaboration with the hub. For example, although the ED physicians at MCG-8 could treat most emergency patients, they had little telestroke experience. Moreover, as few stroke patients arrived at the hospital, the ED sta<sup>f</sup> did not use telestroke very often. As a result, they did not have readily available routines to coordinate telestroke activities. To resolve this risk, the telestroke coordinator and stroke committee at some spokes (e,g., MCG-14. MCG-17. MUSC-3. MUSC-7. MUSC-8. and MUSC. 9, Appendix B) developed standard operating procedures to improve stroke care and enhance coordination within spoke ED. For example, prior to arrival of a stroke patient, the EMS sta<sup>f</sup> sent a “stroke alert” (using a two-way radio communication system) to the ED sta<sup>f</sup>, informing about patient status and expected arrival time. Upon receiving the alert, a spoke ED sta<sup>f</sup> initiated a “stroke protocol” to activate the BAT. Some spokes (e.g., MCG-14, MCG-17, and MUSC-7, Appendix B) developed ongoing training programs (including “stroke drills”) to increase stroke readiness. Further, some spokes (e.g., MCG-5 and MCG-17, Appendix B) adopted process improvement initiatives to improve their emergency stroke response. For example, by storing tPA in the ED (instead of bringing it from the spoke pharmacy after a treatment decision has been made), MCG-5 reduced tPA wait-time from up to 1 h to less than 10 min.

## 7. Discussion

Drawing from research on IT-enabled partnerships (Gallivan & Depledge, 2003: Loebbecke, van Fenema, & Powell, 2016: Mohtashami et al., 2006) and collaboration risk management (Das & Teng, 1999; Dyer et al., 2001; Gulati et al., 2012; Kale & Singh, 2009; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009), we developed an integrated framework for collaboration risk management (Table 2) which included cooperation and coordination risk drivers and resolution mechanisms. We used this framework to examine how hospitals in hub-spoke dyads in two telestroke networks perceived and acted upon cooperation and coordination risks (Tables 5 and 6). Based on our case evidence, we o<sup>f</sup>er important theoretical insights into how organizations manage collaboration risks in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships. First, the partners in the observed asymmetric relationships perceived and approached cooperation and coordination risks di<sup>f</sup>erently, thus challenging the assumption that partnership risks are similar for each partner. Second, as the partners assessed and managed both cooperation and coordination risks on an ongoing basis, these activities resulted in shifting and quite diverse collaboration interactions over time depending on how each partner managed risks based on needs and resource endowments. Finally, as a contribution to practice, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest how organizations may manage sustainable IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships in general, and telestroke networks in particular

## 7.1. Implications for theory

The relational perspective literature (e.g., Dyer & Singh, 1998) assumes that motivated and able partners will “form a cooperative bond and be willing and able to co-create value through thoughtful use of IT (Grover & Kohli, 2012, pg. 231), without explicitly considering whether the partners perceive collaboration risks differently. Other studies also assume that partners face similar collaboration challenges and therefore adopt similar management approaches (Gallivan & Depledge, 2003; Loebbecke et al., 2016; Mohtashami et al., 2006). However, our empirical analysis of inter-organizational collaboration in the two telestroke networks shows significant variation in how the partners perceived cooperation and coordination risks. Related to coopergtion, our findings show that while the hubs primarily faced contribution risks, the spokes primarily faced appropriation risks. and the hub-spoke dvads faced ongoing alignment risks (Table 5). Related to coordination, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that both the hubs and spokes faced structural and institutional risks, whereas the spokes primarily faced adaptability risks (Table 6). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also indicate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variation in how the observed hub-spoke dyads addressed these risks. For example, MUSC-4 (which faced an appropriation risk as it was unable to exploit satisfactory value from its partnership with the hub) left the telestroke network altogether. In contrast, other spokes (e.g., MCG-11, MCG-13, MCG-14, MUSC-7, and MUSC-8 in Appendix B) responded to the same risk by hiring local neurologists and establishing a neuro-ICU that helped them to keep patients at the hospital for post-tPA observation (i.e., drip-and-keep arrangement), allowing them to retain the related reimbursements. MCG-14. MUSC-7 and MUSC-8 developed into a sub-hub and started providing telestroke consultations to nearby small hospitals. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are supported by the resource dependency literature that argues organizations are constrained and a<sup>f</sup>ected by their environments and that they attempt to manage resource dependencies (Pfe<sup>f</sup>er & Salancik, 2003) and that shifts in dependencies result in variations in risk and control strategies (Hillman, Withers, & Collins, 2009).

In 2000, MCG-14 engaged a local neurologist group to provide on-call stroke consultations at the ED. This arrangement worked very well for some time, and MCG-14 even achieved a primary stroke center certi<sup>fi</sup>cation in 2004. However, in early 2009, the neurology group broke up after retirement of two neurologists (who mentioned that they were worn out by frequent after-hour calls, such as during nights and weekends) and departure of a third neurologist. This turn of events jeopardized continuation of MCG-14's stroke operations. With support from a new CEO, the two neurologists (who had retired a few months earlier) set up a telestroke-enabled stroke center in fall 2009 and provided davtime coverage while MCG hub provided on-call neurology support during after-hours. Once again, this arrangement worked well until the MCG-14 leadership perceived an appropriation risk as it failed to get satisfactory <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>t from investments in telestroke (Table 5, #2). To address this risk, MCG-14 established a neuro-ICU, which a<sup>f</sup>orded it drip-and-keep capability (Table 5, #2b), allowing it to mitigate inequitable value appropriation from the partnership. However, as MCG-14 continued to develop its telestroke capabilities and eventually became a sub-hub, an alignment risk occurred in its relationship with MCG hub due to potential competition (Table 5. #3) which threatened the dissolution of their long-standing relationship. To address this risk, MCG-14 and MCG hub made deliberate e<sup>f</sup>orts to maintain the cooperative relationship. MCG-14 reformulated the appropriation of value from the collaboration through transfer of more specialized work to the hub. Accordingly, MCG-14 directed patients requiring neurosurgical interventions to the MCG hub, while providing REACH consultations to its own spokes as a sub-hub (Table 5, #3a). Further, this example suggests that the two collaboration facets—cooperation and coordination—do not operate separately or sequentially (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rst cooperation is settled and then coordination risks come into play). Instead, there is an ongoing interplay within each facet and across the two facets, depending on the resolution mechanisms triggered or employed.

Asymmetric partnerships, such as hub-spoke networks, are particularly susceptible as the smaller organizations depend on the larger organization for access to resources and capabilities. Therefore, partners in such relationships must continually assess collaboration risks and approach risk management depending on needs and resource endowments.

Our understanding of how collaboration interactions shift in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships may be elaborated further by relating to the alliance life cycle model proposed by Gulati (1998) and Gulati et al. (2012). This model suggests that any strategic partnership undergoes three stages: partner selection focused on the criteria by which organizations select their alliance partners; alliance design focused on negotiating the governance arrangements for the alliance; and post-formation dynamics focused on managing the alliance over time. Although Gulati et al. (2012) discuss the roles of cooperation and coordination during these three distinct alliance stages, the model does not explain how collaboration interactions may evolve depending on needs and resource endowments and how partners may engage in context appreciation and collaboration risk resolution to maintain sustainable partnerships, especially those that are IT-based and asymmetric in nature (Kumar & van Dissel, 1996).

Hence, to capture how collaboration interactions and risk management approaches evolved over time in the two observed telestroke networks, we combine the alliance life cycle model (Gulati, 1998; Gulati et al., 2012) and the hub-spoke collaboration interactions shown in Fig. 2. The resulting Fig. 3 captures the observed process from prior to telestroke through establishment and development of telestroke across the two networks. The right-pointing arrow indicates collaboration goal attainment (i.e., continuation of telestroke program between the hub and the rural hospital as a <sup>fi</sup>nancially sustainable service)—which, ultimately, was the case for most of the dyads that we studied. In one case (indicated by downward-pointing arrow), the telestroke-based colla boration between the hub and the spoke ended. Overall, the journey toward collaboration goal attainment was dissimilar for the various dyads, representing how the collaboration interactions emerged over time. Accordingly, some spokes continued being drip and-ship spokes, in which they would transfer stroke patients to hub after tPA administration. This collaboration pattern primarily involved hub-driven cooperation (represented by A in Fig. 2). Other spokes acquired local neurological capabilities and kept stroke patients for continued observation after treatment with tPA, thus becoming drip-and-keep spokes. This collaboration pattern primarily involved hub-driven cooperation and spoke-driven coordination (represented by A and D in Fig. 2). Still other spokes attained a drip-ship-ship status, whereby the hub transferred patients who did not require neurosurgical intervention back to the spokes. This collaboration pattern primarily involved hub-driven cooperation and hub-driven coordination (represented by A and C in Fig. 2). Finally, some spokes that had acquired advanced neurological capabilities developed as sub-hubs and began o<sup>f</sup>ering telestroke consultations to nearby small hospitals. This collaboration pattern primarily involved spoke-driven cooperation and spoke-driven coordination (represented by B and D in Fig. 2).

Fig. 3 also shows how collaboration patterns emerged during di<sup>f</sup>erent alliance stages. Thus, the drip-and-ship collaboration pattern was in evidence during the early stages of the alliance (i.e., partner selection and alliance design), while the drip-and-keep, drip-ship-ship, and sub-hub collaboration patterns were in evidence during the post-formation stage. Hence, most of the collaboration patterns emerged during the post-formation dynamics stage as the partners' needs and resource endowments—as well as their collaboration risk perception and related resolution mechanisms—changed over time. In this way, our study extends the alliance life cycle model (Gulati, 1998; Gulati et al., 2012) by explaining how unique collaboration patterns may develop based on how the involved partners adopt speci<sup>fi</sup>c resolution mechanisms to address various collaboration risks

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that asymmetries between partners can change over time. For example, some spokes (MCG-14, MUSC-7, and MUSC-8) developed neurological capability and started using the REACH technology to become a sub-hub. As such, the resource and capability asymmetry between the sub-hubs and their respective hubs reduced. This finding has implication for small and often resource constrained organizations in asymmetric partnerships, as they seek to manage their resource dependencies (Pfe<sup>f</sup>er & Salancik, 2003) as well as shifts in such dependencies (Hillman et al., 2009).

## 7.2. Implications for practice

Prior research has shown that IT can e<sup>f</sup>ectively enable inter-organizational tasks by improving information processing; allowing decentralized decision-making; facilitating rich communication; establishing standardized rules for exchanging complex information; and o<sup>f</sup>ering a medium to develop a common understanding (Argyres, 1999; Kumar & van Dissel, 1996; Melville et al., 2004; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Research has also shown that cooperative partnerships increasingly rely on interorganizational IT solutions (e.g., Kohli & Grover, 2008; Robey et al., 2008) to access a wide range of valuable resources, including assets and capabilities (Clemons & Row, 1992; Dyer & Singh, 1998; Gulati et al., 2009; Saraf et al., 2007). As a caution, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show how collaboration failures may occur in asymmetric partnerships despite the availability of reliable and comprehensive inter organizational IT. For example, at some spokes (e.g., MCG-7, MCG-8, MUSC-5, Appendix B), although the telestroke system was readily available and provided requisite support, the REACH cart often remained “sitting in a corner” as the spoke ED sta<sup>f</sup> failed to coordinate stroke consultations with the hub. Hence, when inter-organizational IT is available but not fully integrated into day-to-day delivery processes, it may contribute to collaboration failures, such as unresolved questions about responsibilities for execution of an IT-enabled process and doubts regarding which procedures to apply. Further, IT availability may not automatically resolve collaboration risks associated with accountability (i.e., clarifying who is responsible for what tasks), predictability (i.e., enabling interdependent parties to anticipate subsequent task related activity), and a common understanding (i.e., providing a shared perspective) among the partners (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that organizational practices play a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the overall success of IT-enabled relationships than the IT con<sup>fi</sup>guration itself. Thus, while the IT may provide connectivity and useful features, the collaboration may fail if the partners do not adopt (and periodically readdress and revise) complementary practices. This insight is particularly important as the implementation of inter-organizational IT con<sup>fi</sup>guration is only a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in a relationship that will eventually evolve based on how the partners perceive and address the collaboration risks they necessarily face over time (cf., Fig. 3).

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for decision makers in hub and spoke hospitals. Moving beyond advanced information and communication technologies to support creation and delivery of IT-based services (such as telestroke consultations), decision makers must rely on a portfolio of mechanisms—such as contractual speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, resource investments, partnership governance, roles and skills, and routines and improvisations to develop and sustain the collaboration. Table 6 reveals that most coordination risk resolution mechanisms primarily highlight organization development and learning, with few instances of IT management or development. The resolution mechanisms in Table 5 also suggest a limited role, if any, of IT in cooperation risk management. While these <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be because of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of our case, they suggest that although IT can be a powerful enabler of interorganizational information sharing and process integration in collaborative relationships (Ciborra & Andreu, 2001; Kohli & Grover, 2008; Robey et al., 2008), collaboration risks and sustainability of hub-spoke relationships go well beyond technology. To manage ongoing collaboration risks, hospitals should therefore focus on work practices and complementary organizational interventions.

We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that mitigating and resolving contribution failures in IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships requires more than contracts, resource investments, appropriation of value, and periodic renegotiation. Each organization must also contribute to establishing procedures for execution of inter-organizational transactions to establish ways of continuously improving and learning from process execution and outcomes and to mutually adjust both regular activities and the structure of the partnership. For instance, MCG-17 continuously improved stroke care processes, including coordination within the spoke and with the hub, and MCG-14 developed as a sub-hub to nearby spokes. These approaches required continuous commitment and e<sup>f</sup>ort, in addition to relying on the established relationship and renegotiating conditions when incentives became misaligned or relational commitments shifted (Gulati et al., 2012, pg. 13). Thus, our study emphasizes continuous assessment and management of cooperation as well as coordination risks to develop sustainable partnerships. In doing so, it is important to engage in context appreciation (Johns, 2006) to reveal the speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs of individual partners and the resources and opportunities they may have available to help sustain such partnerships (Kumar & van Dissel, 1996).

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also reveal insights into how the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of hub-spoke relationships (Table 1) a<sup>f</sup>ects their sustainability. In contrast to dyads and fully connected networks, a hub-spoke con<sup>fi</sup>guration limits the ability of a spoke to share knowledge with other spokes, especially if the hub does not exploit its central position to enhance the mobility of knowledge between dyads. Knowledge mobility refers to the “ease with which knowledge is shared, acquired, and deployed within the network” (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006) and it includes both formal and informal knowledge held by workers (D'Mello & Sahay, 2007). Lack of network position exploitation to improve knowledge mobility across spokes can contribute to large variations in collaboration e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness within the network. Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings emphasize the need for hub-spoke networks to pay particular attention to network-wide knowledge mobility. A willingness to invest in additional IT infrastructure (Cho & Mathiassen, 2007; Yaraghi et al., 2014) to facilitate such knowledge mobility (rather than only focusing on IT investments that enable dyadic transactions) may help IT-enabled asymmetric partnerships become more sustainable.

Finally, our study provides speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidance to academic and rural hospitals on how they can build sustainable telestroke and other telemedicine-enabled relationships to provide emergency care and other health services. While prior health services literature has examined issues related to e<sup>f</sup>ective telestroke delivery in hub-spoke networks (Demaerschalk et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2014), no study has examined collaboration risks in such networks. As illustrated in Tables 5 and 6, both hub and spoke hospitals must assess and manage collaboration risks continually—especially during the post-formation dynamics stage—to sustain an e<sup>f</sup>ective partnership.

## 8. Conclusion

This study o<sup>f</sup>ers theoretical and practical insights into collaborative risk management in the context of IT-enabled asymmetric dyads operating within hub-spoke networks. Through development and use of an integrated collaboration risk management framework and in-depth empirical analyses, we have extended IS theory beyond consideration of cooperation and coordination risks as distinct levels of activity to contribute an understanding of how IT-enabled asymmetric partners comprehensively manage evolving collaboration risks.

We purposively selected two networks in two di<sup>f</sup>erent states as it allowed us to conduct cross-case comparisons and account for institutional variations (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Yin, 2003). However, the two networks were also similar in some ways: they had comparable size and complexity, they had similar ED expertise, they provided similar telestroke-enabled neurological services, and they had a shared history. These similarities may diminish the institutional variations to some extent, but our two-network study presented a unique opportunity to examine cooperation and coordination risks in asymmetric partners in di<sup>f</sup>erent institutional contexts and how the di<sup>f</sup>erences in management of these risks a<sup>f</sup>ected collaboration interactions over time. Further, our use of a qualitative case study approach (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Yin, 2003) can raise questions of generalizability to other contexts. We addressed this concern by adopting a multiple case study approach to examine 32 hub-spoke dyadic partnerships across two networks. We also provided a rich description of the case context and a detailed account of various hub-spoke interactions, paying particular attention to intra- and inter-organizational dynamics and multiple stakeholder perspectives (Mason, 2002). Finally, we analyzed events spanning nearly 15 years, which can introduce recall bias and multiple interpretations of events. To minimize this e<sup>f</sup>ect, we interviewed multiple stakeholders; triangulated among di<sup>f</sup>erent data sources; used multiple methods and investigators to interpret the data; and iteratively sought key stakeholders feedback to validate our interpretations (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 267; Yin, 2003).

We encourage future studies to apply our research to new contexts to replicate, re<sup>fi</sup>ne, or extend our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Evaluation of networks in which spokes share information and patients (or customers) with each other in a more fully connected network can provide additional insights into how variations in network con<sup>fi</sup>gurations may result in di<sup>f</sup>erent collaboration risks and management patterns. We also encourage future research to incorporate quantitative approaches to further substantiate and develop our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, particularly concerning the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of IT-enabled expertise sharing on outcomes such as sta<sup>f</sup> and customer satisfaction and the <sup>fi</sup>nancial health of the involved organizations. Moreover, future studies could expand the scope to include impacts to hospitals, both within and outside of such networks, and also consider that telestroke consultations may be moving away from EDs and into ambulances that can provide more immediate and timely decision making capabilities (e.g., Lippman et al., 2016).
