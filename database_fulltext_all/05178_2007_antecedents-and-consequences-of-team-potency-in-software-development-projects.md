# Antecedents and consequences of team potency in software development projects

Ali E. Akgu¨n <sup>a,\*</sup>, Halit Keskin <sup>a</sup>, John Byrne <sup>b</sup>, Salih Z. Imamoglu

<sup>a</sup> Science and Technology Studies, Gebze Institute of Technology, Turkey <sup>b</sup> Lubin School of Business, Pace University, USA

Received 17 March 2006; received in revised form 27 January 2007; accepted 13 August 2007 Available online 24 September 2007

## Abstract

Developing new software quickly, successfully, and at low cost is critical in organizations. Ways of assessing the effectiveness of development teams has highlighted measures of factors, such as teamwork, group cohesiveness, and team integration, but the use of group potency theory (the collective belief of a group that it can be effective) is rare. In our study, we investigated antecedents of and consequences to group potency in software development project teams. By examining 53 software development project teams collected from small and medium-sized software firms in Turkey, we found, that team potency positively affected speed-to-market, development cost, and market success of the product. We also found that trust among project team members, past experiences of the members, and team empowerment had a positive impact on the team potency during the project. Managerial and theoretica implications are discussed.

Keywords: Group potency; Team potency; Project management; Software development; Product development

## 1. Introduction

The successful management of projects is critical. However, many software development projects fail to function as intended or fulfill their potential. According to the Standish Group International [25], about 15% never deliver a final product, costing \$67 billion per year. More than 80% are excessively late and over budget [35], and there is a 30% chance of a software project becoming a ‘‘runaway.’’ Therefore, it is crucial to understand what is important in order to complete software development projects on time, within budget, and to meet customer requirements.

One way to improve the chance of a project’s success is by effective management of the project team processes (teamwork, cohesiveness, team learning, and communication, etc.) [43]. Thamhain and Wilemon [40] pointed out that software development includes both technical and teamwork issues. DeMarco and Lister [11] called the skills and interactions of software development team members ‘‘peopleware’’ and argued that this aspect of development is the most critical and least understood. However, ‘‘group potency’’ has yet to be investigated in software development project teams.

The concept of group potency emerged as a key determinant in understanding group effectiveness and performance in the behavior literature [37]. Group potency is the collective belief of a team that it can be effective: the shared belief of team members as a whole. Since, (a) software development is a complex sociotechnical activity, and (b) its tasks are often exacerbated by incomplete user requirements and changing environmental demands, it is clear that the strength of the project team lies in the members’ sense of collective efficiency in solving project problems through a unified effort. To date, however, we have limited empirical evidence suggesting that team potency and project success are related in software development project teams. Even though the role of a team member’s belief in his or her ability to accomplish goals (writing programs) is documented [22], the role of group members’ perceptions is less understood.

Therefore, our goal was to investigate the impact of team potency on software development project success. Also, we wished to study, from a managerial point of view, the factors that influence group potency in the teams.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Group potency and software development teams

Group potency extends Bandura’s work on selfefficacy (an individual’s belief in his or her ability to perform a task; it is situation specific and/or domain sensitive [17]). It contributes significantly to an individual’s motivation and performance: the higher a person’s self-efficacy, the more likely he or she is to engage and persist in task-related behavior [7].

Even though group potency is rooted in self-efficacy, they are theoretically distinct constructs and have different implications for group performance. Lindsley et al. [32] described group potency as a collective belief that the group can accomplish its tasks successfully, indicating homogeneously shared beliefs of members. In this sense, it is a prospective, rather than a retrospective, judgment of group capability. However, the effect of group or team potency on project teams is missing in both the technology and innovation management literature.

In a team that has high-level group potency, team members believe that they can design, code, and develop a software product, and effectively produce, market, and sell it. A key function of team potency is to mobilize and maintain team efforts to help achieve the project’s goals and optimize team performance. Specifically team potency:

\- functions as a means of self-regulation by influencing the amount of effort that the team spends on a task and how long it persists when confronted with obstacles or failure. Adler et al. [1] pointed out that, software development processes are typically immature, chaotic, and unpredictable, thus team members must share ownership and work cooperatively to reduce risk;

\- provides the foundation for team motivation as members’ actions are based on what they believe rather than what is true. Hertel et al. [23] noted that when team members believed they were unable to accomplish their task, their motivation dropped even though the goals were highly valued and the contribution was perceived as necessary for success;

\- influences team functioning. When making a decision to undertake an action during the project, the team should be concerned about the members’ confidence in their abilities to use their ability to perform project related activities. They act and risk trying a new method, approach, or behavior, because the members’ thought patterns, emotions, and actions are influenced by their collective belief of their effectiveness [14].

Thus team potency is important in the software development project process. However, we have limited knowledge of its antecedents and consequences. To address this deficiency, we used the team effectiveness models used in the group behavior literature, generally based on the input – process – output concept of data processing. The theoretical model of our study deals with inputs (antecedents), team potency (process), and outcomes. We used the interpersonal trust, past experiences, goal clarity, and empowerment measures of Guzzo et al. [21] and the project performance outcomes were speedto-market, cost, and market success.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Antecedents

Researchers have noted the importance of trust in group potency development. Kirkman and Rosen [28] pointed out that team members need confidence and trust in their team members to experience potency. Bandura [6] indicated that trust resulting from perceived shared understanding boosted team potency.

The influence of trust on software development is also well known. Linberg [30], in a case study of software developers in industry, found that high trust among team members influenced the process positively. However, there is little research on the effect of trust on developing team potency in project teams. We posited that interpersonal trust would help a team to develop a belief of efficacy, and increase team potency. Specifically, because a project team consists of people with complementary skills and are mutually accountable for success, a high level of interdependence is required for the team to function effectively. Trust is also essential when building a collaborative relationship, whereas mistrust is a major source of conflict. Mutual interpersonal trust is a mechanism for (a) enabling people to work together more effectively, (b) providing team members to expect that their efforts will be reciprocated and not exploited by others, and (c) facilitating a high level of commitment to the relationship among the people [3]. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

H1. Trust among project team members is positively related to development of team potency in software development project teams.

The group behavior literature notes that the experience of group members is another factor that is closely related to development of group potency [26]. Writers noted that experiences involving knowledge and skills improve group potency because prior performance strengthens collective beliefs of ability [42].

The past project experience of the people also influence the collective belief on efficacy in software development teams. The project management literature indicates that effective sharing of past experiences among team members is an important factor that often sheds light on team processes. For instance, experienced teams exhibit greater coordination and teamwork, and facilitate interpersonal relations. In particular, the past experience of team members: (a) expands a team’s capacity for effective communication by ensuring that members have similar understanding of technology, task execution, team interaction, and member knowledge, skills and abilities, and (b) improves the possibilities to share tacit knowledge. What we see is that the project team becomes assured of its capabilities through a blending an interweaving of the team members knowledge and it aids in managing setbacks and failures without being adversely affected. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

H2. Past experiences of project team members is positively related to development of team potency in software development project teams.

Another factor that influences the development of group potency is the goal clarity of the group. For instance, Werner and Lester [41], studying the 107 student case teams, found a significant correlation between task goal clarity and group potency (r = .75, p < .0001).

Goal clarity, which refers to the precision and detail of what the team is trying to accomplish, is also imperative for project teams in general and software development projects in particular. There is considerable evidence that group goals have a positive impact on team performance [29]. Goal clarity also impacts the collective beliefs on the efficacy of the group. Specifically, since knowledge sharing in software development is goal-driven, a clear set of goals helps teams to determine the level of knowledge as well as the degree of knowledge sharing about customers, technologies and tools. Also, when a team has a clear goal, all team members understand why the team exists. It leads to more effective interactions among team members, reduces the effect of functional goals, permits the team to structure its activities [18], and stimulates action and behaviors of team members. As a result, the project team develops a sense of confidence in that it can cope with the demands confronting it, allowing individuals to work together in a climate of goodwil where there is mutual support, conflicts are open to resolution, and a holistic understanding of the task is developed. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H3. Goal clarity of project team members is positively related to development of team potency in software development project teams.

The last factor is team empowerment, which in-lies that the team has the authority and power to direct, manage, and lead itself [36]. The group behavior literature indicates that the concept of group potency is built upon empowerment. For instance, it was pointed out that the collective self-concept of group members, the perception that the team as a whole is effective, is an important characteristic of empowered teams.

As a motivational approach, team empowerment facilitates psychological enabling, by reducing or eliminating the feelings of lack of power of team members, thereby increasing the team members’ sense of control, ego involvement, and identification [39]. Higher level of empowerment informs the team that management has confidence in their ability. This has a positive effect on the team members’ potency judgment, and they will perceive themselves as more capable. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

H4. Empowerment of project team members is positively related to development of team potency in software development project teams.

## 3.2. Consequences

Little and Madigan [33], investigating the effect of group potency on the performance of eight selfmanaged work teams, found a positive relationship between group potency and performance. Spink [38] found that volleyball teams with high group potency had higher performance than teams with lower potency. Campion et al. [8] tested 19 group characteristics and found that potency was one of the strongest predictors of effectiveness criteria. The Meta-analysis by Gully et al. [20] of 67 studies found that group potency was positively related to performance at the group level (r = 0.41). Moreover, they found that the group potency–performance relationship was stronger when the tasks were interdependent. They suggested that when the task and context encouraged coordination, communication, and cooperation among members, group potency was related more strongly to performance. However, even though software development projects require high interdependencies among team members, the team potency–performance linkage has not yet been empirically tested. To address this, we selected the normal performance measures (speed-tomarket, cost, and product success).

We posited that teams that had high group potency completed projects faster than teams that have less. Members’ beliefs on the effectiveness of the team stimulates team members to (1) synthesize, analyze, and disseminate new knowledge and information quickly, (2) embrace a mutual understanding of the software development process rapidly, (3) make decisions about project plans and alternatives faster, and (4) solve software related problems in a timely manner. Also, high team potency lessens the feeling of time pressure evoked by the competitors and management. Gevers et al. [15] found that teams low in potency tended to experience greater levels of perceived time pressure and reacted more negatively to time pressure.

We also propose that teams that have confidence in their abilities and capabilities tend to develop the software at less cost than the teams that have low potency. The rational is that team members’ sense of collective belief that they can solve project related problems and improve the software development process through concerted efforts aid them to use resources judiciously and more efficiently, expedite the design and development efforts, and reduce rework due to errors.

Finally, the crucial goal of forming a project team and developing new software is the success of that product in the market place. This is noted, when a team has confidence in its capabilities, as such, it is reasonable to envisage that a team will detect and correct software related problems accurately and quickly. Therefore, it was hypothesized that:

H5. Team potency is positively related to (a) speed-tomarket, (b) less development cost, and (c) market success of the software.

## 4. Research design

## 4.1. Measures and sampling

To test the hypotheses, multi-item scales adopted from earlier studies from English language sources: each construct was measured using five-point Likert scales ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘strongly agree’ (5). However, the team size was assessed using a ratio scale.

We adopted the parallel-translation method in developing the Turkish language questionnaire; items were first translated from English into Turkish by one person and then retranslated back into English by a second person. The two translators then jointly reconciled all differences. The suitability of the Turkish version of the questionnaires was then pre-tested by four part-time graduate students working in industry; all were involved in at least one software development project. After refining the questionnaire, based on interviews with them, the questionnaires were distributed to the responders and collected by one of the authors. The questionnaire items are available in Appendix A. In addition, team size (number of persons) was the control variable, because both team potency and project performance are influenced by it.

The initial sample consisted of 100 small and medium-sized software development firms located in Istanbul; they all had affiliations with European and American firms. First, the firms’ managers were contacted by telephone and the aim of our study was explained. Of the 100 firms contacted, 75 agreed to work with us. We asked for at least two knowledgeable respondents for each project (to avoid single-source bias), to fill out our surveys. After selecting the respondents, we told each that his or her responses would remain anonymous – not be linked to them individually, their companies, or the product. Also, we assured respondents that there were no right or wrong answers. Further, we developed a cover story to make it appear that the measurement of the predictor variable was not connected with or related to the measures of the criterion variable. These procedures reduced the participant’s evaluation apprehension and made them less likely to edit their responses to be more socially desirable, lenient, and consistent with how they thought the researchers wanted them to respond.

Of the 75 firms that agreed to participate, 53 completed our questionnaires, returning 170 surveys (each firm representing one software development project team). Thus, usable data for our analysis were the 53 software development projects with an average of three respondents from each. Table 1 gives the characteristics of our sample.

## 4.2. Measure validity and reliability

After data collection, the measures were subjected to a purification process to assess their reliability, unidimensionality, discriminant validity, nomological validity, and convergent validity [4]. An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the 34 measured items of eight variables, using a principle component with a varimax rotation and an eigenvalue of one as the cut-off point (since team size was assessed with one item, this variable was excluded from the exploratory factor analysis). A single factor was extracted for each multiple-item reflective scale in this analysis. The items, and their factor loadings after exploratory factor analysis, eigenvalue, and percentage of variance explained, are shown in the Appendix A. After performing this, which is a useful technique for scale construction, a subsequent confirmatory analysis was conducted to assess the scales.

A series of two-factor models was generated, as recommended by Bagozzi et al. [5]. In these, the factor correlations, considered individually, were restricted to unity and the fit of the restricted models was compared with that of the original model. The chi-square change $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } )$ in all models, constrained and unconstrained, was significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , confirming that the constructs demonstrated discriminant validity.

The measures were subjected to further confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using AMOS 4.0. All eight factors were included in one model. For the CFA analysis, subscales or parcels were used, as recommended by Drasgow and Kanfer [12] (aggregating or taking the mean of several items that purportedly measure the same construct as indicators of a latent variable) instead of individual items. Goodness-of-fit measures are affected when the number of items used to identify a small number of factors is relatively large. Consistent with this approach, two sub-scores or parcels were created for each scale, each consisting of a randomly divided subset of the items in the scale. The CFA produced a good fit with an incremental fit index (IFI) of 0.93 and a comparative fit index (CFI) of 0.93 (also, $\chi _ { ( 1 2 5 ) } ^ { 2 } = 2 7 7$ , RMSEA = 0.08).

We evaluated the nomological validity of the measures used by linking eight constructs to market success of software, using a maximum likelihood (ML) structural equation model of AMOS 4.0. During the analysis we used the ‘‘all items’’ for constructs. Overall, the model produced an acceptable fit indices $( \chi _ { ( 4 6 8 ) } ^ { 2 } = 1 0 0 7$ , CFI = 0.88, IFI = 0.87, RMSEA = 0.07), providing an adequate model fit.

Table 2 shows the correlation among all eight variables. The relatively low to moderate correlations provide further evidence of discriminant validity. Also, all reliability estimates, including coefficient alphas, average variance extracted for each construct, and AMOS-based composite reliabilities, are well beyond the threshold levels suggested by Fornell and Larcker [13]. In addition, the squared correlations (ranging from 0.0004 to 0.47) did not exceed the average variance extracted (ranging from 0.51 to 0.82) further suggesting discriminant validity. It was therefore concluded that measures were unidimensional and had adequate reliability, and discriminant validity. Further, skewness ranged from 0.72 to 0.54 and kurtosis ranged from 0.58 to 1.31. These results indicated that the variables were well below the level requiring transformation of variables, e.g., skewness of 2 and kurtosis of 5 as indicated by Ghiselli et al. [16].

## 4.3. Analysis and results

Before performing our analysis, we aggregated the composite scores of each question item, as our unit of analysis was the team. We calculated the mean of the question scores for each project in our sample. The inter-rater agreement on team level measures had to be demonstrated: we used the Spearman–Brown test of the interclass correlation (ICC) to examine the reliability of the aggregated measures. The results showed that ICC for all measures ranged from 0.70 to 0.92, well above the 0.60 benchmark [24], indicating a satisfactory level of inter-rater agreement for each aggregate measure. As a result, our sample for data analyses had all 53 cases.

As our study introduced a new model to examine the antecedents and consequences of team potency and the sample size was relatively small, we felt that the partial least squares (PLS) technique of SEM would be most suitable for testing our hypotheses. We used PLS Graph 3.0 and the bootstrapping resampling method to test the statistical significance [9]. This procedure entailed generating 100 sub-samples of cases randomly selected, with replacement, from the original data.

Path coefficients were then generated for each subsample. T-Statistics were calculated for all coefficients, based on their stability across the subsamples, indicating which links were statistically significant. The model loading of each question item is shown in Fig. 1 whose results indicate that intrateam trust, team experience, and team empowerment have a positive association with team potency, thus supporting H1, H2, and H4. Specifically, when team members are very trustworthy and friendly, i.e., they are able to rely on each other and consider one another’s feelings, the team has a better opportunity to develop team potency. Also, if there is a critical mass of experienced people who previously developed and launched similar products, then they have important information that helps the team build confidence in its ability.

We found that when the team had the authority to make most of the decisions that impacted the project, it developed a collective sense of confidence. Interestingly, we could not find any statistical association between goal clarity and team potency, and H3 was not supported. Finally, our results indicate that intrateam trust, team experience, team empowerment, and goal clarity explained 62% of the variance $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 2 )$ in the team potency.

The results also indicated that team potency has a positive association with speed-to-market, low software development cost, and market success, thus supporting, H5a, H5b, and H5c

Finally, in the model, team potency explained 23% of the variance in speed-to-market, 15% of the variance in development cost, and 38% of variance in market success of the software.

## 5. Discussion and implications

Because project teams involve multi-mental models – due to the different knowledge, expertise, background, and personalities in the organization – collective belief of a team’s capabilities and efficacy is critical for effective team and project management. Our results showed that group potency was important for fast software development and lead to less costly development of software and its market success. The study also elicited the critical success factors of software development. The managers we interviewed helped us put this issue in perspective, as shown in Appendix B.

Our study also showed how to develop team potency. We empirically, found that team empowerment, intrateam trust, and team experience were important factors in increasing team potency (see Appendix C). Further, our study highlighted the competencies of the project teams: even teams that appeared to have equal skills, abilities, and resources, had different team potency as each team had a different belief about its ability.

Managers should enhance the team potency during the project and should:

\- provide rewards and motivation to enhance relations among the team; these should not only involve financial incentives but include recognition, encouragement, etc.

\- establish a psychologically safe environment in which the exchange of knowledge and skills occurs in an environment that is seen as safe and convivial;

\- know the capabilities of their team members. In particualr, they should assign responsibilities based on the each member’s knowledge, skill, and experience;

\- select the core team members based on their experience, and their familiarity with and trust of one another;

\- help team members to analyze and understand the goal and procedures from the start of the project. Teams should be controlled and team members should feel and know that they have responsibility to the manager as well as the company.

## 6. Limitations

There are some methodological limitations—notably, the use of self-reporting in a retrospective study. Our research is thus prone to common method bias since the same respondents answered both the dependent and independent variables in a cross-sectional manner. We checked this potential problem with the Harman one-factor test. An unrotated factor analysis of eight focal variables resulted in an eight-factor solution, as expected, which accounted for 72% of the total variance; and Factor 1 accounted for 32% of the variance. Because a single factor did not emerge and Factor 1 did not explain most of the variance, common method bias is unlikely to be a concern. In addition, following Lindel and Whitney [31], we partialed out the smallest correlation of the remaining correlations in order to remove the effect of common method bias. Given that all unadjusted correlation coefficients remained statistically significant at $p < 0 . 0 5$ after adjusting for common method bias, even under the strictest conditions applied in our sensitivity analysis, we felt confident that the findings were not affected by common method bias.

Utilizing a cross-sectional design with questionnaires was also a limitation of our study. Using only a questionnaire to collect data may not provide objective results. However, our research provided some evidence of associations.

The relatively small sample size is another limitation. Small sample size may cause instability of a study’s findings. A larger sample size would allow the use of confirmatory structural equation modeling, e.g., with LISREL model. Of course, the sampling of small and medium-sized software development firms may also reduce the power to generalize the findings.

In addition due to the nature of the sample, generalizability is another limitation. The study was conducted in a specific national context, Turkish firms and this may not be generalized to different cultural contexts.

This study assumed that team potency is useful and important for project activities. However, team potency may not always be good, especially if the team believes too emphatically in its capabilities.

## 7. Conclusion

We empirically investigated antecedents and consequences of group potency in software development project teams. The results showed that group potency is associated positively with speed-to-market, reduced development cost, and market success. We also demonstrated that group potency can be developed by fostering intrateam trust among team members, providing empowerment to team members, and selecting experienced team members.

## Appendix B. Managers’ comments on the team potency and project outcomes

‘‘If there was low team potency [the team was not yet sure or confident] during the project, project leaders or managers had to handle the most of the burdens of the project’s activities and to direct the team members and their actions. However, when the team had a strong belief of its capabilities, all team members knew what they have to do and will do. In this way, team members were able to ‘‘see the bigger picture’’ and final destination of the project. They worked eagerly to reach that final end. Accordingly, the project team finished the project quickly.’

‘‘The role of each team member was clearly identified and understood in teams which have high beliefs in themselves on the team efficacy. Everybody knew that what he/she was supposed to do. Since you knew what the person A, B, C had to do, and they knew what you had to do, all understood each other and what they must do—one head [mind] automatically developed during the project. Then you grasp the all project stages and processes. And team started to act and do the project activities in a parallel way, simultaneously, rather than linearly. Accordingly, they were able to complete the project in a short time with less cost.’’

‘‘We had a high confidence on our capabilities. We as a team believed in our efficacy. During the project we came across with a bottleneck. We could solve that issue with a simple method. However, we thought that that solution would cause greater problems later in the project. Thus, we needed a way to solve that problem. We then found a way to solve the problem fundamentally. We even thought we knew that way would cause the delay of the project, due to our confidence on ourselves, we took the action. Interestingly, we finished the project faster than what we thought before.’’

‘‘What I saw from my experience is that, when the team had low team potency, team members had anxiety and stress during the project. They were working very hard to finish the project with fewer defects. However, by the end of the project they were exhausted mentally and some physically. But, when the team had a high group potency, team members were acting with less stress, because they were knowing how the handle the complexity of the project and how to reduce the delivery time. They also were able to predict what they will encounter with during the project.’’

## Appendix C. Developing and experiencing team potency

Team members mentioned that:

‘‘When you got empowerment during the project, you feel that you are the chosen person—our special abilities were seen as better than the others. It is really important for a motivation. As a team you know that all your hard work is appreciated with that empowerment.’’

‘‘The empowerment led us to think that our managers gave trust to us. Thus we had high morale to work. Especially, during the hard times we tried our best to complete our tasks. When we heard from other companies how management used its power to force the people in the teams, we started to think that we were lucky. And, our beliefs and attitudes toward our manager, to us and group on the efficacy automatically was increased.’’

‘‘I knew personally my co-workers in the project, because we worked together in different projects before. They were knowledgeable and skillful persons. I knew that I was able to get any information from them whenever I needed. Thus there was a trust among us. Especially, that trust created one belief for all of us that we can do the project whatever it is, because due to that trust we were able to share and communicate our knowledge in the team easily.’’

‘‘Group potency comes from the experience and knowledge of the team members in our industry. If you are a software developer, you have to know the projects’ processes, are able to write codes, and be a good researcher and developer. Off course all these knowledge and skills comes from experiences.’’

‘‘We knew that similar project teams were working on the similar software projects that we were doing from other firms. We were better than others and were able to finish projects faster than them. The reason was not only the skills and knowledge, but our beliefs on our efficiency. We wanted to do this project to increase our reputation and to satisfy our self-esteem. Also during the project there was not a standard working hours, but this does not mean we were totally unorganized, we had plan and procedures. We were also working with joy. While we were working on the project, we were eating pizza and drinking Coke. We were acting outside of normal structure of the organization. We also did not have to wear suits. We thought that we were different from other workers of the firm. And all these increased our beliefs on our capabilities.’’
