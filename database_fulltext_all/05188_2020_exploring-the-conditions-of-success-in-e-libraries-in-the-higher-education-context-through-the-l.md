# Exploring the conditions of success in e-libraries in the higher education context through the lens of the social learning theory

Cláudia Pinho<sup>a</sup>, Mário Franco<sup>b,⁎</sup>, Luis Mendes<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> CEFAGE-UBI Research Center, Estrada do Sineiro, 6200-209, Covilhã, Portugal

<sup>b</sup> University of Beira Interior, Department of Management and Economics, CEFAGE-UBI Research Center, Estrada do Sineiro, 6200-209, Covilhã, Portugal

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Costs E-library Higher education Institutions (HEIs) Information technology (IT) Social learning theory (SLT) Training

## A B S T R A C T

The main aim of this study is to identify and understand the conditions of success in the implementation, use and maintenance of electronic libraries (e-libraries) in the academic context of higher education, through the lens of the Social Learning Theory (SLT). To achieve this goal, a qualitative approach was adopted, through a case study of the e-library of a University in Portugal. The data were obtained from direct observation, interviews with various actors (sta<sup>f</sup> of the library, computing and administration services of this institution) involved in the process of implementing and maintaining the e-library, and also the documentary analysis. The empirical evidence obtained reveals the most relevant factors for e-library success to be: (1) minimization of costs, (2) acceptance and use of e-libraries, and (3) sta<sup>f</sup> training. This study shows the great importance of library sta<sup>f</sup> training regarding use and exploitation of the e-library’s functions. Thorough knowledge of the e-library means improved use and search e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. The minimization of costs associated with its implementation and maintenance is also a factor determining its success. Given the limited number of empirical studies exploring the topic, this study is particularly important and innovative in the context of Higher Education Institutions (HEI). On the basis of the empirical evidence obtained, a framework is proposed, grouping and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the most important factors for the success of e-libraries in the HEI context.

## 1. Introduction

Information Technology (IT) has the potential to trigger the socio economic progress of developed societies. The existence of functional telecommunications infrastructure can allow improvements in the area of education, and easier access to knowledge can lead to substantia improvements in teaching [1,2].

New ITs have brought about a tremendous change in all aspects of our lives. In this sense, it is hard to imagine living without mobile phones, tablets, PCs and other smart devices, which can help us to access needed information in our daily lives [3,4]. Thanks to rapid technological development, Higher Education Institutions (HEIs), by implementing IT and Information Systems (ISs), have made e<sup>f</sup>orts to provide their stakeholders with fast access to pertinent information [5,6].

Digital transformation presents new ways of living an educational experience at the HEIs [7,8]. The technological change in the digital era led to an innovative impact on HEIs, based on educational contents and forms [8]. With the digital era, HEIs: i) consider learners’ needs as a new starting point, ii) reconsider curricular contents, teaching methods, operation process through new ITs, and iii) provide precise and customized educational products and services [4,9].

Following this idea, ISs and electronic libraries (e-libraries) give users incomparable opportunities to access a massive amount of sci enti<sup>fi</sup>c information in the digital format [3]. This tool can give users access to improved services when compared with traditional libraries [6,10]. According to Umukoro and Tiamiyu [3], e-libraries provide electronic services, highlighted among them are e-references, e-jour nals, bibliographic catalogs, etc., to the academic community through IT, such as the Internet, the World Wide Web (WWW), mobile devices and others. Although e-libraries bene<sup>fi</sup>t the academic community, these are complex systems that consume institutional resources and capacities [6]. E-libraries have come to allow access to a massive amount of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c information in the digital format, which if not <sup>fi</sup>ltered can create a great deal of noise and lead to chaotic searching and use [3,11].

Use and acceptance of the e-library, according to Social Learning Theory (SLT) [12,13] can be explained not only by the personality of each user, but also by factors related to the external environment. It is, therefore, necessary to identify and understand the critical factors leading to successful implementation, use and maintenance of e-li braries.

This situation calls for the creation of user-friendly interfaces, adjusted to users’ needs [11]. In the online context, through the use of several resources, learners can explore di<sup>f</sup>erent ways of knowing, and use resources that might better match their learning styles, goals or preferences [14]. Nevertheless, learners should be motivated to learn and it does not matter how e<sup>f</sup>ective online materials are because if learners are not motivated, they will not learn [15,16]. Consequently, HEIs must reconsider their strategy of implementing and supporting use of the e-library, by service providers, to motivate learners how to use this tool in an e<sup>f</sup>ective way.

The possible need for library sta<sup>f</sup> training can help to optimize students’ use of the e-library [17,18]. However, the aspect of cost minimization can a<sup>f</sup>ect the success of this tool [6,19]. Howsoever, according to SLT, the visualization of successful outcomes by HEIs may in<sup>fl</sup>uence other organizations to copy that behavior.

In this context of introducing new IT, the e-library service providers are faced with the challenge of implementation, use and maintenance of this technology. Consequently, setting out from SLT, it is necessary to understand the problems and issues of IT involvement in “traditional libraries” and transformation of these entities into e-libraries, to be able to formulate strategies leading to their successes [5,6,20].

To clarify matters relating to traditional libraries becoming e-li braries, the main objective of this study lies in providing a wide-ranging vision of the factors of successful e-libraries in the HEI context, from the point of view of e-library service providers and according to the main principles of SLT.

This article is structured as follows: The second section presents a theoretical framework supporting the implementation, use and maintenance of e-libraries, based on SLT. The following section justi<sup>fi</sup>es the methodology used, describing the case study and the data-collecting instrument. Finally, the results are presented and discussed, together with the conclusions and implications, and suggesting future lines of research.

## 2. Literature review

Enabling IT has led to the transformation of library services, like card catalogs, printed books, and/or bibliographic instructions to new types of services and delivery modes including, but not limited to, electronic collections, such as e-books, e-journals and databases, virtual reference services and other online resources [21]. In fact, trends in modern libraries now need to go through innovative behaviors in such an online and web environment. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally during the last decade, many libraries, particularly those serving academic communities, have experienced the emergence of new service paradigms in areas of information access and delivery, reference, instruction, technology facility, and support to readers [22,7,23].

## 2.1. Social learning theory

Learning theories assign into the main classes, behaviorism, cogni tivism, humanism, and constructivism [24]. According to behaviorism, learners respond only to environment stimulation. On the other hand, cognitivism defends active learning as a way of engaging learners in the learning process [25]. According to Crittenden [26], constructivism posits self-learning processes, where learners should understand and solve problems by themselves, and the Constructivist Theory of Learning encourages learners to participate in social activities for suc cessful learning.

The SLT explains human behavior in terms of continuous reciprocal interaction between cognitive, behavioral, and environmental in<sup>fl</sup>uences [13,27]. This theory has become one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories of learning and has often been called a bridge between behaviorist learning theories and cognitive learning theories because it encompasses attention, memory, and motivation [28].

SLT posits that learning is most e<sup>f</sup>ective when learners are allowed to observe and interact with other learners and to participate in small study groups [13,24]. According to Bandura [13], some of the main authors in the social learning context see [15,27,29,30], report several conditions as necessary for an e<sup>f</sup>ective learning, namely attention, re tention, reproduction, and motivation.

Indeed, several factors may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the attention paid by learners during the learning process, such as personality traits or past reinforcement. Since early times, the notion of personality has undergone signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes. Personality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as complex, as are all its intimately related components [12,13,31]. Personality represents individuals’ interaction with their environment, and we cannot speak of a personality, internal to the individual, which is independent of the environment. To understand behavior, it is necessary to consider both the individual and the environment [32,33].

To learn, individuals need to be paying attention. Anything that detracts an individual’s attention is going to have a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on observational learning [34]. If the behavior to be copied is interesting or brings some novelty, people are far more likely to dedicate their full attention to learning [13,15,35].

Retention refers to the process of recollecting what learners paid attention to and includes symbolic coding, mental images, cognitive organization, symbolic and motor rehearsal [13,24]. Information that comes from observation will be coded and stored in the learner’s brain in the form of images and verbal constructions [27]. Individuals who are able to transform observed activity into verbal codes or images can learn or retain information much better than those who just observe [24,35].

The reproduction process concerns with physical and mental ability to copy the behavior observed [36]. Individuals are able to reproduce previously learned information (e.g., behavior, skills, or knowledge) when required. Nevertheless, practice through mental and physical rehearsal often improves individuals’ responses [35].

Motivation is also referred to as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant condition for an ef fective learning process. Reinforcement and punishment play an important role in motivation [13,37,38]. Motivation could be intrinsic or driven from within the learner (e.g., interest, enjoyment) and extrinsic (e.g., rewards) [37]. If individuals see positive consequences from a particular type of behavior, they are more likely to repeat that behavior themselves; however, concerning negative consequences, they are less likely to perform such behavior [38,39]. Motivation concerns our observation of someone else being rewarded or punished for something they have done or said. This situation usually motivates people to do, or do not, the same thing [37]. However, in online contexts, learners should be motivated to learn, and it does not matter how e<sup>f</sup>ective the online materials are. Learners will learn only if they are motivated to [38].

## 2.2. Digital transformation in academic libraries

In a comprehensive way, digital transformation refers to a process in which organizations make use of technology to improve performance and to ensure better results. This process includes a structural change in organizations, giving an essential role to technology. Digital transformation is a global process that a<sup>f</sup>ects vision, strategy, people, processes, and technology [40,41].

The impact of technology on services traditionally provided by libraries and the emergence of new services, in the context of access to digital information, led to the creation of e-libraries [23,40]. In this process of digital transformation, academic e-libraries continuously exploit digital technologies to create new sources of value for HEIs and to increase operational agility toward a digital operational excellence [23,42]. Academic e-libraries have the expertise of being early adopters of new technologies to perform activities such as digital curation, preservation, and archiving. Following this idea, academic e-libraries become the core element in HEI’s digital transformation [8,23,43].

Nowadays, academic e-libraries are at the forefront of technological innovations [21,44]. They are developing key information management systems, software applications, such as Learning Management Systems, Current Research Information Systems, Institutional Repository, Data Repository, Digital Archives, Resource Discovery, Virtual Reference, Reference Management Software, among others [44,45].

Libraries are now lending digital devices laptops, and tablets to enable users’ learning. In addition, they have a record of working with system providers to shape their products to meet developed HEIs’ needs [44]. In this sense, e-libraries are being de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the prevalent tech nological innovations and web-based services that they are o<sup>f</sup>ering to all academic sta<sup>f</sup> [3,7].

The di<sup>f</sup>erent types of technology adopted in the digital transfor mation process have changed the services environment provided by traditional libraries. Nowadays, e-libraries provide electronic collections, such as e-books, e-journals, virtual reference services, and other online services [21]. Nevertheless, all these transformations usually imply challenges for academic e-library service environments.

The most common implication reported concerns <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources needed to implement and maintain the huge amount of services provided by academic e-libraries (e.g., [4,21,46]. Costs, above-mentioned, range from the infrastructure needed to support the e-library, to additional costs with hardware and software, including replacements and upgrades whenever necessary [21,47]. Moreover, the exponential development of technologies has led to the need for continuous librarian sta<sup>f</sup> training to meet e-libraries users’ needs. Additionally, costs with the acquisition and creation of electronic resources should be taken into account [3].

## 2.3. Conditions of success in e-libraries

## 2.3.1. Implementation of e-libraries

With the new digital era, several ITs are present in all aspects of society and a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of organizations use WWW, the internet, and other IT as an e<sup>f</sup>ective means to provide services [48]. In addition, the application of IT in processes inside organizations has come to facilitate stakeholders access to, and use of information. Similarly, libraries are changing their concept and have also come to supply products and services in the digital format [3].

Academic libraries have a rich history and heritage and have played an important role in both research and academic communication. They have evolved and developed together with HEIs and are characterized as resilient institutions easily adapted to changes of various kinds, whether social, political or technical [49,50]. At present, e-libraries are faced with an extremely dynamic technological environment. These platforms must have the capacity to give a timely response to increasingly demanding academic users’ needs [48]. From the researcher point of view, e-libraries can be seen as a set of collections in digital format with richer content and are more functional than other information retrieval systems. Compared to traditional libraries, e-libraries have various advantages, highlighted among them are: the ease of accom panying resources stored in digital format, the speed of remote access to information and the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of search techniques [11].

In the evolutionary process of the digital world, academic libraries cannot be considered as simply databases of publications, but rather as resource centers for the learning process [50]. Users’ need for in formation has also diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed and continues to sharply grow. Consequently, libraries are implementing alternative ways of providing services such as e-library platforms [48,50].

However, the exponential growth in the demand for, and use of information has led to the problem of information overload [48,51]. Consequently, users <sup>fi</sup>nd it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to locate the necessary information at the right time, and so there is a growing need for systems that <sup>fi</sup>lter and personalize scienti<sup>fi</sup>c information according to users’ interest. Here, the importance of developing user-centered platforms stands out

[51,52].

According to SLT, the understanding of the acceptance of IT may not depend only on individuals’ personality, but also on the environment in which they use technology [53]. An individual’s personality is not static. If the way people think and the environment changes, behavior will also change. However, the more life experience is built, the greater is the e<sup>f</sup>ort and intervention needed for the change to occur [33]. Rotter [54] conceives individuals from an optimistic point of view, is attracted by their objectives and seeks to maximize their e<sup>f</sup>orts. In addition, according to SLT, often motivation originates from the observation of someone else being rewarded or punished for something done or said [13,27,34].

In HEIs’ context, the user-centered platforms implementation can reduce an individual’s e<sup>f</sup>orts to get e<sup>fi</sup>cient researches and achieve scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge [15,52]. Thus, agents may feel rewarded to use an e-library platform because it provides e<sup>f</sup>ective access to relevant scienti<sup>fi</sup>c information.

For university e-libraries to be able to respond to this concern and provide users with pertinent information, cooperation protocols have been established among various HEIs [10,55,56]. This type of cooperation allows access to information held only by other institutions, through inter-library loans. HEIs have joined forces to provide thei users with information that may not exist on their local database [56]. This exchange of books, articles and theses bene<sup>fi</sup>ts users. Without needing to move physically to other libraries, these agents, and the whole community, can access the e-library online and consult the document in question in digital format [3]. This easy access to digital information has revolutionized teaching, and allows research to be carried out more quickly and comfortably [57,58].

Thus, the cooperation protocols established among HEIs can lead to motivation. The amount of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge o<sup>f</sup>ered to users of elibrary platforms can change the HEI’s behavior [59]. These agents tend to increase the number of protocols by following the behavior of other HEIs who have a large number of inter-library protocols, in line with the SLT principles.

Implementation of the e-library implies <sup>fi</sup>nancial investment in a variety of IT products and services, in sta<sup>f</sup> specialized in its installation and maintenance, and other aspects. However, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of academic e-libraries do not have <sup>fi</sup>nancial autonomy and a limited budget can a<sup>f</sup>ect the right decisions [6]. Indeed, the cost assessment carried out by HEIs’ administration for the acquisition and implementation of the necessary infrastructure for a well-functioning elibrary can be high [60].

Installing this platform implies <sup>fi</sup>nancial investment in hardware, which becomes out-of-date relatively quickly given the rapid development of IT, and software that can be owned or open source [55,6]. It is of note that open-source software, although free, needs to be developed internally, to match users’ needs. Therefore, whatever the type of software chosen to implement in the e-library, this will mean <sup>fi</sup>nancial costs for the institution [19].

The implementation of open-source or enterprise software depends on the visualization of these types of softwares in other HEIs. According to SLT, individuals tend to imitate the behavior of other agents in the same environment [13,36].

The e-contents to be housed in the e-library are possibly the most relevant matter for library sta<sup>f</sup>. Indeed, the cost of subscribing to periodicals consume a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of the budget attributed to the elibrary in the long term [55]. Expenditure related to supporting the hardware, software, and network, on which good functioning of the elibrary depends, is also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and must be reckoned with for im plementation and maintenance of this tool [55,61]. In addition, the cost of library sta<sup>f</sup> training can increase the total cost signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly [17,18].

## 2.4. Use of e-libraries

The <sup>fi</sup>nal users of the services provided by e-libraries may be a restricted community, if access is limited to certi<sup>fi</sup>ed users of the system rather than to internet surfers generally. At least, for certi<sup>fi</sup>ed users it is hoped that the platform will have the capacity to give rapid access to their area of work [19]. Therefore, attention must be paid to matters of a technological nature, because connection to the e-library varies according to the speed and capacity of the device employed by the user [10,62]. In general, e-libraries give users online access to an expressive amount of their resources in digital format. Without needing to physically move to the library, information is provided online and can be accessed from any <sup>fi</sup>xed or mobile device with internet access [3,11].

For correct use of e-libraries, the user interface (UI) must be appealing and functional, as this interface is the <sup>fi</sup>rst contact between the user and the machine [63]. An appealing graphic presentation can create a comfortable virtual environment facilitating the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of functions and help in navigation, making it easier to search more e<sup>fi</sup>ciently [10,17,64]. The ease of sur<sup>fi</sup>ng in the e-library and sorting out relevant from non-relevant information depends on users’ personal experience and can also lead to more e<sup>f</sup>ective searches [17,63].

The SLT explains human behavior in terms of continuous reciprocal interaction between cognitive, behavioral, and environmental in<sup>fl</sup>uence. According to SLT, one of the main principles of social learning is the attention payed during the learning process [16,26,29]. In the case of agents seeing something new or di<sup>f</sup>erent in some way, they will make it the focus of their attention [26,27]. In the e-library context use, the implementation of new features or an appealing graphic presentation may retain the user’s attention [63]. Thus, we can state that the elibrary environment may increase the user’s attention.

Access to <sup>fi</sup>ltered information, which meets users’ needs, may allow greater use of this tool, as access to a massive, disorganized amount of information can produce considerable noise in searches [65]. This situation can lead to discouraging the use of e-libraries, even with the easy access the tool provides [3,11].

The service provided by e-libraries may be little known by a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of academia. As a way to combat this situation, Makori and Mauti [66] mention the need for HEIs to take action to publicize them among the academic community, so that there is greater knowledge and subsequently greater use of this tool. Little knowledge and non-visualization of the use of this tool can lead to greater resistance to use e-libraries. In this context of resistance, SLT considers that the environment in which individuals operate in<sup>fl</sup>uences them in their choices [12,67].

The retention of information is one important principal of the learning process [16,29]. Individuals learn by internalizing information in their memories and recall it if they are required to respond to a situation that is similar to the previous situation from where they have learned the information [39]. Thus, the visualization of others, using the e-library’s potentialities in HEIs, may encourage individuals to learn how to use it.

Support for library users to develop their skills to better explore the resources of these tools is a long-standing concern of many libraries and librarians [50,68]. In the e-library context, library sta<sup>f</sup>’s assistance to users can be one of the conditions to facilitate good use of these plat forms.

The availability of technical support can also lead to greater acceptance of e-libraries. The existence of a helpline and experienced, trained sta<sup>f</sup> can contribute to a more e<sup>f</sup>ective e-library [1,17]. If user training can solve doubts and <sup>fi</sup>ll gaps concerning the e-library’s functioning and lead to these individuals’ more e<sup>fi</sup>cient and e<sup>f</sup>ective use, also librarians, with greater knowledge of the tool, can publicize the functions of e-libraries more e<sup>f</sup>ectively. It is therefore necessary to develop multidisciplinary teams who simultaneously have knowledge of IT and the needs of library sta<sup>f</sup> [1].

According to Bandura [13], environmental factors can predict human behaviors. In the same sense, according to Chen, Wang and Hung [69], the perception of the empowerment process by individuals can lead them to self-learning. In the context of HEIs, the technical support provided by librarian teams can help users in being autonomous in their researches and performing it more e<sup>f</sup>ectively [1,17,50].

Older users who have not accompanied the digital revolution, despite having the potential to develop the skills to allow good human–machine interaction, are rather unfamiliar with IT, namely hardware, software and ISs, particularly regarding e-libraries [17]. To reduce these users’ anxiety and increase their con<sup>fi</sup>dence in using elibraries, there must be well-trained librarian teams to help students use the platform [17,18]. Providing users with manuals or explanatory tutorials about how to use e-libraries can also be of bene<sup>fi</sup>t for searches and thereby improve the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of these platforms [10].

Acceptance of e-libraries can depend on users’ familiarity with this technology. Less successful searches can lead to obtaining undesired information. Here, users must master the key terms of the search [1,11]. Thus, interaction between users and the e-library can lead to acceptance and use of this tool [64]. Furthermore, according to SLT, the environment can lead to changes in behavior, and therefore, to users acceptance of the e-library. Following the evolution of SLT, Bandura [70], one of the most quoted authors in the literature on this subject (e.g., [69,71,72], considers that learning takes place through individuals’ observation and socializing. Here, IT is no exception, and its acceptance also depends on both the environment and on the direct observation of its use [53].

## 2.5. E-library maintenance

Since the 1990s, the exponential development of the internet and digital technology has allowed traditional libraries to be transformed into e-libraries. Paper documents have come to be replaced by publications in an electronic format, namely academic e-journals, e-books, e-theses, e-dissertations, and e-papers [61]. The popularity of the digital environment and the internet has in<sup>fl</sup>uenced practically all aspects of academia, and this has played a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in supporting research.

Today, in the new digital age, the digital format is replacing paper documents, and consequently, e-libraries face the challenge of storing big data [65]. To cope with this situation, it is necessary to implement ISs that can handle a massive amount of information and contribute to spreading knowledge e<sup>f</sup>ectively. It is in this context that HEIs have invested in implementing and maintaining e-libraries [3,66].

Maintenance is a phase of any IS that deals with the process of applying changes to systems over their lifetime, according to the needs of the organization. For this reason, it is essential to ensure the proper functioning of IS [73,74]. The goal of maintenance could be the improvement of the performance of functions, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of existing functions or the addition of new functionalities and the correction of bugs or the reduction of opportunities for system failure or even increase its life [18,75].

E-library maintenance requires investment in resources of various kinds. Among them are the costs of replacing hardware, updating software and those of training specialized sta<sup>f</sup> to maintain these plat forms [19,55]. Although it is necessary in the process of implementing and using e-libraries, training is no less important in the maintenance phase. Generally, in this phase, bugs are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed or additional functions must be installed to make these platforms fully operational [18,76]. In this context, when it is necessary to give a timely response to very speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs, training plays a very relevant role in solving these issues. Here, HEIs are forced to provide the sta<sup>f</sup> with specialized training on this type of platform [19].

Every way of learning involves mental simulation, imitation, and repetition in a wide variety of situations, and attention and optimal context are indispensable for engaging the individual and facilitating learning [77]. In this sense and following SLT, reproduction is a very important principle in the learning process. Individuals reproduce previously learned information when it is required, and practice through mental and physical repetition often improves responses [16,30]. In this context, Yen et al. [78] consider that training is a technique used to obtain success in the social learning process.

## 3. Research methods

## 3.1. Type of study and participants

Qualitative research is often used when it is necessary to investigate individual perceptions or human behavior. These complex behaviors with a high degree of subjectivity would not be fully explained if resorting to quantitative methodology. In most cases, qualitative metho dology is exploratory [79]. This approach should be adopted when little is known about the phenomenon to be studied, and besides, human sciences sometimes deal with phenomena that cannot be quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed [80,81]. Therefore, this study used qualitative methods to provide a broad view of the critical factors of success in implementing, using, and maintaining e-libraries in the academic domain.

Within qualitative research, the case study method also aims to understand and explore complex events and contexts in depth and in detail, giving answers to the “how” and “why” of interactions between the various factors originating the phenomenon studied [80].

The case selected for this study was the e-library of a University in Portugal, and it was chosen because of this organization’s focus on cooperating with other institutions. University of Beira Interior (UBI) is currently developing the digital part of the RIBBSE Project (Inter-Municipal Network of Libraries in Beiras and Serra da Estrela). This project aims to implement open-source document management software (Koha) and create a collective/shared catalogue for all libraries. Therefore, the RIBBSE Project has two major objectives (see https:// cimbse.pt/ribbse/):

1. To equip all municipal libraries with an integrated Koha system that allows them, in some cases, to have a bibliographic management system, which they lacked, and in other cases, reduce the costs involved in using the commercial software.

2. To create an integrated catalog allowing RIBBSE users to search in any library transparently, and in the near future, to allow books to be requested from one of the other libraries. This project aims to create an inter-municipal library with an integrated catalog that can direct requests to libraries in each local authority. This cooperation project between libraries or library networks, after due implementation and maturation, have provided a better management of resources, a more integrative experience for all network users and an easier identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of needs of all agents evolved in this cooperation project. This is an innovative, successful and extremely important project for all entities involved in it and to this region as well. Therefore, the choice of a case study was owing to the importance this HEI gives to cooperation with other institutions and its experience already acquired in implementing and maintaining the UBI e-library.

Another criterion in selecting this institution/university was greater access to the desired information.

## 3.2. Information collection and analysis

The empirical data for the exploratory study about the factors for successful implementation, use, and maintenance of the e-library were obtained from interviews (primary data) and documentary analysis – service provision contract (secondary data) – and direct observation.

Data triangulation consists of combining di<sup>f</sup>erent data sources aiming to prevent possible distortion in applying a single instrument. This procedure ensures in-depth understanding of the phenomenon in question and is a secure way to validate the research [82]. Triangulation should also be seen as a complement rather than a dichotomy, as the weakness of each source can be compensated for by the strength of another technique [82,83]. Therefore, this study resorted to triangulation of the sources of information, namely (1) the interview, in which the questions referred to the conditions of success of e-libraries, (2) the contract to acquire a support service for an integrated library system for UBI, and (3) direct observation. However, in qualitative studies, in the context of Social and Human Sciences, the interview occupies the prime position in the matter of gathering information [80].

Before holding the interviews, the protocol (interview script) elaborated was pre-tested (see Attachment I) on 2 July 2018 with the person in charge of UBI’s e-library. In this process, some shortcomings in the interview script were detected and corrected and the suggestions and observations made by this agent were taken into consideration.

Subsequently, from 9 to 23 July 2018, interviews were held with the sta<sup>f</sup> of the library, computer services and administration of UBI involved in implementing and maintaining the e-library (see Table 1). Ten interviewees participated in this research; 6 belong to the library sta<sup>f</sup>, 2 to computer services and 2 to the institution’s administration. These actors were selected for the study as they have thorough knowledge of UBI’s e-library and are involved in the process of im plementing and maintaining this tool.

According to Bardin [84], frequently cited in qualitative studies in the area of Social and Human Sciences, content analysis consists of three phases: (1) pre-analysis, (2) exploration of the material, and (3) treatment of the results, inference, and interpretation. Therefore, according to the stages de<sup>fi</sup>ned by that author, the <sup>fi</sup>rst step was to do a general reading of the transcribed interviews to identify the topics/ categories most frequently mentioned by the interviewees. In the next stages, the most relevant topics were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and consolidated, through a deeper analysis of the material and direct observation. Parts of the service provision contract were also referred to, to complement the information obtained.

## 4. Results and discussion

## 4.1. Profile of the UBI e-library

Regarding online access to resources, UBI has implemented a bibliographic catalog supported by the open-source Koha software accessible through http://catalogo.ubi.pt/ where authorized entities can access their area and check, among other functions, the state of requests for e-content made. UBI also provides a digital repository on https://ubibliorum.ubi.pt/, which contains all the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c production of researchers, lecturers, and students of this institution. The repository has open access and is included in the Project of Open Access Scienti<sup>fi</sup>c Repositories in Portugal (see https://www.rcaap.pt/). In addition, the institution provides the whole academic community with access to various databases (see http://www.biblioteca.ubi.pt/Pagina/bases-de dados) included in a national consortium. Despite the tools already provided, in the future, it would be desirable to implement a search aggregator and simultaneously including all the databases.

## 4.2. Factors of the e-library’s success

From content analysis and coding of the topics, various critical factors of e-library success were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

## 4.2.1. Minimization of costs

4.2.1.1. Cooperation protocols and use of open-source software. E libraries provide their users with a vast range of resources in digital format, allowing them to complete their research successfully [85]. In this context, the cooperation protocols established between UBI and various institutions have allowed the exchange of e-content at no additional cost. Consequently, a large number of individuals tend to use e-library platforms because the range of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c themes o<sup>f</sup>ered through this tool has also increased [86]. In that sense, and according to SLT, the visualization of others, using e-library platforms, can lead individuals to use this tool. SLT hypothesizes that the more individuals interact with others, engaged in e-library utilization, the greater the probability that they will follow similar behaviors as well.

In this connection, the interviewees were unanimous, in relation to the advantages provided by the cooperation protocols. The Coordinator of the Reference Sector (A4) considers that “The procedure of loans between libraries is much faster. Through the network, readers can find out the state of the loan”. The Coordinator of the Inter-Library Loan Sector (A5) goes further in her intervention, considering that “We are a world library and all together we are just one library and the other libraries’ resources end up also being our resources (between libraries with inter-library agreements). Sharing resources is very important in these times of crisis. We always turn to libraries we know and we have cooperation protocols with UBI .

Owing to these protocols, access to information is processed more quickly. The Interviewee A3 considers that “Academia needs more immediate information. It’s much better to be at the distance of a click than to have physical barriers and barriers of ownership rights, isn’t it? At the time that suits them and when you are available, to have access to information you need is a great bonus. All this is possible just by using devices with internet access, for example, a mobile phone”. According to the person in charge of the vice-rector’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce for the area of research and projects, “at present, these protocols are used for very particular work, since nowadays the university already has great bibliographic resources…and a lot more was already provided online, particularly for work with public access” (A9), in agreement with what is stated by the person in charge of the vice-rector’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce for the area of Finance, Human Resources and Social Responsibility (A10). Therefore, the empirical evidence obtained for the cooperation protocols agrees with the arguments of Tebbetts [55], Schwart [10], Ball [87], Alemna and KAntwi [56], and Schmidt [86].

The central challenge of decision making for organizations is to choose the right behavior in any situation that arises. In that sense, if organizations realize that other organizations achieve desirable results through certain behaviors, they will copy them [24]. Several HEIs have been adopting cooperation protocols to achieve a large amount of free scienti<sup>fi</sup>c articles [86]. This situation is in line with what is referred to by SLT. An e<sup>f</sup>ective learning occurs by observing and copying a desirable behavior.

In relation to minimizing costs and choosing between free or commercial software to implement UBI’s e-library, the Coordinator of Computing Services (A2) states that “Regarding software for bibliographic management, we began with Milénio, commercial software, whose licence was quite expensive, amounting to about 20 000 euros a year…we trained a technician who gave us suficient guarantees to be able to cope on our own and so we cancelled the maintenance contract with that company and began to manage the software ourselves. At the moment, the cost of exploiting that software corresponds to 25% or 30% of human resources, which is not much more than we spent when we had Milénio. At present, after 5 or 6 years, we manage to gain practically what we paid for the annual licence. I think it was the right decision, and UBI has served as an example for other institutions nationally”. According to the service provision contract analyzed, it was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that “the costs of data migration and configuration carried out by the KeepSolutions company were in the order of 12 300 euros/year”.

According to [108], in recent years, university libraries have increasingly invested their <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources, aiming to provide the whole academic community with good quality digital resources. Libraries have witnessed a major change in their focus, toward providing content in digital format. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of e-libraries implementation are unquestionably positive to all academic sta<sup>f</sup> and students [62]. Thus, according to SLT, and considering the tremendous bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceived by users, in general, HEIs may feel encouraged to implement e-library platforms. According to Bandura [13], motivation is one of the main principles of e<sup>f</sup>ective learning.

Regarding the costs of implementing the e-library, the Head of Library Services (A1) says that “When databases or commercial software is acquired, the costs are enormous, extremely expensive. Access for 3000, 4000, 5000, 6000…. 50 000 euros annually. On the other hand, we have free software, as in the case of our Koha. But we have to develop and maintain it, and the free nature of it is very relative. There are maintenance costs, the cost of conserving data, hardware costs, etc. In fact, there are associated costs, and no software is free”. According to the person in charge of the vice-rector’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce for the area of research and projects (A9), “I agree and I am very much in favour of using free software”.

The evidence obtained for the choice of open-source software to implement UBI’s e-library leads to cost minimization in the long term, despite the initial cost of training sta<sup>f</sup> for con<sup>fi</sup>guration and maintenance of the Koha software, in agreement with the conclusions of El-Waily [6] Cobblah et al. [88], and Hinze et al, [19],

According to the SLT, just observing someone else’s actions is not always enough to lead to learning. Individuals’ mental state and motivation plays an important role in determining whether a behavior is learned or not [13,30]. Thus, the implementation of open-source soft ware relies on the visualization of outcomes obtained by HEIs who have implemented this kind of software. According to Hinze et al. [19], the implementation of the open-source software leads HEIs to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reduction of <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources. In this sense, other HEIs may feel motivated to opt for such an option.

## 4.2.2. Acceptance and use of the e-library

4.2.2.1. Ease of access to information, appealing UIs and actions to publicize the platform. According to Pandya and Darbar [89], elibraries have shortened stages in access to information, lowering physical barriers, and thereby facilitating and speeding up the whole search and investigation process. In this connection, the Head of Library Services (A1) says that “with the e-library, all the information necessary is only a click away”. Similarly, the Coordinator of the Inter-Library Loan Sector (A5) states, being able to access the contents of the digital library without any physical barrier is of great benefit to the academic community. The ease of doing so eliminates barriers and makes the research process more agile”. According to the interviewee (A7), “easy access anywhere allows greater freedom in research . Similarly, the interviewee (A8) consider that “easy access to the digital library brings great advantages for users. Using any device with internet access, you can access the resources provided by the library, anywhere and at any time”, in agreement with the conclusions of Umukoro et al. [3].

Access to e-libraries, from devices with internet access has made it easier and quicker to obtain information [90,91]. According to SLT, the implementation of something new in learning processes may increase the learners attention [92]. Following this idea, the possibility to access the e-library platform through devices with internet connection may increase the users’ attention and conduct them to an e<sup>f</sup>ective learning process.

According to the representative of the vice-rector’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce (responsible for the research and project <sup>fi</sup>eld), “access to UBI’s e-library allows international dissemination of the work produced in UBI, and thereby the maximization of our research’s impact in the world” (A9). Thus, the empirical evidence corroborates the literature on the ease of access to information provided by e-libraries [18,50].

The ease of navigation and <sup>fi</sup>nding the desired information, with no great e<sup>f</sup>ort, can lead to users accepting these platforms [93]. The Coordinator of the Sector for Technical and Document Treatment says that “implementation of interfaces based on users’ needs would be of great benefit because less noise would be created in searches. But for this to happen, we should implement very appropriate software” (A3). For the Manager of the Institutional Repository (A7), “users need to have a more appealing interface, or according to their needs. In this way, they will have fewer dificulties in carrying out their searches”. The empirical evidence obtained about intuitive UIs agrees with Thong et al. [11], Baro et al. [94], Kim et al. [93], and Ji et al. [95].

SLT posits that motivation is a request for e<sup>f</sup>ective learning [13]. In the e-library’s context, learners should have intrinsic motivation to learn how to use this tool in an e<sup>f</sup>ective form. The mentioned moti vation could be achieved by introducing appealing UIs. By this way, learners pay more attention to e-library interface and feel engaged with an e<sup>f</sup>ective utilization.

Greater visibility of platforms can lead to greater perception of their usefulness and ease of use [96]. Here, SLT highlights the importance of the environment in which IT is operated [53]. Knowledge of the e-library and the functions it can supply to the whole academic community can lead to greater use of the tool. As stated by the Head of Library Services (A1), who considers that despite all the publicity we do as a library, the academic community is not informed about what they have available”. The concern about publicizing e-libraries expressed by in terviewee A1 is shared by the person in charge of the Sector for Ac quisitions, O<sup>f</sup>ers and Donations (A8), who considers there must be “greater publicity of the resources existing in UBI and the library services, which will help students in their searches and improve their research” (A8). According to interviewee A1, "one of the main eforts to publicise the functions associated with the e-library takes place when new students register. From 2015, the UBI library regulations have been provided in paper format, but now this information is conveyed verbally. UBI is one of the few universities in the country open 24 h a day. This allows students continuous access to information, for example, via the wireless network and PCs available in the institution’s library” (A1). This empirical evidence about the actions to publicize the e-library agrees with the conclusion of Turan et al. [2] and Makori et al. [66]. The mentioned actions lead to a vast knowledge of this tool by academic sta<sup>f</sup> and students. Because such a tool is new to many of these agents, they will pay more attention if they perceive others using it. This idea follows the main principle of SLT, according to which the attention paid by individuals in the learning process, lead them to an e<sup>f</sup>ective learning.

## 4.2.3. Training

4.2.3.1. Internal and external training of UBI staf. With the digital age, the change from traditional to digital resources in e-libraries meant changes in sta<sup>f</sup> routines and implies greater knowledge of IT [97–100]. Digital formats predominate nowadays and changes in IT take place very rapidly. In this context, training means constant updating in IT and provides sta<sup>f</sup> with updated knowledge of e-libraries to be able to improve their performance [101,102].

Here, SLT proposes that reproducing and practicing an observed behavior may lead to the improvement of skills [13,62]. The maintenance of e-libraries, or any other IS, presupposes the existence of sta<sup>f</sup> with good IT skills that could be obtained through training [59].

Internal training for UBI library sta<sup>f</sup> means the transfer of knowl edge between agents, and training is supplied from various sources. Continuous training from the Portuguese Association of BAD (Librarians, Archivists and Documentalists) to UBI agents is recurrent practice (see http://apbad.pt/). Through belonging to FCT (Science and Technology Foundation), library sta<sup>f</sup> also receive training through the B-on database, about the various areas related to librarian practice. In addition, in 2017, the employees of this institution had intensive training in English, to be able to give a better response to the problems of Erasmus students. Training of UBI students is mainly carried out by the head of this institution’s library, and when requested by the dif ferent departments and in the classroom context.

Following the same line of reasoning, SLT posits that an e<sup>f</sup>ective learning is achieved through a mental retention of information in the individual’s memories. During the training process, students memorize how to use the e-library in an e<sup>fi</sup>cient way and reproduce this behavior when required.

This type of initiative aims to provide all UBI library sta<sup>f</sup> with knowledge to be able to support all academia in carrying out their re search and producing better scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge. Even if the institu tion’s sta<sup>f</sup> are not particularly keen on exploiting IT, despite intuitive UIs and easy navigation, training aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap and give greater support to scienti<sup>fi</sup>c production.

In the same line of reasoning, the Coordinator of the Sector of Technical and Document Treatment (A3) says that “librarians’ training did not consider the digital library, and so training is very important”. This empirical evidence about the importance of training agrees with Bawden et al. [103], Baro et al. [94], Shem [104], Kumar et al. [100], and Frandsen, et al. [102]. According to SLT, just observing someone else's actions is not always enough to lead to learning. Individuals mental state and motivation play an important role in determining whether a behavior is learned or not [13,35]. In the e-library sta<sup>f</sup> context, an e<sup>f</sup>ective learning process may conduct these agents to a better job performance and this fact could be an intrinsic motivation to them.

According to the person in charge of the Acquisitions, O<sup>f</sup>ers and Donations Sector (A8), “training is never too much, nor does it take up space, and it could help students to make better searches and save time, and for us it would also be useful to give better support to students who ask us for help”. To optimize the results of searches, interviewee (A1) considers that “in principle, the academic community is an informed community and should know how to search, should know how to use electronic resources, but that is not in fact the case. We, as the library, give the user training mainly on how to search”. Moreover, to provide better service and increase academic sta<sup>f</sup> satisfaction, UBI e-library provides personal computers to its users. UBI e-library has librarian sta<sup>f</sup> with the necessary expertise to suppress users’ doubts arising from the constant evolution of ITs. In addition, this HEI also provides a dedicated e-mail address to support the use and requisition of e-contents.

Moreover, the librarian sta<sup>f</sup> has also acquired the necessary knowledge through training programs to answer users' doubts concerning using e-library resources e<sup>f</sup>ectively. In fact, Interviewee A3 goes further by considering that "training should be held together with the staf of the library and the computing services. Ideally, there would be training of multi-disciplinary teams who with the existing resources were able to maintain and develop more functions of the digital library. Implementation of a search aggregator producing the least noise possible”, agreeing with the statement by the head of the library (A1).

Nevertheless, the Coordinator of the Reference/Request Desk Sector (A4) contradicts the literature on the subject, by considering that “people today manage to have easier access to knowledge, they have some mechanisms that allow them access to the platform without the need for more training”. In the same line of thought, interviewee (A9) considers that “to be efective and for mass use, the platform has to be simple to use… any platform that needs training will never be very successful”. Therefore, the empirical evidence obtained is not consensual. According to the statements of interviewees (A4) and (A9), the role of training is not prioritized, also contradicting the literature on the topic.

## 5. Conclusions and implications

The main aim of this study was to provide wide-ranging under standing of the conditions of success of e-libraries in the academic context, during the process of implementation, use, and maintenance according to the principles of SLT.

The proposed goal was achieved based on the empirical evidence obtained and the literature review carried out. The following critical conditions of success were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: cost reduction, acceptance, and use of the e-library platform, and training. In the current scenario of reduced budgets allocated to HEIs, lowering the cost of installing the elibrary becomes a determinant factor of its success. According to the SLT, the visualization of HEIs that had a successful performance through their resources maximization could take other organizations to copy this behavior.

This study presents implications and evidence of use to anyone proposing to implement and develop this type of system. Although this research focuses on the UBI’s e-library, a slightly closed context, this research can be an example for other HEIs that are implementing an elibrary and are facing the same problems or doubts. Findings of this research could be helpful to other academic e-libraries; wishing to develop and implement processes more successfully.

As implications for practice, we can highlight that the implementation of e-libraries has provided signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements concerning quality and speed in the access of knowledge by users of those platforms, when compared to traditional libraries.

Results obtained suggest that the digital transformation led HEIs’ elibraries to change global processes, and above all, the technology previously acquired. Nevertheless, as previously reported, the use of open-source software is a potential strategic option to minimize costs in implementing and maintaining e-libraries. Opting for commercial or open-source software is a central issue in implementing an e-library [6]. Although the adoption of open-source software requires some devel opment and consequently specialized human resources to develop and maintain this tool, in the long term, this can be the right choice for UBI. The e<sup>fi</sup>cient management of HEIs’ technological resources can take organizations to success [106,107]. Thus, it can be concluded that the use of open-source software to implement UBI’s e-library was the right strategy for successful implementation of the platform, and meant a reduction of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources involved. According to the same line of reasoning, SLT proposes that learners must be motivated to learn [13]. The outcomes obtained by the choice of open-source software are an extrinsic motivation for other HEIs to follow such behavior and adopt this type of software in the implementation of e-library platforms.

This study also shows that from the perspective of cost reduction, those in charge of HEIs should establish a greater number of cooperation protocols with their peers, so that, with the same resources, they are able to provide the academic community with a greater amount of e-content. Cooperation between libraries or library networks, after due implementation and consolidation of mechanisms and cooperation channels, will certainly mean better resource management, a more in tegrated experience for all network users, easier identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of need and channeling of investment and training.

Training is also a factor that those in charge of HEIs should bear in mind in the process of implementing, using, and maintaining e-libraries. Library sta<sup>f</sup> training helps all academia to use this platform more e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Otherwise, the good functioning of the platform may be jeopardized. The continuous training of librarian sta<sup>f</sup> can raise the quality level of services provided by e-libraries, and thus, allows im proving the academic community’s satisfaction level.

Training processes can empower HEIs sta<sup>f</sup> to perform their work in a better way. Thus, they feel motivated to learn because their feeling of self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy increases, following the same line of reasoning of SLT.

Acceptance of this type of platform depends on various factors, namely the ease of access to information, implementation of intuitive UIs appealing to users and training actions with respect to this tool. Easy access to information in the form of online e-content breaks down physical barriers, with greater comfort and speed for the user. Besides being attractive from the graphic point of view, the UI must also be functional to allow simple, intuitive navigation. In this way, the user is more likely to use it again. Furthermore, visual contact with the e-library tends to increase users’ curiosity to try it out and explore its functions. Therefore, actions to publicize its potential are also an important factor for acceptance of this tool. Knowledge and visual contact with the use of this platform can increase its use, in accordance with the forecasts of SLT.

From the perspective of maintenance and development, it is sug gested that HEIs should create multidisciplinary teams that are able to develop the potential of the e-library, for example, through a search aggregator that is able to generate the least possible noise in searches.

From the theoretical point of view, this research contributes to en hancing the literature on this subject, presenting a framework identifying the conditions of success in e-libraries based on SLT principles. Given the lack of literature about the conditions of success in e-li braries, this research has provided an enrichment of the literature concerning the subject in a HEIs context, which has not yet been explored.

Although this study identi<sup>fi</sup>es the conditions of success in e-libraries and thereby contributes signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to theory and practice related to the subject, it is not without limitations. A single case study, together with the subjectivity in interpreting responses, does not allow the generalization of conclusions. It is therefore suggested that similar studies should be carried out in the future, but applied in other geographical regions and in various contexts, to allow comparative studies to be made. Also recommended is the development of instruments to measure the contribution of sta<sup>f</sup> training and the development of work performance in academic e-libraries. In this way, it would be possible to measure the impact of training on the performance of individuals and the institution itself.

This research did not take into account the students who use the HEIs e-libraries. Thus, as future lines of research, we propose studies that identify the conditions of success in e-libraries from the point of view of HEIs’ students. As, students of various scienti<sup>fi</sup>c areas have di<sup>f</sup>erent needs, it is important to identify their gaps, so that they could be solved. According to the same line of thought, e-libraries users should not be seen as undi<sup>f</sup>erentiated.
