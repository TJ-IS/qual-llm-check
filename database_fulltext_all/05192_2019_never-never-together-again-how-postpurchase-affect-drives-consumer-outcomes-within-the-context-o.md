# Never, Never Together Again: How Postpurchase Affect Drives Consumer Outcomes Within the Context of Online Consumer Support Communities

EunHee Park<sup>1</sup>, Ghiyoung Im<sup>2</sup>, Veda C. Storey<sup>3</sup>, Richard L. Baskerville<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Old Dominion University, U.S.A., epark@odu.edu

<sup>2</sup>University of Louisville, U.S.A., ghiyoung.im@louisville.edu

<sup>3</sup>Georgia State University, U.S.A., VStorey@gsu.edu

<sup>4</sup>Georgia State University, U.S.A.; Curtin University, Australia, baskerville@acm.org

## Abstract

Online support communities are popular for consumers of information technology products who might need help identifying or resolving a problem. Information technology products, in general, have their own needs and requirements. Prior research has focused on the intermediate benefits of online support communities to companies, such as knowledge contribution and community participation. This study, in contrast, investigates the less explored issue of value creation by online support communities with respect to consumer postpurchase outcomes. To do so, we develop an affect (emotional) process model to understand how customers’ postpurchase outcomes of information technology products are influenced through cognitive and affective processes after a product failure. Special attention is paid to the roles of affect during the recovery process. An empirical assessment of the model uses two online support communities, with a netnography methodology employed for data collection. The results suggest that consumers’ postpurchase outcomes are influenced by affect and regulation, not just cognition. Key influences emerge as the consumers’ own problem appraisals and affective experiences, the consumers’ social group, and regulation provided by company technicians and/or community experts.

Keywords: Postpurchase Outcomes, Problem Appraisal, Affective Experience, Regulation, Social Influence, Online Support Community, Affect Process Model, Cognition, Information Technology Product Failure

## 1. Introduction

After-sales service is an important part of product life cycle and becomes a crucial source of the competitive advantage of many information technology (IT) companies (Ives & Vitale, 1988). Online support communities are a major vehicle that IT companies use to provide after-sales services, including technical support, troubleshooting, patches and upgrades, and product/service maintenance (Bone, Fombelle, Ray, & Lemon, 2015; Jabr, Mookerjee, Tan, & Mookerjee,

2014). For example, major IT companies rely on such communities to facilitate customer service in the areas of business-to-business (e.g., ACT!, Intel, SAP) and business-to-consumer (e.g., Verizon, Lenovo, DIRECTV). The most salient benefit of online support communities is cost reduction. Companies realize cost reduction through both direct and indirect deflection of customer service (Petouhoff, Gliedman, Band, & Magarie, 2009b). Direct call deflection occurs when customers post questions and receive answers via interaction with other community members. With indirect call deflection, customers obtain answers by reading the posts of others. Lenovo, for example, reported a 20 percent reduction in call volume for laptop problems due to call deflection (Petouhoff, Band, & Magarie, 2009a). Like other types of online communities, an online support community is built on “a cyberspace supported by computer-based information technology, centered on communication and interaction of participants to generate member-driven contents, resulting in a relationship being built up” (Lee, Cho, Gay, Davidson, & Ingraffea, 2003, p. 51).

Despite considerable attention to online support communities (Goh, Heng, & Lin, 2013; Jabr et al., 2014), few studies offer a comprehensive understanding of the process of direct value creation to the sponsoring companies (Aral, Dellarocas, & Godes, 2013). Among the many kinds of after-sales service, this research focuses on troubleshooting support after IT product or service failures. Maintenance and support of IT products may have their own requirements and may or may not be reflective of overall customer support communities. With respect to direct value creation, this research focuses on postpurchase intentions of participating consumers. Failures are considered to be anything that disrupts a consumer’s normal activities (Ives & Vitale, 1988). They can be associated with defects, malfunction, outdated software, interoperability, and misconfiguration (Marcus, 2006). Affective responses are characteristic of consumers who experience such failures (Smith & Bolton, 2002). Affect refers to the feelings individuals experience and share. When failure recovery is not successful, despite substantial efforts, consumers can easily become emotional and irrational. Therefore, affective influence is a major force in shaping the outcomes of consumers in trouble.

This leads to the following research question: How do the problem-solving activities for failed IT products or services in online consumer support communities shape the affective process by which customer postpurchase outcomes are influenced? Addressing this question is important. First, most online support community research has focused on the factors that make consumers join a community, stay, and contribute knowledge (Jabr et al., 2014; Moon & Sproull, 2008), because any community must have a critical mass. Equally important is the understanding of the process of how the accumulated knowledge is used for problem solving. An online support community facilitates collaborative problem solving. Effective problem-solving experiences motivate participants to return to these communities, enabling further growth. Thus, more research is needed to understand the problem-solving process that leads to postpurchase outcomes.

Second, affect has not been a central focus in cognition-oriented postpurchase research (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2012). When consumers enjoy stable

IT products and services, their postpurchase outcomes are likely to be formed by careful rational evaluations based on expectations, past experiences, and available features. When they experience IT product and service failures, their salient affect may challenge previously held beliefs, thoughts, and attitudes (Greifeneder, Bless, & Pham, 2011). Thus, affect may play a dominant role in the formation of postpurchase consumer outcomes.

Finally, despite the pervasiveness of social influence in online support communities, related research has paid minimal attention to social influence. A few studies have identified types of social influence, such as group norms and social identity (Dholakia, Bagozzi, & Pearo, 2004; Dholakia, Blazevic, Wiertz, & Algesheimer, 2009). In information systems research, social influence is recognized, first, in the form of influencing social actors (Lewis, Agarwal, & Sambamurthy, 2003) and, next, by its type (e.g., subjective norm) (Venkatesh et al., 2012). Overall, existing social influence frameworks are static in nature and not adequate for explaining the dynamic development of social influence over time.

Drawing on the theory of emotion process by Frijda et al. (2007; 1989), this research creates an affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes. Frijda et al.’s theory is used to understand affective processes (e.g., event, appraisal, affect, arousal, action readiness, and regulation), and guides the data collection and model development. Our purpose is to build a new process model rather than to extend existing ones. For data collection and analysis, we employed netnography (Hine, 2000) that combines Internet and ethnography simultaneously. Our empirical context is two online support communities managed by IT companies. Each online community provides a virtual place (i.e., forum) for participants to contribute to the content of dialogues and to interact with others. Consumers with failed products visit these forums and solicit assistance from company technicians, voluntary experts, or other participants. Company technicians and voluntary experts are referred to as “regulation” in this research. The forums are organized by types of products so consumers can easily join an appropriate dialogue.

The developed process model captures the progressive development of cognition, affect, regulation, and postpurchase outcome. At the early stages (Time 1), all dialogues show a similar pattern of problem solving by asking questions and receiving comments from other participants. Participants influence and are influenced by affective experiences by others. However, at the latter stages (Time 2), the dialogues divide into two different patterns depending on whether the problem is solved successfully or not. When a problem is solved successfully because of proper regulation, participants reappraise the problem positively and have positive affect, which should lead to repurchase/continuance intentions. In contrast, when the problem is not solved successfully, participants reappraise the problem negatively and are strongly influenced by negative affect, leading to no repurchase/discontinuance intentions. In the former situation, participants are influenced by cognition and affect when forming their postpurchase outcomes. In the latter situation, participants are strongly influenced by affect, leading to no repurchase/discontinuance intentions. This research challenges cognitionoriented models on postpurchase decision-making, emphasizing the crucial role of affect, especially when proper regulation is absent.

## 2. Theoretical Background

This section reviews related research on online support communities, roles of cognition and affect in decision-making, the theory of emotion process, and social influence.

## 2.1. Online Support Communities

Major IT companies have recognized that online support communities can offer enormous benefits for meeting consumer demands after the sales of IT products (Ives & Vitale, 1988; Piccoli & Ives, 2005). After-sales service for IT products has evolved from assisted support (by a dedicated support center), to web-based self-help (via searchable knowledge base), to online support communities (Marcus, 2006). This service has its own requirements and may or may not require online presence for troubleshooting. However, most consumers go online for immediate assistance from similar troubleshooting cases that have been discussed in the past. As such, online support communities have become an indispensable, essential means of customer service. For example, Jabr et al. (2014) report that major IT companies (Apple, Oracle, SAP, and SUN) rely on online support communities to better assist their customers. Despite the increased importance and benefits, research on online support communities has not investigated their direct benefits (e.g., postpurchase). Instead, it has mostly been concerned with indirect benefits, such as knowledge contribution (Jabr et al., 2014; Moon & Sproull, 2008) and community participation (Dholakia et al., 2004; Dholakia et al., 2009; Moon & Sproull, 2008).

## 2.2. Cognition and Affect in Postpurchase Decision-Making

Postpurchase research associated with IT products and services is largely concerned with how a consumer’s cognition impacts his/her decision on postpurchase and continuance after IT is installed and available for use (Venkatesh et al. 2012). According to the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh, Morris, Gordon, & Davis, 2003), individual cognition can be summarized by performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. For example, some research has explained postpurchase using cognitive concepts, including perceived usefulness (Khalifa & Liu, 2007; Polites, Williams, Karahanna, & Seligmand, 2012; Sun, 2010) and efficiency and effectiveness (Cyr, Head, & Ivanov, 2009). Other research has focused on how product and vendor characteristics, environmental influences, consumer cognitions, and consumer characteristics (e.g., age, gender) influence a consumer’s decision to repurchase or continue to use (Cheung, Chan, & Limayem, 2005; Fang et al., 2014). Noncognitive concepts (e.g., enjoyment and satisfaction) have received limited attention (Polites et al., 2012; Sun, 2010).

Cognition-oriented research is important because consumers rely on their established rational, cognitive perceptions when they need to make repurchase decisions, provided the products and services offer value as expected. However, when IT products and services fail, consumers experience frustration and seek assistance from friends, the vendor, and the Internet. Consumers’ cognitive and affective states are influenced by the quality of support they receive from the vendor and social groups (Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994). Consumers can also be influenced by their affect during the problem-solving process and their affect may emerge as a strong influencer of postpurchase outcomes (Westbrook, 1987). Therefore, research on affect is needed to understand how consumers experience IT products and services failures. Although some research has addressed affect (Cyr et al., 2009; Sun, 2010; Wen et al., 2011), affect has not played a dominant role in cognition-oriented postadoption models.

Cognition and affect are interdependent (Sun & Zhang, 2006; Zajonc, 1984). In the traditional view of information processing, cognitive processing precedes affective experiences (Barsade, 2002; Zajonc, 1980). In contrast, marketing research reports that affect can influence cognition (e.g., perceived risk) (Chaudhuri, 2002). Both cognition and affect are, thus, crucial elements in decision-making (Sun & Zhang, 2006).

Psychology and marketing researchers have discovered the dominant role of affect over cognition for judgment and decision-making in certain circumstances (Chang & Pham, 2013; Forgas & George, 2001; Pham, Cohen, Pracejus, & Hughes, 2001). Pham et al. (2001), for example, show that affect, in contrast to cognition-based assessment, directly influences judgment, resulting in faster, more consistent, and more predictable responses. Such a development draws on the affect-as-information research (Schwarz, 1990; Schwarz & Clore, 1996), which indicates that affect carries informational value on a product and, thus, becomes a primary driver of judgment. Such impact on judgment is pronounced when the affect is salient (Greifeneder et al., 2011).

In online community research, affect is known to induce positive and negative activities from community participants (Table 1). Online community participants who have positive affect are more likely to display positive online activities. For example, happy participants exhibit high participation rates (Bateman,

Gray, & Butler, 2011), more knowledge contributions (Ma & Agarwal 2007), and positive word-of-mouth behaviors (Ray, Kim, & Morris, 2014). In contrast, online community participants who have negative affect are more likely to engage in negative online activities. For example, feelings of hatred may lead to crime or racist behaviors (Chau & Xu, 2007). Anxious participants also often display negative word-of-mouth behaviors (Oh, Agrawal, & Rao, 2013).

## 2.3. Theory of Emotion Process

The theory of emotion process (Figure 1) recognizes emotion as a series of interrelated components developed over time, consisting of a sequence of event, appraisal, affect, arousal, action readiness, behavior, and regulation (Frijda, 2007; Frijda et al., 1989; Scherer & Ellgring, 2007). Consistent with the

componential approach (e.g., Mesquita, Frijda, & Scherer, 1997), this theory does not limit emotion analysis to a defined set of universal emotions, but rather decomposes emotional expressions into detailed components (e.g., Frijda, 2007; Frijda et al., 1989; Scherer & Ellgring, 2007) that indicate cognitive or affective processing. In the theory, cognitive components (e.g., appraisal) play a role in driving behaviors, but affective components are more prominent and serve as the ultimate drivers of behaviors in the overall emotion process. Most of these components are observed in emotional instances and each component may be influenced by other components. This theory provides the basis for our data collection and analysis.

The emotion process begins when an individual encounters an event that is emotionally significant and relevant (e.g., IT failure, a dispute with colleagues, or environmental changes). The individual strives to find the meaning of the event and develops appraisal, affect, arousal, and action readiness over time. In the above model, the exact sequences and associations among affect, arousal, and action readiness are not specified. Ultimately, these components lead to visible behaviors (Frijda et al., 1989). Appraisal is a cognitive component of evaluating whether a particular event changes one’s well-being and how it does so (Folkman, Lazarus, Dunkel-Schetter, DeLongis, & Gruen, 1986). Appraisal entails a continuous evaluation process of events and can be understood according to multiple dimensions, including familiarity, goal conduciveness, controllability, modifiability, expectedness, pleasantness, and fairness (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Frijda et al., 1989). Appraisal is an antecedent to affective experiences (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Frijda et al., 1989).

Affect is broadly defined as feelings that individuals experience and can share with others (Barsade & Gibson, 2007). It subsumes feeling states (i.e., moods and discrete emotions) and traits (i.e., positive and negative affectivity) (Barsade & Gibson, 2007). Concepts similar to affect include emotion and mood. Emotion is short-lived and has a specific target (Barsade & Gibson, 2007), whereas mood is a prolonged affect and is diffused with a lack of object directedness (Van Kleef, Van Doorn, Heerdink, & Koning, 2011). Among these three concepts, affect has been employed to explain various emotional experiences of individuals, such as emotion and mood (Barsade, 2002). Affect can be communicated via different forms (body, facial, and text). Arousal indicates bodily changes and symptoms (Frijda et al., 1989). In an online context, arousal is unobservable, but can be inferred from the intensity of language (e.g., adjectives, vulgar words) (Zhe & Boucouvalas, 2002) and the different type of affect (e.g., angry, frustrated) (Russell, 1980). Analogous to dimensional theories of emotion (Russell & Barrett, 1999; Scherer & Ellgring, 2007), this study uses arousal to differentiate various types of affect (e.g., the power level of affect). The arousal level of affective experiences can be presented in a circumplex model (Russell & Barrett, 1999) using an array of affect-related words, showing how powerful each affect is.

Action readiness describes one’s readiness to achieve a particular goal or state in action, and is activated when situations allow (Frijda, 2007). Action readiness can be classified as: (1) moving toward (e.g., I want to be or stay close); (2) moving away (e.g., I am no longer sure I want to have anything to do with something or someone); and (3) moving against (e.g., I want to oppose or hurt) (Frijda et al., 1989). In this research, action readiness resembles postpurchase intentions (e.g., continuance/discontinuance intentions). Action readiness is realized as a behavior based on whether an action is available (Frijda, 2007). Behavior can be described as instrumental, when taking an action to achieve a certain goal (e.g., repurchase, boycott), or as expressive, when simply expressing one’s affective experience through one’s body (e.g., smiling, crying) (Frijda et al., 1989).

Regulation is intervention and originates internally (e.g., self-control, conscience) or externally (e.g., approvals/disapprovals, social norms, social support) (Frijda, 1986). Each component of the emotion processes can be partially controlled by regulation (Frijda, 2007). Thus, regulation processes can enhance or attenuate the affect and arousal of an individual. Regulation also enables the creation of new emotional responses, as well as changes in existing ones. External regulation for IT troubleshooting can include technical intervention and social intervention (e.g., Tolar & Balka, 2011; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). Technical intervention is imposed by fixing hardware via technology support centers and releasing software hotfixes, updates, and patches (Bretthauer, 2002; Hecker, 1999). This intervention is crucial for helping consumers use their purchased IT devices effectively (Tolar & Balka, 2011). Social intervention is concerned with the manner in which technical intervention (e.g., timeliness and continuous communication) is performed.

Research based on Frijda’s emotion process model typically adopts a variance model approach (Bagozzi et al., 1998; Frijda et al., 1989). Our research enriches such studies by developing a process model that illustrates how the emotion process unfolds progressively over time. Process models explain the phenomenon with a sequence of events that develop toward a particular outcome (Van de Ven, 2007). In variance models, the cause is a necessary and sufficient condition for the outcome, whereas in process models, the precursor is necessary, but not sufficient for the successor to occur. The logical form of process theories is similar to “if not X then not Y” but does not imply any relevance of “more X” to “more Y” (Markus & Robey, 1988).

## 2.4. Social Influence

Online communities are places where cognitive and affective forms of social influence actively occur through participants’ activities of knowledge sharing and learning (Dholakia et al., 2009; Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013), problem solving (Bone et al., 2015; Dholakia et al., 2009; Wasko & Faraj, 2000), and networking (Dholakia et al., 2009). Psychology and emotion research identify various types of cognitive and affective social influence: cognitive/affective transformation, cognitive/affective intensification, and cognitive/affective contagion. Cognitive transformation is a change in one’s perception of a situation from negative to positive or vice versa. This concept considers changes in mental models, similar to those described in cognitive transformation theory (Klein & Baxter, 2009). Cognitive intensification is the increase in cognitive activities among individuals. This increase occurs when people spend more time in cognitive activities associated with reasoning, interpretation, and evaluation (Lochman, 1992). Cognitive contagion occurs when an individual or a group influences the shared information, knowledge, or beliefs among other individuals through the conscious or unconscious induction of cognitive fields (Barsade, 2002).

A common form of affective social influence is affective contagion, which is the result of “a process in which a person or group influences the affect or behavior of another person or group through the conscious or unconscious induction of affect states and behavioral attitudes” (Schoenewolf, 1990, p. 50). In a face-to-face context, affective contagion is made through automatic mimicry and synchronization among individuals (e.g., facial expression, body language) (Barsade, 2002; Hatfield, Cacioppo, & Rapson, 1993). In an online environment, although participants cannot observe others’ nonverbal activities, they are still prone to affective contagion via text expressions (Raafat et al., 2009; Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013). Similar to cognitive transformation, affective transformation refers to the change of negative affect into positive affect and vice versa. Prior psychotherapy studies have typically investigated how affective transformation occurs when clients engage in intrapersonal tasks during affect-focused therapy (Pascual-Leone & Greenberg, 2007). Similar to cognitive intensification, affective intensification is the strengthening of positive or negative affect (Barsade & Gibson, 2007). Affective intensification captures affect with respect to valence (pleasantness) and arousal of affect (Barsade & Gibson, 2007). Affective intensification is traced by a change in the arousal level of affect (e.g., from upset to enraged).

Among these types of social influence, contagion has received the most attention. This is understandable because online support communities provide ample opportunities for community participants to interact and easily spread their thoughts, affect, and intentions. Contagion assumes that such thoughts, affect, and intentions have already been developed. However, much research is needed to understand how these are initiated, developed, and transformed. Further research is also needed to understand the development of the other types of social influence (e.g., transformation, intensification) and the role they play in weakening, strengthening, and transforming the thoughts and affect of other community participants.

## 3. Research Methodology

This research employs a qualitative research methodology, netnography, to understand the dynamics of consumers in online support communities. Netnography is a type of online ethnographic research (Kozinets 2002, 2010) that combines Internet and ethnography simultaneously (Kozinets, Dolbec, & Earley, 2013). It has several important benefits (Alavi, 2014; Xun & Reynolds, 2010). First, it is a “naturalistic and unobtrusive methodology [providing] a window into naturally occurring behaviors, such as searches for information by, and communal word-of-mouth discussions between, consumers” (Alavi, 2014, p. 72). Second, it lowers data collection time and cost compared to other qualitative methodologies (e.g., interviews, traditional ethnography). Third, it enables better tracing and deeper analysis of digitally stored data with more flexibility in data collection and analysis.

There are two forms of netnography (Alavi, 2014): (1) participant-observation netnography, in which researchers participate in the online community; and (2) nonparticipant-observation netnography, where researchers lurk and unobtrusively observe community behaviors. The latter is most appropriate when researcher participation changes the essence of community behavior (Quinton & Harridge‐March, 2010). When the presence of a visible researcher disrupts the community in a way that changes the dynamics and relationships in the network, it corrupts the data and limits the levels and types of data (Paccagnella, 1997). Accordingly, we conducted a nonparticipant study to obtain a truer picture of the community (Langer & Beckman, 2005). This approach aligns well with our interest in exploring the natural dynamics of participant behaviors associated with postpurchase outcomes.

The netnography was conducted in four steps (Kozinets, 2010; Kozinets et al., 2013). First, we formulated our research questions, selected three potential online technology support forums managed by global IT companies, and contacted the companies for permission. Ultimately, we obtained permission from two. Second, the theory of emotion process (Frijda, 2007) guided data collection so that the selected events and participants were relevant to the major theoretical concepts. Third, guided by the theory of emotion process (Frijda, 2007), we conducted a rigorous, iterative, interpretive analysis. Fourth, the resulting findings and insights were extracted.

## 3.1. Research Sites

The research sites were two online customer service communities run by the IT companies—InterTech and GlobTech (pseudonyms). InterTech and GlobTech are Fortune 500 companies that manufacture and market desktop computers, laptops, tablets, workstations, servers, storage drives, software, and other related services (see Appendix B for details). The companies’ social media initiatives are used to capture customer opinions and foster close social media interactions with customers. The communities are open to different stakeholders (e.g., customers, employees, experts, and administrators), with each playing a unique role in problem solving. The two communities are similar in that, first, they employ a “kudos” strategy that allows participants to receive kudos from other participants when their feedback is considered helpful and informative. As an incentive, participants earn different labels (e.g., student, teacher, professor), based on their degree of contribution. The dialogue initiator can declare the discussion as “problem solved” when a satisfactory solution is obtained. Second, customer support (regulation in this study) is similarly provided through two groups, the company technicians and community experts. The company technicians include community administrators, company product experts, and repair center technicians, each of whom plays an essential role for problem solving (“regulation by company”). Community experts are volunteers who are knowledgeable about specific technologies. They usually have years of experience and are capable of solving problems. These experts are easily identifiable by a ranking indicator in their profiles reflecting their contributions (“regulation by expert”). There are several differences between the two forums. First, InterTech has minimal intervention in participant discussions, offering more room for free discussion and problem solving among the participants, whereas GlobTech frequently intervenes in and tightly controls the forums. Second, the moderators in GlobTech are more active, removing vulgar or abusive language and frequently sanitizing negative messages, for example.

These research sites are appropriate because not only have online support communities become a popular problem-solving ecosystem for consumers and companies, but the communities’ social dynamics are easily traceable through dialogues. Furthermore, to address customers’ concerns, the communities have instituted support structures—equivalent to regulation in the theory of emotion process—on which we base our model.

## 3.2. Data Collection

We made observations in virtual spaces by reading postings and observing online behaviors reflecting the activities of participants in the targeted online communities (Kozinets, 2010; Kozinets et al., 2013). We identified dialogues in the communities that met the selection criteria below and analyzed the relevant text, emoticons, and multimedia (e.g., video clips, pictures) (Table 2) (Lopez-Rocha, 2010). The events were recorded based on the reference concepts (appraisal, affect, arousal, action readiness, and regulation) in the theory of emotion process (Frijda 2007; Frijda et al., 1989) (Appendix C) and our reflections on the significance of the events. We consulted archival data, including online support community manuals, privacy policies, online articles, and newsletters. This additional information provided an understanding of the companies’ community initiatives and policies.

To identify the data relevant to the research question, we examined thousands of posts in an effort to isolate dialogues on the affective process of consumer postpurchase behaviors in failure settings (Kozinets, 2002). The following selection criteria were applied to distill the relatively small number of relevant dialogues (Kozinets, 2002). First, the dialogues should deal with problems and complaints associated with notebook or desktop computers. These products are very popular, used daily, and have a myriad of issues for troubleshooting (Lee et al., 2006). Product problems in these dialogues should include new problems that may require troubleshooting root cause and also ones where the solution is known by technicians or experts. Second, the dialogues should address mid- to highproblem complexity, which are likely to trigger various affective responses (Wolf 2000; Zhe & Boucouvalas, 2002). Problem complexity refers to the number of features and layers involved in problem solving (Mennecke et al., 2000). The range used is low problem complexity (< 10 numbers of features and layers), medium (≤ 10 to < 50), and high (≥ 50). Third, the dialogues should contain a minimum of 15 postings to display certain levels of activities among the participants. Finally, the dialogues should have explicit or implicit expressions of postpurchase outcomes (e.g., repurchase intentions, complaints, word-of-mouth) (Vázquez-Casielles et al., 2012).

Table 2 summarizes the number of dialogues collected and the characteristics of the data obtained from the two research sites. The data were recorded so that participants could not be identified, directly or through identifiers linked to participants (Parrott et al. 2015). Online ID and other facts that may identify the participants were not collected. Every posting was paraphrased for reporting the results.

## 3.3. Data Analysis

Our process model has two units for analysis. The first is an individual who joins a forum, participates in a dialogue on a problem to be solved, and expresses postpurchase intentions at the end. The next is an emergent group (EG) that is formed voluntarily for problem solving and lasts until the problem is solved or the group concludes that no proper solution is available. Members of the emergent group participate in a problem-solving dialogue, share their experiences on the same/similar problem, and exchange their affect and cognitive judgment to make suggestions. The members are homogeneous in that they share the same or similar problems and have the same goal of successful problem solving. Although the groups do not share any consistent set of attributes, they all rely on a consistent sequencing for problem solving and decision-making. The group members interact with each other in the dialogue and influence each other’s cognitive and affective processes. The multilevel process model captures the interplay between these two separate but interdependent units, delineating the progressive development of cognition and affect toward forming an individual’s postpurchase outcomes. Although prior research has recognized the importance of social influence in online communities (Dholakia et al., 2004; Dholakia et al., 2009), it has not explicitly incorporated multilevel affective processes.

Our netnographic data analysis involves a sequence of two analytic processes: analytic coding and hermeneutic interpretation (Kozinets, 2010). The theory of emotion process provides seed concepts (Appendix C). Analytic coding involves coding and noting concepts that emerge and abstracting, comparing, contrasting, refining, and generalizing the elements (Kozinets, 2010; Miles & Huberman, 1994). The analytic outcome is the basis of the hermeneutic interpretation, which is generated by repetitively reading and rereading the coding in search of new meanings and understanding resulting from each new reading. The results are “coherent and free of contradiction”, “enlightening”, and “fruitful in revealing new dimensions of the problem at hand;” they yield “insights that explicitly revise our current understanding” and should be “persuasive, engaging, interesting, stimulating, and appealing” (Kozinets, 2010, p. 120).

Prior studies show that online users employ emoticons (Wolf 2000), the names of discrete emotions to express themselves (Zhe & Boucouvalas, 2002), and adjectives to express the intensity of their affect (Zhe & Boucouvalas, 2002). During our analytic coding, we coded the names of discrete emotions, vulgar/negative words, and emoticons to denote both valence (positive vs. negative) and arousal level (high vs. low intensity) of affect. An arousal level of affect is generally indicated by capitalized letters, repeated words, repeated special characters, excessive adverbs (e.g., very, excessively), and strong negative words, which are used by participants to overcome the limited means of expressing affect and its intensity online.

During the analytic coding process, the raw data from postings were first broken down, compared, and categorized around the seed concepts provided by the theory of emotion process (Frijda, 2007; Frijda et al., 1989). From the dialogues and archival data, similar text segments were grouped to develop the coding categories: event, appraisal, affect, arousal, action readiness, and regulation. Through hermeneutic interpretation, we sought coherent explanations, revealed new dimensions of each category, and discovered new insights. (See Appendix D for details.)

During Stage 1, partly relying on the theory of emotion process, we first discovered the components of the major concepts (Appendix D). For event, we identified technology malfunction, problem solving status (no, partial, full), and problem-solving stage (Time 1 and Time 2). Time 1 mostly involves problem and potential solution appraisals, whereas Time 2 is largely concerned with resolution appraisals. Appraisal was divided into familiarity, goal conduciveness, controllability, modifiability, expectedness, pleasantness, fairness, and time of event. Action readiness was classified into “move toward”, “move away”, and “move against”. Regulation was classified as either company or volunteer experts.

During Stage 2, the concepts and components from Stage 1 were further refined through iterative rounds of data collection and analysis. Specifically, appraisal and affect were further elaborated for individuals and the emergent group. Regulation was recognized as having both technical and social intervention. Further analysis revealed two types of social influence: cognitive social influence (i.e., cognitive transformation, intensification, and contagion) and affective social influence (i.e., affective transformation, intensification, and contagion). These social influences had varying degrees of influence across the dialogues with satisfactory and unsatisfactory outcomes. The two illustrative cases in Appendices D and E show how analytic coding and hermeneutic interpretation were conducted. In the Appendices, the “example quotes” column shows the process of analytic coding, and the “analysis” row presents the results of hermeneutic interpretation.

During Stage 3, after repeated analytic coding and hermeneutic interpretation, we identified the final, high-level concepts and integrated them into the affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes. Our process model was framed following the guidelines of Newman and Robey (1992), consisting of encounters, episodes, and outcomes. After the initial formulation of the process model, additional data were collected to verify our understanding of the model. Then, we divided the model into Time 1 and Time 2 to recognize the developmental process of episodes, associations, and outcomes. This division was necessary because the dialogues exhibit a similar pattern at Time 1, but fork into two different directions at Time 2 depending on the effectiveness of the intervention. We also finalized the patterns of social influence through further analytic coding and hermeneutic interpretation.

The data analysis and hermeneutic interpretation were preformed iteratively until theoretical saturation was reached and no additional insights were being generated. Overall, development of the main process model required five iterations (three major iterations for Stages 1, 2, and 3, and two minor iterations) to code the results. Because this approach develops nonparticipant-observation netnography, our mode of generalization moves from interpretative observations to theory (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Examples of other IS research using this mode of generalization include Mazmanian et al. (2014) and Sarker and Sahay (2004).

## 4. Results

The affect process model of IT consumer postpurchase outcomes is represented as a sequence of events that lead to postpurchase outcomes in Figure 2. The model is based on the process model framework proposed by Newman and Robey (1992). First, the process model requires antecedent conditions. We identify the following: (1) the infrastructure of online support forums; (2) the presence of regulators; (3) IT problems that draw customers to forums; and (4) customers who come to forums for problem solving. Second, events can be divided into encounters and episodes (Newman & Robey, 1992). An encounter is an event that drives an episode to change. An encounter can indicate where an episode begins or ends. We identify the two types of encounters. An episode is “a set of events that stand apart from others, thus signifying the end of one sequence of activities and the beginning of another”

(Newman & Robey, 1992, p. 253). Each episode moves the development in a certain direction and leads the entire discourse toward a certain outcome (Van de Ven, 2007). This development is needed to make causal connections among the events. There are four types of episodes evaluated at both Time 1 and Time 2. Last, outcomes are the consequence of a developmental sequence of prior events (Newman & Robey, 1992). In a process model, outcomes are not the same as dependent variables in a variance model, and also not entirely predictable from a sequence of prior events.

## 4.1. Affect Process Model of IT Postpurchase Outcomes

## 4.1.1. Encounters

Our process model has two types of encounters: (1) “initiator posts message”, and (2) regulation. Regulation is divided into: “company & experts make successful regulation”; and “company & experts make no/unsuccessful regulation”. The first encounter is made by an initiator who creates a dialogue and describes his/her experiences regarding a product failure. This encounter induces other participants to appraise the problem and share appraisal and affective experience with other participants. The second encounters are made by company technicians and voluntary community experts. Regulation by company denotes direct support from the company’s technicians, whereas regulation by experts indicates support from community experts with relevant experience and knowledge. Regulation comprises the two components of technical intervention and social intervention. Technical intervention refers to the formal activities made by the company and experts to assist participants on the forums (e.g., offering software updates and workarounds, directing customers to technology support centers to fix hardware). This intervention is analogous to organizational support (Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). Social intervention denotes the manner of offering technical intervention (e.g., timeliness of intervention, quality of communication). Technical intervention can be objectively assessed by looking at the status of problem solving at the end, but social intervention is subjectively evaluated by participants. Successful regulation of a dialogue takes place when technical and social interventions are properly made and most participants are satisfied with them. Successful regulation may lead to unsatisfied participants when social intervention is not commensurate with technical intervention. No/unsuccessful regulation results from improper technical and social interventions. Table 3 summarizes the findings in dialogues with respect to regulation, outcome, problem solving, participants, and social influence.

## 4.1.2. Episodes

We identify the four types of episodes occurring at both Time 1 and Time 2: (1) appraisal by individual participant—“participant (re)appraises IT problem”; (2) affective experience by individual participant— “participant has (positive/negative) affective experience”; (3) appraisal by EG—“participants (re)appraise IT problem”; and (4) affective experience by EG— “participants have (positive/negative) affective experience”. The first two episode types occur at the individual level, and the next two, at the EG level. The episode “participant(s) (re)appraises IT problem” is a cognitive component that indicates (re)evaluation concerning the failure. The episode “participant(s) has (positive/negative) affective experience” involves a broad affective component that encompasses: (1) a range of feeling experiences, such as discrete emotions and moods; (2) feeling traits, such as positive or negative affect; and (3) the level (low, high) of arousal. Arousal can be inferred from participants’ language expressions. Participants often express their arousal by using adverbs (e.g., “really” or “very” frustrated), repeated words (“never never buy again” or “!!!!!!!!!”), or capitalized words (“NEVER, EVER”) in the particular negative contexts. This inclusion of arousal in “affective experience” is consistent with dimensional emotion theorists who provide information about the level of arousal of each affect in a circumplex model (Russell & Barrett, 1999; Scherer & Ellgring, 2007). For example, they recognize that anger has a higher arousal level than frustration.

## 4.1.3. Postpurchase Outcome

One primary outcome at the end of the process model is identified: “participant forms postpurchase outcomes”. This outcome is expressed by individual participants at the end of the dialogue, and is action readiness (i.e., intentions) of: (1) move toward (repurchase, continuance, continuance with skepticism); (2) move away (no repurchase, discontinuance); and (3) move against (word-ofmouth, escalation, boycott). The participant’s postpurchase outcomes are influenced by the participant’s affective experience, which is also impacted by regulation and EG’s affective experiences. Participants in the same dialogue may express different action readiness depending on how the proposed solution addresses similar, but unique, situations of participants. These outcomes were readily observable from the sampled dialogues, but we could not infer the actual behaviors, and the vendors did not allow the researchers to contact the customers for such information. Employing intention as the outcome measure is consistent with service failure and recovery research (DeWitt, Nguyen, & Marshall, 2008; Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011; Van Vaerenbergh, Orsingher, Vermeir, & Lariviere, 2014).

Given the basic identification of theoretical elements, we now present the process model over Time 1 and Time 2. Time 1 and Time 2 refer to the first and second half of a dialogue, respectively. At the early stages (Time 1) of problem solving, each dialogue exhibits the similar pattern of asking questions, providing tips for troubleshooting, and showing empathy for other’s problems. However, these dialogues fork into two different directions at some point (Time 2) depending on the quality of intervention. As shown in Table 3, we assessed the outcome status (satisfactory vs. unsatisfactory) of each dialogue solely based on the outcome of technical intervention. This outcome status was clearly displayed by the mark “problem resolved” beside its title. Social intervention was singled out in determining the outcome status of a dialogue because it is difficult to find consensus on the outcome of social intervention among participants. Below, we explain the affect process model following the progressive development of events and their associations based on the outcome status (satisfactory vs. unsatisfactory). Small, annotated examples taken from the data are used to illustrate the major points in our model. Tables

D1-G1 in the Appendices provide additional, illustrative examples from the threaded dialogues.

## 4.2. Affect Process in Dialogues with Satisfactory Outcomes

4.2.1. Episode: IT problem (re)appraisal of individual participants and the EG

At Time 1, individual participants, including the initiator, typically express their concerns with respect to problem description, past endeavors, progress, and service quality of the company. As illustrated in the sample dialogue below, participants appraise the situation using a few dimensions in light of their goals (Frijda et al., 1989), including whether the problem is: (1) familiar (familiarity), (2) conducive to the user’s goal (goal conduciveness/obstructiveness), (3) under a controllable situation (controllability), (4) occurred in the past, and/or is occurring now (time of event), (5) expected (expectedness), (6) pleasant (pleasantness valence), (7) fair (fairness), and (8) changeable (modifiability).

(IN) I’m having some issues with my [product name] [= goal obstructiveness].… When I hold down keys on the keyboard, the repeat rate keeps slowing down every time [= time of event].... I end up just having to restore the keyboard settings… I tried to update the BIOS and drivers, but had no luck [= unpleasantness; unmodifiability]. (Notebook, keyboard problem, 8 postings) <sup>1</sup>

The initial posting is quickly picked up by others with similar problems and motivation, fostering the creation of a new emerging group. Participants engage in the appraisal by articulating the problem, confirming others’ concerns, sharing suggestions and workarounds, and building consensus on the problem and potential solutions (see Appendix E for representative quotes). The appraisals in the EG spawn further appraisals and influence individual participants’ appraisals (Association A1 in Figure 2). At Time 2, after successful resolution, initial negative appraisals are reappraised by participant(s) and transformed into positive ones (e.g., pleasant, modifiable, controllable, or goal conducive). However, positive appraisals are sometimes shared by a limited number of participants because not all participants are satisfied with the suggested solution (Association A2 in Figure2).

## 4.2.2. Episode: Affective experiences of individual participants and the EG

In this episode at Time 1, the negative appraisals of individual participants of the IT problem (e.g., unpleasantness, goal obstructiveness, uncontrollability) generate negative affect (e.g., annoyance, disappointment) (Association B1) whereas, at Time 2, their positive appraisals (e.g., pleasant, goal conduciveness, modifiability) after successful resolution, result in positive affect (e.g., happiness, relief) (Association B2 in Figure 2).

(PT) Same issue here [= goal obstructiveness]. I have had no success with the fixes mentioned here. [= unmodifiability]. When I contacted [company] via email they wanted me to spend hundreds of dollars to fix this design flaw (warranty expired a couple months ago)!… I will NOT be giving [company] any more of my money [= unfairness]. I am getting really angry [=affective experience] with [company] and their unsatisfactory customer service [= unpleasantness]. (Notebook, treble control problem, 6 postings)

The appraisals of Time 1 among the group participants gradually converge, forming shared appraisals of the problem. Shared negative appraisals (e.g., goal obstructiveness) give rise to shared negative affective experiences (e.g., a negative affect with high arousal) (Association C1 in Figure 2). After successful resolution at Time 2, group participants reevaluate the situation and express positive appraisals, inducing positive affective experiences. However, positive appraisals do not always lead to positive affective experiences for all group participants because the discovered solution is not applicable to some participants’ problems (Association C2). To describe the relationship between appraisal and affective experience, we adapt a circumplex model of affect (Figure 3) (Barsade & Gibson, 2007; Russell, 1980; Russell & Barrett, 1999). It displays appraisal and affective experience in a circular graph, with the x-axis capturing the pleasantness level of affect (pleasantness vs. unpleasantness for simplicity) and the y-axis showing the arousal level of affect (low to high). The left side of the x-axis shows negative appraisal; the right side, positive appraisal. The y-axis captures the arousal dimension of affect as a continuum from low to high. Positive (negative) affective experience is found in the area in which the pleasantness (unpleasantness) and arousal level dimensions are combined (Zhang, 2013).

A circumplex model shows the relationship between variables in a circular array along two orthogonal axes (e.g., McGrath, 1984). ## 4.2.3. Encounter: Successful regulation by company and experts

At Time 2, the encounter made by company technicians and community experts influences individual participants and the EG by making them reappraise the problem (Associations E2a and E2c) and reformulate affective experiences (Associations E2b and E2d). Successful problem solving, backed by proper technical (e.g., offering solutions) and social interventions (e.g., timely response, consistent communications), facilitates the transformation of negative appraisals and affective experiences into positive ones, hence leading to satisfactory outcomes. The participants relate their happiness and gratitude to the support provided by the regulators. However, the social influence of such renewed positive appraisals and affective experiences is limited because the problem that is shared among the participants varies in details (e.g., different warranty periods, different levels of technical skills required to fix the problem, or varying statuses of the product).

Company technicians and community experts provide both technical and social interventions for all the participants, but their impact is not identical. As expected, for problem solving, participants rely more on company technicians who, as authorized troubleshooting personnel, have greater resources to support. Thus, participants are more strongly influenced by company technicians in reappraising the problem and reformulating affective experiences. Experts play a limited role in transforming participants’ affect from negative to positive. Even if a solution is introduced by an expert, participants whose problems were resolved would still express negative appraisals and negative affective experience toward the company and its product. The comments below illustrate how, after the intervention by a company technician, participants rely more on the technician than the expert, sometimes even ignoring the expert’s suggestion.

(RG-company) I have received everyone’s feedback.… Happy to see things are headed in the right direction. I know all of us want to get these problems worked out… An engineer with our company has looked at all the postings on this issue… We are hoping to start testing next week once all the info is gathered. Thank you all for your participation and patience.

(PT46) RG-Company, I’m happy you have decided to give this issue some attention.… If I can help troubleshoot problems I’m happy to do so… (Notebook, audio problem, 160 postings)

(RG-expert) Try this for updating your driver in your system: [weblink]. (Notebook, audio, 3876 postings)

(PT48) RG-Company, thank you for addressing this. Do you have any information about [company]’s policy on permitting notebook owners to access [product name]’s reference drivers? (Notebook, audio problem, 7 postings)

Besides technical intervention, social intervention is also important. Improper social intervention (e.g., time delay) tends to cancel out the positive effect of proper technical intervention. When the response is made promptly, negative appraisals and affective experiences are not fully developed among the participants and participants focus more on the interaction with the company than the influence of other participants, even when experts are involved. In contrast, when the company’s regulation is delayed, the effect of its regulation is diminished, even after successful resolution. When the response is delayed considerably, the participants are already being influenced by others’ appraisals and affective experiences during the delay. Also, despite the resolution, some participants suffer a lingering effect from negative appraisals and affect. In the example below, although a resolution is provided by a company technician after five months, some participants are still skeptical, indicating diminished faith in the company.

(RG-company) An official tip was just released today as well as a new production version of the driver… I’ll label this thread “solved” so that others can find it in the forum more easily.

(PT53) I know many of us wasted so much time trying to fix the problem with customer service or agonizing over crashes before coming to the Forum to find a temporary solution. Since the problem was first identified [date], the [company] still makes people pay extra for things such as the useless graphics adapters and operating systems. (Notebook, display driver problem, 5 postings)

(PT56) I understand where you’re coming from... even if this new driver fixes the issue, [Company] did sell faulty machinery to its customers for 5-6 months with full knowledge of what they were doing. ## 4.2.4. Outcome: Postpurchase Outcomes

Postpurchase outcomes (i.e., intentions) are classified into the three types of action readiness: move toward, move away, and move against. For simplicity, we classify repurchase and continuance (with skepticism) intentions into the same category of “move toward” because participants who express continuance intentions are likely to have positive impressions about a company and return as repeat customers. Similarly, no repurchase and discontinuance (e.g., returning the product to the company or otherwise disposing of it) intentions are classified into the category of “move away”. Word-of-mouth, escalation, and boycott intentions are placed in the category of “move against”. In the dialogues with satisfactory outcome, as shown below, individual participants’ positive affective experiences induce “move toward” (association F2).

(IN) [RG-company1] and [RG-company2] are bringing back my trust in your company…. I am considering buying another [company’s] laptop again. (Notebook, wireless problem, 10 postings)

(PT) I finally got it to work using a [product model #]. The key was a specific specification explained by [RGcompany1] in a previous posting.… I now got it to work beautifully. (Notebook, wireless problem, 5 postings)

Table 4 shows the relationship between affective experiences and postpurchase outcomes. Before successful resolution, participants (including initiators) with negative affective experiences show the move away tendencies of no repurchase (NoRP) and discontinuance (DC). Furthermore, they exhibit the move against tendencies of word-of-mouth (WM) and escalation (ES). Negative affect (e.g., anger, annoyance, and frustration) drives such action readiness.

## 5. Discussion

Our affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes presents interesting social dynamics among major actors in an online support. The model suggests that participants are influenced by: (1) their own appraisals and affective experiences; (2) appraisals and affective experiences of other participants via social interactions, and (3) regulation by the company and experts. When the dialogue has a satisfactory outcome because of successful regulation, participants experience weak social influence, are fully satisfied, or are somewhat unsatisfied, leading to positive postpurchase outcomes. “Somewhat unsatisfied” is specifically observed when social intervention is not appropriate, despite technical intervention. When the dialogue has an unsatisfactory outcome, participants are influenced by strong social influence and are unsatisfied, leading to negative postpurchase outcomes. The model reveals that, given the problemsolving context, the influence of negative affect becomes stronger when dissatisfied consumers increase their social interactions and negative forces spread to others. Thus, our model overcomes cognition-oriented models on postpurchase behaviors by properly recognizing the importance of both cognition and affect.

Further analyses of our data provide insights into the patterns of social influence across Time 1 and Time 2 (Table A1). The patterns in each association are gained by observing the recurring themes during collaborative problem solving. The patterns reveal great variations of social influence depending on the resolution status across Time 1 and Time 2. In addition, we identify the three types of cognitive and affective social influence (i.e., transformation, intensification, and contagion) as acting mechanisms that characterize the patterns of social influence. When considering social influence, we focus on the aspect of outcome, not process, which can be easily traced chronologically.

We provided the theoretical background for the concepts of cognitive and affective social influence (i.e., transformation, intensification, and contagion). These concepts need some qualifications and elaboration to make better sense in our empirical context. Cognitive transformation (Klein & Baxter, 2009) is broadly defined as changes in one’s perception. However, we use the concept more narrowly to focus mainly on changes in a point of view (Mezirow, 2000). Affective transformation is employed in psychotherapy studies and used in this research to trace changes in the pleasantness feeling trait (see Barsade & Gibson, 2007). To trace affective intensification, we capture affect with respect to valence (pleasantness) and arousal (Barsade & Gibson, 2007). Affective intensification is traced by the change in the arousal level of affect (e.g., from upset to enraged). In Figure 3, positive affective intensification takes place as the affect moves on an arc from 180 degrees to zero degrees (counterclockwise). Negative affective intensification occurs as the affect moves on the arc in the direction from 180 degrees to 360 degrees (clockwise). Affective contagion is the result of “a process in which a person or group influences the affect or behavior of another person or group through the conscious or unconscious induction of affect states and behavioral attitudes” (Schoenewolf, 1990, p. 50). In a face-to-face context affective contagion is made through nonverbal signals (e.g., facial expression, body language) (Barsade, 2002). In an online environment, although participants cannot observe others’ nonverbal activities, they are still prone to affective contagion (Raafat et al., 2009). Affective contagion is prominent in online support forums in which participants read others’ postings, capture negative feelings of others expressed in the postings, and share (and synchronize) their affective experiences with others by communicating through the (re)postings, ultimately converging on promoting postpurchase intentions of moving away.

The associations in our model entail different types of influence (Tables A1 and A2). Associations A2 and A3 involve the three types of cognitive influence (i.e., cognitive transformation, cognitive intensification, and cognitive contagion). In associations C2 and C3, the three types of cognitive influence lead to the three types of affective influence (i.e., affective transformation, affective intensification, and affective contagion). Associations D2 and D3 contain the three types of affective influence, although affective contagion is much stronger. Associations E2c and E3c include cognitive transformation, cognitive intensification, and cognitive contagion, whereas associations E2d and E3d involve affective transformation, affective intensification, and affective contagion.

## 5.1. Social Influences on Participants’ Problem Reappraisals and Affective Experiences

This section describes how the three types of social influence impact the participants’ and EG’s problem reappraisals and affective experiences, and in turn, postpurchase outcomes. (See Appendices C and D for representative quotes.)

## 5.1.1. EG’s problem reappraisals on PTs’ problem reappraisals (associations A2 and A3)

At Time 1, the EG’s social influence facilitates problem solving and the development of negative appraisals of a company. The participants explore potential solutions by articulating the problem, sharing their experiences, and building consensus on potential solutions. They also promote negative appraisals about the product and company while striving to understand the root causes of the problem.

At Time 2, the impacts of EG’s problem reappraisals are crucial in shaping further directions of the dialogue. Three types of social influence emerge: cognitive transformation, cognitive intensification, and cognitive contagion. When a satisfactory outcome is gained, cognitive transformation occurs. That is, the participants’ reappraisals change into goal conducive, pleasant, modifiable, controllable, or fair. Also, cognitive contagion gradually emerges as the group participants share their positive reappraisals after the problem solving and start building consensus on the positive reappraisals. However, cognitive contagion is limited because the offered solution is not applicable to all the problems exposed in the dialogue (Barsade, 2002). In contrast, when a satisfactory outcome is not obtained, cognitive intensification is frequently observed, while participants continue to evaluate the problem and analyze test results. The data show more cognitive intensification in the unsatisfactory outcome dialogues than in the satisfactory outcome dialogues (the average number of pages being 21.8 vs. 9.1; the average number of postings being 211.5 vs. 86.2). In turn, negative cognitive contagion is formed among the group participants. Compared to the dialogues with satisfactory outcomes, negative cognitive contagion is accomplished much faster and is more widespread because the group participants quickly form consensus on the negative appraisals. (See Appendix G for negative cognitive contagion.)

Table 5 presents the changes in cognitive (re)appraisals over time, leading to cognitive transformation and cognitive contagion. Cognitive transformation is clearly observed in the appraisal dimensions of goal conduciveness and pleasantness when the dialogue reaches a satisfactory outcome. Cognitive contagion is evident in the appraisal dimensions of goal obstructiveness, uncontrollability, unmodifiability, or unpleasantness when the dialogue reaches an unsatisfactory outcome.

## 5.1.2. EG’s problem reappraisals on EG’s affective experiences (associations C2 and C3)

The EG’s problem reappraisal influences the EG’s affective experience differently depending on Time 1 and Time 2 of problem solving. At Time 1, the cognitive influence induces negative affect among group participants. At Time 2, the group participants experience affective transformation, affective intensification, and affective contagion. Our analysis suggests the following interactions among the social influence types.

First, negative cognitive transformation fosters the development of negative affective transformation. Second, affective contagion draws on affective intensification. Third, affective contagion is influenced by cognitive contagion. Finally, negative affective contagion is more frequently observed than positive affective contagion. Positive affective contagion is limited to participants who are fully satisfied with the suggested solution.

At Time 2, after a satisfactory outcome has been realized, the group participants display positive affect (e.g., happiness and relief), leading to positive affective transformation. Furthermore, positive affective contagion is observed but its reach is limited because of the restricted applicability of the proposed solution. Thus, positive appraisals and positive affect do not easily converge to build consensus. In contrast, when a satisfactory outcome is not reached participants show affect with a higher arousal level (e.g., anger and rage) more frequently (Figure 3), showing affective intensification. Besides, the group participants share negative appraisals and build consensus, displaying affective contagion.

## 5.1.3. EG’s affective experiences on PTs’ affective experiences (associations D2 and D3)

The EG’s affective experience impacts individuals’ affective experiences. This finding is consistent with studies suggesting that an individual’s affect is influenced by others’ affect (Van Kleef et al., 2011). All three types of affective influence are observed in Association D2 (limited) and D3 (fully). However, in unsatisfactory outcome dialogues, the Association D3 involves a stronger affective contagion than Association C3, spreading negative affect with a higher arousal level (e.g., anger, rage). The participants who displayed annoyance and frustration became angry and enraged while noticing strong affective expressions from other participants.

## 5.1.4. Regulation on PTs’ and EG’s problem reappraisals and affective experiences (associations E2a-E2d and E3a-E3d)

Regulation plays a central role in changing the problem appraisals and affective experiences of participants and thus postpurchase outcomes. Our analysis shows that regulation has differential effects depending on the intervention stages. First, when regulation is made at the early stages, regulators provide potential solutions in a timely manner and satisfy most participants in the dialogue. Thus, regulation leads to the softening of negative appraisals and affect about the company. However, when regulation is made at Time 2 with a successful outcome, regulators foster positive cognitive and affective transformation and contagion. However, such positive impacts are sometimes limited because the proposed solution is not always applicable to the similar, but different, situation of each participant. Further, while regulation is delayed participants’ cognition and affect are negatively contaminated. Thus, improper social intervention may more than offset the positive effect of successful technical intervention. Second, company technicians provide a stronger influence on positive cognitive and affective transformation and contagion over community experts. They are capable of refunding a product, releasing patches, or sending the product to a customer center for repair.

## 5.2. Social Influences on Postpurchase Outcomes

Associations F2 and F3 indicate the social influence of participants’ affective experiences on postpurchase outcomes. This influence has different implications across the dialogues with satisfactory and unsatisfactory outcomes.

## 5.2.1. Participants’ affective experiences on postpurchase outcomes (associations F2 and F3)

The problem resolution status strongly affects the formation of participants’ postpurchase outcomes. In the dialogues with satisfactory outcomes, only a portion of the participants share positive affective transformation and contagion, displaying limited social influence. Subsequently, a move toward cannot be easily inferred from positive affective transformation and contagion. In the dialogues with unsatisfactory outcomes, most participants show negative affective intensification and affective contagion. In such dialogues negative affective intensification and contagion become good indicators of a move away or a move against (see Appendix H for negative affective contagion).

## 5.3. Implications for Research

## 5.3.1. Online Support Communities

This study makes several contributions to online support community research. Theoretically, the affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes identifies affect (in addition to cognition) as a crucial driver of postpurchase outcomes in online support communities. This is especially true when negative affect dominates dialogues after IT problem appraisals. Satisfactory outcomes lead to positive affect, fostering repurchase/continuance intentions. In contrast, unsatisfactory outcomes induce negative affect, that becomes stronger over time (e.g., from annoyed to angry/enraged) and contributes to no repurchase or discontinuance intentions. In addition, stronger affect sometimes drives the dialogue to a new, drastic direction of antipurchase intentions. Our results show that affect can be a more influential driver than cognition in situations such as product failure encounters, especially when product failure is not properly addressed. Our model is consistent with Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s (2005) coping model of user adaptation. That is, when an IT event is perceived as a threat, users’ adaptation strategies are affectfocused. Our findings suggest that this is also the case within the context of online support communities. Postpurchase research is mostly concerned with cognitive social influence (Venkatesh et al., 2012). Our results challenge this view by arguing that affect may be a more influential factor when affect is salient (Greifeneder et al., 2011) and can better represent individuals’ inner states toward action readiness. Thus, our findings extend our understanding of postpurchase decision-making in the context of online support communities.

This process model advances previous online support communities research by explicating the value creation process associated with problem solving. Although indirect benefits of online support have been examined (Petouhoff, Band, & Magarie, 2009; Petouhoff, Gliedman, et al., 2009), few studies have addressed the problem-solving process with direct value creation outcomes. Understanding consumer postpurchase intentions at the end of the problem-solving process is crucial, considering the fact that poor customer service may cause consumer switching (Wollan et al., 2014) and consumers today constantly reevaluate their (re)purchase choices (Wollan et al., 2014).

## 5.3.2. Affect Process Model of IT Postpurchase Outcomes

Frijda’s model of emotion processes (2007; 1989) focuses on the emotions of an individual from stimulus event, appraisal, affect, action readiness to behavior. The proposed affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes enhances Frijda’s model. First, research (Bagozzi et al., 1998; Frijda et al., 1989) that draws on Frijda’s emotion model adopts a variance approach, whereas our research employs a process approach that presents the progressive development of events and their associations. Second, our model offers a multilevel analysis, reflecting the reality that affect is constantly impacted by surrounding social forces. Finally, we identify various patterns and types of social influence (Tables A1 and A2) that have not been investigated in other studies.

Service failure and recovery research similarly deal with the relationships among cognition, affect, and regulation (Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011; Stephens & Gwinner, 1998; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2014). The affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes extends this research by developing a longitudinal model, involving more complicated problems than previously addressed in service recovery (e.g., simple service issues at a hotel). It reveals how a product failure encounter can become uncontrollable if not contained early. In addition, our process model provides a multilevel analysis involving individuals and groups for the affective process. This multilevel recognition is timely considering that approximately 33 percent of consumer spending can be impacted by social media (Chui et al., 2012). Furthermore, our model offers a further extension to the previous research by integrating consumer postcomplaint handling (Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011) and service recovery process models (Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2014) to create a holistic picture for organizational response (i.e., intervention) and its impact on consumer complaint behaviors. Like postcomplaint handling research (Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011), our research recognizes organizational response but brings it forward by delineating its influence on consumers’ cognition and affect. Our model also extends previous service recovery research (Stephens & Gwinner, 1998) by considering intervention as a crucial component in shaping various postpurchase outcomes in a transforming, weakening, or strengthening manner.

## 5.3.3. Social influence and intervention

This research recognizes social influence by focusing on subjective norms (Liao & Keng, 2013). It increases our understanding of social influence by identifying influential social actors (i.e., participants, EGs, and regulation) as well as types of social influence (i.e., cognitive/affective transformation, intensification, and contagion). As for social actors, regulation and the EG are found to be powerful sources that shape participants’ cognitions and affect respectively. Our findings indicate that although the EG is a voluntary, transient unit, it exerts a strong influence that can shift participants’ postpurchase outcomes in unexpected directions.

Given the temporariness of the emergent group, the question arises as to why it is so strong in affecting others’ postpurchase outcomes. To better understand the social influence mechanisms of an EG, we classify the nature of the three types of social influence (cognitive transformation, cognitive intensification, and cognitive contagion) into informational or normative. Informational influence occurs when one takes the arguments of others as the basis for reality, whereas normative influence takes place when one complies with another’s request (Price, Nir, & Cappella, 2006). First, cognitive (affective) transformation reflects informational influence because participants’ transformation requires the accurate assessment of the situation after regulators’ intervention. Second, cognitive (affective) intensification indicates a mix of informational and normative influence. Intensification first requires cognitive assessment of whether a situation is getting better or worse. Then, the EG’s goal forms the normative climate of group problem solving (Rugs & Kaplan, 1993), and makes the participants engage in continued discursive dialogue (Price et al., 2006). Also, the affect of the group gradually spreads as the participants continue to engage in group problem solving. This emerging group affect exerts normative affective influence on the participants (Wood & Bettman, 2007). Finally, cognitive (affective) contagion reflects normative influence. At the end of the dialogue, the group participants’ goal is elevated from problem solving to obtaining proper rights as consumers. This elevation fosters the formation of affect rules or an agreed-on expectation of affect, providing a stronger normative influence on the participants (Wood & Bettman, 2007), and influencing the thoughts and expectations of the participants.

Our results indicate that the powerful influence of the EG on participants is attributed to the transition of influence from informational to normative over time. This finding advances the understanding of social influence in online support community research that identifies social influence mainly in the form of normative influence (e.g., group norms, social identity) (Dholakia et al., 2004; Dholakia et al., 2009). Our results imply that normative influence is gradually shaped through the process from the inception of informational influence (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004) to its transition to normative influence. Thus, the snapshot of a certain type of social influence may not properly portray the full range of social forces over time.

This research also shows the importance of social intervention. Despite successful outcome, some participants may not be fully satisfied. This occurs when improper social intervention (e.g., time delay, discontinuous communication) eclipses the positive effect of technical intervention. Companies may assume that if the technical intervention is successful, their customers will be almost fully satisfied and become repeat customers. However, our results indicate that some customers are negatively affected by improper social intervention. Proper social intervention helps to establish good relationships between the company and customers and restore trust in the company and products (Tolar & Balka, 2011). On the contrary, delayed support or discontinued communication may lead customers to no recourse (Bretthauer, 2002), prompting anger and distrust in the company and its product.

## 5.4. Implications for Practice

Companies need to manage their online reputations with their customers. For this, companies first need to recognize that online support communities can influence a company positively or negatively. Specifically, a company can: (1) find a solution for a problem via online conversations with customers; (2) evaluate the effectiveness of existing IT products; and (3) obtain useful ideas for future products. Solving a customer’s problem sooner is better than later, but if a problem cannot be solved a company should strive to decrease its negative effects. When a company does not properly manage its online communities, these communities can become venues where the company’s brand image is eroded as negative feedback from customers accumulates and spreads and unexpected, drastic behaviors are fostered by dissatisfied or angry customers.

Regulation by experts within the company as well as by those who participate in the forum has important implications. The timing of an intervention is crucial. When regulation is carried out during the early stages of a dialogue, negative appraisals and affect can be softened and possibly reversed. However, when regulation is delayed beyond the tolerance limits of the participants, the company loses its influence in reversing the negative appraisals and affect of frustrated customers. Companies need to monitor the progress of customers’ appraisals and affect to determine the right time to intervene. Sentiment analysis tools (e.g., automated sentiments and opinion mining tools) (Abbasi, Chen, & Salem, 2008) might be useful in assessing the level of negative emotions (sentiments). When a company decides to intervene in a dialogue, it should provide resources until a successful resolution is reached. Such resolution may contribute to positive affect and high brand loyalty.

Affect is constantly impacted by social forces. There are various patterns and types of social influence, as identified in Tables A1 and A2. To better comprehend both affect and social influence, companies may want to add features that indicate affect (e.g., “thumbs up/thumbs down”) and the accuracy/usefulness of the comments offered by others in a dialogue (e.g., 1-5 scale). Then, companies should be able to better monitor the status of affect and social influence and their spreading, coming up with countermeasures more quickly and accurately. Decreasing negative affect in later stages is difficult. A better strategy might be to monitor the degree of negative affect using the above-mentioned features and to act early to mitigate it as much as possible.

Given that customers can be influenced by social interaction, another challenge is managing bad information. For example, a participant might provide poor or incomplete advice. Companies have policies stating they are not responsible for the information posted on the forum, but may need to monitor the forums to identify such situations or raise the participants’ awareness of potential misinformation.

The inclusion of a user profile on a form can indicate a participant’s level of expertise and the number of posts provided. Additionally, moderators may be needed to sanitize and remove negative or inappropriate reviews. Overall, companies recognize the benefits of online support forms, but also need to recognize the limits of these forms and the needed resource to monitor them so that they remain useful for customers.

## 5.5. Limitations and Future Research

First, the data were collected from the postings of community participants with technical problems. Other consumers who encountered similar technical problems may have utilized alternative outlets (e.g., Geek Squad at Best Buy, Tech Support Services at Staples). Hence, our dataset represents a subset of consumers who chose to make use of online support communities. Second, the influence of affective experience on problem appraisal was not salient in our data analysis; the coding indicated only a dependency (affect depended on appraisal). This relationship might be more interactive in other contexts. Finally, our model has applicability in explaining the diverse continuance tendencies of information system users in organizations. These users may attempt to use new features of a system or apply existing features in novel work contexts, and then require attention from experts or help desks (Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005). If their requests are not properly met over time, their information system continuance tendencies may be greatly influenced by their and others’ affect. Further research is necessary to extend this study to understand such implications in organizational settings.

The units of analysis were the individual and the emergent group. This was sufficient for studying the affect process of the user and community. However, there are other possible units of analysis for this data; for example, a message or dialogue. Such an analysis might yield a variance-style cause-and-effect model as a companion to our process model. Future research is needed to study dialogues and causality in the processes.

Our circumplex model represents a common approach to inferring the degree of arousal and pleasuredispleasure in emotion studies (Barsade & Gibson 2007; Russell, 1980; Russell & Barrett, 1999). Still, as an online study, our observations were limited to text only. Affect is extrapolated in our study on the basis of previous work in psychology and in marketing. Technological advances may enable future research to make more direct observations of user emotions by using the emerging range of voice-controlled speakers, such as Amazon Echo and Google Home.

## 6. Conclusion

This research has developed an affect process model of IT postpurchase outcomes that captures the interplay among cognition, affect, and regulation that influences consumers’ postpurchase outcomes. This study explores the problem-solving process in online support communities, identifying the major events and associations that reflect the development of the dialogues for postpurchase outcomes. The results show that regulation and the emergent group, even though it is a voluntary, transient unit, have a strong influence on participants and can shift their postpurchase intentions in, perhaps, surprising directions.

## About the Authors

EunHee Park is an assistant professor of information technology and decision sciences at Old Dominion University. She received her PhD degree in computer information systems (CIS) from the Georgia State University. Her research interests include IT investment decision, agile project management, IS security, social media, sentiment analysis, emotion ontology, and IT adoption in health care. Her work has appeared in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Information and Management, Information Systems Journal, Computers & Security, and Information Technology and People.

Ghiyoung Im is an associate professor in the Department of Computer Information Systems at the University of Louisville. He received his PhD from Georgia State University and an MS from the New York University Stern School of Business. His research interests center on IT-enabled organizational learning, knowledge management, and cybersecurity, and their implications for firm strategy and interfirm relationships. His research has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems, among others.

Veda C. Storey is the Tull Professor of Computer Information Systems and professor of computer science at the J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University. Her research interests are in intelligent information systems, data management, conceptual modeling, and design science research. Dr. Storey is a member of the Association for Information Systems College of Senior Scholars, an Association for Information Systems Fellow, and an advisor to the Workshop on Information Technologies and Systems. She is also a member of the steering committee of the International Conference of Conceptual Modeling, where she has the honor of being an ER Fellow and a recipient of the Peter P. Chen Award. She received a Georgia State University Teaching Innovation Award for her work on experiential and interdisciplinary teaching. Dr. Storey received her PhD from the University of British Columbia and holds a degree in flute performance from the Royal Conservatory of Music, University of Toronto.

Richard L. Baskerville is the Regents’ Professor and Board of Advisors Professor of Information Systems at Georgia State University and professor in the School of Information Systems at Curtin University, Perth, Australia. His research regards security of information systems, methods of information systems design and development, and the interaction of information systems and organizations. Baskerville is editor emeritus of the European Journal of Information Systems. He is a Chartered Engineer and holds a BS summa cum laude from the University of Maryland, and an MSc and PhD from the London School of Economics. His honors include a PhD (hc) from the University of Pretoria, and a DSc (hc) from Roskilde University.
