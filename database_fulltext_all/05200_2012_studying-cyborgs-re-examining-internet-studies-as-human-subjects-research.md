## Abstract

Virtual communities and social networks assume and consume more aspects of people’s lives. In these evolving social spaces, the boundaries between actual and virtual reality, between living individuals and their virtual bodies, and between private and public domains are becoming ever more blurred. As a result, users and their presentations of self, as expressed through virtual bodies, are increasingly entangled. Consequently, more and more Internet users are cyborgs. For this reason, the ethical guidelines necessary for Internet research need to be revisited. We contend that the IS community has paid insufficient attention to the ethics of Internet research. To this end, we develop an understanding of issues related to online human subjects research by distinguishing between a disembodied and an entangled view of the Internet. We outline a framework to guide investigators and research ethics committees in answering a key question in the age of cyborgism: When does a proposed Internet study deal with human subjects as opposed to digital material?

## Tastes, Ties and Time: a Facebook study

Sue Ash, a member of the Harvard Class of 2009, was doing research for a social science project in the Widener Library when Dan, a member of another class, stopped by her table. They briefly exchanged pleasantries and then Dan asked ‘How did you like Lord of the Rings? A pretty good movie, huh?’ Somewhat nonplused Sue hesitated and then said ‘Fine.’ I hardly know Dan. How did he know I’ve seen the movie? ‘I take it your enthusiasm for Verity Prices’s music grew when you were in South Africa. True?’ ‘Yea’ Where did he get that?!? ‘There are a lot of people living with HIV/AIDS in South Africa. I’ll bet that while you were working for that foundation you encountered a lot of them. Did you strike up any personal relationships while you were down there?’ What’s going on here?!? Flummoxed and outraged Sue pushed her papers into her backpack and, snubbing Dan, stormed out of Widener. Later she told a friend that she was somewhat frightened by the whole interchange with Dan. His facts were true and she recalled that she had shared them with her friends on Facebook. Nevertheless, she found her encounter with Dan menacing. She also felt that her reputation was more than a little threatened by the possible implications of his remarks. Her privacy had been violated and she wanted to know how it happened. Had she inadvertently friended him on Facebook?

his is a fictional vignette but it is based on a real research project, one that continues to raise severe questions concerning the ethics of Internet research. Funded by NSF, in 2006 a team of Harvard and UCLA sociologists began an innovative social science research project called ‘Tastes, Ties and Time’ (T3) the purpose of which was to model the social network dynamics of a large group of students – 1640 in total – by downloading and analyzing Facebook data for an entire freshman class. Data from this cohort was acquired by research assistants like Dan in the vignette above, who downloaded students’ profile information every March for the 4 years the class was at an ‘anonymous’ university. This information was also associated with other sources of data. According to The Chronicle of Higher Education (Parry, 2011), the researchers ‘downloaded each student’s gender, home state, major, political views, network of friends, and romantic tastes. To determine race and ethnicity, they examined photographs and club affiliations; they recorded who appeared in student’s photo albums; and they culled cultural tastes like books, music, and movies (top film: Lord of the Rings).’

The T3 project has been hailed as a major breakthrough in the study of how race and culture affect relationships in college and how friendships and interests evolve over time. In the view of principal investigator, Jason Kaufman, the data set constituted a ‘complete social universe’ and, therefore, provided a unique resource for doing sociological research. He also claimed that concerted efforts were made to anonymize the data and that sensitive data had been redacted to minimize the risk of personal identification, as under the rules of the grant that had funded the research the data set needed to be made available to other researchers. In light of these protections, the Harvard’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved that the students would not be informed that they were subjects in the T3 research project. Kaufman and his colleagues argued that doing so might ‘frighten people unnecessarily.’ Professor Kaufman recalled, ‘We all agreed that it was not necessary, either legally or ethically [to get informed consent]’ (Parry, 2011).

Yet, within days of the first release of the T3 data set, Michael Zimmer, current Director of the Center for Information Policy Research and Assistant Professor at the University of Wisconsin Milwaukee’s School of Information Studies, was able to identify the school as Harvard. His method was straightforward (Zimmer, 2010). The source of the T3 data set was described as a ‘private college in the Northeast US.’ There were 1640 students in the class of 2009 whose data was downloaded for the study. First, Zimmer determined that only seven private, co-ed colleges met that description. Suspecting it was Harvard College he then acquired the online codebook that related in detail how the data set was collected and what it contained. From this, it was possible to determine both the way housing was assigned and what majors were offered, such as Sanskrit, evolutionary biology and women’s studies, and the only school of the seven candidates that satisfied all of the elements was Harvard. He then downloaded the data set and began to examine it to determine individual identities. For example, there was only one student from Wyoming and just three from Utah.

It turned out that several of the graduate research assistants who had downloaded the data knew some of the participants and, importantly, had access to Harvard’s internal Facebook accounts. This discovery made it possible to reveal even more information about the subjects. Indeed, a reporter from the Chronicle of Higher Education was easily able locate the three students from Utah and uncover considerable personally identifiable information (PII) about them. One student thus identified was Sarah M. Ashburn, a 24-year-old working for a foundation in Haiti that helps AIDS victims. So far, none of the student subjects has complained that they have been substantially harmed by the breach but most are uncomfortable and wary. Some believe that they had a right to be informed that they were participants in the study.

## Research ethics in internet studies

The Internet to us is not something external to reality but a part of it: an invisible yet constantly present layer intertwined with the physical environment. We do not use the Internet, we live on the Internet and along it. y there was a natural Internet aspect to every single experience that has shaped us.

– We, the Webkids, Manifesto by Piotr Czerski<sup>1</sup>

Ethics is a social construct that reflects a group’s culture and value system. Value systems change as a result of larger impending forces such as new knowledge and new technologies (Thomas, 2004). Consequently, what is considered acceptable ethical behavior shifts over time and it needs periodically to be re-evaluated and re-examined.

Technology is a key driver of social change and, as the excerpt of the Webkids manifesto above indicates, in recent years Internet technologies, particularly Web 2.0<sup>2</sup> applications, have increased the entanglement between users and the technologies they employ (Madge and O’Connor, 2005; Simpson, 2005; Van Doorn, 2011). This implies that people’s identities (i.e., who they are) are increasingly hybrid, simultaneously performed in physical and digital spaces. They are cyborgs<sup>3</sup>, that is, human beings whose bodies are extended through technology (Borer, 2002) and whose identities are entangled with technology (Introna, 2007; Nyberg, 2009).

With Web 2.0 technologies, the Internet has become a primary site for social interaction, a place people rely on more and more to learn about themselves and to make meaningful connections with others (Jensen Schau and Gilly, 2003; McCullagh, 2008). As people spend more time online and invest more of themselves in virtual communities and relationships, Web 2.0 applications inherently entangle them with the digital material that results from their online activities. Who they are, what they can do and who they can become is inextricably intertwined with the identities their virtual bodies (e.g., emails, texts, images, videos, profiles and avatars) afford (boyd, 2006). These virtual bodies are psychological extensions and projections of each user’s identity (Grimes et al., 2010).

In light of this increasing entanglement between users’ physical and virtual bodies, as well as the greater likelihood of user activism facilitated by Web 2.0 applications (e.g., Facebook has been the target of a number of vociferous, user-led backlashes regarding its privacy policy over the years<sup>4</sup>), we contend that there is currently insufficient discussion, at least in information systems (IS), about the ethics of Internet research. The Association of Information Systems’ (AIS) Code of Research Conduct<sup>5</sup>, for instance, classifies plagiarism as a ‘Category One’ violation, that is, one that must never be committed, while it relegates ‘respect[ing] the rights of research subjects’ to a ‘Category Two’ violation, that is, one that requires only recommended ethical behavior.

Also, recent papers on ethical conduct in both Internet and IS research more generally (Allen et al., 2011; Allen et al., 2006) fail to address the issue of human subjects research in online settings. And research in online domains (e.g., Herring et al., 2004; Kane et al., 2009; Vaast, 2007) is frequently remiss in clarifying the ethical reasoning underlying the investigators’ methodological decisions.

About a decade ago, there was considerable debate about the ethics of online research. For example, a special issue on Internet research ethics appeared in The Information Society in 1996 (Thomas, 1996). Authors took different positions on whether Internet research involved human subjects and what kinds of protections should be afforded members of online communities (Herring, 1996; King, 1996; Reid, 1996; Waskul and Douglass, 1996). In 1999, the National Institutes of Health and American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS) funded a workshop devoted to the development of a report entitled ‘Ethical and Legal Aspects of Human Subjects Research on the Internet’ (Frankel and Siang, 1999); and in 2002, a special issue on Internet research ethics appeared in Ethics and Information Technology (Ess, 2002). At around the same time, the Association of Internet Researchers formulated guidelines for ethical research online (AoIR, 2002).

Since then, however, comparatively little attention has been paid in the literature to human subjects issues raised by Internet research. Yet, with the proliferation of Web 2.0 technologies, smart mobile devices and ‘apps,’ the technological landscape has changed considerably in recent years. Given the pervasiveness of mobile technologies and of technology-mediated social interaction in contemporary society (e.g., Facebook reported 900 million registered users in June 2012<sup>6</sup>), we can anticipate that more and more people are confronted with the ambiguities surrounding the boundaries between actual and virtual reality, living and virtual bodies, and private and public domains online. In light of the increasing entanglement between technology and people in everyday life today, we maintain that revisiting the ethical issues surrounding online research is warranted.

Here, our objective is to re-examine the guidelines for human subjects research as they apply to Internet studies in light of the increasing entanglement between users and their virtual bodies in our contemporary technological environment. We identify and discuss some of the most salient issues related to a key ethical questions that IS investigators and research ethics committees<sup>7</sup> (RECs) need to resolve, when determining whether a proposed Internet study deals with human subjects as opposed to inanimate digital material.

Our goal is to provide a framework for supporting investigators and RECs in making this determination. We also want to provoke discussion and debate among IS researchers regarding the ethical standards for online research. Our main concerns are for the human subjects themselves and the sustainability of IS research into Internet phenomena. Ethical lapses by investigators are likely to lead to complaints to university officials (White, 2002a). They are also likely to poison the well, leading Internet users to resist researcher involvement in their communities (Bruckman, 2002). Given users’ increased access to our research, as well as to Web 2.0 media for voicing any concerns or complaints about how data were collected, we consider questions around the morality of drawing boundaries between a living person and digital material quite pressing.

Our paper proceeds as follows: We begin by defining human subjects research and by outlining the key decisions this definition entails for researchers and RECs. We then apply these decisions to the Internet context and highlight how the disembodied vs the entangled view of the Internet influence how questions around human subjects are answered. We then propose a framework for assessing, in an age of cyborgism, whether a study deals with human subjects. Our paper concludes with recommendations for how the IS discipline might advance human subject protections in Internet research.

## Human subjects research: fundamentals

In general, a human research subject is any individual whose interests or well-being may be compromised as a result of activities undertaken in conjunction with a research study. The US Federal Government<sup>8</sup> defines a human subject and related concepts as follows<sup>9</sup>:

‘Human subject means a living individual about whom an investigator (whether professional or student) conducting research obtains:

(1) Data through intervention or interaction with the individual, or

(2) Identifiable private information.

Intervention includes both physical procedures by which data are gathered (e.g., venipuncture) and manipulations of the subject or the subject’s environment that are performed for research purposes. Interaction includes communication or interpersonal contact between investigator and subject. Private information includes information about behavior that occurs in a context in which an individual can reasonably expect that no observation or recording is taking place, and information that has been provided for specific purposes by an individual and which the individual can reasonably expect will not be made public (e.g., a medical record). Private information must be individually identifiable (i.e., the identity of the subject is or may readily be ascertained by the investigator or associated with the information) in order for obtaining the information to constitute research involving human subjects.

Relevant human subject research activities include taking surveys, conducting interviews, implementing in-person experiments, downloading personal postings, profiles and the like. In summary, the definition of human subjects revolves around three key decision points summarized in Figure 1. The decision tree shows the two conditions under which the research is exempt from human subjects review by a REC (and thus from providing human subject protections), as well as the two conditions under which a REC review should be sought (and human subject protections provided).

The overarching moral requirement for researchers conducting studies involving human subjects and their RECs is that the expected benefits from the research outweigh costs incurred in doing the research. Total costs should include human costs associated with the risk of harm to which subjects are exposed. Research participants must be protected from ‘infringements motivated by a zeal’ on the part of researchers who want to complete a project (London et al., 2012: 544). Ensuring adequate protections of participants’ rights is a key responsibility of the REC. To this end, four criteria need to be considered in order to adequately protect the rights of human subjects:

1. Risk: The anticipated benefits from the project must exceed the risks to which participants are exposed.

2. Selection of Subjects: The criteria for inclusion and exclusion should be ethically appropriate. In particular, vulnerable populations such as children, low-status groups (e.g., the poor, ethnic minorities, negatively stigmatized groups), subject populations (e.g., prisoners, soldiers) and the mentally ill, among others, need to be protected against exploitative research practices.

3. Informed Consent: Human subjects must be fully informed as to the methods and procedures to be used, as well as their risks and benefits. They must indicate that they understand them. This implies a discussion and agreement with the subject covering such elements as the nature and objectives of the research and the responsibilities of each party; the subject’s right to withdraw from the research at any time; the relevant risks, benefits and uncertainties related to the research; the anticipated sharing (including publication) of the data that will be collected; what precautions the researcher is taking to protect the privacy and confidentiality of the data sources; and the opportunities subjects have to obtain appropriate feedback about the results and conclusions of the research when it is completed.

4. Confidentiality: All PII and related data and records must be fully protected. Providing this protection requires attention to PII security and implementation of an effective system of protections. Whereas privacy pertains to a subject’s right to control the extent, timing and circumstances under which he or she will share personally identifiable information with researchers, whether physically, behaviorally or intellectually, confidentiality pertains to the treatment of the PII that a subject has in fact disclosed to a researcher.

## Human subjects research in online settings

Applying the above definition of human subjects to Internet research requires that we explore how the following three concepts translate into the online context: living individuals, intervention and interaction, and identifiable private information. Given that the meaning of these dimensions is dependent on the assumptions made by the investigator and the members of the REC, we highlight different perspectives that can be brought to bear on each question.

Living individuals: when is the user separated from her<sup>10</sup> virtual body?

Most definitions of a living individual share three common characteristics: life, sentience and rationality. Life is defined as ‘the condition that distinguishes animals and plants from inorganic objects and dead organisms, being manifested by growth through metabolism, reproduction and the power of adaptation to the environment through changes originating internally’ (Webster’s Encyclopedic Unabridged Dictionary of English Language). This implies that a living individual has a body that is capable of growth and adaptation. It is sentient and conscious, and it is capable of feeling, of perception and of responding emotionally (rather than just intellectually) to outside forces.

In the context of computer-mediated communication, the notion of ‘living individual’ is problematized as a distinction can be made between the embodied user and her presentation of self online, which is frequently seen as an objectification of her inner being and consciousness (Bakardjieva and Feenberg, 2000). Not only is the user’s body geographically separated from her digital rendering, but there are also temporal distantiations: a user’s words and actions are stored as digital material and remain accessible even after the originator is deceased. Furthermore, there might be multiple users behind one virtual body (White, 2002a).

Seeing the user as distinct and separate from her virtual body, online actions are frequently viewed as disembodied (Slater, 2002; Turkle, 1995) and the traces users leave online are regarded as text (Hardey, 2002; Whitley, 1997). This leads to a framing of the Internet as a medium of cultural production (Bassett and O’Riordan, 2002; Bruckman, 2002; Herring, 1996), analogous to broadcast media such as television or the newspaper, and performance spaces such as a gallery or stage. From this perspective, Internet users are regarded as authors or artists who have intentionality in their performance and a high degree of control over the digital material with which they manifest themselves online.

In this view, online performances are regarded as cultural productions such as published books, works of art, theatre productions and television shows (Bassett and O’Riordan, 2002; White, 2002b) and treated as material that is independent of the living individual who produced it. This separation implies that no harm will accrue to a living individual when this digital material is acquired, manipulated, summarized and communicated. Such a perspective seems especially justified since users may create false and multiple identities online, which makes it difficult to connect virtual representations to living individuals (Herring, 1996; Walther, 2002).

However, the Webkids manifesto suggests that this disembodied view of the Internet fails to represent contemporary users’ experiences with the technology. The manifesto suggests a view of the Internet as a social space (Bassett and O’Riordan, 2002), in which experiences and encounters emerge out of the entanglement of social and technical agencies (Introna, 2007). Consider the following example:

Tweeting Rioters – London, 2011

On the night of August 6, 2011 rioting simultaneously broke out in several London boroughs in the midst of an initially peaceful demonstration that had been organized to protest the August 4 police shooting of a 29-year-old Tottenham resident named Mark Duggan. As they gained steam the uprisings resulted in assaults, looting, muggings, arson and, eventually, murder. The riots were stimulated and coordinated by calls to action broadcast to groups of participants and to their contacts using social media, especially Twitter, Blackberry BBM and Facebook. (Solon, 2011) The Telegraph painted a picture of rioters totally at one with technology during the riots: ‘The disorder was captured second-by-second on Twitter with rioters so caught up in the frenzy of destruction they thought nothing of posting incriminating pictures of themselves stealing from ransacked shops.’ (Holehouse and Millward, 2011)

This example is illustrative of an increasing intertwining between users and the technology that has becomes an integral part of their lives. This entanglement is marked by intense social interaction among networked users, as well as between these users and their digital representations. Embodied users are seen as inextricably interlinked with their digital bodies, which extend their senses and presence across time and space. A perspective of entanglement acknowledges that ‘to be is to be digital’ (Capurro and Pingel, 2002: 190), suggesting that users may not have the ability to separate their on- and off-line lives.

As the example of the tweeting rioters indicates, to be a participant in these protests meant reading and writing tweets about what was happening on the street, even if this resulted in self-incriminating posts. Analogizing these swept-up-in-the-moment tweeters with cool-headed authors who edit their work to perfection or trained actors who have rehearsed their performances is to ignore the situated, relational and social nature of taking action in today’s technology-infused world.

Thus, if entanglement between an individual and her virtual body is assumed, the ethical focus shifts away from presuming the existence of ontologically distinct, independent objects (i.e., digital representations and living individuals) to recognizing that the person whose online actions generated the content is inseparable from this digital material. As such, human subject protections for people who take action with words online may be warranted (Elgesem, 2002; King, 1996; Scharf, 1999).

## Intervention and interaction: is lurking a form of interaction? Intervention and interaction: is lurking a form of interaction?

Internet research inevitably involves the observation of users’ actions. These observations may be passive, unobtrusive and conducted at a distance. If the Internet is regarded as a disembodied space of cultural production, practices ranging from screen scraping to lurking are deemed to be passive in the sense that the researcher is not directly interacting with the content producer and thus is not influencing her content production in any way (Webb et al., 1966). Such non-interactionist and noninterventionist research occurs whenever a researcher is not seen, heard or directly experienced by the respondent. This form of Internet research is similar to archival research evident in the humanities, for example, history, art criticism, literature and so on (Herring, 1996), where human subject protections are very rare (Bruckman, 2002).

While many researchers who participate in online communities assume they are unobtrusive and invisible observers, questions about what it means to interact in virtual situations nevertheless arise (e.g., Kendall, 2004; LeBesco, 2004). In particular, researchers are confronted with questions about whether lurking is a form of participation, thus qualifying as interaction. Clegg Smith’s (2004) account of her internal debates regarding research ethics while she was conducting research on the listserv ListX (a pseudonym) illustrates the complexity of this question.

ListX was an UK-based listserv that Clegg Smith studied between 1997 and 1998. It was used by more than 500 general practice physicians (GPs) and did not have a moderator. The listserv’s objective was to support its members’ professional development by fostering discussions of new ideas, promoting workshops and enabling collaborative work and, in general, building community. Even though members had to register and create a member profile before they could join, ListX was not restricted to GPs. Also, the listserv owner regularly sent out messages reminding members that any messages posted on the list should be regarded as being in the public domain. Periodically, members of the press accessed ListX and referenced information discussed on it. This often led to expressions of concern over proper attribution.

For a number of reasons, including the listserv’s size (4500 members), the listserv owner’s reminders that all list content was public and the professional focus of the discussion, Clegg Smith determined that this setting did not fall under the definition of human subjects research. She deemed the list contributors to be authors whose published work could be used freely for secondary purposes. Nevertheless, Clegg Smith’s (2004: 231) conscience caused her to wrestle with this classification. She began her inquiry as a distant lurker, concealed in a shadowy corner, but as she became more immersed in ListX’s activities she felt that her role changed. She had become more of a participant observer than merely a document researcher:

I faced access issues (although these were not particularly challenging ones), the research involved active participants, and I had a ‘role’ within the setting (although it was not a particularly active one). Furthermore, the action of ‘reading’ a record of postings to the list (which had led me to consider whether my research was mostly similar to documentary analysis) directly reflected the form of interaction with which the participants themselves were engaged. Thus, in reading ListX messages, I was engaging in ListX interaction.

Clegg Smith’s internal dialog is indicative of an entanglement perspective in that she notes that the meaning of participation and interaction is dependent on the online community’s norms and practices and on the researcher’s sense of immersion and participation in this community. In this online setting, the configuration of the social and technical elements mobilized during the practice of lurking made her an active, although unseen, member of the ListX community.

While Clegg Smith wrestled with whether active reading constituted a form of interaction in online communities, many research methods require that a researcher interact directly with a subject, for example, online surveys, email interviews, listserv discussions. All of these methods imply intervention in people’s normal activities in that they expose the subject to some stimulus so that her response can be observed, recorded and analyzed. For instance, a researcher may author a blog in order to evoke reactions and comments from, as well as discussion among, Internet users.

A method called ‘Garfinkeling,’<sup>11</sup> is among the most interventionist experimental methodologies. Garfinkeling refers to a breaching experiment in which a researcher intentionally upsets an ongoing social system (e.g., a newsgroup, discussion board or micro-blog) and disturbs the subject’s normal sense of reality by breaking the rules and violating conventions (Garfinkel, 1966). By recording and examining the responses, researchers are able to surface crucial background assumptions that underlie conventional behaviors. For example, the Social Media Collective at Microsoft Research New England was recently looking for participants in the following online breaching experiment:

WAY OFF TOPIC. ‘On Facebook or a similar site that has threaded conversation (e.g., status updates with replies), over a period of three days leave a large number of comments that are all completely and obviously off-topic and not relevant to the thread. For this to work, there can be no relation between the reply and the topic at all; just start talking about something else. If you like, address some of them to the wrong person as well. Describe the results.’<sup>12</sup>

While some might argue that such interventions are unlikely to cause harm to human subjects because they are happening in virtual rather than actual reality, the following example serves to illustrate the impact virtual activities can have on people’s actual lives in situations of cyborgian entanglement:

## Rape in cyberspace – LambdaMOO, 1993

On an evening in March 1993, a character by the name of Mr. Bungle used a sub-program known as ‘voodoo doll’ to attribute actions to other characters in the text-based virtual world, LambdaMOO<sup>13</sup>. He specifically sought out two characters named ‘exu’ and ‘Moondreamer.’ Importantly, Mr. Bungle’s actions were taken without the knowledge or consent of the real life MUDders behind these characters. Mr. Bungle forced exu and Moondreamer into unwanted and graphically violent sexual liaisons with other characters. Even though no direct physical harm came to the real life MUDders whose characters had experienced this virtual rape (later even exu’s owner insisted that the rape was not ‘real’), they nevertheless felt emotionally traumatized by the violence that had been committed on their alter egos (although it was done by words alone). The virtual event had ‘real’ consequences for the users behind the text-based characters that were the victims of this acts of violence. (Dibbell, 1993)

This example illustrates that users are deeply attached to their avatars and respond viscerally to interventions that, in this case, break conventional norms. The MUDders whose avatars were raped textually in LamdbaMOO could not separate the virtual violence perpetrated against their digital alter egos from their own emotional, embodied experience of the events. At an affective level, they were completely entangled with their virtual bodies and identities.

Identifiable private information: when are online spaces private? Much has been written about the issue of whether the Internet is a public or private space. Many argue that the Internet is public and that any expectations of privacy are misplaced (Herring, 1996; Walther, 2002; White, 2002a). These researchers regard privacy as a technology’s capacity to limits others’ access. In effect, they equate technological accessibility with publicness (Bakardjieva and Feenberg, 2000). The Harvard researchers, who harvested the students’ Facebook profiles, presumably regarded the social networking data as public and thus dispensed with informed consent requirements.

However, others point out, that just like in other social settings, what is considered private in this virtual space is determined by social norms and practices (Frankel and Siang, 1999; King, 1996; Waskul and Douglass, 1996). For instance, in a small town where everyone knows everybody’s business, there is some information that people pretend not to know because it is considered private. Similarly, in everyday life, we pretend not to have overheard a conversation or avert our eyes when an embarrassing mishap occurs. In these ways, privacy is enacted even in public spaces.

Such social norms around what constitutes private vs public activities have yet to be negotiated in online contexts (McCreary, 2008). There is thus a great danger that subjects of an Internet study do not agree with researchers’ assumption that the Internet is inherently public (Bakardjieva and Feenberg, 2000). Consider, for example, the note posted by a member of a list dedicated to sufferers of sexual abuse, whose community had been studied without its knowledge:

y when I joined this I thought it would be a \*support\* group, not a fishbowl for a bunch of guinea pigs. I certainly don’t feel at this point that it is a ‘safe’ environment, as a support group is supposed to be, and I will not open myself up to be dissected by students or scientists. I’m sure I’m not the only person who feels this way.’ (King, 1996: 122)

Clearly, some users can feel betrayed by researchers who impose on their life online without making themselves known to the community and without asking the participants’ permission to collect data. King (1996) and others (e.g., Elgesem, 2002; Scharf, 1999; Schrum, 1995) point out that online communities operate under situated assumptions of privacy, that is, perceived privacy, and that these expectations need to be respected by Internet researchers. Indeed, privacy is a dialectic and dynamic boundary regulation process that is negotiated among social and technical entities (Palen and Dourish, 2003).

Empirical research on different communities’ privacy expectations indicates that Internet users do not view their communications as public. Hudson and Bruckman’s (2004) study of requesting permission to record chatroom conversations reports that two-thirds of chatrooms contacted kicked the researchers out. Also, Chen et al. (2004), who surveyed members of listservs and online newsgroups asking them whether researchers should be required to disclose their identity and their data gathering intent to the groups they study, found that the survey participants’ unanimous answer was ‘yes.’ One somewhat exasperated respondent asked: ‘Do you actually expect \*anyone\* to say no?’ (166).

By focusing on situated practices, researchers can gain insight into users’ perceived privacy. Instead of classifying an entire web site as either public or private based on the creator’s intention and the access rights facilitated by the technology, attention should be focused on the different kinds of social interactions made possible by a given application. For instance, Bassett and O’Riordan (2002), who initially classified a community-based magazine site they studied as public, later recognized that there were aspects of the web site that were more private. For this reason, they anonymized the web site in their publications in order to afford the community and its participants human subjects protection.

## Entanglement: ‘We are all Cyborgs now’<sup>14</sup>

The concept of entanglement originated in physics, in Schro¨dinger’s critique of quantum theory. Einstein’s biographer, Walter Isaacson (2007: 454), summarizes the concept: ‘The quantum states of two particles that have interacted must subsequently be described together, with any changes to one particle instantly being reflected in the other, no matter how far apart they now are.’ Schro¨dinger (1935) referred to this possibility as entanglement: ‘Any “entanglement of predictions” that takes place can obviously only go back to the fact that the two bodies at some earlier time formed in a true sense one system, that is, were interacting, and have left behind traces on each other (emphasis as in Trimmer’s 1980 translation: 332).

When an Internet user is viewed through the lens of entanglement, two interacting bodies – one flesh and blood, the other digital – are revealed. Together, they form a single system or assemblage (Barad, 2007), and leave mutual traces on each other no matter how far apart they are located. As the ‘rape in cyberspace’ illustrates, users are deeply attached to their virtual bodies and find it difficult to separate themselves emotionally from them. Thus, the owners of the violated avatars experienced the virtual abuse and felt harmed by it.

The entanglement between the London rioters and their mobile phones operates at a more physical and existential level. A frenetic picture is painted of rioters madly running around, cell phone firmly grasped in hand and tweeting like crazy. These rioters’ identity – at least for the week they were immersed in the protests – emerged out of the confluence between the social action in which they were engaged and the mobile, social media technology they were employing.

Tweeting was an essential part of being a full participant in these riots. The technology became an integral part of their activities, and, hence, of them. The posting of selfincriminating pictures of themselves in the act of looting was a socio-psychological signal alerting others to their existence: I tweet, therefore I am.

These examples paint a picture of users who have emerged as cyborgs. As their presence and bodily senses were extended across time and space, these living individuals’ identities became a hybrid, composed of physically embodied and virtual components (Borer, 2002; McLuhan, 1964). At the same time, their identities became more malleable, fluid, mobile and, in some cases, multiple (Slater, 2002; Turkle, 1995). Users have become inseparable from the virtual bodies to which they delegate various activities and through which they engage in personal experimentation.

## Studying cyborgs: human subjects research in an era of entanglement

The framework we propose (Figure 2) is premised on the increasing entanglement between users and technology that needs to be considered in Internet research. Instead of a linear decision, where one dimension of the definition of human subjects research is considered after the other, our framework considers all three dimensions (entanglement, interaction/intervention and privacy) in concert before making a judgment on whether the research deals with human subjects.

In addition, instead of answering binary yes/no questions, the investigator or the REC rates each dimension on a low-to-high scale, thereby acknowledging the ambiguity of the boundaries between living and virtual bodies, between unobtrusive observation and interventionist interaction, and between public and private spaces in life online. As a result, the framework promises a more holistic treatment of the social and technical issues surrounding Internet research in an age of cyborgian entanglement.

Adopting a perspective of all users as cyborgs, assessing whether an Internet study deals with human subjects is fundamentally grounded in an understanding of the technology in use (Introna and Hayes, 2011), that is, the situated and patterned interactions of a technological artifact and human action (Orlikowski, 2000). This implies a significant departure from a disembodied, textual view of the Internet, which relies on taken-for-granted, fixed technological properties such as virtuality and accessibility to make determinations about whether a study deals with human subjects vs digital material.

Since the decision about whether a proposed study deals with human subjects needs to be made before the researcher starts collecting data, the challenge is to gain insights into a given technology in use before the study. To gain such insights, researchers might rely on prior research on older versions of the technology (e.g., MUDS and MOOS are text-based versions of 3D virtual worlds) or similar technologies studied in different contexts (e.g., healthcarerelated bulletin boards provide insight into practices of other online communities dealing with personal issues). In addition, researchers could use a given technology for a while in order to gain an appreciation and understanding of the norms and practices of a user community. Such an experience would also allow researchers to put themselves into the shoes of the people whose online actions they are studying and to envisage how they might react to and want to be treated in a proposed study.

Armed with insights into how a given technology is used, the investigator will need to assess the three key dimensions of human subjects research. With regard to the entanglement dimension, the degree to which most users of a given technology are psychologically and emotionally attached to their virtual bodies (which range from traces of online activities such as bidding on auctions to Facebook profiles and 3D avatars) needs to be determined. How large a role does the user’s virtual manifestation play in her sense of self? It is probable that a virtual body made up of the user’s history of purchases is less entangled with her sense of self than, say, a blog persona that reflects her aspirational, idealized ‘me,’ or a self-presentation on Facebook that reveals her relationships, deviant behaviors and inner most thoughts. While the former scenario is likely to be rated ‘low’ on the degree of entanglement, the latter is likely to earn a ‘high’ rating.

To determine the extent to which the researcher’s engagement with Internet content and online communities constitute an interaction or intervention, the degree to which the researcher becomes actively involved with the online material and the people who produce it needs to be assessed. Any interaction with users, be it through on- or off-line interviews, online surveys, or blog posts intended to draw out reactions and comments from online users, qualify as interactions with human subjects and should thus be given a ‘high’ rating. Also, the design of virtual social spaces for the purpose of studying people’s reactions to and behaviors in them should be given a ‘high’ rating since they represent interventions.

Lurking and the active, contemporaneous reading of messages as they are posted to listservs or discussion boards, for instance, as well as non-participant observations of avatar behaviors in virtual worlds, should most probably earn a ‘medium’ rating. However, research on archival data, for example, a defunct virtual world, closed auctions or online discussions of a few years prior, would qualify for a ‘low’ rating as it entails a more passive analysis in which the researcher is distant from the events and the people that generated the content (Herring et al., 2004). To the extent that such research focuses on information and interaction patterns rather than the actions of individuals, it should be rated as ‘low’ on the question of interaction and intervention.

In assessing the expectations of privacy in an online research site, the characteristics listed in Table 1 should serve as a guide. The more an online research site fall under the ‘private’ column in Table 1, the higher its rating for privacy expectations should be. Conversely, the more the characteristics of the ‘public’ column apply, the lower the rating on the question of privacy expectation. In other words, the more private the activity and the space within which it occurs, the more the source agent is viewed as a human subject; the more public, the more the source agent is viewed as the author of a text.

For example, a study of how Wikipedia entries are generated would most likely earn a ‘low’ rating on the question of privacy expectations, as Wikipedians are a large community engaged in the production of facts intended for a global audience. The knowledge production process is regarded as a socially desirable activity that is transparent and public, as well as guided by semi-professional editors. In contrast, a 30-member community of role players engaged in deviant social relations, which vets new members carefully, would have ‘high’ expectations of privacy, as small groups convey a sense of intimacy and hence can justifiably be viewed as private spaces (Kendall, 2004).

Once all three questions have been rated on the low-to-high scale, they will be weighed in the final human subjects determination. A ‘low’ rating on all three dimensions is indicative of a study that most probably does not qualify as human subjects research and that can thus be exempted from REC review. However, if even one dimension is rated above ‘low,’ a review by the REC is warranted in order to ensure that the rights of the Internet users being studied are protected.

While our proposed framework may appear very conservative, classifying more Internet studies as human subjects research than some in the IS community might be comfortable with, we believe that it is necessary to err on the side of protecting Internet users’ rights particularly at a time of increased human–technology entanglement. In addition, powerful search engines are making it ever more difficult to anonymize online research sites and authors of texts, as these technologies are able to identify the source of Internet texts quoted in academic papers. Furthermore, thanks to the increasingly networked nature of the Internet, online communities are no longer isolated from each other, making it easier to identify the individual behind an online pseudonym.

As protecting subjects’ true identities is increasingly challenging for researchers, we believe that researchers would be well advised to seek REC approval for their Internet studies. Not only will an REC review help them identify strategies for protecting their subjects thus avoiding a possible Internet campaign that might tarnish their reputation, but it will also help protect the individual researcher against possible legal actions from subjects that have been harmed by their study.

## Internet research ethics in an age of cyborgism

Contemporary Internet researchers face conceptual and moral problems similar to those faced by the field of archeology, which started to address new challenges to its research approach in the relatively recent past. From the discipline’s beginning in the 15th century, its methods primarily entailed the systematic measurement and study of the material remains of human cultures and the application of such archeometric techniques as radiocarbon dating and trace element analysis to these found objects. The original research paradigm focused on describing artifacts such as shards and arrowheads and developing taxonomies to order them. This approach relied on the fairly straightforward detection of the properties of a found object such as its weight, size, color and so on (Kyburg, 1968).

The limitations of this research paradigm were highlighted (Taylor, 1948) and a more inclusive paradigm for the discipline was advanced by sweeping in insights from anthropology, ethnography, sociology, economics, religious studies, politics and even interpretive humanities such as art history (Binford, 1962). The new theory became known as ‘processual archeology’ and was summarized by asking new questions: ‘What are the properties of this arrowhead? was replaced with ‘Who is the Indian behind the arrowhead?’ (Cassells, 1997: 40).

Operating under this new paradigm archeologists soon learned that they had to deal with the human rights, interests, sensitivities, cultures and attributes of the past inhabitants of research sites and, especially, with the rights and concerns of these ancient parties’ contemporary surrogates. The 1990 passing of the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), together with a spreading concern for indigenous patrimony worldwide, resulted in an ever-increasing number of stakeholders who claimed a right to be involved in making interpretations of the past. Crucially, the ethics of this new state of affairs required archeological researchers to make methodological tradeoffs and to reach compromises between, on one hand, the rigor and control required by the positivist scientific method and, on the other, respecting the participatory rights of other implicated human beings. Internet researchers, as they respect the person who is entangled with her virtual bodies, may need to make similar ethical and methodological trade offs.

## Conclusion

Several implications for IS research flow from the arguments, concepts and examples presented in this paper. First, due to the evolution of Internet technology, especially Web 2.0, and the increasing entanglement between living individuals and their virtual bodies, the AIS Code of Research Conduct should be changed to promote the provision ‘Respect the rights of research subjects, particularly their rights to information privacy, and to being informed about the nature of the research and the types of activities in which they will be asked to engage’ from ‘Category Two’ (recommended ethical behavior) to ‘Category One’ (must always be adhered to.)

Second, as part of the review of the Common Rule for human subjects research, initiated by the Office of the Secretary of the Department of Health and Human Services in June 2011, some investigators are proposing that Internet research is not threatening enough to require REC review (Schrag, 2011). The motivation behind this revision is that many researchers, especially historians, humanists and some social scientists, are dissatisfied with what they perceive to be REC interference in their academic freedom. While their concerns should be taken into account, a carteblanche exemption of Internet research potentially places many unsuspecting users at undue risk. Furthermore, ethics violations may poison the well, thus putting future research at risk. AIS and other related profession societies may want to take a stand on this.

Third, the IS community should do empirical research on what Internet researchers and users regard as ethical research behavior on specific Web 2.0 applications, for example, (micro)blogs, social networking sites and virtual worlds. As part of such empirical inquiries, users’ assumptions of privacy associated with Web 2.0 applications should be studied, as well as the why, when and how of the practices that users rely on to draw boundaries between reality and virtuality, their actual and virtual identities, and their physical and virtual bodies.

Fourth, another form of Internet research that is sorely needed is to develop better methods and technologies to secure the confidentiality of any PII that is obtained during a study. Importantly, RECs must take this requirement into account during proposal reviews and must have committee members who are technologically competent to make assessments and able to recommend solutions to securing subjects’ privacy. Helping RECs understand the intricacies of Internet technology and its implications for human subject protections might be an opportunity for IS researchers to take a leadership role with regard to research ethics in virtual environments.

Fifth, in order to develop an ongoing dialog on online research ethics in IS, Internet researchers should articulate the ethical choices they made during their studies when they write journal and conference papers. Reviewers and editors of studies conducted in online settings should demand that authors be explicit about the moral choices they made with respect to data collection and reporting. In this way, an ethical standard for online research in IS will evolve despite the dynamically changing and emergent nature of Internet technologies.

As stated in this paper, human subjects research issues associated with Web 2.0 applications have not been given the level of ethical scrutiny they deserve. This should be remedied. On today’s Internet, living individuals are increasingly entangled with their virtual bodies. As cyborgs, they deserved human subject protections.
