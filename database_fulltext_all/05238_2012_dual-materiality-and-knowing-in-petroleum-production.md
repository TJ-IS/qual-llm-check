# Dual materiality and knowing in petroleum production

Thomas Østerlie <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Petter G. Almklov <sup>b</sup>, Vidar Hepsø <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Computer and Information Science, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, 7491 Trondheim, Norway

## a r t i c l e i n f o

## a b s t r a c t

This paper explores the relationship between materiality and knowing through the notion of dual materiality. Dual materiality highlights how digital technology becomes important, as its materiality plays an integral part in creating, not simply representing, the materiality of the physical world. We elaborate upon this insight through a theory on sociomaterial knowing grounded in ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork within a petroleum company. The main theoretical proposition of this theory is that knowing arises from the emerging patterns of interaction between material phenomena, the material arrangements for knowing about these phenomena, and knowledge practices. We elaborate upon this through three predominant modes of knowing in petroleum production: instrumentation, interpretation, and learning. This paper contributes to the broader discourse on sociomateriality by re<sup>fi</sup>ning ideas of materiality through the notion of dual materiality. We conclude by encouraging further exploration of different materialities in contemporary work and organizing.

## 1. Introduction

Petroleum is produced from wells drilled deep into geological formations. On the Norwegian Continental Shelf (NCS), a subsea plateau off the Norwegian coast, reservoirs are located thousands of meters beneath the seabed. Directed along kilometers of pipeline towards the surface, the well <sup>fl</sup>ow – a mass of liquids, gases and solid particles streaming out of the wells – is physically inaccessible to human inspection. With no way of physically inspecting the mass <sup>fl</sup>owing towards the surface, engineers draw upon real-time data, which is generated by a network of sensors mounted at <sup>fi</sup>xed positions within the wells and along the pipelines, to monitor and control production.

These material sensor arrangements and knowing are deeply intertwined in petroleum production. Thus, the premise of this paper is that knowledge cannot be studied and understood separately from the material arrangements of sensors and other computing equipment that generate and visualize data about the mass streaming out of the wells. Due to the inaccessibility of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow and the engineers' dependence on sensor data for knowing about the well <sup>fl</sup>ow, it is meaningless to attempt and distinguish between what they know about the well <sup>fl</sup>ow and the means by which they know it. We therefore approach knowing in petroleum production from a sociomaterial perspective, which emphasizes the intertwined nature of human knowledge, sensors and other computing equipment for generating and visualizing data about the well <sup>fl</sup>ow, as well as the knowledge practices involved in understanding and controlling the <sup>fl</sup>ow of <sup>fl</sup>uids, gasses, and solids streaming out of subsurface wells.

Knowledge has been a sustained interest within information systems (IS) literature. Broadly speaking, this literature can be organized into two perspectives on knowledge: an objectivistic perspective on the one hand, and a human-centric perspective on the other (Walsham, 2001). From an objectivistic perspective, knowledge is seen as representations of the external world with a focus on capturing and classifying real-world objects. This view is prevalent within IS literature in general, underpinning the many forms of modeling that are central to IS development such as requirements modeling, software design, as well as data and information modeling. From an objectivist perspective, the relationship between knowledge and computing technologies is threefold (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). Through data collection, ICTs aim to capture aspects of reality that can be represented in computerized models. When objecti<sup>fi</sup>ed through these models, knowledge can then be stored in repositories and transferred throughout the organization.

To a certain degree, a human-centric perspective offers a response to and critique of the objectivist perspective on knowledge. At its core, this critique is leveled against the predominant focus on representational notations, which has been repeatedly argued as excluding the knowing subject – humans (Boland, 1987; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Orlikowski, 2002). From a human-centric perspective, knowledge is “not an external, enduring, or essential substance” (Orlikowski, 2006, p.460) that is inseparable from the knowing subject. Instead, knowing is constituted through everyday work, through human actions and through practice (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Pentland, 1992; Schultze, 2000). Emphasizing practice, a humancentric perspective offers a counterweight to the objectivist tendencies of reducing knowledge to representations of the material reality to be known. Yet, in emphasizing human action and practice, this perspective tends to minimize the role of technology itself (Orlikowski, 2007). As such, this perspective falls under Leonardi and Barley's (2008) broader critique of a strain of research on technology and organizing that tends to focus on “how people organize around the technologies they employ [and] attention gravitates towards the social: interactions, interpretations, behaviors and so on” (p.163).

Both of the above perspectives offer limited analytical traction for studying knowledge in petroleum production, in which real-time data generated by a sensor network plays a central role in the everyday work of monitoring and controlling production. Limiting ICTs to technologies for mirroring and representing the material world, or focusing on how people organize around the ICTs used, reduces these perspectives' ability to analytically reveal the deep intermingling between human knowing and the material arrangements for knowing in petroleum production. This, however, is only part of the more fundamental problem of a tenuous relationship between knowing and materiality (Tsoukas, 1998). By materiality, we mean ‘material reality’; the stuff the world is made up of (Iedema, 2007). We are interested in bringing material reality – both the physical phenomena to be monitored, and the material sensors and other computing equipment used to generate data about the phenomena – into our analysis. Hence, we ask: What is the relationship between materiality and knowing in petroleum production?

We propose the concept of dual materiality as one way of exploring this relationship. The material arrangements of sensors and other computing equipment that generate and visualize real-time data described in this paper reside along the boundary between the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow and a world of semantics that says something about this well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Dual materiality distinguishes between these two modes of materiality: the material phenomena that the engineers are trying to grasp versus the materiality of the tools from which they approach it. Offered as the main contribution of this paper, the concept of dual materiality contributes towards the broader discourse on sociomateriality within IS and IS-related research by showing how digital technology becomes important because its materiality plays an integral part in creating, not simply representing, the materiality of physical phenomena. Much of the existing research on sociomateriality focuses predominantly upon the materiality of various computing technologies.

Nevertheless, since the crux of the petroleum engineers' work lies in grasping material phenomena through the material arrangements for knowing about these phenomena, it is useful to make the distinction of dual materiality.

We pursue the concept of dual materiality through a substantive grounded theory on sociomaterial knowing. Through this substantive theory, we conceptualize and describe how knowing in petroleum pro duction arises from the emerging patterns of interaction between material phenomena, the material arrangements for knowing about these phenomena, and knowledge practices. We elaborate upon this through three predominant modes of knowing in petroleum production: instrumentation, interpretation, and learning. This theory is not intended to be exhaustive; instead it is offered as a <sup>fi</sup>rst step towards elaborating how knowing emerges from the patterned interactions between material phenomena, material arrangements for knowing about these phenomena, and knowledge practices. Although empirically grounded in the petroleum domain, we believe future studies in other sensor data-intense domains should add or modify the theory's properties and their interactions. Iteratively developing theory this way is one possible approach to cumulatively building data-driven theory within IS research, while at the same time remaining <sup>fl</sup>exible to the emergent character of the phenomena under study (Keen, 1980; Lyytinen, 2009). The notion of dual materiality may also be used in formulating new problems and issues to be pursued within other <sup>fi</sup>elds in which instrumentation plays a central role such as computerized medical imaging and smart power grids. Along with other works on sociomateriality, we believe this paper contributes towards the broader program of theorizing IS in its context of use and development.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, we present the issue of dual materiality in petroleum production in Section 2. Next, we present the research setting and methods in Section 3, while in Section 4 we pursue the topic of dual materiality through a substantive grounded theory on sociomaterial knowing in petroleum production. We then conclude the paper by discussing the implications of the notion of dual materiality in Section 5.

## 2. Dual materiality in petroleum production

Lyytinen and King (2004) describes the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld as “the study of the design and management of information and associated technologies in organized human enterprise” (p.221). Theorizing the relationship between computing technologies and the social contexts where they are embedded has therefore been a central concern for the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Initiated by the socio-technical school in the 1960s (Mumford, 2006), and more recently pursued through the adoption and adaptation of structuration theory (Barley & Tolbert, 1997; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski & Robey, 1991), a predominant stream of research has analytically approached technology and organizations as two separate, but interacting systems. Leonardi and Barley (2008), however, observe that such a distinction tend to lead to analyses of “how people organize around the technologies they employ” (p.163).

At the heart of research on sociomateriality lies a dedication to studying how technology, work, and organizations are inextricably related through recursive intertwining (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). While there is a growing body of research on sociomateriality within IS and IS-related <sup>fi</sup>elds, Orlikowski remains one of the most prominent proponents of the notion (Orlikowski, 2006, 2007, 2010b; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). Orlikowski (2007) proposes sociomateriality as an alternative to the dominant modes of addressing technology “as separate and distinct phenomena occurring within organizations” (p.1436). Sociomateriality represents an analytical break in that it constitutes an effort to avoid the dichotomy between the social and the technological underpinning previous research on socio-technics and structuration theory:

Research framed according to the tenets of a sociomaterial approach challenges the deeply taken-for-granted assumption that technology, work, and organizations should be conceptualized separately, and advances the view that there is an inherent inseparability between the technical and the social. (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p.434)

Instead of taking the inherent separateness as an analytical point of departure, sociomaterial research doubts the relevance of the distinction. Drawing upon Barad's (2007) concept of constitutive entanglement, analytical emphasis is on how technology, work, and organizing intertwine through practice. This builds upon a performative approach to practice. Performativity makes the analytical claim that “[a]ll social reality is understood to be constituted in and through practices, and as such is seen to be an ongoing dynamic and practical accomplishment” (Orlikowski, 2010a, p.27). Building upon a performative approach, Mol (2002) posits that not only the social is constituted through practice. Rather, both the social and the material are constituted through practice. The implication here is that material reality is neither social nor material, but always part of the same performances: it is sociomaterial. This has been a central point within actor network theory, programmatically argued by Latour (1993) in that nothing is purely social or material, but always hybrid. Instead, that something appears as social or material is in itself to be understood as an effect of a hybrid performance. Explanatory power, then, is not on the objects themselves, but on relationship between the social and the material.

Existing research on sociomateriality tends to front stage the relation between computing technologies and work in organizations. Materiality, then, <sup>fi</sup>gures predominantly as different computing technologies. This follows from IS research's historic focus on of<sup>fi</sup>ce and of<sup>fi</sup>ce-related work settings where computers make up a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of the organization's materiality. Whereas Barad's (2007) observation of the constitutive entanglement between phenomena and material arrangements for knowing about them is acknowledged within research on sociomateriality, most of the research on sociomateriality has focused on the constitutive entanglement of computers and work. Suchman's (2007) discussion of how civil engineers work with their CAD workstations illustrates this:

Immersed in her work, the CAD interface becomes for engineering the simulacrum of the site, not in the sense of a substitute for it but rather a place in which to work with its own specific materialities, constraints, and possibilities. (p.279)

Turning our attention to petroleum production, however, computers remain an important element, but there is no denying the material importance of real-world phenomena, too. Still, the petroleum engineers only have access to the real-world phenomena to be monitored and controlled through software applications. When studying petroleum production it is therefore of critical importance to understand the interfaces where the physical well <sup>fl</sup>ow is transformed into computerized information. Limiting materiality to only encompass computing technologies precludes us from this.

We propose that the implications of Barad's (2007) work on materiality, while a formative point of reference for much of the present work on sociomateriality, have not been fully exploited in theorizing the relationship between materiality and knowing. To understand how Barad's work can get us out of this bind, we need to reconsider the genealogy of the notion of sociomateriality. The notion of sociomateriality can be traced back to Mol's (2002) study of perfomativity and disease. Here, Mol criticizes the predominant view that science passively represents phenomena. Rather, Mol argues, developing representations is a movement from one sociomaterial practice (typically observation or experiment) to the practice of drawing and writing. Suchman (2007) uses the notion of sociomateriality in discussing the implications of Barad's observation of the constitutive entanglement between phenomena and material arrangements in the context of human-computer interaction:

I argue for the value of research aimed at articulating the differences within particular human-machine configurations, expanding our unit of analysis to include extended networks of social and material production, and recognizing the agencies, and attendant responsibilities, involved in the inevitable cuts through which bounded sociomaterial entities are made. (p.6–7, emphasis in original)

To better analyze the inseparability of material phenomena and sensor arrangements for knowing about these phenomena, we need to bring the material phenomena back into the analysis. A central aspect of the concept constitutive entanglement is that materiality is in itself performed. The key here is not that material reality does not exist. But rather that what we perceive as more or less clearly delineated material objects with traits and characteristics are an effect of sociomaterial practices. In Bateson's (1972) epistemology, differentiation is the basic element, transporting the unnamed world into the world of semantics. At the interface between the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow and the information system, the sensors are arrangements that let variations in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow trigger differentiations in the information system. Employing Bateson's insights on how a petroleum reservoir is turned into information, Almklov (2008) writes:

Given the endless possibility of variation available for sensors or people to differentiate, the tuning of the ‘seeing device’ to certain aspects of reality influences what kind of reality is perceived. At the divide between the unnamed and the named, the unknown and the known, these differentiations, made by humans and their tools, are the vehicles of information. (p.878)

Like the lines on a map, differentiations are of a different logical type than the terrain, and they are not the terrain. But like the map, the information system and its representations of the terrain have a materiality of its own. A key to understanding the work of petroleum engineers is to understand the practices where they employ material tools to understand and control the materiality of the well flow.

Barad (2007) draws upon Bohr's quantum physics philosophy to explain this. That objects have a position is an ontological assumption in classical physics. For Bohr, however, theoretical concepts – such as position and momentum – do not refer to inherent characteristics of atomic particles. Rather, they are specific physical arrangements. Position, for instance, only has meaning when an apparatus with <sup>fi</sup>xed position is used to establish a <sup>fi</sup>xed frame of reference for specifying a particle. This leads Barad to the conclusion that “‘[m]atter’ does not refer to inherent, <sup>fi</sup>xed property of abstract, independently existing objects; rather, ‘matter’ refers to phenomena in their ongoing materialization” (p.151, italics in original) As such, phenomena have no inherent properties and boundaries, while objects are always the product of sociomaterial practices.

Given existing research on sociomateriality's emphasis on the materiality of computers, we propose the term dual materiality as a way of talking about the relationship between sensor arrangements and phenomena in their ongoing materialization. This should not be construed in any way as an attempt to privilege either sensors or phenomena. Nor should it be construed as an implication that the two are independent. Rather, it is intended as a label for bringing our attention to what we believe is an unaddressed issue in the emerging literature on sociomateriality. While we believe it is both timely and relevant to turn our attention to dual materiality at this point in the development of concepts and issues for research on sociomateriality, we also believe the term is of limited relevance once the issue of dual materiality is embraced more fully in the sociomateriality discourse.

We will elaborate on the notion of dual materiality in petroleum production through the substantive grounded theory on sociomaterial knowing. Before progressing to this theory, though, we will <sup>fi</sup>rst outline the research setting and methods used for the reported research.

## 3. Research setting and methods

Alpha Petroleum Company (APC) is a global energy company. It is ranked among Fortune 500's top 40 companies and among the top 15 petroleum companies. The company's primary activity is petroleum production. Over the past decade, APC has made signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investment in technological as well as organizational changes to improve productivity and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Centralizing operational expertise by moving professional communities previously located offshore to onshore operations centers has been pivotal to these changes.

This paper draws upon the <sup>fi</sup>rst author's ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Fetterman, 1998; Myers, 1999) among petroleum engineers working in one of APC's onshore operations centers. This facility houses onshore personnel for several oil and gas <sup>fi</sup>elds on the NCS. The <sup>fi</sup>eldwork speci<sup>fi</sup>cally concentrated on the daily work of the professional community of production engineers responsible for planning, optimiz ing, and monitoring daily petroleum production from the 50 wells of the Pan Field. The production engineers are one of multiple professional communities in the <sup>fi</sup>eld's larger production organization that encompasses both onshore and offshore personnel. The production engineers are organized under the petroleum technology department, one of the Pan Field's three onshore departments. Production planning and optimization is but one of numerous activities in petroleum production. An integral part of the pro duction engineers' daily work is therefore to interact and coordinate activities with the other professional communities on the <sup>fi</sup>eld, both onshore as well as offshore. This interaction takes place in formalized daily and weekly status, coordination, and planning meetings, as well as in more informal conversations faceto-face, on the phone, or through video conferencing.

The Detailed Production Optimization (DPO) room houses the production engineers directly involved in planning, optimizing, and monitoring daily production from the Pan Field's many wells. The DPO room is a collaboration room equipped with multiple large, wall-mounted monitors and video conferencing facilities. The group of production engineers working in this collaboration room is colloquially referred to as the DPO. The DPO draws extensively on real-time sensor data in their day-to-day work. Sensor data is visualized in a host of different engineering applications, corporate reporting databases, as well as in locally developed spreadsheets.

The DPO is the formal link between the land-based organization and the two operators manning the central control room (CCR) on the offshore production platform. The formal responsibility between the DPO and CCR is clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned: the DPO has the authority with respect to decision-making about how to control production on the <sup>fi</sup>eld's many wells. The CCR operators have executing responsibility. Yet, the practical relationship between the two groups is characterized by professional deference and respect as they seek to retain good working relations at all times. During of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours, the DPO monitors the <sup>fi</sup>eld's many wells, instructing the CCR. Outside of of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours, the CCR operators run production according to the priorities speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the Well Contingency Plan. The Well Contingency Plan is a spreadsheet sent by email to the CCR operators at the end of regular of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours. In case of emergencies, there is always an engineer on an ambulating on-call duty for the CCR to confer with.

The DPO is a nexus of activity during regular of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours. Production optimization requires detailed knowledge of the wells and the strategies for draining the reservoir. This is a job left to experienced engineers. The engineers in the DPO all have Master's level engineering degrees or higher. Due to their intimate knowledge of the wells, the DPO becomes a valuable information source for the other engineers working on the Pan Field. Diagnosing wells and assessing their state is part of production optimization. The CCR therefore calls upon the DPO when it experiences incidents with the wells. It is common for engineers from other disciplines to gravitate towards the DPO room during production-related incidents, either to get information or to participate in diagnosis and troubleshooting (Fig. 1).

The <sup>fi</sup>rst author had a permanent seat in the DPO room for the duration of the ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, observing and interacting with the <sup>fi</sup>eld's production engineers as they went about their daily activities. Throughout this period, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author had full access to the onshore operations center. This made it possible to follow up on events and issues with engineers throughout Pan's onshore organization, either by seeking them in their of<sup>fi</sup>ce, in conversations over lunch, or during breaks in the communal areas of the operations center. During such <sup>fi</sup>eld talks, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author would ask questions about how the engineers used different software tools in performing their many tasks, as well as more generally engage them in conversations about central problems they were dealing with in daily petroleum production. Several other engineers would often join these conversations, making such informal talks an invaluable source of information. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author conducted three periods of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork over a span of 10 months. During each of these periods, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author spent between two and four days a week at the onshore operations center. Each of the three periods of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork lasted six to eight weeks. While working in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author would hastily jot down <sup>fi</sup>eld notes in a notepad. The <sup>fi</sup>eld notes would be written up more fully whilst in the <sup>fi</sup>eld during periods of inactivity, or at least later the same day (Emerson, Fretz, & Shaw, 1995).

The <sup>fi</sup>eldwork was conducted as part of a grounded theory study, drawing upon Charmaz' (Charmaz, 2006) explication of the method towards a constructivist epistemology. Grounded theory offers a speci<sup>fi</sup>c approach to theory construction through a continuous interplay between data collection and analysis (Urquhart, Lehman, & Myers 2010). Once the <sup>fi</sup>rst author had written up the day's <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, they were immediately analyzed by assigning codes to data segments. Such initial coding is one of three analytical strategies in Charmaz' variant of grounded theory. Initial coding is the initiating step towards making decisions about core theoretical categories for the study. Through initial coding, the researcher aims at fracturing and opening up the theoretical possibilities of the data, whilst remaining open to exploring the breadth of the empirical <sup>fi</sup>eld. This study started with initial coding, which blended with and was eventually replaced by fo cused coding, the second analytical strategy. During focused coding, the researcher aims at integrating codes developed through initial coding into more abstract concepts with properties and dimensions. Coding was accompanied by memo writing, the third analytical strategy. Memo writing was crucial to recording analytical insights that emerged during coding. As the study progressed, memo writing turned increasingly conceptual, becoming central to developing and re<sup>fi</sup>ning the categories that emerged from focused coding. A total of 121 memos were written as part of this study. The length of these memos ranges from a few sentences, to several pages, most predominantly a handful of paragraphs long. Analytical memos also formed the primary basis for joint analysis between the three authors.

The reported study has been conducted as an independent part of a joint industry research and development (R&D) project within the Norwegian petroleum industry. The purpose of this R&D project is to demonstrate the technological feasibility of autonomous production beneath the polar icecap (Verhelst et al., 2010). In parallel with the ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author participated along with the third author in regular meetings and workshops in the R&D project. Participating in project arrangements provided ample opportunity for engaging representatives from vendors and operators in conversations about emerging issues in the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. The issue of sensor data, the kernel for the theory presented in this paper, emerged in the intersection between the ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and participating in these project arrangements. Discussions about data were a central feature in both arenas, albeit in different ways. Whereas the engineers in the operations centre would talk about the meaning of data, participants in the R&D project talked about the mechanics of generating data as well as using historic data to advance domain knowledge.

The centrality of such data talk emerged towards the end of the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. Together with the second and third authors' extensive experience from ethnographic studies of computer-mediated work within the petroleum industry (Almklov, 2006, 2008; Almklov & Hepsø, 2011; Hepsø, 1997, 2009; Hepsø, Monteiro, & Rolland, 2009), this led to the decision of pursuing the interaction between data gen eration and interpretation as the main topic of the study. This decision had implications for further data collection. While continuing the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork among the DPO engineers, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author also started attending a series of bi-weekly meetings between reservoir and production engineers in another <sup>fi</sup>eld located in the same operations center. Having learnt that data discussion were central in these meetings, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author saw these meetings as an occasion to study the interpretation processes that often were carried out silently by a single DPO engineer at his desktop computer. A <sup>fi</sup>eld trip to two of the industry partners participating in the R&D project was performed towards the end of the study to saturate the part of the emerging theory focusing on the research activities related to developing new instrumentation and software.

## 4. Sociomaterial knowing

Petroleum is bound in hydrocarbon molecules contained within cracks and pores of geological formations. Petroleum professionals refer to formations with significant hydrocarbon deposits as reservoirs. The reservoirs on the NCS are located thousands of meters beneath the seabed. Petroleum is produced from subsea wells drilled deep into these reservoirs. Since reservoir pressure is higher than atmospheric pressure, the <sup>fl</sup>uids contained within the reservoir stream out of the wells. Petroleum professionals refer to the mix of liquids, gases, and solid particles streaming out of the subsurface wells as the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. The well <sup>fl</sup>ow is the main material phenomenon to be monitored and controlled in petroleum production.

The wells, pipelines, and the well <sup>fl</sup>ow streaming through them are physically inaccessible to humans. A main concern in petroleum production is therefore to monitor and control phenomena and processes that are not directly available to human senses. Petroleum production unfolds within a conglomerate of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c models describing typical well behavior under speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions. Codi<sup>fi</sup>ed in varying degrees of rigidity – from qualitative descriptions to formal mathematical algorithms – the models of well behavior are circulated within petroleum engineering communities in the form of textbooks, research papers, or internal reports and training materials. These materials form the codified basis of the petroleum production domain knowledge. Control and systems theory is the underlying knowledge system for the models of well behavior. Control and systems theory frame the production process as a regulatory system to be monitored and controlled. The knowledge practices involved in controlling petroleum production are therefore tightly related to framing the production process as a regulatory system.

Most of what the production engineers know about the speci<sup>fi</sup>c moment-to-moment developments in the many wells they monitor and control is based on real-time data generated by a network of sensors. These sensors are mounted at <sup>fi</sup>xed positions within the wells and along the pipelines leading to the topside platform. The sensor network is part of a larger information infrastructure (II) (Bowker, Baker, Millerand, & Rhibes, 2007). Situated between the well and the engineer, this petroleum II forms a connection between the two, bridging the gap between non-quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed, undifferentiated natural phenomena and the graphic displays and quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed world of engineering. In addition to the remote sensors and the controller systems to which the sensors are connected, this II consists of production control systems, databases for storing sensor data, corporate production reporting databases, as well as a host of expert applications supporting production monitoring and planning. Together with the hardware infrastructure of sensors, network cabling, devices, and the many computers placed offshore and onshore, the material arrangements for knowing about the well <sup>fl</sup>ow also consists of intangible aspects such as software, protocols, and other standards for generating, transforming, storing, transmitting, and visualizing sensor data.

In this section, we describe and discuss the sociomaterial practices of understanding and controlling the well <sup>fl</sup>ow, and we will pursue the notion of dual materiality through a substantive grounded theory on sociomaterial knowing. This theory seeks to describe and conceptualize the actual ways in which knowledge emerges in petroleum production. The main theoretical proposition forwarded through this theory is that knowing emerges through the patterned interactions of 1) material phenomena, 2) material arrangements for knowing about these phenomena, and 3) knowledge practices. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c patterned interactions between particular con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of the three elements above are conceptualized as modes of knowing. Knowing in petroleum production emerges through three main modes of knowing: instrumentation, interpretation, and learning.

There are multiple relations between the three modes of knowing. While presented as discrete entities, the modes overlap and blend into each other in practice. Instrumentation precedes interpretation. By generating sensor data, instrumentation offers the basis for monitoring and controlling everyday petroleum production. Sensor data is also central to learning, but so is also experience from interpretation. Through learning codi<sup>fi</sup>ed models of well behavior are fed back into daily petroleum production (i.e. interpretation), as well as into the process of developing new hardware and software technologies for instrumentation. As such, learning also precedes both instrumentation and interpretation, even though we have chosen to present this mode of knowing last.

To empirically illustrate the theory on sociomaterial knowing, we will draw upon a particular kind of situation facing petroleum engineers, sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Unchecked, sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow may have catastrophic outcomes. It generates a sand blasting effect inside the production equipment, threatening to puncture pipes and valves. Punctured equipment on the topside platform sends a high-speed jet of liquids, gases, and solid particles onto the deck. This is dangerous, and may cause human injury or even death. Punctured equipment, both topside and subsea, causes environmental harm as petroleum pours uncontrolled into the ocean. A punctured pipe or valve also creates a gas leak. Gas leaks are considered the single most dangerous situation on any petroleum installation. The effect of leaking gas that ignites and explodes can have disastrous consequences, as illustrated by the massive oil spills in the Gulf of Mexico in the aftermath of a gas explosion onboard Deepwater Horizon in 2010.

## 4.1. Instrumentation

On my way back from lunch, I pop by the office of a production engineer. A few days earlier he had participated in preventing a major sand incident. “Do you have time to explain what happened?” I ask. Inviting me in, the production engineer starts explaining the situation using remote sensor data from the incident Pointing at the plotted graphs in the application window open on his desktop computer, he explains: “On all pipelines, in all wells, we have a sensor, simply a set of erosion probes to measure sand content in the well flow. When we have erosion on these probes, electrical resistance changes.” (Excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

The production process is controlled through a series of valves mounted within the wells, as well as on the topside platform itself. Instrumentation is a mode of knowing that emerges in the intersection between the undifferentiated, non-quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed, continuously unfolding well <sup>fl</sup>ow and the activities of controlling production as a regulatory system. It is a mode of knowing that arises from central scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowing practice that use sensor technologies for transforming material phenomena into quanti<sup>fi</sup>able objects of knowing. These practices span multiple social worlds. They span the world of the construction engineers who design and plan production installations as regulatory systems, the world of instrumentation vendors developing the hardware and software components for generating sensor data for controlling these regulatory systems, as well as the world of sensor engineers who test, calibrate, and replace sensors on operational installations. From the perspective of daily petroleum production, instrumentation is integral to monitoring and controlling the well <sup>fl</sup>ow as a regulatory system.

Sensors generate measurements of key characteristics of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Technological arrangements for transforming material phenomena into objects of knowing are inextricably entwined with scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowing practices and the material phenomena to be known through a set of interrelated instrumentation practices: material differentiation, characteristic numeration, and phenomenon punctuation. We will go through each of the practices in turn.

## 4.1.1. Material differentiation

Material phenomena lack clear boundaries and speci<sup>fi</sup>ed properties: they are undifferentiated. As an undifferentiated phenomenon, the well <sup>fl</sup>ow contains a multitude of potentialities. Through material differentiation, sensors single out an aspect or characteristic from this multitude of potentialities. Differentiation is a generic concept drawn from Bateson's (1972) epistemology. Bateson argues that the undifferentiated world can never enter into communication or mental processes because it contains a multitude of potentialities. Only through differentiation can the undifferentiated world enter into these processes.

Taking Bateson's concept as a point of departure, material differentiation is a relation between undifferentiated material phenomena and objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation, a central practice of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowing (Knorr Cetina, 2001). Objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation is the process of the framing of an object through a set of speci<sup>fi</sup>ed properties. The object resides within the boundaries outlined by its properties. To control daily petroleum production, the well <sup>fl</sup>ow is framed as an object with a set of speci<sup>fi</sup>ed properties such as pressure temperature, or sand content. Individual sensors are designed to isolate and measure a single property by letting speci<sup>fi</sup>c variations in the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow trigger electric signals, while not reacting to other aspects of the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow.

Pursuing the case of sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow, there is no denying that there are sand particles in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow when pipelines puncture and liquids, gases, and other solid particles jet onto the platform deck. Still, it is not before a sensor is mounted within a well or along pipelines that sand content is differentiated as a characteristic of the undifferentiated black, viscous <sup>fl</sup>uids streaming towards the topside platform. The properties isolated by sensors are not inherent in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Nor are they abstract concepts. Rather, these properties emerge in the intersection between the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow, objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation practices, and sensor arrangements.

## 4.1.2. Property numeration

In rendering the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow amenable to regulation control, construction engineers design a sensor network for measuring characteristics of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow at <sup>fi</sup>xed positions within wells and along pipelines. Through material differentiation, sensors transform the well <sup>fl</sup>ow into standardized electrical signals that can be readily communicated throughout the II to computers located on the offshore platform, in onshore operations centers, and beyond.

Sensor controller software generates numerical data about a single property of the non-quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed well <sup>fl</sup>ow through a series of transformations. Through this process of property numeration, the output of one transformation becomes the input of the next in the process of assigning a numerical value to the well <sup>fl</sup>ow as a material phenomenon (Latour, 1999). Material differentiation is the <sup>fi</sup>rst of these transformations. Sensors generate an electrical signal about a single property of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. After material differentiation, the electrical signal goes through two transformations. Engineers often refer to the outcome these two processes in terms of raw data and calculated values:

“What do you mean by calculated values, really?” I ask an engineer during a coffee break. “Sensors generate raw data,” he replies. “This is usually an indirect measure of the phenomenon. ER [erosion-resistance] sensors, for instance, measure sand content as a change in electric resistance across a metal probe. The change in resistance between the two measurements is the raw data. Sand content, however, is measured as grams of sand flowing across the probe per second. This is the calculated value. It is calculated from the raw value based on an algorithm developed by the vendor.” (Excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

Signals are transformed through mathematical algorithms inscribed in the sensor controller software. With the ER sensors, the transformation to raw data uses Ohm's law. Ohm's law is a mathematical algorithm which states the causal relationship between the electrical charge sent into a conductor, the measured voltage across the conductor, and the conductor's electrical resistance. The ER sensor vendor has developed an algorithm stating the causal relationship between changes in a particular metal's electrical resistance and amount of sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. The ER sensor controller software uses this algorithm for transforming the change in electrical resistance across the probe over a preset time interval into a standardized measure of grams of sand <sup>fl</sup>owing across the ER sensor per second.

Mathematical algorithms are an intrinsic part of the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c practice of making causal statements about the relationship between characteristics of objecti<sup>fi</sup>ed material phenomena. Raw data and calculated values arise in the intersection between the electrical signals produced through material differentiation, the practices of formulating causal relationships, and the software inscribed with the output of these practices. Again, sand content is not an intrinsic characteristic of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Nor is it an abstract concept. Rather, it emerges in the intersection between the electrical signals generated through material differentiation, algorithms that expressing causal relationship between characteristics of an object, and the software making the three transformations of property numeration.

## 4.1.3. Phenomenon deconstruction

The well <sup>fl</sup>ow is a continuously unfolding phenomenon, both in temporal and Euclidean space. The distribution of liquids, gases, and solid particles streaming out of a well evolves over time. The well <sup>fl</sup>ow changes weight, viscosity, and temperature depending on the mix of hydrocarbons streaming out of the well. While the distribution of liquids, gases, and solid particles streaming out of the well constantly <sup>fl</sup>uctuate, gas and water content steadily increase as the well ages. Pressure also drops as the reservoir is drained of <sup>fl</sup>uids. Spatially, the well <sup>fl</sup>ow evolves as it streams towards the topside platform. Through heat exchange with the icy waters surrounding the pipelines, the well <sup>fl</sup>ow cools down, becoming thicker and less <sup>fl</sup>uid as it streams along kilometers of subsurface pipelines. As the well <sup>fl</sup>ow thickens, the velocity drops, and heavier molecules start accumulating in the bends and manifolds along the line, increasing the pressure within the column stretching all the way back into the well.

A sensor is itself unable to measure change. It can only take measurements at speci<sup>fi</sup>c points in time. Instrumentation vendors draw upon the practice of expressing change through the Greek letter Δ (delta) to capture the well <sup>fl</sup>ow's unfolding character. A delta is the difference between two measuring points. By relating two sequential measurements from a sensor, the delta between these two measurements captures how a single characteristic in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow changes over time. Similarly, to capture how the well <sup>fl</sup>ow changes as it streams towards the topside platform, construction engineers draw upon this knowledge practice by placing sensors at standardized positions within wells and along pipelines. This way, it is possible to compare measurements from different positions to determine how the well <sup>fl</sup>ow evolves as it streams towards the platform.

The well <sup>fl</sup>ow is punctuated in Euclidean space by placing sensors at <sup>fi</sup>xed locations within wells and along pipelines. By taking measurements at given time intervals, sensors punctuate the well <sup>fl</sup>ow along temporal space. Measuring speci<sup>fi</sup>c properties of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow through material differentiation, sensors punctuate the well <sup>fl</sup>ow in conceptual space. We collectively refer to these ways of punctuating the well <sup>fl</sup>ow as phenomenon deconstruction. Phenomenon punctuation is a central premise of the mode of knowing we will now turn to: interpretation.

Some minutes past 9 am, Peter's mobile phone rings. Peter is this week's production controller. The production controller is an ambulating role among the onshore production engineers. The offshore operator tells Peter that they have a sand alarm in well E-3: Please advise further actions.

Peter says the curve shows no increase in pressure. If E-2 had been shut in, the down-hole pressure would have increased. “E-3 has a history of false sand alarms,” says Barry. “Let's just wait and see how the situation develops.”

## 4.3. Interpretation

The above vignette illustrates how production engineers work to assess the situation before acting upon a sand alarm. They approach the sensor data triggering the alarm with suspicion and caution. The procedures for handling sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow cause production disruptions. Dedicated equipment has to be diverted from other operations, and the suspected well's production volume is turned down to minimize the effect of sand on the production equipment and the processing plant. The task of the production engineers, however, is to optimize daily production. This is made explicit by tying their annual salary bonuses to annual production volumes. As such, acting blindly upon the alarm is not an option for them.

Yet, not acting upon the situation is not an option, either. Even when there is no danger of human injury or environmental harm, sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow can cause signi<sup>fi</sup>cant production disruptions, potentially ruining the well for good. Furthermore, sand clogs up the processing equipment. This requires clean-up work, reducing the amount of hydrocarbons the topside petrochemical plant can process. Unchecked sand also erodes pipes and valves, and maintenance personnel have to inspect the production equipment for damage, requiring a shutdown of parts of the processing plant, which in turn reduces production. Therefore when the sand alarm sounds, the production engineers choose to assess the situation before making any corrective measures.

To assess the situation, the production engineers engage in the second mode of sociomaterial knowing, interpretation. Interpretation consists of a set of interrelated practices: visualized pattern decoding, sort ing out interferences, and circulating background information.

## 4.3.1. Visualized pattern decoding

“We don't work with decimals here [a commonly used euphemism for exact numerical data values]. The data values may themselves be entirely off. We look at trends, whether they go up or down.” (Produc tion engineer, excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

A trend is a pattern in a graph. Plotting data in graphs is a technique for visualizing how the numerical value of data changes over time. Data visualization techniques are central in most science and engineering endeavors (Ihde, 1999). Engineers identify data signatures by decoding visualized data. Such visualized pattern decoding is part and parcel of visualization techniques. Visualized pattern decoding is made possible by, but also gives meaning and utility to, visualization techniques. Data visualizations have limited function, beyond aesthetics, if not accompanied by decoding practices. Similarly, decoding practices have limited function without relating to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c visualization technique.

Laying out a series of temporally punctuated data (see Section 4.1.3) as a graph is the most common visualization technique used in petroleum production. It is a visualization technique where the graphical display capabilities of desktop computer applications are used to illustrate the continuously unfolding nature of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. It is also a visualization technique that draws upon knowledge practices that are well known by engineers. While seldom explicitly taught as a skill in universities and colleges, engi neering students are domesticated into visualized pattern decoding as authors of textbooks and training materials plot data in graphs. Students consequently learn the skill implicitly through reading and trying to understand these materials. Visualizing data in graphs is also one of the predominant ways of present ing data within engineering disciplines, in internal reports as well as in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c papers. Similarly, most of the engineering applications used by the production engineers visualize sensor data as graphs. Some applications have yet to implement such plots. In these cases the engineers will plot the required data using standardized of<sup>fi</sup>ce spreadsheet software that they connect to the corporation's backend databases.

In decoding visualized data, engineers name particular patterns. They use terms such as ‘sand production’, ‘swallowing’, and ‘slugging’. To the ethnographic researcher with little or no prior knowledge of petroleum production, these terms have limited meaning. Yet, this naming serves the function of relating patterns in the data to generic models of well behavior that describe of how wells behave under speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions. Identifying these behavioral signatures in visualized data constitutes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of what production engineers themselves refer to as domain knowledge, and forms the link between data and appropriate actions to handle the situation. As well as describing how wells behave under speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions, generic models of well behavior also prescribe the actions that have to be taken to control wells under the described conditions. By laying out the expected sequence of events, these models prescribe how to handle the situation, what the production engineers need to do, and in what sequence.

Sensor data has little meaning without relating behavioral signatures with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c model of well behavior. Some months prior to the <sup>fi</sup>rst author starting his <sup>fi</sup>eldwork at the Pan Field, a down-hole sand avalanche had <sup>fi</sup>lled up hundreds of meters of pipelines from one of the <sup>fi</sup>eld's wells, effectively destroying it. Talking about the post-mortem analysis of the sensor data from this incident, APC's leading expert on sand management explained:

“The data was clear for those of us with knowledge of how sand producing wells behave. The production engineers on the Pan Field lacked this knowledge. They did not recognize the indicators [read: behavioral signature] before the pipeline was filled with sand and irreparable damage had been done.” (Excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

It is by embodying knowledge of data signatures and their relation to the generic models of well behavior that engineers recognize patterns in confusing data. Experience and expert judgment are to a certain extent grounded in generic models of well behavior that guide the attention, perceptions, and actions of the engineers. However, experience and expert judgment also have tacit components that guide the production engineers' perceptions and gut feeling analyses. Through experience, they come to recognize patterns in confounding data, and their attention is drawn towards anomalous patterns.

An important aspect of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow is its temporal dimension. The gradient of graphs, indicating the tempo in which events down-hole unfolds, is not only part of determining a problem, but it is often a key indicator of the urgency of actions.

## 4.3.2. Sorting out interferences

Instead of taking the prescribed steps to control sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow, the production engineers in the vignette above start discussing alternative causes for the sand alarm: can it be an interaction effect with another well, is it caused by a faulty sensor, or is there really sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow?

Engineers and operators approach sensor data with caution and suspicion. They know the data may resonate with other phenomena even though sensors are designed to isolate and measure a single property in the mix of <sup>fl</sup>uids, gases, and solid particles <sup>fl</sup>owing past it. These interferences (Mol, 1999) are a product of the material interaction between the particular material arrangements of individual sensors and the well <sup>fl</sup>ow (see Section 4.1.1). Interferences emerge from a particular sensor type's material arrangements for differentiating properties of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. At times, interferences remain silent in the background. At other times, under different conditions, they may show up in the sensor data.

Engineers and operators interrogate sensor data to sort out interferences. Sorting out interferences builds on the knowledge about the mechanics of a particular sensor types' material differentiation, as well as local knowing about the history of individual sensors and ongoing activities in the <sup>fi</sup>eld that may affect the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Sorting out interferences may be triggered by engineers or operators recognizing anomalies in visualized data. It is, however, also a routine activity when assessing a potential incident, as illustrated in the above vignette.

While interferences arise from interactions of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow and the material arrangements of sensors, interferences may be caused by different kinds of phenomena. Different interactional causes require different kinds of sorting out interferences activities that build on different knowledge practices. The ER sensor can illustrate these differences:

“It [the sand probe] is vulnerable to rises in temperature. The probe is based on a measuring element, and you send a current through this element to measure the ohmic resistance. And then, when it is subjected to erosion. the element becomes thinner, and the ohmic resistance increases. But ohmic resistance is a function of temperature, so the probe may cause false alarms if the temperature rises.” (Senior production engineer, excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

The ER sensor is designed to measure the amount of sand flowing across the probe each second Through material differentiation, an electric current is used to measure resistance across a metal probe. A change in electrical resistance indicates erosion. Drawing upon the interaction between metal probes and the well <sup>fl</sup>ow to measure sand content may bring another phenomenon into being: temperature increase. That a medium's ohmic resistivity increases as its temperature increases in<sup>fl</sup>uences a sensor based on the change of the same resistance caused by erosion. The way the ER sensor differentiates sand content as a characteristic of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow by transforming sand into an issue of ohmic resistance introduces interference in the sense that the ER probe potentially brings about two phenomena at the same time: increasing sand content and/or increasing temperature.

While material differentiation (see Section 4.1.1) is the cause of interferences, phenomenon deconstruction (see Section 4.1.3) offers a way to sort out interferences by correlating measurements from different sensors. The sand monitoring software has a function that correlates erosion curves with a temperature curve from the same location along the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Making this correlation, engineers recognize the parallel pattern of increasing erosion and temperature as interference. A lack of parallel pattern dismisses temperature as a potential source of interference

Similarly, increased velocity may cause another phenomenon to interfere with transformation to a numerical resistance value:

“When we have erosion, resistivity across the probes changes.” The production engineer points at a rising curve in the plot displayed by the engineering software. “You see, this effect can be caused by erosion, but it can also be caused by changes in the well's flow pattern [read: the velocity of the well flow].” (Excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

As the well <sup>fl</sup>ow velocity increases, the amount of sand <sup>fl</sup>owing across the sand probe increases even though the amount of sand in the well flow remains constant. These two phenomena are immanent in the data generated by the sand probe. Therefore, we say that the temperature changes and increased <sup>fl</sup>ow velocity are always present in the sand data, even though these phenomena may not manifest themselves. When the production engineers in the above vignette look at E-2 and the MPM, they are evaluating phenomena such as whether there might be changes in the <sup>fl</sup>ow pattern, temperature, <sup>fl</sup>uid composition or velocity of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Similarly, when one of the production engineers asks how long it has been since the down-hole MPM was tested they are trying to assess whether the sensor itself could be a source of interference in the incoming production data. MPMs are known to drift, meaning that over time the data they generate become increasingly unreliable.<sup>1</sup> The production engineer, therefore, checks the spreadsheet that summarizes when the <sup>fi</sup>eld's wells are tested to determine how long it has been since the MPM was calibrated to determine its reliability.

The information stored in this spreadsheet is part of the background information that goes into situated diagnosis. Interferences often have their roots in activities external to the DPO's immediate environment. Production data are always seen in light of a more general background of local knowing.

## 4.3.3. Circulating background information

Local knowing is the product of organizational-level practices for circulating background information. Background information takes two different forms: information about ongoing activities, and biographical information about wells and equipment.

Weekday mornings are a busy period in the onshore operations center. The hallways are <sup>fi</sup>lled with people scuttling to and from meeting rooms, as engineers from the <sup>fi</sup>eld's many departments circulate between numerous coordination meetings held between 8 am and 9.30 am. By circulating engineers from different communities among multiple meetings, a key function of these coordination meetings is to circulate information about ongoing and scheduled activities throughout the organization. Information about ongoing activities is particularly useful for sorting out interferences. This is illustrated in the vignette, as one of the production engineers frames the sand alarm in terms of a scheduled shut-in announced during a morning meeting that they. More generally, however, information about ongoing activities serves the role of more general information that guides the production engineers' attention and perception when monitor ing everyday production.

Biographical information about particular wells and particular pieces of equipment is also important for monitoring everyday production. This type of background information is more commonly circulated in the form of narratives about particular incidents, a particular well or piece of equipment:

“Do we trust E-3's multiphase meter?” asks Julie. Barry looks at her: “Absolutely. 100%.” They both laugh. Puzzled, I inquire about the joke. Julie explains that the sand probes in E-3 had been causing false alarms. A supply ship was requisitioned to replace the sand probes. After this operation, the multiphase meter on E-3 has been giving consistently bad data. (Excerpt from <sup>fi</sup>eld notes)

Narratives, like the one above, offer biographical information about of sensors and production equipment. Narratives are often supplemented with electronically recorded information. Numerous sources of electroni information exist. Different kinds of logs are available throughout the organization as shared electronic resources. Some logs are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as structured electronic reports in APC's enterprise resource planning system. Others are simply word processor documents or spreadsheets where the engineers enter log information available in different shared spaces in the corporation's document management system. Information is also stored in APC's document management system, where engineers store different kinds of reports. Some forms of electronically recorded information, like the Well Contingency Plan (see 3.1), offer concrete information about the state and typical well behavior. Other forms of electronically recorded information, such as the logs on well tests that Julie consults in the vignette above, offer indirect information.

Background information therefore forms a context for working with sensor data. When background information is missing, engineers have no such grounding for their attention and perception. Background information about ongoing activities in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and the state of sensors and production equipment is, therefore, important for the production engineers to identify interferences in the production data as it draws their attention towards the possible interaction effects with which production data can resonate. Neither is a given instance of situated sensor data completely independent from previous or similar data elsewhere. Similar data patterns appear in different oil and gas <sup>fi</sup>elds with the same or similar instrumentation and technologies, like the MPM, for instance. Local knowing, as in knowing the instruments, is also based on <sup>fi</sup>nding similarities across contexts. The point here is that seeing or envisioning the problem at hand is not a passive reception or mechanized matching of features of visual data but a bodily activity where tools, representational forms, and training shape one's vision. In this setting “similar” is not the “same” but shows a degree of sameness, a kind of family resemblance. “Similar” interpretation practices emerge by taking the “same” work practice but transforming it to local instances.

## 4.4. Learning

As <sup>fi</sup>elds evolve, so do their reservoirs and wells. Wells in mature <sup>fi</sup>elds tend to behave differently from wells in newly opened <sup>fi</sup>elds. As such, over time there is a shift in which models of well behavior are most relevant to describe how the particular wells on the <sup>fi</sup>eld behave. Knowing which models of well behavior are relevant at a particular point in time constitutes part of what the production engineers refer to as local knowledge. As different models are required to describe well behavior at different points in the <sup>fi</sup>eld's lifecycle, the emphasis on meaningful correlations also shift. When meaningful correlations from multiple sources are not available in a single visualization, the production engineers will typically use the dual monitors at the front of the DPO room to display one plot on each monitor and make manual correlations between the two plots.

When such manual correlations turn out to be useful over time, the production engineers use off-the-shelf of<sup>fi</sup>ce spreadsheet software to automate the correlation. Backend production databases can be accessed with a standardized data communication and transfer protocol supported by the spreadsheet software, allowing it to retrieve data from relevant sources and correlate them in plots. Although not part of APC's corporate IS portfolio, these spreadsheets proliferate throughout the corporation, forming an undergrowth of specialized software that addresses the immediate, local needs of the engineers. Local learning unfolds through developing new and modifying existing spreadsheets. Local software development sediments local knowing as the production engineers inscribe which models of well behavior are most relevant at a particular point in time of a <sup>fi</sup>eld's evolution in the spreadsheets they develop.

There is limited sharing of spreadsheets between departments and <sup>fi</sup>elds. Still, because of the high rotation of personnel between <sup>fi</sup>elds, there is a tendency towards standardized spreadsheets. Engineers tend to recreate the spreadsheets they used in previous jobs when they arrive at new <sup>fi</sup>elds. More systematic efforts at automating data correlations are made in APC's many research-based projects. These projects are typically part of the ongoing effort to develop a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c basis for the corporation's operations by formalizing domain knowledge. Drawing upon the post-mortem analysis of historical production data. these projects often go hand in hand with software development. Even though these projects are run by APC's R&D division, they unfold at the corporate level because the software developed is intended to be part of the corporation's IS portfolio and used across <sup>fi</sup>elds.

The sand monitoring software used at the Pan Field is an example of such a corporate-level initiative. Based on a post-mortem analysis of several sand incidents at the Pan Field, researchers in APC's R&D division re<sup>fi</sup>ned existing models of well behavior for sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Sand data had previously been displayed as data values in a simple dashboard-like application supplied by the vendor of the ER probes. The production engineers themselves collectively refer to this original application as “completely useless”. The vendor acknowledged this by saying that “data values are of little use for engineers and operators”. Based on the re<sup>fi</sup>ned models of well behavior, the vendor developed a new version of the sand monitoring application in close collaboration with the researchers and representatives from APC's production engineers. This new application automates meaningful data correlations for determining whether there is sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow through multiple plots correlating trends from different data sources.

Although the automation of data correlates is most apparent in visualizations, the automation of meaningful correlates is also part of generating calculated values. To elaborate, the concept of calculation itself needs some explanation. Some calculations put single inputs through an algorithm, such as the transformation from resistance measured across the ER probe's metal element to a grams of sand per second value. This involves the correlation of data to a model. In this case, the model is an algorithm describing the physical relation between particular metals' erosion and the amount of sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. This algorithm has been developed as part of a study made by the probe vendor. Other calculations, however, take multiple input sources, both in calculating as well as aggregating. The acoustic sand probe used at several of Pan's sibling <sup>fi</sup>elds on the NCS exempli<sup>fi</sup>es this. Acoustic sand probes are mounted on the outside of <sup>fl</sup>ow lines, picking up the sound of particles hitting the piping. Yet, sound is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient data to determine the amount of sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow:

“In the piping, you depend on the velocity [of the well flow]. Sand particles will hit the pipe wall, which will be detected by the [acoustic] sand probe. The higher the flow rate, the better data quality from the detectors. (…) We have a system set up like this: we have a multiphase meter on all wells providing the [well's well flow velocity], meters per second, to the sand detection system. The sand detection system then calculates the gram per second sand rate.” (Leading corporate expert on sand in well <sup>fl</sup>ow)

The sand rate is thus an aggregated value generated by correlating acoustic data generated by the sand probe with velocity data generated by the MPM. Aggregations are, therefore, a low-level automation of data correlations. The correlations, however, are based on a model describing the physical relation among the acoustic sound, well <sup>fl</sup>ow velocity, and amount of sand in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. This model was devel oped as part of a research-based initiative for improving sand monitoring within APC, and is formalized as a conversion table that has been inscribed in the production control system as an algorithm

Research-based initiatives thus show how the situated activities of well diagnosis are encircled in an ongoing process of analyzing historical data to develop new and re<sup>fi</sup>ne existing models of well behavior. In turn, these models are fed back into instrumentation in the form of new sensors, improved calculation algorithms, and new software applications. New instrumentation generates new production data, which in turn are used to re<sup>fi</sup>ne existing models and develop others.

By automating data correlations, the modus of knowing shifts between the production engineers and the ICTs in the petroleum Il. Inscribing locally relevant data correlates in a spreadsheet moves part of the local knowing about which models of well behavior are most relevant at a particular point in time in a <sup>fi</sup>eld's lifecycle to that spreadsheet. Knowing which models of well behavior are most relevant for the <sup>fi</sup>eld is not only constituted through the engineers' actions in correlating data, but also through the data correlates availability in locally developed spreadsheets. Local knowledge, understood as knowing which data correlates are most relevant for the <sup>fi</sup>eld, is distributed between the engineers and the spreadsheets. Automating data correlates, therefore, shifts the emphasis of knowing between the engineers and the ICTs. Developing spreadsheets locally to transform data points into visualizations entwines the engineers' local knowledge with that of the spreadsheets.

Knowing does not simply shift between engineers and software, new forms of knowing in practice also manifest in the ways the engineers use the software. Knowing how to use particular applications activates the knowing inscribed in the software, The production engineers in Pan's DPO room describe the new sand monitoring application as having completely changed the way they work. This is partly because they are now involved in handling sand incidents since they have real-time sand data available, whereas it was only the CCR who handled such incidents prior to the advent of the application. But it is also partly because the sand monitoring tool through its multiple plots leads the engineers towards new ways of working with the data: not only correlating trends, but also tracing along the mediation chain to compare data at differ ent stages in the transformation from raw to calculated values. This leads engineers to more actively interrogating sand data. This interrogation, however, builds upon the research-based knowing of common sources of interference in the data signals. This knowing is activated as the engineers <sup>fl</sup>ip through the different visualizations in the sand monitoring software.

For the production engineers, knowing how to use the software applications becomes a way of activating the knowing inscribed in the ICTs. As such, the practical knowing becomes distributed and the transformations made through the mediation chain – whether these are calculations or visualizations – become entwined with and inseparable from the human practices of well diagnosis. The transformations made throughout the sensor network become even more entwined with well diagnosis practice when it comes to sorting out interferences in the data.

## 5. Discussion

We have explored the relationship between materiality and knowing in petroleum production through a theory on sociomaterial knowing. Through three modes of knowing, we empirically elaborate on what Orlikowski (2006) more programmatically refers to as the “deep intermingling of knowing, practice, and materiality” (p.461) through an analysis of how knowing emerges in the patterned interactions between the well <sup>fl</sup>ow (material phenomena to be known), the petroleum II (material arrangements for knowing about the phenomenon), and knowledge practices. Along with the rest of the literature on sociomateriality, we see this paper as part of ongoing discussions about the relationship between technology and its social context of development and use that stretches back several decades. With a joint focus on theorizing the relationship between information technology and organizations, IS and IS-related literature within management and organization sciences have contributed towards these discussions. This paper is, therefore, located within and draws upon a broader theoretical landscape that encompasses science and technology studies (Bijker, Hughes, & Pinch, 1987; Bijker & Law, 1992), as well as distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995) and the philosophy of technology (Ihde, 1999).

The notion of dual materiality is the main contribution of this paper. This notion contributes to the broader discourse on sociomateriality by re<sup>fi</sup>ning ideas of materiality, which has thus far been predominantly limited to the materiality of technological artifacts. Dual materiality highlights how IS becomes important, as its materiality plays an integral part in creating, not simply representing, the materiality of the physical world, in our case, the well <sup>fl</sup>ow.

While the term dual materiality may be new within research on sociomateriality, we claim no provenance for the insight it sheds light upon. The insight of dual materiality is central to Barad's (2007) interpretation of the phenomenon-instrumentation relationship in Bohr's quantum physics philosophy. Ihde (1999) argues for a similar relationship between phenomenon and instrument. Using the sound of a pen tapping on a table as an example, Ihde argues that the sound resonates with both the table and the pen. Change pens or tap with the same pen on another surface, and the sound changes. In the same way that Bohr finds conceptual properties – such as particle position – to be the effect of specific physical arrangements that atomic particles are a constituent part of, so will sound resonate with both the instrument (pen) and the phenomenon (surface). In phenomenon-instrument relations, “the instrumentation can never totally disappear” (Ihde, 1999, p.163, italics in original). What the instrument generates, sound in Ihde's example or sensor data in this paper, will always resonate with both the material phenomenon and the instrument for measuring the phenomenon.

We are con<sup>fi</sup>dent that our study of the deep intermingling of work, technology, and organizing would be incomplete if we did not take into account the duality of materiality in petroleum production. As such, a more re<sup>fi</sup>ned view on materiality can have several implications for the study of sociomateriality.

An analytical implication of dual materiality is to de-emphasize the focus on human interpretation as a form of workaround for “individuals and organizations [to] achieve reliable performance when working with unreliable systems” (Butler & Gray, 2006, p.2). Without an understanding of how sensor data arises through the interaction between physical phenomena and sensor technologies, the practice of sorting out interferences (Section 4.3.2) may appear as a way of overcoming the technical shortcomings of sensor data. The consequence of this may be that of an analysis of how people organize around technologies (Leonardi & Barley, 2008). In contrast, dual materiality actualizes the process of data generation and shows how interferences arise out of the interaction between physical phenomena and sensor technologies. For this reason, sorting out interferences is not the product of an unreliable technology, but rather the product of sensor data that resonates with both the instrument and phenomenon.

Emphasizing the duality of materiality in sensor-intensive work also questions the appropriateness of the idea that technology represents physical phenomena. Somewhat broadly speaking, both objectivistand human-centric perspectives on knowing are grounded in the view that the knowing subject is ontologically separate from the object to be known. In such a view, representations mediate between the knowing subject and the object to be known. This ontological separation may explain why much of IS research tends to focus on data/information representation on the one hand and human interpretation on the other as distinctly separate activities. The underlying assumption here is that representations are passive descriptions of reality (Tsoukas, 1998). With basis in such a view, interpretation becomes the activity of decoding representations. The theory presented in this paper brings attention to how engineers constantly explore new ways of generating, calibrating, and visualizing sensor data by modifying hardware, software applications, and work activities. Such a sociomaterial conception of interpretation suggests that it is highly problematic to study representation and interpretation as distinctly different activities when studying the relationship between human activity and technology.

Instead, we focus on visualization practices, rather than data representations, as an analytical strategy to overcome this challenge. With this analytical strategy, representations – in our case visualizing sensor data as graphs – become the end product of speci<sup>fi</sup>c visualization practices. Visualization practices bring out the duality of materiality in petroleum production by showing how these practices themselves are the product of the interaction between sensor technologies that are unable to measure change and the undifferentiated well <sup>fl</sup>ow (Section 4.1.3). Visualizing a single data point gives the production engineer no understanding of the unfolding character of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. As visualization practice, then, plotting temporally punctuated sensor data as a graph encompasses both the temporal dimension and data values. The visualization practice chosen to interpret sensor data is therefore also inextricably entwined with the unfolding nature of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow and the interaction between the well <sup>fl</sup>ow and instrumentation in generating data.

We need to point out that this emphasis on dual materiality should by no means be taken as a deterministic argument that visualization practice follows by necessity from the interaction of physical phenomena and sensor technologies. The impetuous towards including the temporal dimension in the visualization practice can be traced back to the skills acquired by engineers through formal education. Engineers are trained in interpreting graphs, and their choice of visualization strategy should therefore be understood as being contin gent upon and inextricably entwined with the knowledge practices of formally trained engineers. This way, a sociomaterial conception of interpretation emphasizes how engineers not only “read” data, but how they actively intervene in the relationship between the materiality of the instruments and the phenomenon to be known. While several researchers have illustrated how representations contribute to shaping priorities and organizational reality (Pollock & Williams, 2011; Tsoukas, 1998), the notion of dual materiality contributes to re<sup>fi</sup>ning the idea of materiality by bringing attention to how the work involved with interpreting sensor data is inseparable from both data visualization and the process of data generation in the intersection between material phenomena and the material arrangements for knowing about these phenomena

The analysis presented in this paper also brings attention to the materiality of theory, which is often regarded as immaterial or abstract. Theories of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow as an object of knowledge play an important role in determining which properties to isolate through material differentiation. The mechanics of material differentiation (Section 4.1.1), and later the transformations of property numeration (Section 4.1.2), build on theories of physical regularities. In the case of the ER sensor measuring the sand content in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow, these include general physical theories (Ohm's law), as well more context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c theories of physical regularities (the relationship between changes in electrical resistance and erosion, and the relationship between erosion and sand content in the well <sup>fl</sup>ow). Because of this, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the engineers' “mediated vision sees the real through the medium of technology, and consequently, the theories underlying that speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology” (Styhre, 2010, p.71). The materiality of the engineers' mediated vision is informed by accumulated knowledge, from abstract theories to local experience, which are instilled in the IS from the sensor to the display. More directly, the centrality of theory in mediated vision is related to the practice of sorting out interferences (Section 4.3.2). Knowing that erosion data has to be correlated with temperature changes is directly related to how ER sensors use Ohm's law to differentiate sand content as a property of the well <sup>fl</sup>ow. Even so, theory also plays an equally important background for the entire endeavor of interpretation, as the ecology of generic models of well behavior, together with theories of physical regularities, form the wider context against which the data has meaning. This background is important in the process of making sense of the clearly delineated parts of material reality constituted through the data that is punctuated in Euclidean, temporal, and conceptual space (Section 4.1.3). Hence, the theory of sociomaterial knowing presented in this paper shows how knowing in petroleum production is inextricably entwined with the underlying theories of material reality through instrumentation and interpretation.

By being speci<sup>fi</sup>c on materialities in knowing, we have shown how the situated activity of interpretation stretches across multiple contexts. Following Leonardi and Barley's (2008) methodological advice of studying the interplay between development and use, we have sought to unpack the role of materiality in petroleum production. The relationship between the three modes of sociomaterial knowing shows a circular motion, in which historical sensor data feed into learning processes where they are further interpreted and codi<sup>fi</sup>ed as models of well behavior. These models then form the basis for improving existing or developing new instrumentation and software. New instrumentation generates more sensor data, which in turn feeds back into the formalization practices for developing new theory. This movement shows the cross-situatedness of sociomaterial knowing in petroleum production. Each context of work has its own peculiarities, and studies of situated practices are important for understanding how data are subject to situated interpretation. The theory on sociomaterial knowing shows the connectedness of the situated context to its surroundings. We argue and demonstrate that the data are not givens passively presented to the group's situated interpretation, but instead actively manipulate and adjust the machinery by which the data are generated. We also show how the experience and knowledge of the group travels out of their immediate context, which manifests itself in new tools, procedures and software.

In this paper, we have elaborated on the notion of dual materiality. Earlier in this section, we have drawn some analytical implications of this notion. In conclusion, a more general implication of this paper on the study of sociomateriality is the need to take into account material multiplicities in order to increase the precision of our analyses. We therefore hope that the notion of dual materiality can encourage further exploration of different materialities in contemporary work and organizing.
