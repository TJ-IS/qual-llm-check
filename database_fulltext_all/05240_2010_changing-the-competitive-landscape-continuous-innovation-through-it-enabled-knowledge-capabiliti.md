W<sup>e</sup> <sup>theoretically</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>empirically</sup> <sup>investigate</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>relationship</sup> <sup>between</sup> <sup>information</sup> <sup>technology</sup> <sup>(IT)</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>firm</sup> innovation. Invoking absorptive capacity (ACAP) theory, we introduce and develop the concepts of three types of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities. Firm innovation is examined through two observable innovation outcomes: patents, and new product and service introductions. These innovation outcomes are often labeled as competitive actions aggressively undertaken by firms to gain market share or to achieve profitability. We use secondary data about IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and innovation outcomes of 110 firms. Our data results provide strong support for our main assertion that knowledge capabilities that are enhanced through the use of IT contribute to firm innovation. The study’s findings suggest that the three types of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities have differential effects on firm innovation. This study substantially contributes to the information systems (IS) research, methodology, and practice in multiple ways.

## 1. Introduction

Innovation is defined as “the design, invention, development and/or implementation of new or altered products, services, processes, systems, organizational structures, or business models for the purpose of creating new value for customers and financial returns for the firm” (Advisory Report to the Secretary of Commerce of the United States 2008, p. i). Along the innovation pathway, there are multiple outcomes that could be observed at different stages of innovation (Jensen and Webster 2004). These innovation outcomes involve developed new ideas or technical solutions that often take the form of patent inventions (Nerkar and Shane 2007), new product and service introductions (Hansen and Birkinshaw 2007), diffusion of newly released products and services (Hansen and Birkinshaw 2007, Rogers 1995), and revenues generated from new product and service sales (Nerkar and Shane 2007). Two of them—patent inventions and new product and service introductions—are often labeled as competitive actions that firms aggressively undertake to gain market share or to achieve profitability (Miller and Cheng 1994, Ferrier et al. 1999, Smith et al. 2001).

In the market, while incremental new product and service introductions redistribute shares within an existing market, radical new product and service introductions provide a high potential for market expansion that substantially increase the size of the existing market (Golder and Tellis 1997, Sorescu et al. 2003, Aboulnasr et al. 2008). Newly developed ideas in the form of patent inventions often imply a high potential for market destabilization, not only cannibalizing business from existing competitors but also repositioning existing products relative to each other (Aboulnasr et al. 2008). These innovations undertaken as competitive actions in the product-market space are agents of “creative destruction” (Schumpeter 1942)—they can destroy existing markets and yet often yield vast new market opportunities (Aboulnasr et al. 2008), substantially changing the competitive landscape in the market. Thus they are the focus of this study for this Information Systems Research special issue, which centers on competitive dynamics and digital systems.

This study is motivated by several factors. First, today, to compete and survive, firms increasingly depend on their knowledge capabilities to continuously innovate (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Information technology (IT) is critical for supporting knowledge management initiatives and nurturing innovation (Alavi and Leidner 2001). IT enables the creation, dissemination, and use of knowledge (Davenport et al. 2008), thus greatly augmenting and enabling firms’ knowledge capabilities (which we refer to as IT-enabled knowledge capabilities). IT’s contribution and assistance in augmenting, building, and strengthening firms’ knowledge capabilities have been recognized (e.g., Alavi and Leidner 2001, Sambamurthy and Subramani 2004, Tanriverdi 2005). The link between knowledge capabilities and firm innovation has also been emphasized (e.g., Leonard-Barton 1995). Yet, limited research has been done to examine theoretically and empirically the link between IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and firm innovation, although a few conceptual and anecdotal studies (e.g., Davenport 1993, Holsapple and Singh 2003, Davenport et al. 2008) and even fewer empirical studies (e.g., Tippins and Sohi 2003, Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2005) have examined this relationship. As more companies turn to IT in an attempt to enhance firm competitiveness through innovation, this gap must be systematically addressed by explicitly examining the relationships between IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and different innovation outcomes along the innovation pathway.

Second, mixed results have been found in the literature in establishing a positive relationship between IT and firm performance (Kohli and Devaraj 2003). Emergent empirical evidence has increasingly shown that there is no significant direct relationship between IT investment and firm performance outcomes such as profitability (e.g., Powell and Dent-Micalef 1997, Kohli and Devraj 2003, Tippins and Sohi 2003). It is our premise that to extract the business value from IT, firms need to cultivate IT-enabled knowledge capabilities to innovate continuously so as to enhance firm performance. This relationship is yet to be systematically investigated.

Third, research in competitive dynamics has widely examined patterns of competitive actions (e.g., action repertoire or a series of actions) (Ferrier 2001) and their antecedents and consequences (Smith et al. 2001). Some research has also examined a specific type of competitive actions such as market entry and exit (Baum and Korn 1996). However, patent inventions and new product and service introductions, as two important types of competitive actions that have a great potential to disrupt the competitive status quo and cause disequilibrium in the market, have not yet been carefully examined in the existing literature.

To bridge the gap in the literature, we draw on absorptive capacity (ACAP) theory to examine whether and how IT-enabled knowledge capabilities affect firm innovation. We argue that IT-enabled knowledge capabilities enhance firm innovation by facilitating creation of patent inventions and introduction of new products and services into the market. Our findings provide strong support for our thesis. We also uncover that IT-enabled knowledge capabilities have differential effects on firm innovation. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin by discussing the theoretical underpinning of our research model. We then describe our research method and discuss our data analysis and results. We conclude the paper with our research contributions and future research.

## 2. Theoretical Background

In this section, we develop the concept of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and provide a theoretical underpinning for our research model by invoking the theory of ACAP. The application of ACAP in various studies has not been literal, and each study instantiates ACAP and its components to accommodate its unique context. This is the case in our work as well. We first invoke the notion of ACAP to frame diverse information technologies that enable firms’ dynamic knowledge capabilities. We then build on ACAP theory to theorize the differential effects of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities on firm innovation.

ACAP is often viewed as a dynamic capability of processing knowledge that enhances firm innovation (Zahra and George 2002). This study nuances the broad notion of ACAP by theorizing and empirically testing IT’s contributions to firms’ abilities to manage knowledge. ACAP literature argues that a set of organizational routines and processes by which firms acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit knowledge can produce dynamic capabilities (e.g., Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Mowery and Oxley 1995; Kim 1997a, b; Zahra and George 2002). These dynamic capabilities build on each other to yield ACAP. ACAP helps create and deploy the knowledge necessary to build other organizational capabilities (e.g., marketing, distribution, and production). ACAP has been widely invoked in the prior literature. Some studies have invoked ACAP broadly to indicate firms’ receptivity to technological change (Kedia and Bhagat 1988); some have invoked the concept to gauge firms’ ability to use outside knowledge (Koza and Lewin 1998) or replicate new knowledge (Tsai 2001); others have invoked the concept to link firms’ IT usage patterns in the supply chain to their knowledge creation ability (Malhotra et al. 2005). In this study, we invoke ACAP to conceptualize firms’ IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and link these capabilities to firm innovation.

## 2.1. IT-Enabled Absorptive Capacity (IT-ACAP)

ACAP has two subsets of potential and realized absorptive capacities (RACAP) (Zahra and George 2002). Potential absorptive capacity (PACAP) consists of knowledge acquisition and assimilation capabilities, making the firm amenable to acquiring and assimilating knowledge (Lane and Lubatkin 1998,

Zahra and George 2002). RACAP consists of knowledge transformation and exploitation capabilities, which reflect firms’ capacity to leverage the knowledge that has been absorbed (Zahra and George 2002). PACAP and RACAP have distinct roles that are complementary.

2.1.1. IT-Enabled Potential ACAP (IT-PACAP). Information technologies that help support knowledge acquisition and assimilation provide IT-PACAP. Knowledge acquisition involves firms’ capability to identify and obtain knowledge that is critical to their operations. There are several information technologies that can support and enhance firms’ knowledge acquisition capability by enhancing the speed, intensity, and directionality of knowledge identification and selection. Firms that use these technologies are likely to enhance their ability to acquire and retrieve useful knowledge (Tippins and Sohi 2003). For instance, sophisticated retrieval technology such as query software, search engines, expert finder, and knowledge maps can help identify, collect, and extract useful knowledge from a wide variety of knowledge resources with a high speed and accuracy. Employee knowledge directories drive the identification of knowledge in the right direction. Moreover, intelligent mechanisms built into search and retrieval technologies, plus sophisticated data structuring, indexing, and tagging techniques, help navigate the knowledge acquisition process in the right direction.

Knowledge assimilation involves firms’ capability to incorporate and make the knowledge part of their organizational memory (Holsapple and Joshi 2002, 2004). This capability allows new knowledge to be understood and integrated into organizational knowledge resources (Zahra and George 2002). Newly acquired knowledge often comes in forms and formats that cannot be readily internalized (Leonard-Barton 1995). The assimilating capability helps with the comprehension and internalization of newly acquired knowledge. Alavi and Leidner (2001) argue that IT can enhance firms’ assimilating capability by creating organizational memory in the form of electronic repositories. Through organizational learning, firms accumulate valuable knowledge that needs to be stored and preserved for future use (Tippins and Sohi 2003). IT can provide the necessary mechanisms and tools to preserve and store this knowledge. IT can help store information in formats such that it can be accessible to firm employees and enable them to interpret it in a consistent manner, thereby becoming a part of the entire firm’s memory. There are several information technologies that can help enhance organizational memory. For instance, asset management systems can help assimilate and manage firms intellectual property. Content management systems, databases, and data warehouses can help store various forms of data, information, and knowledge.

2.1.2. IT-Enabled Realized ACAP (IT-RACAP). Information technologies that help support knowledge transformation and exploitation provide IT-enabled realized absorptive capacity (IT-RACAP). Knowledge transformation involves firms’ capability to synthesize, develop, and refine knowledge through addition, deletion, or reinterpretation of the absorbed knowledge. Transformation also allows firms to view and develop knowledge through bisociation. This is achieved when knowledge from “two self-consistent but incompatible frames of reference” (Koestler 1966, p. 35) is visualized and integrated to create new insights, to facilitate the recognition of opportunities, and to alter the ways in which firms see themselves and their competitive landscape. Exploitation capability allows firms to apply and use existing knowledge to create new organizational competencies. It requires using knowledge that already exists and has been internalized for use (Lyles and Schwenk 1992).

Knowledge transformation capability can be enhanced or enabled through IT. IT can help create new knowledge by merging, categorizing, reclassifying, and synthesizing existing knowledge. For instance, Sabherwal and Sabherwal (2005) empirically demonstrated that this IT-enabled capability was associated with high innovations within an industry. There are several information technologies that can help support and enhance firms’ knowledge transformation capability. For instance, business intelligence tools such as data mining and analytical software allow firms to transform existing data and knowledge to gain new insights and understanding (Sabherwal and Becerra-Fernandez 2010). Visualization techniques can support bisociation by integrating and mapping disparate knowledge sets to uncover new knowledge.

IT can enhance exploitation capability by facilitating the use of knowledge preserved and embedded in IT systems to conduct organizational operations (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Gold et al. 2001, Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2005). With the aid of IT, individuals who exploit knowledge do not need to comprehend it (Conner and Prahalad 1996), nor do they need to internalize it (Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2005). There are several information technologies that embody knowledge (such as rules, procedures, routines, directives, instructions, and standards) that can help individuals conduct their knowledge work (Alavi and Leidner 2001) without fully understanding the embedded knowledge. Examples of information technologies that can help enhance knowledge exploitation capability include case-based reasoning and expert systems.

## 2.2. IT-Enabled Social Integration Capacity (IT-SIC)

Social integration mechanisms are structures that build firms’ social capital (Zahra and George 2002). These structures promote connectedness, interaction, coordination, and communication among members of firms by creating seamless networks of people, devises, and knowledge. In the ACAP literature, the conceptualization and empirical testing of social integration mechanisms within the context of potential and realized ACAP are thin and widely different. Zahra and George (2002) used these mechanisms to suggest ways to bridge the gap between potential and realized capabilities. Jansen et al. (2005) argued that these mechanisms are antecedents to potential and RACAP. Todorova and Durisin (2007) argued that social integration mechanisms can positively or negatively impact both potential and realized capabilities and organizational innovative outcomes.

In this study, we extend the notion of social integration mechanisms by arguing that IT can provide a capability to help augment firms’ social capital and support direct interactions among human members of the firms to cultivate shared frame of references. We conceptualize this IT-enabled capability as IT-SIC. IT-SIC enables the development of social capital through direct human interactions and discourse. IT can support and cultivate knowledge synergies by creating electronic networks of alliances and collaborators that can facilitate cross-firm socialization essential for knowledge integration (Tippins and Sohi 2003). For instance, e-community of practice, Web conferencing, and groupware systems are all instrumental in cultivating social interactions and connectedness among individuals and groups; message boards, electronic message software, and chat rooms can help with communication and coordination; RSS technologies can help employees synthesize and share information from multiple sources; wikis and blogs have opened up new opportunities to integrate knowledge and ideas, accelerating knowledge discovery and innovation (Shneiderman 2007). These information technologies augment participation and discourse among individuals for the purposes of knowledge development and integration. Thus, IT can foster and provide both formal and informal SIC by supporting various social integration mechanisms. For instance, the use of video conferencing and groupware facilitates formal integration, while various tools like e-community of practice and blogs create opportunities for informal integration.

## 2.3. Firm Innovation Outputs

Prior literature on ACAP has found a strong relationship between ACAP and innovation output. However, this literature does not unpack the notion of firm innovation. In this study, we focus on two specific innovation outcomes: ideated innovation and commercialized innovation (CI ).

2.3.1. Ideated Innovation. Drawing on extant literature, we define ideated innovation as knowledge that is created through firms’ innovation efforts and embodied in forms such as inventions, discoveries, developed ideas, and/or solutions of technical problems. This knowledge representation is novel, original, relevant, and valued and can be commercialized into new products or services with potential financial returns (Luecke and Katz 2003). Ideated innovation is widely invoked as an intermediate outcome, which results from knowledge transformation and exploitation but precedes innovation commercialization (Nerkar and Shane 2007, Furman et al. 2002, Penner-Hahn and Shaver 2005, Gold et al. 2001). Ideated innovation often takes the form of patent inventions (e.g., Dutta and Weiss 1997, Decarolis and Deeds 1999, Penner-Hahn and Shaver 2005, Nerkar and Shane 2007, Heeley and Jacobson 2008).

2.3.2. CI. The CI brings ideated innovation to market (Nambison and Sawhney 2007, Andrew and Sirkin 2003, Nerkar and Shane 2007). Past research posits that successful commercialization of innovation is necessary for firms to be competitive (Nevens et al. 1990, Nerkar and Shane 2007). CI often takes the form of new product and service introduction that has the potential commercial significance (Katila 2002). In this study, we specifically examine product and service innovation. Product innovation involves the introduction of products that are new or substantially improved, e.g., improvements in functional characteristics, technical abilities, ease of use. Service innovation involves novelty in the way in which services are produced or in the delivery of new creative content of service offerings.

## 3. Model and Hypotheses

Here, we propose a theoretical model that characterizes the relationships between IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and firm innovation. IT-RACAP directly impacts innovation outcomes because of its generative abilities, whereas IT-RACAP and IT-SIC provide supportive capabilities and may impact innovation indirectly either through other factors (mediation) or through working with other factors (moderation). In this study, as an initial effort to investigate differential impacts of IT on different innovation outcomes, we do not attempt to investigate all possible relationships between IT and innovation; rather, we select the most significant relationships that are grounded in ACAP theory. Thus we focus our investigation on the direct effects of IT-RACAP and indirect effects of IT-PACAP and IT-SIC on different innovation outcomes. Figure 1 depicts this model. The model posits that IT-enabled knowledge capabilities significantly affect firm innovation. Specifically, the model posits that firms’ IT-PACAP enhances IT-RACAP (H1), which, in turn, directly positively affects firms’ ideated innovation (H2) and CI (H3). Ideated innovation positively affects CI (H4). IT-SIC interacts with ideated innovation to affect firms’ ability to commercialize on innovation (H5). The extent to which firms are able to commercialize on their innovation is also affected by firm size and industry type.

## 3.1. IT-PACAP and IT-RACAP

ACAP theory views potential and realized capabilities as two complementary capabilities necessary to improve firms’ innovative outcomes. Firms cannot fully exploit knowledge without first acquiring it. In essence, PACAP forms the basis and foundation for firms’ RACAP, while RACAP is largely viewed as directly impacting firms’ innovative outcomes (Zahra and George 2002).

Extending this notion to the IT context, IT-PACAP such as organizational memory systems, databases, data warehouses, and content management systems enables firms to acquire and assimilate new knowledge in a variety of structured and unstructured forms, such as data, audio, video, and image received from diverse sources such as supply chain partners (Malhotra et al. 2005). IT-PACAP enhances firms’ IT-RACAP to analyze and transform large volumes of information from diverse sources in different formats that otherwise could not be handled (Scott 2000, Alavi and Leidner 2001). IT-PACAP increases firms’ ability to identify and renew their knowledge stocks by spotting industry trends, customer interests, and competitors’ moves from their external environment and storing this knowledge in the firms’ IT systems, thus avoiding knowledge stagnation or loss. Moreover, IT-PACAP enables firms to accomplish knowledge acquisition and storage in a timely and cost-effective fashion.

Although IT-PACAP may not directly generate inventions, it does continually infuse knowledge, which can be used by firms’ IT-RACAP to generate new knowledge. This is exemplified by Overstock.com’s real-time data warehouse, which feeds the company’s analytical algorithms that analyze the actual selling patterns to adjust the merchandise price in real time. In the absence of timely purchasing data provided through its data warehouse, its ability to generate knowledge about the selling patterns is compromised. Many other examples also demonstrate that data warehouses, by storing, extracting, cleaning, and transforming large volumes of data from heterogeneous sources into usable forms, can augment firms’ data analytical capability to obtain new insights and knowledge (Davenport and Harris 2007). For instance, First American Corporation’s (FAC) data warehouse that contains extensive customer, product, and distribution data is instrumental in enhancing FAC’s data analytical and mining capabilities (Cooper et al. 2000). Similarly, 3M’s global enterprise data warehouse is foundational to its decision support infrastructure (Watson et al. 2004). On the other hand, many business intelligence efforts fail because firms are deficient in acquiring and assimilating clean, relevant, consistent, and timely information into their knowledge repositories (Elliott 2009). Therefore we posit that IT-PACAP enhances IT-RACAP. Without sufficient IT-PACAP, firms’ IT-RACAP will be hindered.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). IT-enabled potential absorptive capacity positively affects IT-RACAP.

## 3.2. IT-RACAP and Firm Innovation

Firms’ RACAP is often viewed as directly providing the firms with unique knowledge generative capability to harness and leverage their knowledge resources to create innovations (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Zahra and George 2002). A large bulk of empirical studies has found significant positive relationships between RACAP and innovation (see Zahra and

George 2002 for a review). Similarly, within the IT context, we argue that firms’ IT-RACAP to transform and exploit knowledge resources not only help create new inventions but also enhance introduction of new products and services to market.

3.2.1. IT-RACAP and Ideated Innovation. Although firms’ ability to use IT to acquire and store knowledge is critical, it is the transformation and exploitation of knowledge that directly impact creation of inventions. For instance, firms’ knowledge repositories may be of little consequence to knowledge creation if they lack adequate capabilities to mine, analyze, visualize, and exploit knowledge to create inventions. Furthermore, IT-RACAP provides support for innovative activities. For example, simulation and visualization tools are empowering firms’ engineers and scientists and greatly enhancing knowledge creation of firms (Shneiderman 2007).

The bisociation capability that allows the bridging of technologically distal knowledge domains is shown to be very critical to valuable inventions (Nerkar and Roberts 2004). IT-RACAP can help firms generate new knowledge and develop new perceptual schema or changes to existing processes through the process of bisociation (Kazanjian et al. 2002). For instance, Accelrys, Inc.’s data analytics and visualization tools directly enable new drug discoveries through bisociation (Taschek 2003). Accelry’s DS Gene (an analytical tool for chemists) and DS Modeling (an analytical tool for biologists) can help synthesize data and enhance firms’ bisociation process by bridging between two disciplines of chemistry and biology and can help facilitate new drug discovery. Accelrys has helped biotech biggie Biogen Inc. create patents on drugs. Similarly, Harrah’s Entertainment case exemplifies that IT-RACAP can help generate new service ideas to serve customers in the gaming industry (Loveman 2003). Harrah’s Entertainment has used advanced data mining techniques to develop a unique algorithm (which they have patented) for differentiating a customer from other customers at any of a plurality of casino properties.

Taken together, IT-RACAP helps in understanding, synthesizing, and using complex technical knowledge, which enhances creation of new inventions. Therefore we posit that with higher IT-RACAP, firms are able to generate a greater number of inventions.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). IT-RACAP positively affects ideated innovation.

3.2.2. IT-RACAP and CI. Release of new products and services into the market often requires knowledge assets that can help formulate and implement product engineering and launch strategies, develop market intelligence and analysis, and establish distribution channels for new product and service sales. We argue that IT-RACAP can help exploit knowledge and generate new insights, which can be acted on to directly influence the release of new products and services into the market.

IT-RACAP assists in product engineering through information technologies such as CAD and CAM (computer-aided design and manufacturing), virtual reality, and other simulation and visualization tools. This capability could allow firms to create virtual mock-ups by recombining or refining existing products and services and by incorporating customers feedback into new products and services before they are released. Virtual mock-ups using computer simulations could help eliminate the time required to make numerous product models and reduce the need to conduct extensive product testing, cutting launch costs and time while increasing product marketability and sales. For instance, DaimlerChrysler uses simulation and graphical planning tools such as Dassault Systemes’ Catia 3-D CAD and CAM software to digitally design vehicles and test them in a virtual world. The company can digitally create a passenger, watch that passenger enter and exit the vehicle, and gather data that can be used to improve comfort of new vehicle models (Bacheldor 2003).

Furthermore, IT-RACAP also facilitates market intelligence and analysis. With the aid of data analytics and visualization tools, IT-RACAP increases firms ability to analyze, interpret, and synthesize large volumes of data from a variety of sources to derive new insights about changing product, customer, and market preferences and conditions. New insights derived from such analysis can help generate a wide array of strategies for introducing new products and service concepts to market and identify crossselling opportunities by recommending new product and services options to customers in real time. For example, Kimberly-Clark’s Customer Immersion and

Design Center provides 3-D virtual reality technology to retailers such as Kroger, Target, and Walmart, enabling retailers to interact with Kimberly-Clark’s new products and displays in their stores in the same way retailers would, and thus facilitating retailers to release its new products more effectively. Kimberly-Clark’s internal researchers also use this design center by bringing new product models developed using 3-D design software to the immersion room, where they can better visualize and sense the product design for market introduction (McGee 2007). Taken together, we posit a positive relationship between IT-RACAP and CI.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). IT-RACAP positively affects CI.

## 3.3. Ideated Innovation and CI

Here, we argue that highly innovating firms have a greater potential for exploiting and converting their innovations for commercial gains. Although creating new knowledge does not guarantee commercialization, firms that continually invent new knowledge are likely to have a greater number of inventions that can be converted to a greater number of new products and services. These firms are better able to compete by commercializing their innovations not only in mature market segments through incremental innovations but also in emerging market segments through radical innovations than firms with fewer inventions (Burgelman et al. 2006, Burgelman and Grove 1996, Galunic and Eisenhardt 1996). Therefore we posit the following.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). Ideated innovation positively affects CI.

## 3.4. Moderating Effect of IT-Enabled Social Integration Capacity

CI in the form of new product and service introductions requires integration of relevant knowledge and stakeholders among various functional units of firms to promote coordination, mutual understanding, and comprehension (Hansen and Birkinshaw 2007, Rogers 1995). IT-SIC can facilitate the creation of shared frame of references among diverse stakeholders, which could promote eventual conversion of new inventions into commercialized products or services.

Innovations that are not successfully commercialized are often potentially good but have been rejected or shelved for a variety of reasons, e.g., lack of financial resources or poor fit with firms’ strategic goals. These causes may vary between firms. However, some causes for innovation failure, such as poor communication, poor empowerment, poor knowledge collaboration and coordination, or poor fit with market needs, are common across all firms (O’Sullivan 2002). These causes often can be mitigated through the use of IT. IT-SIC can greatly facilitate knowledge integration and reduce communication barriers through supporting formal and informal social mechanisms among individuals and teams within and outside the firm. IT-SIC could help connect new patent ideas with appropriate organizational resources, which could help push the invention into the market. IT-SIC could also facilitate collaborative problem solving, which has been shown to accelerate the rate of innovation (Reagans and McEvily 2003). For example, emergent unified communications, by integrating separate communication channels of voice, data, video, and graphics and allowing users to switch back and forth seamlessly across these channels, greatly facilitate tacit knowledge exchanges among users. Discourse and interaction enabled and supported by these technology tools can greatly accelerate the conversion of knowledge discovery into commercialized products by enhancing firms’ understanding of market needs and by allowing for better customization of new products and services (Shneiderman 2007). In addition, conversion of patent inventions to new products and services often requires communication, coordination, and governance across different functional departments (Tarafdar and Gordon 2007), such as R&D, manufacturing, marketing, and operations. IT-SIC empowered by collaboration tools such as groupware could greatly facilitate the communication, coordination, and governance of the conversion process.

IT-SIC could also speed up new product inventions to market by opening up firms’ innovation processes to employees, customers, and suppliers and by facilitating idea selection and conversion to new products and services. As a response to pressure to innovate today, many firms have gravitated toward generating larger numbers of promising ideas and increasing the speed with which these ideas are taken to the market. The product development literature has frequently highlighted the virtues of increased speed to market

and increased generation of new ideas and inventions. However, recent studies (e.g., Chandy et al. 2006) have found that a strong focus on speed to market and invention generation may actually hurt firms in the pharmaceutical industry by lowering their conversion ability. They argue that development costs per launched product vary substantially across firms. Efficiency in product development because of higher conversion rates can yield resource savings that can be reallocated in other ways, such as in lower prices, higher profits, or greater investment in future innovation. They have found that in the pharmaceutical industry, only about 20% of patented drug ideas make it to product launch. Firms with the highest conversion ability are those that (1) focus on a moderate number of ideas, in areas of importance and in areas where they have expertise, and (2) deliberate for a moderate length of time on promising ideas. These prior findings are insightful, but they were obtained without considering the role of IT. We postulate here that IT-SIC could not only greatly enhance prioritization and selection of patent ideas with the greatest commercial potential but also expedite the deliberation and conversion of promising ideas. Particularly when firms have a large number of technological inventions, selecting the right inventions that have the greatest potential for successful commercialization becomes increasingly complex and costly. IT-SIC greatly increases firms’ ability to efficiently select the best ideas that have the potential to be commercialized for profits, thus allowing firms to concentrate their resources to cost effectively introduce new products and services to market speedily. For example, Web 2.0 technologies such as wikis, blogs, and multimedia online toolkits greatly empower individual customers and end-users of products and services to engage in firms’ innovation process (Von Hippel 1998) to help better identify and commercialize good ideas and inventions for greater financial returns. Indeed, most emergent forms of innovation, such as open innovation and crowdsourcing, are based on these technologies. Big innovators such as Eli Lilly and P&G (Huston and Sakkab 2006) have successfully used these emergent forms of innovation and created online communities to assist with selection of good ideas and inventions and successful commercialization of innovations. Small start-up companies such as Cambrian House have also used these new forms of innovation via soliciting feedback and votes on new game designs from members of large online communities to develop popular games like Gwabs (McAfee 2009). According to a recent McKinsey survey (McKinsey 2008), 20% of companies have opened up their innovation processes and have reported a 20% increase in the number of new products on average.

Taken together, IT-SIC enhances firms’ ability to connect and integrate understanding of market needs and technical expertise in designing and manufacturing new products and services. This shared understanding increases firms’ conversion of inventions to new products and services in the market. Therefore we postulate that a high level of IT-SIC is likely to increase firms’ conversion rate from patent inventions to new products and services introduced to the market.

Hypothesis 5 (H5). IT-SIC moderates the relationship between ideated innovation and CI. Specifically, a greater number of ideated innovation will be converted into new products and services in the market when firms possess a high level of IT-SIC.

## 3.5. Control Variables

Firm size may significantly influence patent inventions and the introduction of new products and services (e.g., Chen and Hambrick 1995, Aldrich 2000). For example, inventions are embedded in complex product systems, and small firms with limited resources may not have the resources to support litigation and enforcement required for patenting their innovations. Therefore, in this study, we control for firm size. Different types of industries have different emphases on innovation. Highly dynamic industries with constant changes require firms to innovate fast and constantly to survive and succeed, while in stable industries, constant innovations consume expensive resources and thus may not allow firms to achieve the optimal benefits. Thus, industry type is also controlled in this study.

## 4. Methods

To test our model, we collect secondary data about firms’ IT-enabled knowledge capabilities, ideated innovation, and CI based on an extensive examination of news articles from multiple data sources. Although primary data collection through survey is valuable for assessing IT use, primary data is susceptible to common method bias. Additionally, primary data collection only collects data about respondents’ perceived system use, which may not be consistent with actual use because of respondents’ difficulty in recalling past use (Devaraj and Kohli 2003) and biased selection because of respondents’ individual preferences and perceptions. There is an increasing recognition that actual system use provides better measures than self-reported use (DeLone and McLean 2003). In this regard, we collect longitudinal secondary data about firms’ use of IT for supporting knowledge management initiatives to capture ITenabled knowledge capabilities.

Secondary data collection provides us with relatively objective and rich information about firms’ actual practice of IT use to support knowledge processing (Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2005). Furthermore, this approach is perhaps the most feasible one, where the data collection involves assessing IT use across multiple years, because it is unrealistic to expect respondents to provide reliable information about IT over pastime because of the difficulty in recalling past system use and biased selection toward a specific system. However, we recognize the limitation of secondary data collection in that secondary data was susceptible to media bias because of unbalanced media attention and reports about different companies across various industries. To address this limitation, we search a wide range of data sources (more than 4,000 news outlets) to reduce potential media bias.

## 4.1. Sample Selection

To test our model, we initially compiled a sample list of 226 firms that were ranked by the InformationWeek 500 (IW500) in 2002–2005. Doing so could help reduce media bias, because those IW500 ranked firms were less likely to be underreported. Some of the IW500 surveyed industries (e.g., retailing) did not have a common patenting practice. Based on the two-digit SIC code, we identified 96 firms in those industries and removed them from our sample list, resulting in a final sample of 110 firms.

## 4.2. Data Collection

IT-Enabled Knowledge Capabilities. Collecting secondary data to assess IT-enabled firm capabilities has been suggested as a feasible approach in a number of prior studies. For example, Sabherwal and Sabherwal (2007) estimated firms’ IT-enabled capability for knowledge sharing, creation, and use, using news announcement counts of the firms’ IT-enabled knowledge management initiatives collected from leading news sources of Lexis-Nexis. Chi et al. (2007, 2008) suggested collecting secondary data about IT use of firms by using a number of popular computer journals, such as Computerworld (CW), eWeek, and InformationWeek (IW). A number of other studies (e.g., Lichtenberg 1995, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Bharadwaj et al. 1999) used IW and CW as the major data sources, because IW and CW cover 80%–90% of the news about corporate IT spending and various IT practices in the United States (Bharadwaj 2000). Based on these prior studies, we used two major data sources for our study: (1) LexisNexis database, which includes more than 4,000 news sources and major computer journals, such as CW, IW, and eWeek and (2) KM World, which is a major magazine covering comprehensive news about how IT is used to bolster organizational knowledge capabilities. We believe our data sources are comprehensive and broad enough to cover all the major news events about firms’ IT use for knowledge processing to address the media bias concern.

In this study, we collected a total of 7,546 IT-related news announcements about the IT use from the earliest reporting dates to 2004 for the 110 firms. We assume that if an IT application was reported in use at a specific year, then it would be used in future years unless the use of this technology was reported to be terminated or upgraded to a new system.

4.2.1. Ideated Innovation. One way that ideated innovation is reflected is in the form of patents (Schmookler 1966). Patents are embodiments of new knowledge and provide a trace of firms’ intermediate innovation. They are a measure of novel invention that is externally validated through the patent examination process (Griliches 1990). We collected patent application data, because patent applications provide a good proxy for firms’ innovative ideation (Chandy et al. 2006). Patent application data were obtained from the Patent Statistical Database, release of April 2007 published by the European Patent Office. A total of 4,272 patents were applied by the 110 companies over three years of 2004–2006.

4.2.2. CI. New product and service introductions capture commercialized firm innovation (Chandy et al. 2006). We used FACTIVA database to collect news reports and articles about new product and service introductions of the target companies. To reduce possible media bias, we also searched the target companies’ corporate websites to gather new product and service announcements. A total of 4,463 relevant news items over a three-year period from 2006 to 2008 were collected. Two coders coded the news items based on the key terms introduce, unveil, and launch to identify new product and service introductions. To check the coding reliability, about 10% of the news items were randomly sampled and recoded. The coding reliability was tested using the reliability index of Perreault and Leigh (1989). When disagreements on coding were identified, a third judge was brought in and discrepancies were resolved on a majority rule basis. The interrater reliability about the new product and service coding was 0.85.

4.2.3. Other Data. Control variable data were obtained from COMPUSTAT and corporate annual reports.

## 4.3. IT News Coding

This study represents an early effort in collecting secondary data to assess IT-enabled knowledge capabilities. A significant amount of effort and time was involved in developing technology coding protocol and conducting formal coding of IT news. Below we describe the process used to develop and test the coding protocol, as well as the process used to code the news announcements. These processes are also illustrated in Figure 2.

4.3.1. Technology Coding Protocol Development. Our goal is to develop a systematic coding protocol that objectively characterizes firms’ IT-enabled knowledge capabilities as oppose to subjectively inferred (i.e., coder specific) assessment of capabilities. Our process of developing the coding protocol was not linear but rather a recursive analytical process that continued until a list of fine-tuned categories of technologies were comprehensively elicited and accurately mapped to the theoretical categories.

Identifying Technology Categories. First, we elicited a comprehensive list of relevant IT application titles. To do so, we started with a thorough literature review of more than 300 articles concerning knowledge management. We used a list of keywords to search articles in 20 major management and IS journals. This list of keywords and journals can be found in Table A of the online supplement.<sup>1</sup> We reviewed more than 300 articles and identified a list of IT application titles that were specifically mentioned and tied to one or more knowledge activities and capabilities in these articles. We also elicited a set of synonym terms for certain technologies, e.g., Web conferencing, computer conferencing, video conferencing, and teleconferencing.

Next, using an iterative process, technologies in the initial list were grouped into broader technology categories. To do this, we obtained standard definitions and descriptions of technologies from ZDnet.com, which enabled us to assess the nature and functionalities of each of the technologies identified earlier. The detailed technologies were grouped into broader categories that delivered similar functions. For example, data mining, web mining, data analytics, and business intelligence were all grouped into one broad category of analytical software. We used constant comparison of various technology categories to detect similarities and dissimilarities between and among these categories and then merged closely related IT applications into broader themes. A technology expert reviewed and verified the technology grouping.

Mapping Technological Categories to Theoretical Categories. We systematically mapped the technologies to theoretical constructs. The literature review conducted earlier to elicit technologies from the extant literature provided a starting point for linking the technology categories to theoretical categories. We created mapping between IT technologies and knowledge capabilities based on the technology capability linkages provided in the literature. For example,

We went through six iterations of individual to collective mapping until consensus was reached. Individual assessments from both experts were more than 90% consistent with our coding protocol and mapping schema, except for five instances of disagreement, including e-community of practice, computer-aided design and manufacturing, e-mail, enterprise application integration, and portal. After discussing with the two external experts about these discrepancies, we reached an agreement that the mapping of these five technology applications to knowledge capabilities could be more contextual based than other applications. In other words, these five technologies could be mapped to different knowledge capabilities, depending more on the application contexts described in the IT news, where they were actually used by specific companies.

Pilot Coding. We randomly sampled 1% of the total 7,546 news articles and conducted a pilot coding by two authors. The disagreements among the coders were discussed among all the authors until consensus was reached. These discussions led to further refinement of the coding protocol. During this process, more technology application titles were added to take advantage of the emerging technologies.

4.3.2. Formal Coding Procedures. In the formal coding, we followed a hierarchical coding procedure, with which it was relatively easy to make adaptations and revisions (Zelner et al. 2009). All news articles were divided evenly and separately coded by two authors. The coders first used the coding template to remove repeating news and irrelevant news. News announcements not only provide information about successful IT use but also provide information on unsuccessful investments. In our coding, only IT technologies that were reported useful or successful were coded. News articles that reported unsuccessful IT technologies or associated problems were coded as “irrelevant” and not considered evidence of firms’ IT-enabled knowledge capabilities. For example, in early 2002, General Motors (GM) Corporation was reported to encounter such severe problems during a rollout of SAP AG’s ERP R/3 software that its spare parts business virtually ground to a halt, forcing GM to launch an emergency turnaround effort six months after the software went live (Computerworld 2002). Such news was carefully coded as “irrelevant” and was not aggregated into the data measures, because the report of ERP application here did not indicate that GM actually possessed the related IT-enabled capabilities. Two coders coded the relevant news by identifying the initial list of technologies and corresponding broader technology categories based on the coding protocol. The knowledge management context provided in the news articles that linked technologies to specific knowledge activities was also considered during this coding. The identified technologies were then mapped to the knowledge capabilities based on the coding protocol. When news announcements were identified by the two coders as ambiguous, they would then be coded individually by all the authors and the disagreements were discussed among all the authors until consensus was reached.

To ensure reliability and robustness of the coding, 10% of the news was recoded to test the interrater reliability. The disagreements were resolved jointly by all the authors on a majority rule basis. The coding reliability was tested using Perreault and Leigh’s (1989) reliability index. The test yielded an index value of 0.75, which was higher than the convention of 0.70 (Ryan and Bernard 2000). In addition, we randomly selected 50 news articles and asked an external expert judge to independently code them. We then tested the interrater reliability between this expert’s coding and our coding, yielding a reliability index of 0.7. We discussed with the external expert judge and resolved most of the discrepancies. This result suggested that the external expert’s coding was relatively consistent with our coding, thus mitigating the concern of potential coding bias because the two coders were also authors who were involved in developing the technology coding protocol and research model and whose knowledge about this research could potentially bias their coding.

In our coding, our main focus was on identifying the actual use of various technology applications, which provided IT-enabled knowledge capabilities, involving IT-PACAP, IT-RACAP, and IT-SIC. Technology applications identified in the news announcements were respectively mapped to the three IT-enabled knowledge capabilities. Sample news announcement coding can be found in Table B of the online supplement. We aggregated the actual use counts as a proxy for each of the IT-enabled knowledge capabilities.

## 4.4. Construct Measures

IT-PACAP. IT-PACAP is reflected in IT applications such as data reading technology, databases (data warehouses), document management systems, and enterprise applications. News announcements about a firm’s IT applications are mapped to this construct. The counts of mapped IT applications are used as a measure for IT-PACAP (Chi et al. 2007, Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2007).

IT-RACAP. IT-RACAP is reflected in IT applications such as business analytics, data mining, visualization, simulation, decision support system, and CAD and CAM. The counts of news announcements, which are mapped to this construct, are used as a measure for IT-RACAP (Chi et al. 2007, Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2007).

IT-SIC. IT-SIC is reflected in IT applications such as conferencing (e.g., Web conferencing, video conferencing), messaging (e.g., e-mail, instant messaging, VoIP, mobile messaging), collaboration technology, and groupware. The counts of news announcements, which are mapped to this construct, are used as a measure for IT-SIC (Chi et al. 2007, Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2007).

Ideated Innovation. Ideated innovation is measured through patent application counts of a firm. Patent counts have been used to measure innovative output in a number of previous studies (e.g., Bound et al. 1984, Henderson and Cockburn 1994, Penner-Hahn and Shaver 2005, Nerkar and Shane 2007) and have been shown to be valid and robust indicators of ideated innovation (e.g., Basberg 1987, Trajtenberg 1987). We calculated the total number of patent applications that were made in 2004–2006 by each firm as a measure for this construct. We followed Penner-Hahn and Shaver (2005) and used a three-year aggregation for patent application counts to lessen the chance that a random extreme event in one year may disproportionally influence the measure of patent count.

CI. CI is captured by new product and service introduction (Chandy et al. 2006, Aboulnasr et al. 2008). Similar to ideated innovation, we aggregated the number of new products and services introduced to the market by a firm in a three-year period from 2006–2008 (considering a two-year lag to patent applications) to measure CI.

Control Variables. In this study, firm size and industry type are controlled. We measured firm size as a natural log of the number of employees in a firm. Industry type was captured using the industry-level R&D spending. The greater the industry R&D spending, the greater the industry dynamism.

The BIC approach attempts to identify the model with the highest probabilities of being the “true” model for the data, assuming that one of the models under consideration is true. While AIC is defined without reference to a “true model.” Instead, AIC uses expected prediction of future data as the key criterion of the adequacy of a model (for more discussion, see Sileshi 2006, Acquah 2010). Both indices of AIC and BIC are commonly used to assess the relative fit of models with count variables, although they differ in their definitions of good model fit.

In the second sensitivity analysis, we recoded the data such that one technology application could be mapped to one or more types of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities. The new data set was identical in all other ways except for the aforementioned change. As indicated in Table 4, results for the most part (except for the relationship between IT-RACAP and CI) did not alter significantly, which again indicates the robustness of our coding.

We consider both alternative coding approaches to be inferior to our coding approach in this research. The first alternative coding approach, by restricting one news announcement to one IT-enabled knowledge capability, not only misrepresents a company’s full capabilities but also relies on the coder’s judgment to pick the most appropriate IT-enabled knowledge capability, which may potentially increase coder-based bias. The second alternative coding approach, by using one-to-multiple mapping, may result in a high correlation among the three ITenabled knowledge capabilities. However, our coding approach not only allows us to identify all possible IT applications used by a firm but also allows us to focus on the most salient function and feature of a technology by selecting the primary focus of the technology and using a one-to-one mapping of technology to IT-enabled knowledge capability. This coding approach thus better enables us to capture the distinctions among the three IT-enabled knowledge capabilities. As seen from correlation coefficients shown in Table 1, the three IT-enable knowledge capabilities present low correlations between them. These distinctions are critical in examining the differential roles each plays in firm innovation.

## 6. Discussion

Overall, results from this study show a strong support for our main assertion that IT plays a significant role in enhancing firm innovation. Furthermore, we uncover that different types of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities have differential effects on firm innovation.

The support for H1, which extends the ACAP theory’s claims within the IT context and hypothesizes that firms’ IT-PACAP can help strengthen their IT-RACAP, confirms the importance of IT in creating a strong foundation for acquiring and internalizing quality data necessary to build firms’ analytical capability for knowledge discovery (Davenport and Harris 2007). A high level of IT-PACAP strengthens IT-RACAP, by continually renewing and reviving firms’ knowledge base, which stultifies the ossification effect because of knowledge stagnation.

The strong support for H2 suggests that IT’s enabling role in understanding, synthesizing, and using complex technical knowledge is essential for patent inventions. Firms that enhance their realized capacity for transforming and exploiting knowledge through the use of IT are better able to accumulate new knowledge, that can be embedded into products and services. The support for H2 extends ACAP theory’s arguments within the IT context suggesting that the generative capabilities of IT-RACAP augments the creation of knowledge in forms of embodiment of significant discoveries, technical solutions, or developed ideas.

We did not find support for H3, which postulates a positive relationship between IT-RACAP and CI using our sample of 110 firms. We reason that possibly IT-RACAP does not play a salient direct role in the release of products and services, while it may play a significant role in influencing CI indirectly. Its impact on CI could be mediated through patent inventions, which as an intermediate innovation outcome subsequently facilitates the release of new products and services. This may be because commercializing new products and services is complex; a critical part of which requires coordination, collaboration, and knowledge integration across multiple parties (e.g., customers, suppliers, retailers, distributors) and multiple business functions (e.g., marketing and sales, finance and accounting, manufacturing, R&D, and supply chain) for new products and services to successfully reach the market. As such, the generative capabilities provided by IT-RACAP may not be sufficient for enhancing CI. Although the generative capabilities of IT-RACAP could facilitate innovation generation and patent inventions, other capabilities such as knowledge integration across multiple parties and business functions, as well as managerial capabilities of coordinating and directing the commercialization could be more important in successful new product and service launches in the market. In addition, it could be possible that IT-RACAP could indirectly impact CI through interacting with patent inventions by facilitating the selection of patent inventions and providing capabilities for developing and analyzing complementary knowledge assets, such as market-related knowledge, insights about distribution channels and customers, which could enhance firms conversion from patent inventions into new products and services. For instance, pharmaceutical companies could use predictive models and computer simulations to improve the prediction accuracy of efficacy and safety of new drug patents so as to facilitate selection of drug patents with greatest commercial potential and early termination of eventual failures without stifling would-be successful drugs (Schachter and Ramoni 2007), thus enhancing the success of drug commercialization. In this study, we did not examine the moderating effect of IT-RACAP on CI. Future research could specifically examine this indirect effect of IT-RACAP on CI to gain more insights.

The support for Hypothesis H4 is consistent with our contention that when firms possess a greater number of patent inventions, they are more likely to transform these inventions to a larger number of new products and services introduced to the market.

The strong support for H5 confirms our contention that IT-enabled SIC interacts with ideated innovation to positively affect CI. Figure 4 depicts the interaction effect between IT-SIC and ideated innovation on CI. As seen in Figure 4, when ideated innovation is high, a high level of IT-SIC (one standard deviation above the mean) can significantly increase the rate of commercialization. In other words, when firms have a large number of patent inventions, a high level of IT-SIC can significantly increase the conversion rate from patents to new products and services introduced in the market. While at a low level of IT-SIC (one standard deviation below the mean), the conversion rate of bringing inventions to market is about 1.76 times lower than that at a high level of IT-SIC.

This result suggests that IT-SIC plays a critical role in converting patent inventions to new products and services, especially when firms possess a large number of patents. We reason that commercialized patent inventions is an innovation outcome that results from building cross-unit, cross-functional, and even crossfirm knowledge synergies, which can be aptly supported and enabled by IT-SIC. IT-SIC facilitates the free flow of information as well as knowledge integration within and across firms, and allows firms to mitigate the communication barriers common to innovation conversion failures, thus greatly enhancing conversion of patent inventions to new products and services. The extant literature in management has argued that participative decision making, support for collaboration, and communication among key stakeholders who control financial, material, human, and knowledge resources are crucial to the conversion of patents to new product and service commercialization (see Hurley and Hult 1998 for further discussion). Our finding in H5 extends this common finding in the literature by highlighting the moderating role of IT.

Interestingly, the presence of intermediate innovation outcome, i.e., ideated innovation, is critical in explaining the differential effects of IT on CI. Hypotheses H2, H3, and H5 collectively suggest that IT-enabled knowledge capabilities play a salient role in indirectly supporting the release of new products and services. In particular, the role of IT-RACAP and IT-SIC in the launch of new products and services can be explained through the intermediate innovation output of ideated innovation. Thus, disaggregating the construct of firm innovation into ideated innovation and CI not only uncovers IT’s nuanced effects on innovation but also highlights the complex relationship between IT and innovation, which needs to be further explored.

## 7. Contribution

This study makes important contributions to research, methodology, and practice in IS, competitive dynamics, and firm innovation. From a theoretical standpoint, first, this study contributes to the IS literature by empirically establishing the link between IT and innovation. This work expands Zahra and George’s (2002) reconceptualization of ACAP theory by invoking it within the IT context. It systematically establishes that knowledge capabilities that influence the nature and sustainability of firms’ competitive advantage through innovation can be, in part, created and augmented through IT. Building on ACAP theory, this study develops the concept of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities, which allows us to both theoretically and empirically demonstrate the roles and importance of different information technologies in driving firms competitive behavior as manifested in the forms of two innovation outcomes—patents and new product and service introductions in the market. In other words, IT provides and enables a set of knowledge capabilities, which build on and support each other to differentially impact firm innovation.

Second, our work disaggregates the construct of firm innovation into ideated innovation and CI and uncovers the differential impacts of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities on these two innovation outcomes. Our study thus greatly advances our understanding of how IT can be used to affect firm performance through influencing intermediate variables such as innovation outcomes. As firms increasingly use digital systems to support and enable their knowledge management initiatives, IS scholars call for more research to better understand IT’s roles in knowledge management and firm innovation (e.g., Alavi and Leidner 2001, Sambamurthy and Subramani 2004). Thus our conceptualization and empirical investigation of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities greatly enriches this body of literature.

Third, this study contributes to the competitive dynamics literature. Research in competitive dynamics has widely examined patterns of competitive actions (e.g., action repertoire or a series of actions) (Ferrier 2001) and their antecedents and consequences (Smith et al. 2001). Some research has also examined a specific type of competitive actions such as market entry and exit (Baum and Korn 1996). However, patents and new product and service introductions, as two important types of competitive actions that have a great potential to interrupt the competitive status quo and cause disequilibrium in the market, have not yet been carefully examined. This study, by examining how IT-enabled knowledge capabilities affect two innovation-based competitive actions, thus enriches our understanding of how IT affects competitive dynamics in the market.

From a methodological standpoint, our study contributes to IS research by introducing a comprehensive development and empirical measurement of three types of IT-enabled knowledge capabilities using secondary data sources. The secondary data collection about actual IT use proposed in this study offers a fairly novel, but very useful, methodology with the potential for greater application in the IS research. By providing literature-driven characterization of three types of knowledge capabilities, which are grounded in ACAP theory, we offer a potentially useful approach to measure IT-enabled knowledge capabilities using secondary data.

From the practice standpoint, the importance of understanding how IT affects firm performance can be better appreciated in light of the significant percentage of capital investment that is being allocated to IT (Tippins and Sohi 2003). Businesses around the world continue to spend more than \$2 trillion per year on IT, and according to the U.S. Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Economic Analysis, U.S. firms spent 50% of their total expenditure on IT in the late 1990s (Carr 2003). Moreover, insufficient and mixed results in establishing a positive relationship between IT investment and firm performance, makes it necessary for the IS researchers to educate the business community regarding the strategic value of IT. This study takes a small but critical step in that direction by providing empirically driven evidence and insights into the complex relationship between IT and strategy. Specifically, our results illustrate that simply investing in IT alone does not provide strategic value; rather, IT can help support and strengthen firms’ key organizational capabilities for innovation and competitive gains. Our results show that firms could focus their attention on intervening factors such as knowledge capabilities and innovation outcomes to determine what benefits can be derived from IT. Results from this study suggest that managers should not focus on examining the direct impact of IT on the bottom line but may instead find ways to enhance and cultivate IT’s roles in enabling and enhancing organizational knowledge capabilities, which can make a firm more innovative.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that to reap full benefits from IT to facilitate firm innovation, managers need to understand that differential impacts of IT on various innovation outcomes along the innovation pathway. For example, firms need to focus IT investment on enhancing IT-ACAP (e.g., by investing in knowledge acquisition, assimilation, and generation technologies such as databases, intelligent search tools, data mining, and simulation tools) to facilitate generating more patents and new ideas, while to enhance CI, firms should focus more on IT-SIC (e.g., by investing in technologies such as e-community of practice, wikis, blogs, groupware) to facilitate boundary-spanning activities and sharing the understanding of inventions, related marketability, producibility, and potential commercial impact.

## 8. Conclusion and Future Research

This study builds on ACAP theory to examine the roles of IT in firm innovation. It theoretically and empirically investigates the relationship between IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and firm innovation. Firm innovation is observed through two innovation outcomes—patents and new product and service introductions—that directly tie to firm competitive actions often undertaken to gain market share or to achieve profitability. The study’s findings provide a strong support for our main assertion that knowledge capabilities that are enhanced through the use of IT contribute to firm innovation.

Our findings highlight that firm innovation is a complex construct and needs further investigation. Given that the IS literature has paid limited attention to the roles of IT in firm innovation, we believe that inclusion of two separate and distinct innovation outcomes is a step forward. Future research could include other innovation outcomes, such as diffusion of new products and services and sales revenues, and examine how IT-enabled knowledge capabilities affect different innovation outcomes along the innovation pathway. This examination will allow us a fuller understanding of IT’s roles in firm innovation.

In this study, we only examine five relationships that we consider to be most interesting and impactful between IT-enabled knowledge capabilities and firm innovation. However, there could be many other interesting relationships between these firm capabilities and firm innovation. Future research could further explore these unexamined relationships to enrich our understanding of the relationship between IT and firm innovation. In particular, given the importance of IT-SIC in the conversion of ideated innovation on CI, future research could consider to examine in detail novel ways of employing IT-SIC to help support this invention-product conversion. This study only investigated the indirect effect of IT-SIC on CI through moderating ideated innovation. The direct relationship between IT-SIC and CI was not examined because the theoretical arguments more favorably pointed toward IT-SIC’s critical role in the conversion of patent inventions into commercialized products and services. Moreover, the significant coefficient of interaction between IT-SIC and II in the statistical analysis along with the good model fit statistics strongly supports the moderating effect of IT-SIC. Although, in the presence of significant moderating effects, main effects, i.e., direct link between IT-SIC and CI, may not be interpreted in any meaningful fashion, it is worth pointing out that the model fit for the base model (where IT-SIC’s direct effects on CI are modeled) is significantly worse than our research model, which includes the interaction effect of IT-SIC on the conversion of patent to commercialized products and services. Thus statistically speaking, IT-SIC’s moderating effect is more robust than its direct effect. This suggests that IT-SIC could enable firms to benefit more as a facilitator in the inventionproduct conversion. However, future research needs to further examine this aspect. Furthermore, there could be other interesting associations between IT-SIC and different innovation outcomes. With the increasing use of social media tools and Web 2.0 technology, which constitute main components that enable IT-SIC and their use in emergent forms of innovation such as open innovation and crowdsourcing, IT-SIC assumes an increasing importance on firm innovation. Future research could specifically examine whether and how IT-SIC directly and indirectly impacts different innovation outcomes along the innovation pathway.
