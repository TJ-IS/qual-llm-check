# IT capabilities and firm performance: A contingency analysis of the role of industry and IT capability type

M. Dale Stoel <sup>a</sup>, Waleed A. Muhanna b,

<sup>a</sup> Department of Accountancy, Farmer School of Business, Miami University, Laws Hall, Oxford, OH 45056, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 6 July 2007 Received in revised form 13 June 2008 Accepted 8 October 2008 Available online 20 February 2009

Keywords: Business value of IT IT capability Resource-based view Environmental factors Contingency models

## A B S T R A C T

Previous empirical studies examining the relationship between IT capability and accounting-based measures of firm performance reported mixed results. We argued that prior work has relied on aggregate overall measures of the firm’s IT capability, ignoring the specific type and nature of IT capability; and also has not fully considered important environmental conditions that influence the relationship. Drawing on a resource-based view, we advanced a contingency perspective and proposed that IT capabilities’ impact on firm resources was contingent on the ‘‘fit’’ between the type of IT capability/resource a firm possesses and the demands of the industry in which it competes. Specifically, using publicly available rankings as proxies for two types of IT capabilities (internally and externally focused), we empirically examined the degree to which three industry characteristics (dynamism, munificence, and complexity) influenced the impact of each type of IT capability on measures of financial performance. After controlling for prior performance, we found there was general support for the posited contingency model. The implications of these findings are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Understanding the link between information technology (IT) and firm performance has been the subject of considerable interest to IS researchers and practitioners. While progress has been made, significant gaps remain. For example, while studies have shown that IT spending is associated with increased firm output, dispelling the so-called ‘‘productivity paradox’’, empirical studies examining the relationship between IT spending and measures of financial performance have reported mixed findings [6,14].

Drawing on the resource-based view (RBV), the contemporary theory of competition in the strategy literature, IS researchers have argued that performance differential depends on differences in IT capabilities [13,16,19,21] as opposed to differences in IT spending. However, the few empirical studies examining the relationship between aggregate measures of IT capability and accounting-based (profit and cost) measures of firm performance also show mixed results. In particular, using inclusion on the list of ‘‘leaders’’ in the InformationWeek 500 as a proxy for IT capability, Bharadwaj’s [3] univariate analysis suggested a link between IT capability and accounting-based measures of firms performance. However, subsequent analysis by Santhanam and Hartono [20] that controlled for prior financial performance found no association between many (21 out of 24, in one case) performance measures and IT capability. Additionally, in some cases, the effects on some performance measures were opposite to expectations.

Our research sought to reconcile these conflicting results and advance understanding of the relationship between IT capability and firm performance. Drawing on RBV and the work of Wade and Hulland [23], we advanced a contingency perspective and proposed that the impact of IT capabilities on firm resources was contingent on the ‘‘fit’’ between the type of IT capability/ resource a firm possessed and the demands of the industry in which it competes. This general proposition is developed in this paper into a set of specific hypotheses that are then tested using archival data.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

## 2.1. The value of IT resources: a contingency perspective

The RBV, which has become an integrating paradigm for strategy research, explains sources of competitive advantage, sustained or otherwise [1]. Differences in performance are explained in terms of the types of resources and capabilities that different firms control. A variety of labels (inputs, assets, capabilities, competencies) are used in the literature to describe a firm’s resource endowments. Following Grant [9], we used ‘resource’ to refer to all these concepts. The term ‘capability’ was defined as a special resource, encompassing a firm’s capacity to coordinate and deploy other resources to effect a desired end. According to resource-based logic, resources that are valuable but common can only be a source of competitive parity; resources that are valuable and rare can provide temporary competitive advantage; and resources that are valuable, rare, and costly to imitate can be a source of sustained competitive advantage. A resource can be imperfectly imitable in the presence of insolating mechanisms, such as path dependence, causal ambiguity, social complexity, or team-embodied skills.

The competitive potential of resources/capabilities can only be realized if they improve the efficiency and effectiveness of key business processes [2,4,17,18]. However, as Barney [1] observed, ‘‘Firm resources are not valuable in a vacuum, but rather are valuable only when they exploit opportunities and/or neutralize threats’’ in the firm’s operational environment. Competitive advantage is contingent on industry conditions. Strategic fit is a core concept in models of strategy formulation [15]: an organization must find a match between the demands of its competitive environment and its internal features/capabilities in order to survive and succeed. A fit between the firm’s IT resources/capabilities and the demands of the environment will positively affect performance; a misfit may (in the worstcase) negatively influence the firm’s competitive position. This notion of fit is consistent with Venkatraman’s ‘‘fit as moderation’’ perspective [22].

## 2.2. IT capability types

Some authors considered that IT assets (human, technology, and relationships) led to business value, while others considered that relationships such as governance, business systems thinking, relationship building, designing architectures, informed buying, contract facilitation and monitoring, and vendor development important. Wade and Hulland [23] discussed how eight ‘‘key IS resources’’ (external relationships management, market responsiveness, IS-business partnership, IS planning and change management, IS infrastructure, IS technical skills, IS development, and cost effective IS operations) might be organized into three classes of capabilities: inside-out, outside-in and spanning.

One common thread in these typologies is that they were developed from the perspective of the IS unit (i.e., they focused on functional skills and resources of the IS unit) rather than from the perspective of the business and the firm’s choices about how and where IS resources were to be deployed. However, we focused on higher level meta-IT capabilities that captured the manner in which the IS unit’s resource endowments were deployed in support of organizational processes.

We defined organizational IT capabilities as complex bundles of IT-related resources, skills and knowledge, exercised through business processes, that enable firms to coordinate activities and make use of the IT assets to provide desired results. We further distinguished between externally and internally focused IT capabilities based on the primary business process area supported, thus reflecting the firm’s choices of where and how IT resources were deployed. We defined externally focused IT capabilities as bundles of IT-related resources, skills and knowledge that help the firm sense and respond in a timely way to changes in its markets and shifts in the needs of customers and suppliers. Examples of these include IT resources and skills deployed to support market research, customer-facing e-commerce interfaces and CRM processes. Then internally focused IT capabilities are bundles of ITrelated resources, skills and accumulated knowledge that help the firm offer reliable products and services and minimize overhead costs (back-office production, operational support, and fulfillment processes). ERP systems are examples of internally focused IT resource intended to integrate internal operations and data and thus increase efficiency and reliability.

Because of decisions affecting the acquisition and deployment of IT resources and related learning, a firm may have reasonably well-developed externally or internally focused IT capability, but not necessarily both. Path dependency, which reflects the role of the firm’s past and cumulative nature of capability building, can affect their relative maturity. Similarly, shifts in strategy/tactics and changes in stakeholders or technology may result in the firm emphasizing the building of one type of IT capability over another. Finally, consistent with RBV, for a capability to be a source of competitive advantage, it must be considered relative to other firms with which it competes. In the face of change and competitive pressure, continuous development becomes a necessary but not sufficient condition for sustained leadership: a firm with a superior externally focused IT capability, for example, is likely to lose its leadership status if it fails to upgrade that capability at a rate faster than its competitors.

## 2.3. The role of industry characteristics

Studies that control for industry fixed-effects have generally focused on the average impact across industries. However, event studies examining the shareholders wealth effects of IT-related announcements have suggested that the nature and significance of their impact may differ across industries, depending on the role that IT plays [11]. Researchers have also called for considering industry as an important contextual variable [5]. In our research, we drew on Dess and Beard’s [7] conceptualization and measures of the environment; they had showed that three were important: environmental turbulence (dynamism), munificence, and complexity. We focused on these in our examination of the impact of the fit between IT capabilities and the environment. Fig. 1 shows our model.

## 2.3.1. Environmental turbulence

Environmental turbulence (dynamism) is the rate and instability of the environment, which is the result of changes in customer preference, development of new products, new technology, or the competition.

IT resources that enhance business efficiency tend to require well-defined requirements and stability to increase organizational learning and become effective. Turbulent business environments may require frequent change of internal business practices and limit the value of new technologies Examining the impact of ERP investments, Hitt et al. [10] noted a slow down in business performance and productivity shortly after ERP implementation, and attributed it to organizational need for learning and adjusting before investments could pay off. Internally focused IT resources are likely to be in a constant state of flux in turbulent environments. Therefore, we believed that possession of superior internally focused IT capabilities was likely to yield greater benefits in stable environments. Thus:

Hypothesis 1A. The relationship between internally focused IT capabilities and firm performance will be stronger for firms in stable (low dynamism) business environments than for firms in turbulent business environments.

Firm performance in environments where new threats can materialize suddenly and opportunities may be short-lived is likely to be determined by its ability to recognize these changes and trends and respond quickly. Accordingly, externally focused IT capabilities that sense the market and respond to shifts will be more valuable for firms in turbulent environments. Consistent with these arguments, Li and Ye [12] found that the payoff from IT investments appeared to be highest for firms operating in dynamic environments and with externally oriented strategy. Therefore, we believed that the value adding potential of superior externally focused IT capabilities was likely to be higher when the firm operated in a turbulent industry.

Hypothesis 1B. The relationship between externally focused IT capabilities and firm performance will be stronger for firms in turbulent (high dynamism) business environments than for firms in stable business environments.

## 2.3.2. Environmental munificence

This term is used to refer to the extent to which the environment can support sustained growth. Industries that are mature or shrinking are characterized as having low munificence and competition is intense, leading to price wars, giving low-cost producers a distinctive advantage. By streamlining operations and enhancing efficiency, superior internally focused IT capabilities can enhance their competitive position. Thus the hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2A. The relationship between internally focused IT capabilities and firm performance will be stronger for firms in low-munificent environments than for firms in high-munificent environments.

High-munificent industries enjoy growth in demand and an increasing customer base. While such markets tend to be relatively more forgiving than low-growth environments, we believed that being more in tune with the market would still confer competitive advantages. A superior externally focused IT capability that enhances a firm’s ability to sense market change and customer desires is likely to help organizations make better decisions on inventory levels and to choose products that satisfy consumer demand, giving that firm an advantage. This approach is evident in the fashion industry where a specific color or style of clothing may become the top seller, and suppliers need to sense when this specific item is desirable. Thus, the value adding potential of superior externally focused IT capability is likely to be more pronounced in highly munificent environments.

Hypothesis 2B. The relationship between externally focused IT capabilities and firm performance will be stronger for firms in high-munificent environments than for firms in low-munificent environments.

## 2.3.3. Environmental complexity

Environmental complexity depends on both the number of different products offered by the firm and the industry in which it operates and the level of knowledge sophistication that firms must have about the products and their consumers as well as the number of external actors who must be influenced if the firm is to be successful. Firms facing a complex environment perceive greater uncertainty and have more information processing requirements than those in a simpler environment. This is consistent with the information processing view of the firm [8]:

decision makers faced with task uncertainty need more information in order to achieve a high-performance level. Both types of IT capabilities are likely to be important in highly complex environments, since IT increases the overall information processing capacity of the firm. Firms with superior externally focused IT capabilities are better able to collect, process, and assimilate complex external information and formulate and coordinate an effective response in a timely manner. Similarly, firms with superior internally focused IT capabilities are better able to cope with the complexity induced by characteristics of the processes or products needed to serve the market and coordinate such complex operations more effectively. Thus:

Hypothesis 3A. The relationship between internally focused IT capabilities and firm performance will be stronger for firms in high-complexity environments than for firms in low-complexity environments.

Hypothesis 3B. The relationship between externally focused IT capabilities and firm performance will be stronger for firms in high-complexity environments than for firms in low-complexity environments.

## 2.3.4. Synergy among environmental conditions

Interactions of all three aspects of the environment may compound the moderating role of environmental condition on the relationship between IT capability type and performance. In particular, superior internally focused IT capabilities are likely to be even more consequential in stable, low munificent, complex environments. Similarly, having a superior externally focused IT capability is likely to be even more important and valuable in dynamic environments that are also munificent and complex. Thus:

Hypothesis 4A. The relationship between internally focused IT capabilities and performance will be stronger for firms operating in environments characterized by low dynamism, low munificence, and high complexity.

Hypothesis 4B. The relationship between externally focused IT capabilities and performance will be stronger for firms operating in environments characterized by high dynamism, high munificence, and high complexity.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Sample and measures

## 3.1.1. Measurement of IT capabilities

The rankings provided by Information Week (IW) in their annual special issue, IW 500, were used to identify firms with superior IT capabilities; we relied on a set of IW rankings from 2000 to 2001 to serve as proxies for the two distinct types of IT capabilities. The criteria used and composition of the identified users of technology has changed over the years; the initial list focused on the largest users of IT but now the list identifies the most innovative users of technology. During the 2-year period, 2000–2001, Information Week editors identified the top 500 innovative users of technology and ranked each based on its ‘‘early adoption and creative use of technologies that can help create significant business value’’ in two categories: e-business and technology enabled business practices. To be considered for inclusion (see Information Week methodology statement for the year 2000, http://informationweek.com/803 methodology.htm), a company must ‘‘demonstrate a pattern of technological, procedural, and organizational innovation’’. Subsequently, IW ranked the firms’ innovative use of IT in e-business and business practices by assigning them a gold, silver or bronze medal in each of these categories. The rankings were made on a curve and, for each category, a third of the companies received gold, a third received silver, and the others received bronze medals.

Information Week described the e-business technology innovation category as focusing on e-commerce and supply-chain management. Both involved the deployment of innovative externally facing systems to serve customers and suppliers; therefore, we employed innovation in this category as a proxy for the firm’s externally focused IT capabilities. We identified a firm as having superior externally focused IT capability if it received a gold medal in the e-business innovation category. We believed that this was an appropriate proxy for externally focused IT capability as innovation was a key component in developing unique capabilities and ecommerce was directly related to the firm’s ability to understand and fulfill customers’ requirements. Similarly, IW ranking in the technology enabled business practices category was used as a proxy for internally focused IT capabilities. IW cited ERP implementation and IS integration across business processes as examples of technology enabled business practices. Since such efforts were typically aimed at improving the ability to manage the day-to-day operations and enhance overall efficiency, we designated a firm as having superior internally focused IT capability if it received a gold medal in the business practices innovation category.

Although the IW specific rankings we used as proxies for IT capability types had not been previously employed, we were not the first to use IW rankings as proxies for superior IT capability. Both Bharadwaj and Santhanam and Hartono employed aggregate rankings (yielding somewhat mixed results) as measures of aggregate IT capability. In fact, the use of secondary data in theory testing is well accepted.

IW rankings were performed using a comparative methodology where the results were generated relative to other members of the IW 500 who were similarly analyzed. By using the list of gold medalists as a proxy for superior IT capability, we were assuming that failure to be included on the list indicated that the firm did not have a superior IT capability. Any IW misclassification would make it more difficult for us to reject the null hypothesis. However, the rankings themselves might be influenced by prior financial performance. Bharadwaj tested for a financial halo in earlier IW rankings of IT capability and found none. Santhanam and Hartono argued that the halo may exist and that the most appropriate treatment was to control for previous financial results when examining the association between current performance and the rankings. We followed Santhanam and Hartono’s (more conservative) approach and control in our models for firm performance in the year prior to the IW 500 listing.

## 3.1.2. Data sample

In 1998 and 1999, IW gave medals for the e-business category but the business practices category was labeled as business processes/ERP. This category was relabeled as business practices in 2000; therefore, for consistency, we limited our sample to the rankings published in 2000 and 2001, since these were the only 2 years IW provided such rankings for both innovation categories. The measures that we employed were relative only to the other firms in the IW 500, and thus we examined only differences among listed firms. This – we believed – was a more conservative approach, since we avoided the possibility of detecting performance effects solely because of bias in the IW selection process of the innovative users. (Our inferences were unchanged when we expanded our sample to cover the entire 4-year period.)

## 3.1.3. Dependent measures

Our research model and hypotheses focused on examining the relationship between IT capability types and firm performance. We choose to follow both Bharadwaj and Santhanam and Hartono who employed a series of accounting measures as proxies for firm performance: we felt that the benefits of some types of IT capabilities were likely to be reflected in certain measures but not others. This choice allowed our results to be compared with previous studies that considered the impact of IT capability and allowed the detection of differences in results based on the type of performance measure.

The performance measures used were: return on sales (ROS), return on assets (ROA), operating income to sales (OIS), operating income to assets (OIA), operating income to employees (OIE), cost of goods sold to sales (COG/S), selling and general administration expense to sales (SGA/S). Return on assets and return on sales are widely used as measures of performance; however, these measures include the impact of interest and revenues and expenses not directly related to firm operations. Therefore, we considered that OIS and OIA provided more direct measures of the IT impact on operations. Scaling the dependent variables by sales, assets, or the number of employees, assisted in controlling for size differences among firms.

We examined the cost of goods sold (COGS) and sales expense (SG&A) as some business impact may only be seen through measures of internal business operation and may not necessarily be visible in overall measures of business performance due to other capabilities of the firm, Data for all these measures were obtained from the Compustat database for the fiscal year ending before the ranking by IW. The use of Compustat reduced our data set, since it provided data only for publicly traded firms and some of the companies did not report all of the data required. The availability of data reduced our sample of firm-year observations to between 686 and 537, depending on the specific measure of performance.

## 3.1.4. Environmental measures

Following Dess and Beard, the dynamism and munificence of each industry in our sample were assessed using archival data extracted from Compustat at the two-digit SIC industry segment level. For each industry segment and each year, the industry-level total sales for 5 years (t0 to t - 4) were regressed on the year variable. Munificence was operationalized as the growth rate in annual industry sales, measured as the regression slope coefficient (the coefficient of the time variable) divided by the average annual sales over the 5-year period. Dynamism was operationalized as the variability in annual industry sales and measured as the standard error of the regression slope coefficient of annual industry sales divided by the industry mean for the 5-year period.

Dess and Beard framed industry complexity as homogeneity– heterogeneity of its inputs and outputs, and we followed their suggestion and use input/output concentration as a proxy for the level of industry complexity. We used the Burea of Economic Analysis input–output tables to calculate the concentration of each industry’s inputs (source of supply), measured as

$$
C _ {i} = \frac {\sum I _ {k} ^ {2}}{(\sum I _ {k}) ^ {2}}\tag{1}
$$

where $I _ { k }$ is the dollar volume of inputs from industry k. This measure increases with the number of industries supplying the inputs and as they become more evenly distributed across the suppliers, capturing both the structural and distributive differences in complexity that may occur across industries. (Similar results were obtained when complexity was measured in terms of output concentration.)

For each of the three industry characteristics, we ranked the values by year and split the industries into two sets (high and low), based on the median value for that characteristic. The industry characteristic variables for munificence, growth and complexity are non-normally distributed based on Shapiro-Wilks tests; therefore, we split the industries into high and low groups and used dichotomous variables to represent the industry characteristics. Each firm-year observation in the sample was then assigned three dichotomous variables to proxy for its industry’s munificence, dynamism, and complexity scores for that year; these were assigned 1 for high and 0 for low-value sets. ## 4. Data analysis

## 4.1. Direct effects of IT capability types

## 4.1.1. Aggregate IT capability

We argued that disaggregated measures should be used when possible in assessing IT capability, as aggregation provides a noisy coarse-grained indicator that confounds the effects of different types of IT capabilities, leading to incorrect conclusions about the performance implications of IT. To demonstrate this and provide a base-line comparison with prior work, we first examined the IT capability to firm performance relationship using an aggregate measure.

IW articles referred to the top 100 as the ‘‘leaders’’ in IT and the more recent listings only ranked the top 100 while identifying 400 followers. Therefore, as a way of considering the aggregate effect on our dataset, we adopted this convention and designate a firm as having superior aggregate IT capability if it appears in the list of 100 leaders.

To assess the direct effects of the aggregate IT capabilities on firm performance, we estimate the following base model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} F P _ {t} & = F P _ {(t - 1)} + \text { Aggregate\_IT\_Cap. } + \text { muni   licence } + \text { dynamism } \\ & + \text { complexity } + \text { industry   dummies } + \text { year   dummies } \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where FP denotes financial performance, t represents the time, and Aggregate\_IT\_Cap. is a dichotomous variable that represents the aggregated IT capabilities. We also included industry dummies and year dummies to control for fixed effects (e.g., effects of macroeconomic factors on stock prices). If correlated with the independent variables, these effects could bias the regression coefficients. Similarly, we included dummies to control for environmental conditions (munificence, dynamism, and complexity) that might explain variation in performance values across industries.

Table 2 summarizes the regression results (only the coefficients on prior performance and IT capability are tabulated). The residuals satisfied distributional assumptions. The normal probability plot of the standardized residuals suggested that they were normally distributed. The plot of standardized residuals against the standardized predicted values indicated linearity and equality of variance. The residual plots did not indicate the presence of any influential outliers. Multicollinearity was consistently low, as evidenced by VIF values. Aggregate IT capability was found to be associated with only one performance measure (SGA/S), and the sign was opposite to that expected. There was no association between most performance measures and the aggregate measure of IT capability. A firm can excel in one category of IT capabilities but not the other. An aggregate IT capability measure that lumps all firms together regardless of the category in which they excel is not likely to explain variation in performance. Similarly, an additive aggregate measure that requires superior IT capabilities across categories may not explain variations in performance, since firms that excel in only one category of IT capabilities (and therefore not be classified as having superior aggregate IT capability) may actually perform better. The risk involved in using an aggregate measure therefore is in the increased likelihood of erroneously concluding that the posited association between IT capabilities and firm performance is not supported (a type II error).

The externally focused IT capabilities were positively and significantly associated with the profit ratios and negatively associated with the cost of goods ratio under high dynamism. These results were consistent with our hypothesis 1B We also saw moderating effects (pooled sample with interaction terms) for dynamism on key variables (OIS, OIA, and OIE), providing additional support for this hypothesis.

The internally focused IT capabilities appear to be significant only in high-dynamism industries. However, contrary to expectation, the association was negative (and significant for three of the five) profit ratios and positive (and insignificant) for the cost ratios. ## 4.2.2. Environmental munificence

Table 6 shows the regression results of our analysis of the moderating role of industry munificence on the relationship between the two types of IT capabilities and measures of firm performance.

The analysis found few significant associations between external capabilities and measures of firm performance under either high or low munificence. Additionally, an examination of the models with interaction terms found no evidence of moderation for externally focused capabilities. We expected that externally focused resources (hypothesis 2B) would have a stronger association with performance in high-munificent industries as the market sensing capabilities may have assisted in identifying new trends. The association between performance and internally focused capabilities was generally negative, which was similar to results observed in the overall sample. Internal resources were expected (hypothesis 2A) to have a stronger association with performance in the low-munificent industries as strong internal capabilities were predicted to make a firm more competitive then. Thus, both hypotheses (2A and 2B) were not supported by the data.

## 4.2.4. Combined effects of environmental conditions

Overall, our results suggested that environmental conditions moderated the relationship between IT capability types and performance. However, the results were mixed and this may be because the analysis focused on one environmental characteristic at a time. The relative importance and value of an IT capability depends on the overall environmental condition, and the fit along one dimension (e.g., dynamism) may be counteracted by a misfit along another dimension (e.g., complexity). In other words, the performance implication of an IT capability type depends on its net effect under various environmental characteristics.

We hypothesized that internally focused IT capabilities were more likely to have positive performance implications when the firm operated in low munificence, low dynamism and high-complexity environments (H4A), and that externally focused IT capabilities were more likely to have positive performance implications under conditions of high munificence, high dynamism and high complexity (H4B). ## 5. Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that the relationship between IT capabilities and firm performance was more complex than previously theorized. In particular, the effect of the external environment appeared to be significant. Specifically, our analysis provided general support to the proposition that the impact of an IT capability depended on the characteristics of the environment/ industry in which the firm competes. Our results also suggested that a fit between the type of IT resources/capabilities possessed by the firm and the demands of the environment would positively affect performance with respect to externally oriented IT capabilities. The results also highlight the risk involved in using overall aggregate measure of IT capability, as it could lead to type II errors.

Our study points to the important role of industry as an important contextual factor in developing and testing a theory about IT impacts. Analysis of the contingency model added to knowledge about the relation between IT capabilities and the performance of firms. First, it offered an explanation for the seemingly conflicting findings regarding the impact of aggregate IT capability. Second, it suggested that aggregate measures of IT investments or capability should be used with caution: a firm may excel in one dimension of IT capability, be only average in others, and be below average in the rest. The association between aggregate measures of IT capabilities and firm performance depends on the net effect of individual components of IT capability in the context of the environment in which the firm operates.

For practitioners responsible for developing and nurturing such capabilities, our findings reinforced the need to align IT investment and deployment decisions with the demands of the environment of the firm since the proper match is likely to increase the payoff from IT. The results especially suggested that firms operating in environments characterized by high dynamism, high munificence, and high complexity, should place greater emphasis on the development and maintenance of externally focused IT capabilities in order to maximize the payoff from IT investments. Overall, our findings suggested that firms should consider their unique industry conditions before making the case for and adopting the latest technology. Improved understanding of these environmental conditions could help firms determine which parts of the business stands to benefit the most from investment in IT and act proactively.
