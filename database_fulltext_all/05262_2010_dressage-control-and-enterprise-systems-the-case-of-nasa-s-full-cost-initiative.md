# Dressage, control, and enterprise systems: the case of NASA’s Full Cost initiative

Nicholas Berente<sup>1</sup>, Uri Gal<sup>1</sup> and Youngjin Yoo<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Systems, Case Western Reserve University, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Information Systems, Temple University, U.S.A.

## Abstract

In 2004, National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) implemented Full Cost, an activity-based accounting program through an agency-wide enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementation. We apply Foucault’s notion of ‘dressage’ to highlight aspects of demonstrative control associated with this implementation effort and to outline the different dimensions along which control was distributed. We suggest that some elements of Full Cost and the ERP system were geared toward satisfying disciplinary requirements without necessarily contributing to productive activity. We term these elements dressage-as-control. Our findings indicate that dressage-as-control breeds dressage-as-response: employees’ responses to control imperatives that are intended merely to satisfy the demand for control by exhibiting public compliance, but make no direct contribution to the execution of work. Accordingly, we propose that the ideal of complete enterprise control remains unattainable, as aspects of demonstrative control are inevitably implicated within broader enterprise-wide control systems within contemporary organizations. We conclude with implications for research and practice. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 21–34. doi:10.1057/ejis.2009.47; published online 23 February 2010

## Introduction

Enterprise systems are a unique form of information technology in that they are intended to standardize applications and business processes across entire organizations (Davenport, 1998; Markus et al., 2000; Robey et al., 2002). These systems are intended to perform as organizational infrastructures that enhance managers’ ability to align business processes with organizational strategies and to control organizational resources (Ciborra, 2000). In practice, however, this control is elusive as reactions to and appropriations of enterprise systems are emergent, difficult to predict, and rife with misalignments between locally situated organizational practices and those which are inscribed into the system (Davenport, 1998; Markus et al., 2000; Robey et al., 2002; Gosain, 2004; Soh & Sia, 2004; Wei et $a l . ,$ 2005).

An assumption often implied by much of the literature on enterprise systems is that the control that they enable will enhance organizational productivity. Although there is widespread acknowledgement that complete organizational control is difficult to achieve (Ciborra, 2000), it is broadly assumed that control enabled by enterprise systems does, in fact, contribute to organizational functioning, agility, and competitive advantage (e.g., Weill et al., 2002; Sambamurthy et $a l . ,$ 2003). However, it is clear that this increased organizational productivity is not uniformly evident across contexts (Davenport, 1998; Markus et al., 2000; Stefanou, 2001; Ash & Burn, 2003; Davenport et al., 2004; Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Volkoff et al., 2005). It is our assertion that, in some situations, elements of the control that is facilitated by enterprise systems do not serve to increase organizational productivity. Rather, aspects of this enhanced control can be demonstrative in nature, helping create a fac¸ade of a highly ordered and well-structured organization without necessarily contributing to better organizational functioning. We maintain that those situations where enterprise systems do not support organizational productivity may offer critical insight into the successful appropriation of enterprise systems and the difficulties that are associated with their implementation. Thus, using a language of control, it is important to examine those situations where instruments of control do not promote productive ends.

To investigate situations of control associated with an enterprise system that do not contribute to organizational productivity, we report on a process of an enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementation at the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), a U.S. government agency charged with radical scientific innovation which includes the country’s space program. In focusing on the ‘Full Cost’ activity-based costing aspects of the implementation, we identify systemic, cascading imperatives for control stemming from the highest reaches of the U.S. government through the multidimensional NASA management hierarchy, to individual scientists in a local NASA Center. We find that, in certain cases, these control imperatives do not derive from concerns of productivity, but instead appear to serve largely demonstrative purposes. In these situations productive work continues outside of the scope of the system.

To make sense of these findings, we utilize Foucault’s (1977) notion of dressage and situate our observations within his framework of distributed control. Foucault uses the concept of dressage to explain the way societies exercise power to regulate their members’ behaviors and ensure their obedience. Dressage involves efforts to enforce complete control by a governing body over the activities of its inhabitant population along multiple dimensions of social time and space. It emphasizes the public display of obedience by individuals and implies that individuals may engage in certain observable behaviors merely to satisfy the centralized demands for control ( Jackson & Carter, 1998), but not necessarily for any productive ends.

Based on NASA’s case, we suggest that dressage has two ‘faces’ – dressage-as-control and dressage-as-response – the former represents a centralized top-down drive for control and the latter represents individuals’ reactions to it. We further suggest that the two faces are interrelated: the manifestation of the former facilitates the occurrence of the latter. We finally propose that the relationships among the different dimensions along which control is distributed can offer important insight for managing and assessing enterprise systems implementation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on control and power in information systems (IS) and present Foucault’s notion of dressage. Then, we present our research into NASA. We conclude by reflecting on our findings and address implications of our research for both theory and practice.

## Power and control in IS literature

Power relations and political processes are endemic to situations of IS development, implementation, and use. A substantial body of work in the field of IS has demonstrated that processes of design, introduction, and deployment of IS are entrenched within an intricate web of power relations and political tactics (Kling & Scacchi, 1980; Markus, 1983; Markus & Bjorn-Andersen, 1987; Ciborra, 2000; Gray, 2001; Mlckova & Whitley, 2004; Lin & Silva, 2005; Howard-Grenville & Carlile, 2006; Silva, 2007).

While the relationship between IS and power has been addressed in a variety of practical contexts (e.g., Markus, 1983; Zuboff, 1988; Robey & Smith, 1993; Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997; Davidson, 2002; Bowker, 2005), the very notion of power has been subjected to multiple interpretations. Conventionally, power is treated in the literature as a capacity that can be possessed by individuals or groups and that can be exercised to impact the behaviors of other individuals or groups (Markus, 1983, Gray, 2001, Jasperson et al., 2002). This understanding of power equates it to a commodity that one could gain, lose, or have more or less of (Introna, 1997). Most commonly, IS research adopting this view has focused on the implementation of IS by organizational management as a way to increase the level of control over employees’ activities and enhance their efficiency and overall productivity (e.g., Allen et al., 2000; Howcroft & Light, 2006).

An alternative understanding of power focuses on social perceptions and knowledge and the processes by which they are shaped and reshaped. In this view, power inheres in the capability to manipulate the meaning of shared artifacts, experiences, or events. The exercise of power can be covert and does not necessarily manifest itself or lead to open conflicts or signs of resistance (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007). This view of power emphasizes the capacity of IS to constitute organizational discursive resources thereby reaffirming or challenging the legitimacy of existing practices, norms, social structures, and roles (c.f., Doolin, 2004).

Other researchers have integrated different views of power to offer multi-dimensional explanations of the dynamics of IS implementation and use. For example, Clegg’s ‘circuits of power’ framework (Clegg et al., 2006) has been utilized by a number of researchers to demonstrate how power relations are inherent in the capacity of some groups to influence the activities of other groups, in the institutionalized norms and social structures that bestow some individuals or groups with the legitimacy to influence the behaviors of other individuals and groups, and in the techniques of production and discipline that enable an individual or a group’s compliance (Silva & Backhouse, 2003; Backhouse et al., 2006).

By and large, research focusing on issues of power has, either explicitly but mostly implicitly, viewed the exercise of power as motivated by a desire to enhance the efficiency of and control over the activities of organizational members. Power is therefore understood to serve some functional or productive purpose.<sup>1</sup> This holds true for research on enterprise IS. Much of this literature conceptualizes the system as an extension of managerial control, which is fundamental to the rationale of organizational productivity (Ciborra, 2000). Even in the enterprise system literature that views control efforts as incomplete and exercised through an emergent, distributed power mechanism (e.g. Sia et al., 2002; Scott & Wagner, 2003; Elmes et al., 2005), the enterprise system is largely characterized as an apparatus of managerial control – a control that is thought to support organizational productivity from the point of view of organizational managers.

In the literature, we find little attention to the possibility that enterprise systems may embody elements of demonstrative control, that is, where the system reflects a symbolic, legitimizing, or disciplinary character. Further, by characterizing enterprise systems as blunt, undifferentiated instruments of management control, the distributed, systemic nature of such all-encompassing systems is generally not taken into consideration. Thus, we look to contribute to the discourse on enterprise systems and control in two ways: (1) by focusing on situations that reflect demonstrative control; and (2) by characterizing enterprise systems as apparatuses of control that are distributed across multiple differentiated dimensions. To further explore these ideas, we next elaborate on Foucault’s notion of dressage and situate it within his framework of control.

## Dressage and dispositif

Dressage is commonly used to refer to the training of a horse in obedience and deportment and to the execution by a horse of precise movements in response to its rider. It therefore implies a complete and undisputed adherence to received orders. For Foucault (1977), the notion of dressage represents an ideal form of control regime that he derives from and situates within a larger sociohistorical context. He describes the emergence of an allencompassing disciplinary drive that manifests itself when societies develop a network of distributed apparatuses, which Foucault terms dispositif, to exercise strict control over their citizens.<sup>2</sup> The desired effect of these apparatuses is a disciplinary society with a population that may be easily managed, used, and made more efficient (Foucault, 1977). Under such conditions people are committed to compliance to the point that they willingly delegate their moral autonomy and responsibility to obedience to the rules, and to being observed and governed by an external force – the state (Jackson & Carter, 1998). The dispositif rests on four principles of discipline and control that are distributed along multiple dimensions of social time and space and that constitute the base for its functioning. These principles combined should be viewed as an ideal type: all four of them are not likely to fully manifest in a given situation. However, they can be used to assess levels of control in different contexts and compare different control regimes. They are summarized in Table 1.

The first principle is the art of distributions (Foucault, 1977). It involves allocating people into prescribed physical and social spaces. Allocation into physical spaces entails specifying special purpose, self-enclosed spaces inside of which individuals can be confined. Army barracks, school or factories are some examples of such spaces. In contemporary organizations, offices or prescribed workstations are examples of such spaces. The intention of this confinement is ‘to know where and how to locate individuals y to be able at each moment to supervise the conduct of each individual, to assess it, to judge it y to calculate its qualities’ (Foucault, 1977, p. 143). Allocation into a social space implies the division of society into functional and hierarchical compartments (e.g., schools and classes, or organizations, departments, and organizational ranks) and the assignment of individuals to specific ‘social slots’ within them. ‘Assigning individual places made possible the supervision of each individual and the simultaneous work of all’ (Foucault, 1977, p. 147).

The second principle is the organization of geneses (Foucault, 1977). This principle involves the division of temporal sequences into successive or parallel segments, each of which must end at a specific time. These temporal segments need to be simple and combine according to increased complexity. This succession ‘reveals a linear time whose moments are integrated, one upon another, and which is oriented towards a terminal, stable point; in short, an ‘evolutive’ time’ (Foucault, 1977, p. 160). Training programs for new employees are often structured according to this principle. They are divided into consecutive segments or modules, starting from simple modules, for beginners, and developing into more advanced modules, resulting in the qualification of individuals for work.

The third principle is the composition of forces (Foucault, 1977). Within the system of the dispositif, the individual body becomes an element that may be placed, moved, and articulated on others. Its core qualities are no longer the principle variables that define it; but the place it occupies within a larger system that organizes and classifies people, places, and functions. ‘The body is constituted as a part of multi-segmentary machine’ (Foucault, 1977, p. 164). An example of this principle is the standardization of organizational processes and routinization of work in large organizations that is often facilitated by the implementation of different types of enterprise systems. Such standardization is likely to diminish people’s capacity to be creative, improvise, or bring to bear their unique personal characteristic in the execution of their work tasks (Kallinikos, 2004). Therefore, it facilitates the constitution of an organizational system wherein people are no longer evaluated based on their individual qualities, but on their execution of a predefined standardized task, and the location of this task within a larger chain of inter-linked organizational processes.

The fourth principle concerns the control of activity (Foucault, 1977). This principle is meant to constrain the range of actions that individuals perform and keep them under close scrutiny by establishing a temporal rhythm and by regulating cycles of repetition for their activities. Furthermore, it specifies the precise sequence of bodily movements that are appropriate for occupants of specific physical and social spaces and defines in detail the manner in which objects are to be handled and manipulated by individuals (e.g., the proper way for a soldier to march and handle his weapon). Thus, control of the body is often achieved through its interaction with an object, such as a specialized machine or technology. For example, the interface of many packaged software, combined with anticipated use practices, is intended to control the user’s actions not just while interacting with the technology, but also while performing activities outside the technology’s scope. For instance, in using the purchase requisition form of an enterprise system, the system’s interface guides the user in this activity by providing a template for purchase requisitions, and by requiring that certain fields be filled out in their entirety. If certain fields cannot be filled out immediately, the user must engage in detailed, complementary processes to complete the information required for the requisition.

The four principles of the dispositif comprise a system that is made up of a carefully measured combination of forces, which require a precise organization of command. All activities of the disciplined individual must be sustained and punctuated by injunctions whose efficacy rests on brevity and clarity; the order does not need to be explained; it must trigger off the required behavior without eliciting any questioning or inquiring (Foucault, 1977). Thus, the dispositif is the mechanism through which complete control is implemented and enforced, and the medium through which disciplinary expectations are both signified and manifested. In short, the dispositif is the apparatus through which dressage is realized.

Unlike typical surveillance which observes what is done for corrective purposes, dressage requires the body to perform and demonstrate compliance to the demands that are imposed by the controller ( Jackson & Carter, 1998). Therefore, it implies that individuals will carry out tasks simply for the sake of compliance. That is, individuals will engage in tasks not for reasons of productivity but for the sole purpose of satisfying the disciplinary demands imposed on them by the dispositif. Dressage, therefore, implies not only control for the sake of organization and efficiency, but also demonstrative control, or control for the sake of control.

While the literature has established the importance of enterprise IS as mechanisms of organizational control and organizational theorists have analyzed various aspects of Foucault’s work on power, the goal of this effort is to focus primarily on demonstrative control and the four principles of dispositif in the context of an ERP implementation. To do so, we next present NASA’s ERP implementation and associated ‘Full Cost’ initiative and highlight the elements of demonstrative control that they exhibited.

## Research methodology

Our research focused on NASA’s implementation of an ERP system and, in particular, the Full Cost program that is associated with a subset of the system’s overall functionality. This setting offered a unique opportunity to examine the manner in which highly structured control imperatives were received by members of one of the largest and most innovative governmental agencies in the U.S. Few other organizations are charged with radical innovations on the order of sending manned vehicles to Mars, while governed by a classic bureaucracy, and at the same time subject to the budgetary and mission goals of ever-changing executive and legislative branches of a government. Furthermore, NASA’s multiple locations across the country, highly heterogeneous workforce, and complex matrix organizational structure made it a particularly interesting case to study which could provide interesting insight into the challenges that accompany the implementations of large-scale enterprise systems. Therefore, NASA’s ERP implementation constitutes an extreme case that can be used for generating valuable theoretical inferences (Yin, 2003; Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007).

Our study relied on data that were collected during a 9-month field study of NASA’s agency-wide implementation of an ERP system in 2004. A total of 68 interviews were conducted throughout the Agency Headquarters in Washington D.C. and two additional centers, Glenn Research Center in Cleveland, Ohio, and Marshall Space Center in Huntsville, Alabama. Interviewees consisted of 22 finance and administration personnel and manager, 15 ERP implementation team leaders and members, 23 center managers, research managers, and researchers, and 8 project managers.

Data were collected using semi-structured interviews and lasted for approximately 1 h. During the interviews, we looked to understand events relevant to the implementation process within their broader contexts before embarking on any given line of questioning. Therefore, interviewees were treated more as ‘informants’ than ‘respondents’ throughout the interviewing process (Yin, 2003, p. 90). Informants were asked to bring examples of positive and negative experiences associated with the enterprise system implementation with them to the interview. They were asked about their personal history with the organization, position requirements, and the activities that they engage in on a regular basis. Then, two broad areas of the enterprise system implementation were specifically addressed through open-ended questioning, namely, the Integrated Financial Management (IFM), and the Full Cost program, which is activitybased costing enabled by ERP. In this study, we focus primarily on the Full Cost-related functionality of the system.<sup>3</sup>

The interview data were analyzed following Strauss & Corbin’s (1998) model of grounded theory development, which involved three rounds of coding with continuous iteration between the data and our theoretical interests (Eisenhardt, 1989). This iteration helped amplify our theoretical sensitivity (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) through the extraction of themes such as ‘control’, ‘misalign ments’, and ‘locally situated actions’. In addition, a research database and central repository were created for the research project (Yin, 2003). The research repository included every transcribed interview as well as all digital documentation and a number of internal NASA reports about the implementation. It additionally contained audit reports concerning the enterprise system implementation from the U.S. General Accounting Office (GAO), the NASA Advisory Council, and the Inspector General. In order to triangulate our interpretation of the events (Yin, 2003), a full case write-up was collaboratively created by two of the authors and submitted to NASA management. They agreed that our depiction of events was accurate.

The case write-up was followed by three rounds of coding of the interview transcripts. The first round involved note-taking and open coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) through which we became immersed in the data and began to explore for recurring themes. The second round involved selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The notion of misalignments between practice and the enterprise system, as well as the espoused reasons for these misalignments, accompanied by themes of power and control began to emerge. At this stage, the notion of dressage was identified as an applicable theoretical lens for engaging the data and rendering it meaningful (Boland, 2005). To organize and display the data we used ‘conceptually ordered descriptive matrices’ (Miles & Huberman, 1994), which are used to order qualitative data for subsequent clustering, partitioning, and axial coding.

## NASA’s Full Cost initiative

NASA was established in 1958 with the passage of National Aeronautics and Space Act.<sup>4</sup> From the organization’s inception, separate centers were established in order to meet the unique challenges of its mission. Over time, unique competencies, culture, organizational structure, and technical infrastructures have been developed within each center. This has resulted in ‘stove-piped’ IS, organizational processes, and accounting standards at each center and for the wide array of functional groups within each center. Each center built its own systems to meet its idiosyncratic needs without much regard to compatibility with other locations. Furthermore, similar capabilities and knowledge resources were established in different centers, resulting in redundant investment.

In addition, NASA has been historically unsuccessful in accounting for funds and keeping projects within budgetary constraints. To address this issue, a series of regulatory and statutory requirements that concerned NASA’s financial management were imposed on the agency by the American congress. These included the Chief Financial

Officer’s Act of 1990, the Federal Financial Management Improvement Act of 1996, and OMB Circular A-127 that required a single integrated system for financial management.<sup>5</sup> At NASA, this requirement took the form of the ‘Integrated Financial Management Program’ (IFMP).

The Full Cost initiative – which is the focus of this research – occurred in parallel with IFMP. Employed through the implementation of an agency-wide enterprise system planning system, the purpose of Full Cost was to provide complete and accurate cost information to decision-makers using principles of activity-based cost accounting. Full Cost was intended to ensure that all cost elements in NASA are associated with particular crosscenter projects.<sup>6</sup> For example, before Full Cost, civil servant salary and certain elements of the agency’s infrastructure, such as facilities and human resources functions, were funded outside of projects. In the Full Cost approach, all organizational costs including civil servant salary were to be directly or indirectly charged to projects. This means that time and resources were directly allocated to specific enterprises and in turn to their programs and projects. Anything not billed directly to projects was billed to an overhead account, or general and administrative (G&A). These overhead expenses were then distributed over the projects by allocating center costs proportionally. By enforcing these procedures, the Full Cost program, as operationalized through the ERP system, was expected to be a key factor in providing the appropriate accountability and visibility necessary for Congressional control of the agency. As Full Cost was entirely based on the standardized structure of the new ERP system with regard to time and budget reporting, this standardization was intended to ensure fairness in internal resource distribution.

## Full Cost and dressage

The Congressional efforts to control NASA’s financial management practices contain elements that are reminiscent of Foucault’s notion of dressage and the four principles of the dispositif through which it materializes: (1) art of distributions; (2) organization of geneses; (3) composition of forces; and (4) control of activity. Next, we will examine the organizational control associated with the Full Cost program and the way the four principles were manifest in the program. We will further observe how the principles played out during the implementation of the program’s primary technological apparatus – the ERP system – and suggest that the control that they yielded was, to a significant extent demonstrative.

## Full Cost as demonstrative control

NASA’s matrix organizational structure coupled with its multiple and disjointed center-specific financial management systems and practices have made the agency unable to properly account for its finances. At the time of the research, for example, the PriceWaterhouse Coopers (2004) audit indicated that NASA could not document \$565 billion in adjustments. This deficiency in accounting for its finances, however, appears not to have had an effect on the continued funding of the agency, as NASA is quite popular among the U.S. public and a fiercely defended jewel in the districts of many powerful U.S. legislators. Even though the PriceWaterhouse Coopers audit findings were announced on the same day as the Senate budget hearings for NASA’s 2005 budget, the news did not impact the approval of the 2005 budget (Frieswick, 2004).

Some lack of financial accountability was also evident in a few of NASA’s enterprises. For example, the U.S. GAO, which audits the space program annually, indicated in its 2005 report that since 2001 ‘NASA remains unable to provide reliable and accurate detailed support for the amounts obligated in all years against the spending limits. As such, GAO is unable to verify the amounts charged against the ISS [international space station] and shuttle spending limits y ’ (GAO, 2005, p. 18). However, the program continued to be funded, and the 2004 President’s ‘New Vision for Space Exploration Program’ involved additional funding.

In each of the GAO’s reports, the implementation of an agency-wide ERP system and its associated Full Cost program were seen as vehicles to tighten the scrutiny of NASA’s financial management. The ERP system was intended to standardize the agency’s financial management practices by imposing a unified set of procedures that were to be applied across its 10 centers. These procedures were intended to distribute the agency’s financial resources strictly through its multiple strategic enterprises and their related programs and projects.

Although much of the implementation of the ERP system went according to plan, the application of Full Cost was rife with problems (GAO, 2003, 2004, 2005; Frieswick, 2004). Centers were inconsistent in their interpretation of Full Cost standards, which led to a variety of unintended consequences including the artificial bloating of certain budgetary pools, as well as the incentives to employ contract labor in lieu of civil servants for projects. Yet, according to a Presidential rating system of Congressional projects, the ERP system was hailed a success and given a ‘green light’. Some of our informants indicated that this green light was itself designated as a goal by the implementation team and that many of the complex organizational and technical issues were left to be ironed-out after the project has received this green light. One manager at NASA commented that Sean O’Keefe, NASA’s administrator at the time, was unaware of the technical issues associated with the implementation process, as he was buffered from it by his ‘lieutenants’ who recognized the political importance of having the system up and running:

In other words, we find the presence of demonstrative control that is associated with the Full Cost implementation and the requirement for budgetary conformity.

## Art of distributions

The Full Cost initiative, through the ERP implementation, was intended to standardize the agency’s financial management practices by imposing a unified set of procedures that were to be implemented across its 10 centers. The procedures related to the Full Cost initiative were aimed at distributing the agency’s financial resources strictly through its multiple strategic enterprises and their related programs and projects. Essentially, the enterprise system was expected to enable the accountability necessary for Full Cost to be implemented effectively throughout the organization by locating individuals and their implements of work strictly within budgetary limits. This budgeting system reinforced the allocation of researchers, engineers, and other individuals into specific social spaces (projects) and physical spaces (centers) through the tracking of budgetary funds across centers, departments, and work groups via the enterprise system. Individuals and the specialized facilities within which they performed their activities were to be categorized together with the enterprise budgets that benefited from their activity. If a number of enterprises benefited from it, then the budget should be spread proportionally across enterprises. Activities that did not apply directly to a specific enterprise were expected to be discontinued.

While the promise of a neat budgetary categorization scheme seemed straightforward, achieving this in practice proved to be a difficult task. For instance, NASA’s goals are subject to frequent changes, often yearly, whereas the fundamental research necessary for NASA’s radical innovation may require decades to come to fruition. The example of ‘magnetic suspension’ illustrates this idea. The work of the people who dealt with magnetic suspension did not apply to any active research projects, yet the researchers and their managers felt that this capability will one day be beneficial to NASA. As one researcher indicated: ‘using that particular technology may not be something that’s looked upon as having an immediate impact or whatever, but yet still people see that in the long term it could be’ (Researcher F, pp. 3–4). Thus, with the support of their management, the researchers evaded strict categorization and continued to move the allocation of magnetic suspension work around between different funded projects within the Center to keep their research going.

While a number of informants indicated that Full Cost limited. to some extent. the tendency of researchers to ‘play in the sandbox’ and engage in research that is not of immediate value to the agency, a salient and repeated theme in the interviews was that the reason the Agency exists is to fill these long-term research needs that are not realistic in the private sphere. In such cases, researchers and their managers were often successful in staying away from being strictly allocated to physical (enterprises) and social spaces (projects).

## Organization of geneses

To ensure the allocation of individuals, activities, and resources into strategic enterprises and their affiliated programs, controlling the timing of budgetary outlays was deemed necessary. This occurs on two levels – that of an overall agency, and that of specific projects. On both levels, funds are allocated and expected to be spent in even distributions in accordance with calendar time.

On an agency level, multi-year program budget estimates are spread across multiple years and even decades. In the case of engineering-related tasks, such as vehicle design and launch, program progression is intended to culminate in the delivery of a specific outcome at a given point in time. Often the overall time frames of the budgets are imposed externally, and are politically motivated.<sup>7</sup> In enterprises relating more with pure science, such as space or materials research, goals are more equivocal, but are expected to progressively build on past work and result in scientific publications. In either case, time frames span years. Given the discontinuous and unpredictable nature of scientific research, actual expenditures can fall short or exceed estimates in any given time period.

On a project level budgets are parsed in equivalent monthly and weekly dollars. The expectation inherent in the Full Cost approach is that these funds can be evenly distributed across time frames. Project managers are required to continually monitor projects and report any deviations, yet often deviations are not evident on a week-to-week basis, as one project manager indicates:

Agency management y says they want to come in this Tuesday and status a program, and on Thursday, they want to status it again to see are we are improving well from Tuesday to Thursday, program level at HQ may find that the cost went from an under-cost condition on Tuesday to an over-cost condition on Thursday. Well, why did that happen? Could be that a major program problem came up. More likely it could be that on Tuesday, the contractor 533 [invoice] didn’t come in, but by Thursday it came in. (Project Manager E, pp. 2–26)

From the agency management’s point of view, having a remaining budget, as well as going over-budget, is a continuing problem. However, this problem is often addressed informally, outside the scope of the enterprise system and the requirements dictated by the Full Cost initiative. One Center manager appointed an administrative person to reconcile the budgetary impacts of the various programs using an off-line spreadsheet to keep budgets looking tidy and controlled in the enterprise system.

If you’re the head of an office and you’re running low on money, you can come to the Budget Meeting and say ‘Look, I need this money’ and I can say ‘Well, I know so and so has it and he’s looking for travel money y Each month I reconcile it back. So you can cut deals among each other or I can reallocate money, and my boss has given me pretty much full authority to do what it takes to get it done. (Administrative Project Support A, pp. 2–9)

Thus, the time-based allocation of funds inherent in Full Cost does not appear to match the unpredictability and fluctuating temporal rhythms of discontinuous research from the agency level down to the level of specific projects. In these cases, managers respond by decoupling the actual expenditures from the data in the system, indicating that the control afforded by such budgetary timing is more demonstrative than productive in nature.

## Composition of forces

In order to distribute individuals across enterprises in accordance with Full Cost, researchers and other resources are typically viewed in the eyes of NASA’s management as mobile, modular, and adaptable assets. If an enterprise or center has extra researchers or capabilities, and another center needs these researchers or capabilities, then those individuals and capabilities should be able to be utilized by the enterprise in question. Individuals should therefore be able to be retrained, if necessary, to perform the new tasks that are required by the enterprises that need them.

So if, for instance, we have a lot of money to devote towards the new space exploration initiative, and funding for aeronautics is going down, then we might find ourselves with people who have been working in the aeronautics area that we don’t have enough funding to cover, and we’ll have a surplus of funding in space and we need more people. Fortunately y in some cases, a minimal amount of retraining you can redirect the people to do gainful research in a completely different area y (Research Manager A, pp. 2–3)

The assumed mobility of researchers and the reconfigurabilty of inter-personal associations and work processes have proven to be a challenge in practice. Several factors have made the movement of labor across Centers difficult and led to a bloated overhead labor pool, which runs counter to the purpose of Full Cost. First, eliminating civil servants from the labor pool is extremely difficult even when they do not directly contribute to any project. In such cases, the costs associated with maintaining these workers are billed to enterprises indirectly through the proportional G&A expense associated with the relevant center’s overhead, resulting in an oversized labor pool.

Second, there are often barriers to acquiring researchers across centers. External contract labor is actually less expensive than civil servant labor according to Full Cost. Therefore, there is little incentive for centers to hire civil servant labor from other centers for funded projects, when they can readily hire outside contractors. Additionally, centers compete for resources, and therefore do not want to relinquish individuals with specialized knowledge in potentially valuable areas.

Thus, the control associated with this depersonalized mechanistic view of the organization has apparently not been attained within the context of NASA's Full Cost. and any structures in accordance with this vision of the organization appear to be, at least, partially demonstrative, as their operationalization requires incentives (hiring civil servants from other centers) and capabilities (letting go of unneeded labor) that are not in alignment with results of the intended control.

## Control of activity

The Full Cost initiative aimed to tighten control over employees’ actions by increasing the specificity of employee activity reporting to the level of minute tasks. Through the ERP system, administrators aspired to control the way individuals and resources were used within the different enterprises, which involved time reporting. For example, researchers were expected to report the amount of time spent on each project task, and any work not directly attributable to an enterprise program or project was reported to a catchall G&A category.

Because many of the capabilities borne of pure research might be applicable to a number of enterprises, often researchers would ‘spin’ their work to fit to funded enterprises. If those funds dried up, researcher groups would attempt to sell their services to another enterprise and continue doing largely the same work. Further, there was often question as to whether the work belonged in G&A or whether it was attributable to an enterprise:

Where you ask employees to charge all their time to a project and they push back saying, well the time we spent at a branch meeting, we can’t charge that to a particular project. I say, spread it across y it may not directly be in support of a project, but it indirectly supports it, it makes you a more knowledgeable person about the things that are happening at the center. So [there are] some benefits to a project. (Research Manager B, pp. 2–19)

Individuals were particularly concerned with attributing time that involved work that was not directly attributable to enterprises and their affiliated projects. Another issue that complicated time reporting has to do with that researchers were not always consistent in the way they allocated their time. As one researcher indicated, sometimes they lump all overhead activity into one project or ‘if they spread it out, they may not spread it evenly’ (Researcher F, pp. 3–4).

Thus, the visibility required for the control of activities was quite problematic. Although the ERP system did present a level of standardization and granularity that enabled productive control, his control was not allencompassing and often demonstrative in nature.

## Discussion

It certainly can be argued that by increasing control over organizational activities enterprise systems can support organizational productivity and efficiency (e.g., Weill et al., 2002; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; etc.). However, our research into NASA’s ERP implementation and the associated Full Cost program indicates that an enterprise system may also embody elements of demonstrative control which does not support organizational productivity but rather serves to advance the appearance of a neatly ordered organization. Such control is well captured in Foucault’s usage of the term dressage. Unlike functional control that is exercised for corrective purposes and intended to make work processes more efficient or effective, demonstrative control, as reflected in the notion of dressage, focuses on the public display of compliance. Therefore, it requires those who are controlled to perform their dictated scripts in a way that would be readily visible to others. The appearance of compliance to the apparatuses of control distributed throughout the organization is, therefore, a critical factor in its application, as was demonstrated in NASA’s case.

## The two faces of dressage

Our analysis of NASA’s case, in addition to demonstrating the centrality of demonstrative control to the implementation of the ERP system, illustrates that such control is often associated with employees’ demonstrative compliance: employees’ responses to control imperatives that are intended largely to satisfy the demand for control by exhibiting public compliance, but make little direct contribution to the execution of work tasks. Thus, there appear to be two faces to dressage – the demonstrative control we name ‘dressage-as-control’ and the resulting demonstrative compliance, or ‘dressage-as-response’.

On the one hand, dressage-as-control represents a relentless pressure that originates from a centralized source of authority for an ongoing obedience on the part of the members of an organized social system. This pressure, as reflected in the four principles of the dispositif, demands that individuals conduct themselves according to a strict set of rules in a manner that leaves no room for independent reflection or action. On the other hand, the very demand for obdurate observable behavioral obedience enables the creation of a space in which individuals can express the organic logic of their local social ecology. In its ideal form, the dispositif enforces the exercise of performative obedience, which encapsulates the full range of behavioral possibilities of members of a population. However, for the most part, the dispositif has only limited ability to control the emotions or intents that motivate such behaviors. Although Foucault’s ideal type may characterize the dispositif in a state of perfection where such control will no longer be needed because the individual’s psyche has already been fully disciplined and tamed, this is the case only in extreme situations. More commonly, individuals are able to perform certain acts in ways that may appear compliant yet attach meanings to their behavior that deviate from the requirements set up by the dispositif. Further, the dispositif concerns itself with making sure that adherence is kept to a set of prescribed activity scripts and that the expected outcomes of these scripts are achieved. The most important outcome of the activity scripts, however, is not their productive effect but rather their immaculate execution (i.e. control for the sake control). Therefore, as long as this is accomplished, there remains a possibility for individuals to engage in alternative scripts that do not conflict with the ones that have been established by the dispositif. We call this dressageas-response.

Although researchers have not identified the phenomenon of dressage in the context of enterprise systems, similar concepts have been discussed in a variety of contexts, often fleetingly. For example, the notion of ‘gaming the system’ arises in economic discussions of agency and incentive theory, where agents frame their work to better align with incentives (Courtey & Marschke, 2004). March & Olsen (1976) list various reasons for formal decision processes, including ‘an occasion for executing standard operating procedures, and fulfilling role expectations, duties, or earlier commitments, rather than sincerely participating in an effort to make and implement a decision’ (March & Olsen, 1976, p. 11). Parnas & Clements (1986) contrast the organic, iterative reality of software development with the rationalized formal structure, and argue that it is important to ‘fake’ the rationalized process. Marakas & Hornik (1996) describe a form of covert resistance to a new information technology that may lead to sabotage of the implementation process, but takes place alongside its overt acceptance.

Within the context of enterprise systems, Ciborra (2000, 2002) maintains that such systems act as infrastructures, often implemented as tools of centralized control, and can actually promote a drift of system appropriation away from its intended usage, as the new processes interact with the existing routines. Drift is caused, in part, by in situ responses to enterprise systems – what Ciborra calls derive, one example of which involves users working around the system to engage in productive activity.

Perhaps the concept most similar to dressage is Brown & Duguid’s (2000) processing, which is described as the attempt of individuals to reconcile the gap between formal routines and their actual practices. Processing ‘provides a screen between what people do and what people say they do’. ‘It helps turn unauthorized practice, however effective, into authorized routine, however inept’ (Brown & Duguid, 2000, p. 110).

The fundamental difference between dressage-as-response and these related concepts lies in Foucault’s framework of dispositif as an ideal apparatus of distributed control. Situating the phenomenon of dressage within a detailed theoretical framework yields a refined view of the phenomenon itself and helps place it in a broader context.

## Dispositif

Foucault’s framework provides the insight that control is not a monolithic concept but rather a multi-faceted one that operates on several interrelated dimensions represented in the four principles of the dispositif. As Foucault points out (1977), the principles of the dispositif are not distinct or mutually exclusive, but are interdependent and overlap to form a systemic apparatus of control. This is evident in NASA’s case and the functioning of the apparatus of control that dominates it – the ERP system and Full Cost program. NASA’s case also indicates that the four principles are hierarchically interrelated in the following way: in order for the apparatus of control to be able to locate individuals in social and physical spaces in accordance with strict budgetary constraints (art of distributions), these budgets must be systematically distributed across structured time segments (organization of geneses). The constitution of time segments, in turn, is closely associated with the parsing of the structure and rhythm of the work of individual researchers (control of activity). The resulting modularity of work and time enables the commoditization and depersonalization of research labor (composition forces). To achieve the result of a fully controlled and ordered organization, therefore, requires that the four principles are effectively combined together. As NASA’s case demonstrates, however, realizing this in practice is a difficult task as the drive for enhanced control is often mitigated by the ability of individuals and groups to create buffer zones for themselves wherein the dictated injunctions can be bypassed. A deeper examination of the principles of the dispositif and the social dimensions along which they are manifested may shed some light on the reasons for this difficulty (see Table 3).

Table 3 indicates that although the dimensions along which the principles of the dispositif are manifested are interrelated, they are very different in nature. The principle ‘art of distributions’ deals with the compartmentalization of organizations into departments, sections, and demarcated areas and focuses on the allocation of individuals into clearly delineated social and physical spaces. The principle ‘organization of geneses’ centers on the structuring of time by segmenting and cementing the temporal sequencing of people’s activities. It is therefore closely associated with the principle ‘control of activity that aims to manage individuals’ daily routines to render them more predictable, manageable, and configurable. ‘Control of activity’ therefore facilitates the principle ‘composition of forces’ that focuses on the individual’s identity and seeks to harness it for the benefit of the overall functioning of the organization by stripping it off its unique characterizing features. It can be argued that together, the different dimensions along which the principles of the dispositif are manifested combine to encompass all aspects of social experience.

The heterogeneity that characterizes the different dimensions may help explain why achieving total control in organizations is such a difficult task. Whereas a certain tactic may be suitable to enforce control along one dimension (e.g., setting up strict and detailed schedules that must be followed by individuals to control a time dimension), a different tactic may be needed to ensure control along another dimension (e.g., shaping organizational and physical architecture to control a dimension of space). The practical requisites for implementing the different tactics may be radically different and emphasize different aspects of an organization (e.g., the progression of the activities that maintain its functioning over time vs its physical and social design). Yet, in an overarching control apparatus, the different aspects of control are intended to work together in a systemic fashion and each is vital to the success of the others. Given the vastness of social experience that is embedded in the four dimensions, institutions that can effectively enforce such an allencompassing system of control are likely to be totalitarian in nature, such as Goffman’s ‘total institutions’ (Clegg et al., 2006), and not often materialize in contemporary business settings.

## Implications

## Implications for theory

In traditional management literature, management activity is thought to be important for coordination of productive activity among individuals and functional groups (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Malone & Crowson, 1994). Therefore, management control is assumed to contribute to the productivity of the organization. Rarely is the notion of control for the sake of control addressed, although it is likely a component in many rationalized, top-down programs such as an enterprise system. We do not claim that such control is the prevailing logic within an enterprise system, nor do we argue that it is necessarily part of every aspect of an enterprise system. Rather, we seek only to call attention to the possibility that it is an existing element in management practice. By drawing on the idea of dressage-as-control we emphasize the possibility that some mechanisms of control may exist largely to satisfy disciplinary requirements without contributing to productive activity and may, in fact, stand in the way of organizational productivity.

Further, we extend Foucault’s notion of dressage by articulating the notion of dressage-as-response to describe employees’ responses to demonstrative control – responses that are similarly demonstrative in nature and do not support productive work. In applying the concept of dressage-as-response to enterprise systems, we question two implicitly held assumptions made by management researchers. The first is that compliance with an enterprise system indicates compliance with the processes prescribed by the system. The notion of dressage-as-response demonstrates that both acceptance and resistance to a system can take place simultaneously and adds another dimension to discussions of user acceptance and adaptation of technology. Our paper therefore challenges the validity of those models and theories, which present technology use as a one-dimensional construct. In addition, our work highlights the activities that take place outside of a user’s interaction with a system, which adds an additional layer of complexity to discussions of technology adaptation or system-user alignment.

Another implication of Foucault’s framework comes from the four principles of the dispositif. These principles provide a refined view of the concept of control, which is often treated as one-dimensional. They constitute a preliminary classification scheme with which to approach and make sense of data, and provide a basic framework to analyze, explain and compare varied forms of control systems or different aspects of enterprise systems. Also, our work indicates that there is a systemic aspect to the four principles and suggests a potential hierarchical relationship among them.

Finally, this research joins with the voices of those commentators that question the possibility of enterprise control through all-pervading, tightly coupled infrastructures such as ERP systems (i.e., Ciborra, 2000). Where the cybernetic model of Ciborra and associates (Ciborra, 2000), for example, indicates that it is impossible to fully account for all situated action within a single integrated infrastructure, thus necessitating continuing cycles of increased control and drift, our research adds a perspective rooted in Foucault’s view of power. In emphasizing the elements of demonstrative control and compliance through a distributed framework of control apparatuses, our research further points toward the impossibility of complete, authentic, tightly coupled control in modern enterprises – where demonstrative aspects of control are inexorably intertwined with productive concerns.

## Implications for Practice

A first practical implication from applying Foucault’s framework to enterprise systems is that when implementing such a system, managers should recognize the relationship between the two faces of dressage as they look to understand the reasoning behind certain mandates, as well as anticipated employee reactions. If data from an enterprise system are used to drive strategic decisions, a managerial team would be well advised to question the validity of a good bit of the integrated, rationalized data present in the system, given the potential presence of dressage-as-response.

A second practical implication is that the exercise of managerial authority to ensure organizational productivity cannot be executed solely by enforcing control mechanisms, as these cannot guarantee complete command over the organization. As we demonstrated, employees can often find ways to circumvent control mechanisms to carry out their jobs in ways that reflect their local social ecologies while appearing to be compliant with the demands for control. Instead, we suggest that to ensure organizational productivity, managers need to maintain a delicate balance between the two faces of dressage. On the one hand managers need to make sure that some structure is developed or put in place for people to draw upon in their daily activities, and that this structure indeed promotes organizational productivity. On the other hand, this structure has to be sufficiently lenient to allow for expressions of locally situated practices by employees when they perceive these practices to support organizational productivity. Foucault’s four principles of dressage – relating to control of space, time, activity, and identity– offer managers a parsimonious framework for reflecting on this balance in identifying which dimensions of control are vital to operations, and at what level to allow the loose coupling of organizational activity from the mechanisms of control.

Also, it is important to note that ERP systems are one of the many mechanisms for control that may exist within an organizational context and exhibit patterns of dressage-as-control which breed dressage-as-response. For example, one may readily find demonstrative control evident through a distributed apparatus across diverse contexts such as process management methodologies (e.g., ISO/9000), robust computeraided software development tools (e.g., IBM’s Rational Suite – see Parnas & Clements, 1986), and within the post-Enron wave of government regulation (e.g., Sarbanes-Oxley). This research provides a new perspective on the notion of compliance in the context of such control initiatives.

## Limitations and future research

In our attempt to theorize around the notion of dressage in the context of NASA’s ERP and Full Cost implementation, we have focused on a fraction of what is, in fact, a much more complex reality of multiple interrelated modules and initiatives. For example, we have not fully addressed the authentically productive intensions and outcomes related to the enterprise system effort and the interaction of demonstrative and productive control throughout the entire effort. Further, we report on a highly institutionalized and unique government agency, where issues of legitimacy and rationalization are particularly compelling. We make no claims to direct transferability of these concepts into more organic commercial settings.

Our work suggests several paths for future research. First, ERP implementation is just one context where the notion of dressage can be examined. There is a need to examine other contexts that exhibit similar characteristics and assess the relevance of the notion of dressage in these situations. Second, the interplay between dressage-ascontrol and dressage-as-response suggests that there is an interesting recursive relationship between top-down directives and bottom-up reactions to these directives. A more systematic examination of this relationship is required not only in the context of ERP implementation, but also in other contexts. Finally, we proposed that the four principles of the dispositif may be hierarchically interrelated. This proposition warrants further inquiry to evaluate possible relationships among the different principles. Such an inquiry could contribute to the generation and testing of generalizable, measurable propositions relating to the relationships among the four principles and their composition across a variety of contexts.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we drew on Foucault’s concept of dressage to address the complexities that can arise when enterprise systems are implemented in organizations. Our examination of NASA’s ERP and Full Cost implementation revealed that there often exists a gap between the publically displayed image of the process and its outcomes and actual everyday activities. At NASA, both system implementers and system users worked toward creating a neat representation of the implementation process and the reactions to it although, in actuality, things were much messier. While managers, administrators, and researchers spent significant amounts of time and resources to maintain an orderly representation of the organization, these efforts did not contribute to organizational productivity. We make sense of this phenomenon in terms of a relationship between ‘dressage-as-control’ and ‘dressage-as-response’ and situate it in a parsimonious framework of control apparatuses, in an effort to arm future research and practice in dealing with issues relating to demonstrative control.
