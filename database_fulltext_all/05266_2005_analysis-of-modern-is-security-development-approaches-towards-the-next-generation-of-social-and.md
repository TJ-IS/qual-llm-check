# Analysis of modern IS security development approaches: towards the next generation of social and adaptable ISS methods

Mikko T. Siponen

Department of Information Processing Science, University of Oulu, P.O. Box 3000, 90014 Oulu, Finland

Received 21 January 2004; received in revised form 16 January 2004; accepted 19 November 2004

## Abstract

Various modern approaches to Information Systems Security (ISS) development, influenced, e.g., by information systems (IS) development methods, have been presented. While we see these approaches as serious attempts to improve ISS, they have not received much attention in the literature. One reason for this is that these methods have been developed by scholars from diferent research traditions and disciplines. This article first identifies the disciplines and research communities which underlie the modern ISS approaches. Second, the article reveals the assumptions behind these modern approaches. Finally, the article places these ISS approaches in a five-generational classification. It is argued that the extant ISS methods reside on the first four generations, and future ISS methods should move towards the fifth generation, social and adaptable (empirically grounded) ISS methods.  2004 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

The functioning of modern society is increasingly reliant on computers and global networks. In such a society, ISS security becomes an important issue. Security breaches not only cause losses to organizations (e.g., by stopping organizations from trading) but security breaches, such as the violation of privacy (e.g., stealing of credit card or health information), can also have a direct impact on ordinary computer users well-being. Therefore, it is important that systems are properly secured from the outset (Baskerville, 1992; McDermott & Fox, 1999).

Unfortunately, IS development methods lack security features (Baskerville, 1993; Booysen & Elof, 1995). To overcome this problem, several stand-alone ISS methods have been proposed. These have been classified into three generations, from firstgeneration checklist methods to third generation logical modeling (Baskerville, 1993). During the last 10 years, several third ‘‘logical modeling’’ or higher generation ISS approaches have been produced (Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001). However, these modern ISS approaches have received little attention in either the ISS literature or practice. The discussion has largely got bogged down on the topic of first-generation methods, such as ISS checklists and management standards (Elof & von Solms, 2000a; von Solms, 1999).

One reason why these various modern ISS methods are poorly known is that they have been developed by research groups operating under diferent paradigms often unmindful of each others work. Recognition of these paradigms along with the research communities whence the alternative ISS approaches derive can help us to make sense the myriad of modern ISS methods and understand their underlying differences. Although, ISS methods are tools, underlying them are implicit assumptions and features that guide or set limits to their use. It is therefore important to make these underlying assumptions explicit. In order to do this, this paper analyzes four such underlying features (research objectives, the organizational role of ISS, research approaches used and applicability to IS development). Finally, by uncovering this information, and placing it in the context of the generational classification of ISS methods by Baskerville (1993), we are able to lay down normative guidelines for the next generation of ISS methods. The results of the study have implications for (1) ISS research, (2) ISS practice and (3) ISS education.

Expected contribution to ISS research. First, since there are diferent views on how to develop (secure) IS, it is important that ISS method developers and scholars are aware of the diferent assumptions underlying diferent methods (Hirschheim, Klein, & Lyytinen, 1995). Second, due to the relative isolation of the existing ISS approaches, a comparative study would appear to be as valuable as the addition of yet another new method (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Namely, improvements in ISS methods can only take place when we a have full understanding of the existing methods and their limits. Finally, this study points to areas where such improvements are most urgently needed.

Expected contribution to ISS practice. Information on the diferences as well as the possible strengths and weaknesses of the existing ISS approaches are relevant for practitioners. Such information helps practitioners avoid becoming lost in the ISS methods jungle.

Expected contribution to ISS education. While students should acquire a knowledge of alternative ISS methods in an ISS course, only few ISS methods can be studied within such a course. The classification of ISS methods into five modern ISS paradigms is one resolution of this problem (cf., Iivari, Hirschheim, & Klein, 2001): it is easier to study five ISS paradigms and selected ISS approaches within these paradigms than 17 ISS approaches. In this way, by only learning five ISS paradigms, students can obtain a grasp of the range of diferent ISS approaches (via the ISS paradigms).

## 2. Selection of modern ISS approaches and their paradigms

## 2.1. Selection of modern ISS approaches

Baskerville (1988, 1993) classified ISS methods into three generations. Common to the first-generation of checklist methods is the selection of components from a checklist or a security management standard. The second generation encompasses mechanistic engineering methods, while the third generation entails logical modeling methods that focus on abstracting ISS problems (Baskerville, 1993, p. 379). The first and second generation ISS methods can be labelled naturalistic-mechanistic. The first and second generation ISS approaches seek to determine what can be done with the help of the technical solutions available (Baskerville, 1993). Philosophically, they violate Humes law ‘‘no ought from an is’’ (Hare, 1981). Proponents of the first and second generation ISS design approaches do precisely this, i.e., infer ‘‘ought’’ (what organizations should do) from ‘‘is’’ (what it is possible to do, or what exists). The first and second generation approaches present general, even universal, ISS imperatives founded on the observation of existing techniques/practices. Hence, the first generation approaches deserve the label naturalistic, on account of the ‘‘is from ought’’ inference. The second generation methods difer from those of the first generation by recognizing organizations ISS requirements and seeking to take these into account, for example with the help of control points (Baskerville, 1993). The term mechanistic captures the emphasis on the functional, technical and natural science attitude to ISS design in these methods and their neglect of the social nature of organizations (Baskerville, 1993; Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001). A notable diference between the first/second generations and the third and subsequent generations, is that the later generations do not attempt to derive ‘‘ought from is’’. That is, the later generations (modern ISS approaches) take organizations security requirements as their point of departure, rather than substitute for the organizations unique information security requirements a generic checklist and standard produced by outside ISS experts. We may call such approaches from the third generation onwards modern ISS approaches. Since Baskervilles (1993) classification, several such modern ISS methods have been proposed. This paper concentrates on these modern ISS approaches (i.e., ISS methods which take into account the fact that organizations may have unique ISS requirements, and that awareness of discovering these requirements should be the point of departure of ISS development).

According to Webster and Watson (2002, p. XV–XVI), a good review article is one that does not confine itself to methods originating in a certain geographical region and does not concentrate on premier journals only, ignoring articles presented in conferences. Therefore, to ensure that all the diferent modern ISS approaches would be covered, we analyzed IS journals and conferences and ISS-oriented journals (information management and computer security, IS security, computers and security) and conferences (e.g., IFIP annual TC11 security conference) as well as ACM and IEEE digital libraries to find articles that met the criteria of a modern ISS approach. Ultimately, we found 17 individual ISS methods that can be classified into five ISS paradigms. In this paper, ISS paradigms are simply sets or classes of ISS methods which have common key ideas or goals with respect to the development of secure IS. ISS approach and ISS method are used as synonyms to describe individual ISS methods within these five ISS paradigms. These paradigms, including the respective ISS approaches within these paradigms, and their origins, are discussed next.

## 2.2. Research communities and the paradigms of modern ISS approaches

The origins of the diferent ISS approaches can be traced back to research communities and disciplines. A research community consists of diferent sets of researchers sharing a certain orientation, whether owed to their education (e.g., disciplines in Fig. 1) or personal/work experience. First, proceeding from the bottom up, modern ISS approaches can be traced back to four ‘‘information security research communities’’ (Fig. 1): IS/MIS security, computer security, database security and cryptology.

## 2.2.1. Information security research communities

The four information security research communities can be further traced back to four disciplines: IS/Management Information Systems (MIS), software engineering, computer science and mathematics (Fig. 1). Cryptology is a part of mathematics, IS/ MIS security scholars are part of the IS/MIS community (or at least influenced by the contributions in the field of IS), while computer and database security scientists typically have a computer science or mathematical background. At any event, computer science relies on mathematics (McDermid & Bennett, 1999; OLeary, 1997). There is disagreement as to whether computer science and software engineering are separate disciplines (Werth & Werth, 1991, p. 355). For example, Denning et al. (1989) regard software engineering as a sub-field of computer science. In turn, McDermid and Bennett (1999, p. 179) and Parnas (1999) insist that software engineering should be seen as a diferent discipline from that of computer science. For McDermid and Bennett, computer science, which aims at contributing to scientific knowledge, is more theoretical and independent of practical applications than software engineering, which is strongly driven by problems of developing software in practice (McDermid & Bennett, 1999, p. 179; cf., Boehm, 1976, p. 1226). Parnas (1999) agrees: computer science is about science, while software engineering is practical-oriented engineering. We find this clarification by McDermid and Bennett (1999) and Parnas (1999) useful in understanding the influence computer science and software engineering has had on certain modern ISS approaches; hence we use it.

The computer science community has a positivist orientation, i.e., the necessity of applying natural scientific method in studying computing (Denning et al., 1989; Hirschheim, 1985). Mainstream software engineers also stress positivism (e.g., Basili & Lanubile, 1999; Boehm, 1976; Humphrey, 1988), although a few software engineering thinkers disagree with this view (Pfleeger, 1999). Accordingly, software engineering is classified as belonging to the domains of positivism and interpretivism. Also, while software engineers have also employed ideas from the social sciences (Sharp, Robinson, & Woodman, 2000), software engineering difers from the field of IS/ MIS (Davis, 1999) in the sense that the IS/MIS community also takes into account social and organizational aspects (Baskerville, 1988; Hirschheim et al., 1995) and uses a variety of research approaches, including those common to the social sciences.

The background of database security can be traced back to computer science (including information modeling). Even though database security can be seen as part of computer security, the database security community difers from the computer security community in that the former has favoured security issues specific to databases, while the latter has concerned itself with the security aspects of computers in general, looking at technical means only.

## 2.2.2. Modern ISS approaches in terms of five paradigms

The viable and survivable IS paradigm is formed by the ISS methods of two research groups, who were unaware of each others work at the time: the survivable IS approach by Karya, Kokolakis, and Kiountouzis (2001) and the viable system model by Hutchinson and Warren (2000). Both approaches have their roots in Beers viable system model, which consists of five systemic functions which need to be performed in order for an organization to be viable and survivable: the operational element, co-ordination, control, intelligence and identity. The viable system model by

Hutchinson and Warren (2000) investigates how successful attacks can be seen in the light of the five functions, whereas the survivable IS approach by Karya et al. (2001) aims at a complete ISS design method. The survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) views the viable and survivable IS approach as a paradigm shift in ISS thinking. In their opinion consideration should be shifted from secure systems to viable and survivable IS, i.e., systems which stress survivability in future attacks. The view of the viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) is not so radical; Hutchinson and Warren merely propose yet another ISS method without arguing for its preeminence over alternative ways of ISS thinking.

The security-modified IS development paradigm includes the logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988, 1989), the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995), the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996), the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998). These approaches are influenced by the work done in the domain of IS/MIS security. The spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) is also influenced by software development methods in the discipline of software engineering (Fig. 1).

Even though IS scholars have adopted both positivist (e.g., Straub, 1989) and interpretive (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1996) research strategies, we see the ISS approaches within the security-modified IS development paradigm influenced by IS (security community) as interpretive rather than positivist. Therefore, the approaches with the security-modified IS development paradigm are classified within the domain of interpretivism (Fig. 1). Within the security-modified IS development paradigm, the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) is interpreted as lying on the border between positivism and interpretivism. ISS planning methodology, on the one hand, has adopted an interpretive research strategy, including action research, which we see as implying interpretivism using the positivism/interpretivism classification employed in this study. On the other hand, their theoretical frameworks stem from quantitative studies (e.g., Straub, 1990) that are interpreted as positivist.

The logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988, 1989) is related to structural IS development methods. It focuses on logical design, and processes in order to add the necessary controls into them. The cross-references of these controls are incorporated into a Data Dictionary, which lists threat classes and their respective controls. The logical control approach consists of five phases: (1) identify entities; (2) identify risks; (3) identify controls; (4) evaluate; and (5) implement (Baskerville, 1988). The first phase, identify entities, is carried out by following the process of structural analysis (an IS development method). The second and third stages are not found in structural IS development methods, and should thus be incorporated into those methods (Baskerville, 1988, p. 93).

Following the idea of the logical control approach, the automated secure system development method, referred to in this study as the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) aims to integrate ISS with IS development. In this way security aspects can be taken into consideration in the early stages of IS development. This approach utilizes Boehms (1988) spiral model to integrate ISS activities into the IS development process (hence, the name spiral approach). This goal is achieved by following the ISS spiral, paralleling that of Boehm. The ISS spiral is divided into four quadrants (Booysen & Elof, 1995, p. 259): (1) determine objectives, alternatives and constraints; (2) evaluate alternatives; identify and resolve risks; (3) develop, and verify the next-level product; (4) and plan the next phases.

Whereas the spiral (Booysen & Elof, 1995) and the logical control (Baskerville, 1988, 1989) approaches reflect structural IS development thinking, the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996) adopts a softer perspective drawn from the Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland, 1985). While the spiral and the logical control approaches focus on technical issues such as the modeling of processes, the virtual methodology emphasizes organizational, cultural and human issues in developing ISS. For this purpose, the virtual methodology provides three models: the organizational, IS and risk models. The organizational model defines authorized and unauthorized (e.g., a hacker breaking into a system) relations, the IS models specify the access rights of user groups, and the risk model describes the potential risks.

At the same time as the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996) was developed, a soft approach to the planning of ISS was crafted which was also based on Soft System Methodology (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000). Like the virtual methodology, this approach also focuses on the human component of security. This approach emphasizes the role of user participation in ISS development in order to (1) utilize the users knowledge of the activities of the organization; and (2) increase users ISS awareness and commitment towards ISS solutions (users are able to feel that they are ‘‘owners’’ of the ISS solutions).

The ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) consists of three elements: a security risk planning model, an awareness program and a countermeasure matrix. The five stages of the security planning model – (i) recognition of security problems, (ii) risk analysis, (iii) generation of alternatives, (iv) decisions and (v) implementation – is the overall process to be followed (Straub & Welke, 1998, p. 450, 454). The awareness program and countermeasure matrix are integrated into these stages (p. 454).

The responsibility modeling paradigm consists of four approaches: the approach by Strens and Dobson (1993), the semantic responsibility analysis approach by Backhouse and Dhillon (1996), the abuse case approach by McDermott and Fox (1999), and the task-based authorization approach by Thomas and Sandhu (1994). All these approaches take the view that security requirements can be found by scrutinizing the job responsibilities in organizations; hence, the name responsibility modeling. The use of responsibility as a unit of analysis for ISS has been recognized in both the computer science (Strens & Dobson, 1993; Thomas & Sandhu, 1994; McDermott & Fox, 1999) and IS communities (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996).

The approach by Strens and Dobson (1993) holds that the context in which ISS security requirements arise is poorly understood. In their view paying attention to responsibilities solves the problem. According to Strens and Dobson (1993, p. 146), ISS requirements, such as need-to-do and need-to-know, can be drawn from job responsibilities. Need-to-do refers to actions that employees need to take in order to do their work. Need-to-know means that users should only be allowed to access those assets (e.g., files) in organizations that they need to know to be able to carry out their duties (need-to-do).

In the same vein as the approach by Strens and Dobson (1993), the semantic responsibility analysis approach by Backhouse and Dhillon (1996) also proposes that security requirements should be inferred from work responsibilities. Backhouse and Dhillon (1996) attempt to identify the relevant agents and the patterns of behavior and communication that subsist between these agents by analyzing their responsibilities in organizations (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996, p. 5). Agents are defined as those parties that can have a responsibility, including humans, organizations and groups.

McDermott and Fox (1999) derive the idea of the abuse case from use cases (commonly used in IS and software development methods to capture system requirements). While the use cases model authorized use scenarios (e.g., an ofice employee querying a database), the abuse case describe unauthorized scenarios, such as a hacker breaking into an IS, against which the IS should be protected.

The task-based authorization approach by Thomas and Sandhu (1994) originates out of the limitations of the extant formal computer security access control models. For Thomas and Sandhu, access control models represent a centralized notion of computing, which is inadequate view from an overall organizational standpoint. To improve this situation, they construct a model of task-based authorizations (requiring formalization in later research). A better authorization model can be achieved by analyzing the responsibility and authorization structures in an organization.

The approaches within the responsibility modeling paradigm are associated with IS (security), software engineering and computer (science) security (Fig. 1). The semantic responsibility analysis of Backhouse and Dhillon (1996) can be associated with the IS/MIS community, while the abuse case approach of McDermott and Fox (1999) under the responsibility modeling paradigm is influenced by software engineering methods (use cases in particular). Finally, the abuse case approach of McDermott and Fox (1999) and the task-based authorization approach of Thomas and Sandhu (1994) within the responsibility paradigm are influenced by the computer security and computer science communities (Fig. 1). One of the hallmarks of computer security research is the use of formalism (Landwehr, 1981). The influence of formalism on the task-based authorization (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) and the abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999) approaches is central. First, they both demand formal proofs. Also, these two responsibility modeling approaches implicitly favour falsificationism. By falsificationism is meant theories that are preliminary rather than final, and that must be expressed explicitly (Popper, 1970). This view, of achieving explicitness through the use of formalism, can be seen in the task-based authorization (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) and the abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999) approaches within the responsibility modeling paradigm.

The business process paradigm consists of two ISS approaches: an approach by Herrmann and Pernul (1999) and the fair and secure electronic markets approach by Ro¨hm and Pernul (2000). Common to the ISS approaches with the business process paradigm is an attempt to construct a modeling notation for describing security constraints in business process models (business process models are interpreted as falling within the domain of IS research issues). Business process approaches have been developed within the same research group. Herrmann and Pernul (1999) suggest a high-level modeling notation for describing the security, mainly confidentiality and integrity, requirements for business processes. Their framework includes Entity Relationship Diagrams to model relevant entities and their relationships, as well as Data Flow Diagrams to model processes. The fair and secure electronic markets approach (Ro¨hm & Pernul, 2000) sketches an overall infrastructure for ensuring the security and fairness of market transactions. Business process approaches have also been interpretive in orientation, and they are influenced by the IS community (Fig. 1).

The information modeling paradigm has considered the security aspects from the information and data modeling point of view, with the aim of extending the existing research in Database security. Hence, the reference to information modeling (cf., Hirschheim et al., 1995) and the influence from Database security (Fig. 1). Studies in this domain include the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992), the objectoriented security semantics (Ellmer, Pernul, & Kappel, 1995), the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994), and the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul, Tjoa, & Winiwarter, 1998) approaches. The main incentive for developing approaches within the information modeling paradigm sprang from Smiths (1989) work. Smith proposed a notation to model ‘‘security relevant data semantics’’ in a database context (p. 1195). The underlying philosophy of science of the information modeling paradigm would appear to be positivism. Their research methodological orientation, explicit modeling and formalizing of the technical system is borrowed from the natural sciences.

## 3. The framework for the analysis

Any review of the ISS literature calls for an appropriate conceptual framework with which to assess the scope of existing ISS approaches. Fortunately, several such tried and tested candidate frameworks already exist. Dhillon and Backhouse (2001), Hirschheim et al. (1995, 1996) and Iivari et al. (2001) have analyzed IS or ISS methods in the light of four sociological paradigms by Burrell and Morgan (1979). In recognition of the critique leveled at the concept of the four paradigms by Burrell and Morgan, namely that the paradigms are mutually exclusive so that any given IS or ISS method can only be subsumed under a single paradigm (cf., Burrell & Morgan, 1979, p. 25; Chua, 1986; Iivari, 1991, p. 255, 258), alternative frameworks have been developed. <sup>1</sup> These include research methods, organizational role of IS, and research objectives (Iivari & Hirschheim, 1996; Iivari, 1991; Iivari, Hirschheim, & Klein, 1998). In contrast to the four paradigms by Burrell and Morgan, these viewpoints are not mutually exclusive and an IS or ISS method can in theory be characterized by more than one viewpoint (e.g., an IS method can entail more than one research objective). Hence, according to Iivari (1991, p. 258), these viewpoints ofer a richer view than the paradigmatic framework of Burrell and Morgan (1979). This is one reason for choosing research objectives, organizational role of ISS, research approaches used and applicability to IS development as the framework for this analysis (Fig. 2).

The second rationale for using these four viewpoints is that they have not been used to analyze ISS methods (in contrast to sociological paradigms, used to analyze ISS contributions in the seminal work of Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001), despite their potential to yield valuable information about ISS approaches (cf., Iivari, 1991, p. 258). A key issue in IS research is the selection and use of appropriate research methods (Galliers, 1992). The research objectives dimension shows what scientific research goals the various ISS approaches entail (e.g., whether an ISS method is aimed at controlling a phenomena or increasing our understanding of it), while the organizational role of ISS discloses the underlying assumptions of the ISS method regarding its relationship to the organization. Also important is the applicability of ISS methods to IS methods from the viewpoint of secure systems development (Baskerville, 1993). These viewpoints are discussed next.

## 3.1. What are the research objectives of diferent ISS methods?

Chua (1986) distinguished three possible research objectives or goals of science: (1) critical, (2) interpretive, and (3) means-end oriented. <sup>2</sup> The goal of critical research is ‘‘. . . the identification and removal of domination and ideological practice’’ (Chua, 1986, p. 615). Cryptonanalysis, aimed at pointing out the flaws in cryptographical systems, is an example of critical research.

Interpretive research means increasing peoples understanding of the meaning of their actions (Chua, 1986, p. 615), and it is therefore interpretive and qualitative in nature. Interpretive research can be empirical (e.g., action research) or non-empirical (e.g., historical research to interpret text by an ancient writer). The importance of interpretive research is widely advocated by historians, theologians, educationalists, social scientists, and has recently gained increased attention in the IS world (Hirschheim, 1985; Walsham, 1996; Klein & Myers, 1999). Means-end oriented research is aimed at predicting and controlling natural or social processes, or finding solutions to a concrete problem (Chua, 1986). Natural science, computer science and engineering research are typically means-end oriented, as is positivistic IS research aimed at finding causal explanations.

## 3.2. What are the organizational roles attributed to ISS?

The organizational role of ISS is concerned with the relationship between IS and the organization. The possible organizational roles of IS (security) are (a) technical, (b) socio-technical, and (c) social (Iivari & Kerola, 1983; Iivari & Hirschheim, 1996). Table 1 summarizes these views and illustrates how they respond to four ISS design concerns (ISS design priority, cause of ISS problems, ISS design success, and users impact on ISS design).

As can be seen from Table 1, the technical view regards IS as a technical artefact only. In this view, the focus in ISS development is on a technical system, social implications being at best afterthoughts (Iivari & Hirschheim, 1996, p. 555–556). Poor technical quality and user resistance are the source of ISS problems, according to the technical view. In technical view, the users preferences have no (or very little) impact on ISS design. Instead, users are directed or forced to follow the given security policy, systems features, etc. (user control).

The social view regards IS as an social-organizational system where an IS is primarily seen ‘‘as integral, constitutive part of organizational communication, control, coordination, cooperation and work arrangements and not only as a separate support system for these organizational activities’’ (Iivari & Hirschheim, 1996, p. 553). The social view emphasizes the development of organizational systems before technical matters, and in this view the key to ISS design success is socio-organizational desirability and meeting users preferences. (Table 1). Indeed, the social view values users preferences above all, and therefore users have a huge impact on ISS design.

The socio-technical view emphasizes that technical and organizational systems are equally important, and contends that the lack of fit between social and technical systems is the primary cause of ISS problems (Iivari & Hirschheim, 1996). The sociotechnical view recognizes users preferences, and if users preferences are in conflict with the security requirements, the socio-technical view attempts to find a compromise between these views (users have a moderate impact on ISS design).

## 3.3. What research approaches have been used?

The use of proper research methods are part and parcel of IS research. By discovering the underlying research methodology we are able reveal how the various ISS approaches have been developed and validated and what evidence they are based on.

There are several alternative categorizations of IS research methods, including Jenkins (1985), Galliers and Land (1987), Nunamaker, Chen, and Purdin (1991), Galliers (1992), March and Smith (1995) and Ja¨rvinen (2000). In selecting an appropriate classification from among all these diferent IS research method taxonomies, one important criterion emerged: the need for a vehicle to categorize overall research approaches or strategies, in addition to specific research methods. Such a level of abstraction is needed, since some authors do not state the research method they have used and the exact research method can not be inferred from the text. Notwithstanding, the overall research strategy (e.g., empirical research to test an ISS method) can be found from these studies.

Comparing the alternative classifications of IS research methods against this criterion, we ended in selecting those by Ja¨rvinen (2000) and Galliers (1992). Ja¨rvinens classification incorporates the concept of the research approach. The research approach taxonomy operates at a higher level of abstraction than that of research method, and thus Ja¨rvinens classification meets the criterion. In his classification, research approaches include the mathematical, conceptual-analytical, empirical theory-testing, empirical theory-creating and artefact building or evaluating research approaches. In mathematical approaches, a lemma, theorem or assertion is proved mathematically, while conceptual-analytical research is a conceptual (non-empirical) and an argumentative approach (Ja¨rvinen, 2000). In IS research, conceptual-analysis is commonly used in theory-development and review studies. Typically, in the former, a set of arguments or a conceptual-theory is advanced (e.g., papers on design theorizing by Walls, Widmeyeer, & Sawy, 1992), while in the latter, literature is analyzed or compared against some conceptual criterion or framework (e.g., as done by Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001). In positivistic IS research, a typical example of empirical theory-testing research is a quantitative survey attempting to confirm (or falsify) a theory or a set of hypotheses (see Straub, 1989). Interpretive IS research can also utilize theory-testing research, for example, to explain an IS phenomenon in the light of a theory with reference to a case. In turn, in theory-creating research, a theory, hypothesis, model or framework does not guide the research, but instead scholars attempt to create an explanation, a model, hypotheses or a theory that explains the phenomena under investigation (see Eisenhardt, 1989). Grounded theory is an example of a research method used in theory-creating research. Artefact building research approach attempts to develop an artefact, such as modeling notation (e.g., Unified Modeling Language), whilst research evaluating an artefact is aimed at validating the efectiveness or usefulness of the artefact for its intended purpose (Ja¨rvinen, 2000).

One research approach can by addressed by means of several research methods. For example, case studies or action research can be used to test a theory (e.g., theory-testing empirical research), a complete ISS method or a new notation to model some part of the IS (artefact-evaluating research approach), or find success factors in ISS development by observing practice (theory-creating empirical research).

Where possible we also identify the use of research methods, including laboratory experiments, field experiments, surveys, case studies, simulation (Galliers, 1992, p. 152–153), ethnographic method, grounded theory, action research and phenomenography (Ja¨rvinen, 2000).

## 3.4. Are the ISS approaches applicable to IS development?

The applicability to IS development (Baskerville, 1988, 1992) criterion reveals whether the ISS approaches can be integrated into IS development methods. If an ISS method cannot be integrated into IS development, this means that IS development and ISS development are separate activities. This developmental duality leads to conflicting requirements between security and the normal functionality of systems, increased costs (security is added afterwards), user resistance, security problems (since it is more dificult to add security afterwards) and malfunctions (Baskerville, 1992). For these reasons, it is imperative to perceive which ISS approaches are applicable to IS development processes (Baskerville, 1993, p. 410). Accordingly, we separate three views with respect to the applicability of ISS approaches to IS development. An ISS approach is (1) applicable to a certain (set of) IS development method or process, (2) potentially applicable, or (3) not presently applicable to an IS development method. Potentially applicable means that even though the ISS approach does not explicitly recognize the integration possibilities, we see a link or a similarities with IS development methods, making the integration possible. In a case where the ISS approach is not applicable to IS development, the approach does not ofer any hint on how the present version of the approach can be integrated into IS development methods, nor do we see any connection to the diferent phases of IS development methods.

## 4. Results of the analysis

## 4.1. Security-modified IS development paradigm

## 4.1.1. Research objectives

The primary research objective of the logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988, 1989), the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1996), the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995), the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) is means-end oriented. Additionally, the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996), the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) also encompass an interpretive research objective, while the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) entails all three research objectives.

The logical control approach criticizes the existing ISS approaches (first and second generation), since they do not merge with IS development (critical research objective): ‘‘current security methodologies, still locked in the second generation, are too narrow in scope to permit the best integration of security into information systems.’’ (Baskerville, 1988, p. 87). On these grounds, the goal of the logical control approach (Baskerville, 1989) is to improve ISS thinking by ofering a better way (than the competing approaches ofered at that time) to systemize and control ISS design by embedding it into a structured IS development process (means-end oriented research objective).

Like the logical control approach, the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) aspires to develop an ISS method that can be integrated into IS development (means-end oriented research objective). In this way, the spiral approach ensures that ISS considerations are addressed at all stages of IS development. Additionally, there is also reliance on interpretive research: ‘‘the only way to efectively assess all the information contained in an application system is to study how the information flows within the application system.’’ (Booysen & Elof, 1995, p. 258). This creates the impression that the spiral approach functions as an interpretive tool with which to understand the information flows in an IS.

The goal of the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996) is to ofer a method for building secure systems (means-end oriented objective). However, to achieve this, a strong interpretive dimension is built into the method. The purpose is to understand the diferent ISS actions and relationships with help of the three IS models.

The soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) criticizes the extant ISS approaches for not paying enough attention to the user in ISS design (a critical research interest): ‘‘the concept of user responsibility for information security is still in its infancy. [To improve this situation] a high level model [i.e., the soft approach to the planning of ISS] to encourage users to accept responsibility and ownership of information security [was presented]’’ (Armstrong, 2000, p. 20). In her articles (Armstrong, 2000; James, 1996), she also criticizes the extant approaches as technically biased, and, as can be inferred from the extract above, she advocates a change in ISS thinking towards paying more attention to the human dimension involved in ISS (critical research). For her, user participation is a key means to achieving this goal. The soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) aims to improve ISS development by providing a method that addresses such user issues (a means-end oriented research objective). Yet, the approach uses interpretive tools (e.g., rich pictures) to increase users understanding and communication in relation to security requirements (interpretive research objective).

The ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) advances a theory-based, but practical ISS model for managers use aimed at the efective minimization of ISS risks (means-end oriented research objective). Such an attempt to increase managers understanding of security management through introducing the ISS planning methodology is indicative of an interpretive research objective.

## 4.1.2. Organizational roles

These approaches within the security-modified IS development paradigm (Baskerville, 1988, 1989; Booysen & Elof, 1995; Hitchings, 1995, 1996; James, 1996; Straub & Welke, 1998) favor all three organizational roles of ISS. The technical role was implicit in the logical control (Baskerville, 1988, 1989) and the spiral (Booysen & Elof, 1995) approaches. The virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1996) entails the sociotechnical organizational role of ISS, the view of the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) lies between the technical and socio-technical roles, and the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996) favors the social role.

The organizational role of ISS of the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) is technical: the design interest (ISS design priority) is in the technical system. By crafting an ISS method that can be integrated into normal IS development, the spiral approach aims to enhance the technical quality of the system. In the same vein, poor technical quality, resulting from a lack of security methods that can be integrated into IS development methods, is seen as the main cause of ISS problems. A technical role with respect to the impact of users on ISS design is also visible in the spiral approachs aim of eliminating human errors by the use of automated CASE-tools (Booysen & Elof, 1995, p. 268). This demonstrates that users have a minimal role in ISS design.

Similarly to the spiral approach, the logical control approach of Baskerville (1988, 1989) is focused on technical systems (ISS design priority is on the technical system), in particular, on processes, aimed at improving technical quality (indicating a technical view). However, even though logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988, 1989) suggests that ISS design methods should adopt a socio-technical orientation (Baskerville, 1988, p. 72) and reports a lack of user-satisfaction considerations in ISS design (Baskerville, 1989, pp. 244, 247), we do not see these socio-technical ideas, regarding users impact on ISS, reflected in the method.

The virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995) lays equal emphasis on technical and social factors: ‘‘[this] approach does not just consider the technical factors of an IS. Other factors, such as the human issues should be equally important. . .’’ (Hitchings, 1995, p. 369); hence, the design priority is socio-technical. Virtual methodology provides a few hints that security problems arise due to lack of fit between technical and socio-technological systems: ISS ‘‘is not just a technology problem, but it also concerns people.’’ (Hitchings, 1995, p. 369). According to virtual methodology, the conceptual models and major changes in IS should be negotiated with users (Hitchings, 1995, p. 376). This implies that the key to ISS design success is a match between the technical and socio-technical systems and that users preferences and concerns are as equally recognized as technical concerns in ISS design (socio-technical view on users impact on ISS design).

The ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) lies between the technical and socio-technical views. Straub and Welke (1998, p. 442) see that security problems result from managers indiference on the importance of security; an indication of the social view. They also stress the use of ISS awareness programs and criticize the current ISS approaches as too technical, omitting the behavioural side of ISS, thereby inclining towards the socio-technical view. Finally, they see the role of ISS awareness programs as a means by which a deterrence system can be introduced into organizations: ‘‘A major reason for initiating this training [within an ISS awareness program], however, is to convince potential abusers that that the company is serious about securing its systems and will not treat intentional breaches of this security lightly.’’ (Straub & Welke, 1998, p. 445). This suggests a technical orientation in terms of the organizational role of ISS. We see this as indicating that users preferences have little impact on ISS design (technical view). We also interpret this to mean that Straub and Welke (1998) hold that user control, through the use of deterrence, is a key issue in successful ISS design (technical view).

Similar to the ISS planning methodology, the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996) advocates the use of ISS awareness programs. However, the soft approach to the planning of ISS regards awareness programs as a means towards gaining employees acceptance of ISS policy. Such user participation through ISS awareness programs (James, 1996, p. 11), and the requirement that security must be fitted to the organizations business objectives and culture, indicate a social role. That is, the design priority is on the social system; social desirability and meeting users preferences is seen as the way to ISS design success. James (1996, p. 10–11, 12) also sees the previous ‘‘over-technical’’ ISS methods, which lack user involvement, as behind the problems facing ISS (social view regarding the cause of ISS problems).

## 4.1.3. Research approaches used

All the approaches within the security-modified IS development paradigm use conceptual analysis as their preferred research approach. In addition, the logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988) employed artefact building and evaluating, the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995) utilized artefact building, while empirical theory-testing research was adopted by both the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998).

The logical control approach (Baskerville, 1989) is based on conceptual analysis and artefact building and testing. Conceptual analysis is used to scrutinize the limitations of the extant ISS approaches from the viewpoint of IS development. Artefact building is present in the attempts to sketch a security notation (an artefact) to be embedded in structural IS development methods. Artefact testing comes into play in the form of a case study assessing the usability of the logical control approach (consists mainly of a modeling notation, which is an artefact). However, the results of case studies have not been fully reported. Similarly, the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) has used conceptual analysis to develop and modify the security spiral and its phases from Boehms spiral model. The spiral approach has also utilized artefact building to construct modeling notations.

The research approaches used to develop the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995) are conceptual analysis and artefact building research. Influenced by the Soft System Methodology, conceptual analysis is used to argue for the need for a more humanistic ISS approach and to develop the virtual methodology approach. The virtual methodology uses the artefact building research approach to sketch the modeling notation. The virtual methodology has also used the artefact testing research approach ostensibly to test the virtual methodology in practice. However, no specific results of such theory-testing have been reported.

Like the virtual methodology, the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) has applied conceptual analysis to argue the need for a human-centred approach and is derived from the Soft System Methodology. The soft approach to the planning of ISS has also utilized empirical research (both theory-creating and theory-testing). First, a quantitative (n = 60) and then a qualitative study were conducted. These results were used to build an approach (theory-creating research). Second, the approach was validated empirically and redefined in a hospital environment using the action research method (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) (empirical theory-testing and theory-creating).

The ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) has employed conceptual analysis on the basis of Goodhue and Straub (1991), Nance and Straub (1988) and the argument that managers are willing to use theory-grounded ISS approaches if exposed to such approaches. Straub and Welke (1998) also utilized action research as their research method to test and validate their ISS approach in practice (empirical theory-testing).

## 4.1.4. Applicability to IS development

Two approaches within the security-modified IS development paradigm can be integrated into IS development: the logical control (Baskerville, 1989) and the spiral (Booysen & Elof, 1995) approaches. Since the logical control approach embeds its notation in Data Flow Diagrams, it can be integrated into structural IS development methods using data flow diagrams. Also, because the spiral approach adopts Boehms spiral model, it can be integrated into IS development methods which are in consistent with Boehms spiral model. At first sight, it may appear possible to integrate the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995) into structural IS development processes, since the virtual methodology has adopted ideas from structural IS development methods. However, in attempting to do this, two obstacles arise. First, the virtual methodology has developed its own notation, which difers from that of IS development methods, thereby making the integration of the virtual methodology into such methods dificult. Second, the virtual methodology ofers no guidance as to how developers can integrate the virtual methodology into IS development methods.

The soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) do not propose any means for integrating these approaches into IS development. Also, we cannot connect these approaches to IS development methods through the use of a similar notation or process. Hence, we conclude that the soft approach to the planning of ISS and the ISS planning methodology cannot be integrated into IS development methods.

## 4.2. Information modeling paradigm

## 4.2.1. Research objectives

The primary research objective of the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992), the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994), the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) and the object-oriented security semantics (Ellmer et al., 1995) approaches is means-end oriented. They all aim to provide security notations and processes which will improve the implementation of ISS, and consequently, the resulting end-product (IS or software). To start with, Pernul and Quirchmayr see that ‘‘. . .a systemic and thorough execution of the early phases of design will pay of immensely in the long term because it will significantly reduce database maintenance and redevelopment eforts.’’ (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994, p. 96). Similarly, the security constraints modeling approach (Pernul, 1992) holds that ISS design, in relation to databases in particular, is often carried out by trial and error and ‘‘It is not clear how this process [ISS design] should be decomposed into welldefined steps.’’ Pernul (1992, p. 75). By proposing an ISS method (the security constraints modeling approach), Pernul (1992) aims to fill in this gap in research (means-end oriented research objective). The view of the object-oriented security semantics approach (Ellmer et al., 1995) is similar: this approach proposes a notation aimed at conceptualizing and systematizing ISS development, which, in turn, it is believed will improve ISS design (means end-oriented research objective).

In addition to advancing a novel security-notation (means-end oriented research objective), the DFD and ER modeling approach (Pernul et al., 1998) also entail an interpretive research objective: ‘‘What can be done to handle the additional complexity [involved in mandatory access control]? How can we overcome the ambiguities identified and what can be done to better understand applications that make use of that technology [i.e., mandatory access control solutions]’’ (Pernul et al., 1998). Here Pernul et al. (1998) argue that they aim to enhance understanding of the use of access control models through their modeling notation (an interpretive research objective).

## 4.2.2. Organizational roles

The view on the organizational role of ISS development of the object-oriented security semantics (Ellmer et al., 1995) and the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) approaches is technical, while the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992) and the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) approaches lie between the technical and socio-technical view. Common to all the approaches within the information modeling paradigm, their design priority is on technical systems, ISS problems stemming from poor design and mistakes in implementation. It is these issues which these approaches aim to address by ofering modeling notations to describe security requirements and constraints in ISS development. For example, the object-oriented security semantics approach (Ellmer et al., 1995) is aimed at ‘‘improving the quality and understandability of design, [which, in turn, will lead to] higher productivity and less maintenance costs.’’ (Ellmer et al., 1995). Users have no active role in ISS design, according to the object-oriented security semantics approach (Ellmer et al., 1995). This view is also evident in the DFD and ER modeling approach (Pernul et al., 1998), which supports polyinstantiation (and the resulting need for cover stories). The planned use of polyinstantiation (cover stories) violates the autonomy of users in Kants sense by tampering with information and furnishing users with false information. The support for cover stories indicates that the organizational role of ISS with respect to the impact of users on ISS design is technical in the DFD and ER modeling approach (Pernul et al., 1998).

Both the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992) and the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) approaches imply a socio-technical view with regard to success of ISS design and the impact of users on ISS design. Similar to Pernul and Quirchmayr (1994), Pernul (1992) recognizes that an IS must ‘‘satisfy the requirements of all users and at the other hand must satisfy and enforce the security policy of the organization properly.’’ (Pernul, 1992, p. 75); implying a socio-technical view. Otherwise, the view of the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992) and the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) approaches on the organizational role of ISS is similar to that of the object-oriented security semantics (Ellmer et al., 1995) and the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) approaches (technical view). The security constraints modeling approach holds that security flaws result from errors in technical design, which the method seeks to remedy by providing a systematic modeling notation (Pernul, 1992, p. 75). Similarly, according to the data and security semantics approach (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994, p. 96), maintenance and redesign eforts are reduced through the use of their approach. This indicates that ISS problems are caused by flaws in designing technical systems (causing the need for maintenance and redesign), and that the design concern is technical. Also, both the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992) and the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) approaches focus primarily on the technical aspects of ISS modeling, again indicating that the design priority is on technical systems. We conclude that the organizational role of ISS of the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992) and the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) approaches lie between the technical and socio-technical view.

## 4.2.3. Research approaches used

The research approach used by all the approaches within the information modeling paradigm is artefact building, which is used to construct the modeling notations. The data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994), the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) and the object-oriented security semantics (Ellmer et al., 1995) approaches have also applied logical and mathematical modeling, and can therefore be classified under mathematical modeling.

## 4.2.4. Applicability to IS development

The security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992), the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) and the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) approaches utilize Data Flow Diagrams and Entity-Relationship diagrams. These notations may thus be integrated into structural methods that use Data Flow and Entity-Relationship diagrams. They do not, however, provide any guidance on how to perform such integration. Hence, we deem the integration into IS methods as potential. Of all the approaches within the information modeling paradigm, the object-oriented security semantics approach (Ellmer et al., 1998) seems to be the most dificult to integrate with IS methods, provided that its notation is not used in IS development methods (hence, we regard it as not applicable to IS development).

## 4.3. Responsibility modeling paradigm

## 4.3.1. Research objectives

The interpretive research objective is evident in the approach by Strens and Dobson (1993), and in the semantic responsibility analysis (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) and the abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999) approaches, while the task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) entails means-end oriented and critical research objectives.

Strens and Dobson (1993) propose that finding and describing work responsibil ities overcomes communicational gaps in organizations in two respects. First, responsibilities are understandable to both normal users and systems designers, thereby bridging a communication gap between users and designers. Second, the concept of work responsibility is helpful by unifying diferent models of IS; thereby, also bridging a possible communication gap between users/management and developers. Moreover, by analyzing responsibilities they argue that it is possible to ‘‘gain an understanding of the organizational context in which they [work responsibilities and respective security requirements] arise.’’ (Strens & Dobson, 1993, p. 144). This is an interpretive research objective.

The semantic responsibility analysis approach of Backhouse and Dhillon (1996) aims to understand the various ISS requirements in organizations by making these requirements explicit with the help of their modeling notation. Similarly, the abuse case approach by McDermott and Fox (1999) is intended to assist in understanding security requirements and activities in organizations: ‘‘[abuse cases] are particularly useful in communicating with users and customers during requirements analysis and may be easier to understand when a trade-of must be made between security and functionality.’’ (McDermott & Fox, 1999, p. 63). The modeling notations of the semantic responsibility analysis by Backhouse and Dhillon and the abuse case by McDermott and Fox can be understood as modeling languages which, like languages generally, are used for communication purposes (interpretive research objective).

The task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) proposes an improved model of authorization by analyzing responsibilities in organizations (means-end oriented research objective). They also entail critical research: ‘‘existing [access control] models are formulated at too low a level of abstraction to be useful for modeling organizational requirements, policy aspects...’’ (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994, p. 66). The critical view contained in their argument is that the existing research on computer security models is inadequate when it comes to the development of organization-wide policies; hence, tools for bridging this gap are needed (and the taskbased authorization approach is such a tool).

## 4.3.2. Organizational roles

The approach by Strens and Dobson (1993, p. 143), and the semantic responsibility analysis (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) and the abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999, p. 63) approaches are socio-technical (Strens & Dobson, 1993, p. 143), while the task-based authorization (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) approach entails a technical role.

For Strens and Dobson (1993), success in ISS design stems from a good fit between social and technical systems: ‘‘Design methods appropriate for technical systems cannot simply be applied to socio-technical ones, since equal consideration must be given to both human and technical issues. . .’’ (Strens & Dobson, 1993, p. 143). For Strens and Dobson, security problems originate from a gap between technical and social systems, which manifests itself as problems of language and understanding between users of the systems and system developers (Strens & Dobson, 1993, p. 143). Furthermore, the method advanced by Strens and Dobson aims to function as a boundary domain ‘‘where these two worlds [users and systems designers] can meet.’’ (Strens & Dobson, 1993, p. 143). Such an attempt to unite users and designers preferences points to a socio-technical view insofar as the users are seen as having an impact on ISS design.

The abuses case approach stresses the importance of technical quality in minimizing ISS design problems (McDermott & Fox, 1999, pp. 55, 63). However, technical solutions must be fitted to the organization (McDermott & Fox, 1999, pp. 63), which implies a socio-technical view. In the same vein, although the abuse case approach views technical quality as the key to ISS design success, user acceptance and resulting organizational fit is also seen as important (McDermott & Fox, 1999, pp. 60, 63). The following citation illustrates this. While ‘‘Users can decide [with a reference to abuse cases], in terms of their own application domain, which threats apply and which threats should be countered by product security features’’ (McDermott & Fox, 1999, pp. 63), there is need ‘‘to explain security-related design trade-ofs’’ (McDermott & Fox, 1999, pp. 63) to users. Such trade-ofs point to a socio-technical role.

The semantic responsibility analysis approach (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) entails a socio-technical view: ‘‘Research into security is often concerned with technical issues [. . .] This approach allows us to integrate these [technical security] issues with considerations of norms, purpose, and interpretation of information as regards the security of organization.’’ (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996; p. 4). Such integration of technical solutions with organizational aspects inclines towards a socio-technical view in relation to the priority accorded to ISS design and its success. We interpret their view on the impact of users on ISS design as socio-technical. Users preferences are part of social norms and organizational culture, which are regarded as equally important as the technical system.

In contrast to other approaches within the information modeling paradigm, the task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) is technical: ISS problems are due to inadequate technical quality in general and to the narrowness of scope of the existing computer security models in particular. Their design concern is related to technical systems. Existing job responsibilities are considered in the belief that this will improve the technical quality. This suggests that the technical system and technical quality have priority, and these are seen as the key to success of ISS. They do not mention the role of users in identifying and analyzing authorization structures, responsibilities and obligations, hinting that users have no active role in ISS design (technical view).

## 4.3.3. Research approaches used

All approaches within the responsibility modeling paradigm uses conceptual analysis. The semantic responsibility analysis approach (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) has used conceptual analysis to build the approachs theoretical underpinnings on the basis of semantic analysis and philosophy of language. The argument by Strens and Dobson (1993, p. 143) that analyzing responsibilities helps to identify security responsibilities and overcome communication gaps in ISS design is an example of their use of conceptual analysis. The argument on the limitations of the access control policies and the need for a holistic approach by the task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) is conceptual (based on conceptual analytical research). Additionally, the approach by Strens and Dobson (1993), and the semantic responsibility analysis (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) and the task-based authorization (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) approaches have used artefact building to sketch their modeling notations. In creating abuse cases, McDermott and Fox (1999) have borrowed their artefacts and concepts (e.g., stick figures) from UML developers (hence they have not engaged in artefact building). Consequently, McDermott and Fox have used conceptual analysis.

## 4.3.4. Applicability to IS development

Only the abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999) approach within the responsibility modeling paradigm, is applicable to existing IS development approaches. Since abuse cases are derived from use cases, they should merge into IS methods that employ use case notation. Other approaches with the responsibility modeling paradigm do not ofer any guidance as to how they might be integrated into IS development. Moreover, the fact that their notations difer from IS development methods is another obstacle to the integration of these methods into the IS development process.

## 4.4. Business process paradigm

## 4.4.1. Research objectives

The approach by Herrmann and Pernul (1999) and the fair and secure electronic markets approach by Ro¨hm and Pernul (2000) within the business process paradigm entail a means-end oriented research objective. Herrmann and Pernul (1999) also engage in an interpretive research objective.

Both Herrmann and Pernul (1999) and Ro¨hm and Pernul (2000, p. 343–344) see a lack of methodological support with which to address security aspects regarding business processes. They endeavor to improve this situation by introducing their ISS method (Herrmann & Pernul, 1999; Ro¨hm & Pernul, 2000, p. 343–344), which shows a means-end oriented research objective. Also present in the approach of Herrmann and Pernul (1999), is an interpretive research objective: the modeling notation they advance is needed to understand and communicate the security requirements between the diferent people involved in IS development (Herrmann & Pernul, 1999, p. 89–90).

## 4.4.2. Organizational roles

The organizational role of ISS of the approach by Herrmann and Pernul (1999) is technical, while the fair and secure electronic markets approach by Ro¨hm and Pernul (2000) lies between the technical and socio-technical views.

The fair and secure electronic markets approach of Ro¨hm and Pernul (2000, p. 343) sees security problems as stemming from a lack of security on the Internet, implying that security problems are due to inadequate technical quality. They also see that the existing technical solutions to the problem of Internet security, such as digital signatures, are dificult to apply, rigid and have not therefore gained user acceptance (Ro¨hm & Pernul, 2000, p. 344). This can be read as hinting that there is a lack of fit between technical solutions and user requirements, indicating thereby a socio-technical view of success in ISS design. It also hints that users preferences should be recognized in ISS design (socio-technical view in relation to allowed impact of users on ISS design). Despite the fact that their work is focused on technical system and technical control, it can be seen that they aim to bridge the gap between social systems (user requirements) and technical solutions (socio-technical view). In the light of the above, we conclude that their view lies between technical and sociotechnical.

While Herrmann and Pernul (1999) provide a high-level framework to model security constraints related to business processes, their perspective is rather technical and mechanistic (technical control). While they highlight the roles of developers and security administrators in securing IS, they do not mention the role of users. This hints that users do not to have an active role in ISS design. They do not ofer any suggestions on the cause of ISS problems. On the basis of the information on the priority of ISS design and the lack of users impact on ISS design, we interpret their organizational role of ISS as technical.

## 4.4.3. Research approaches used

Both approaches (Ro¨hm & Pernul, 2000; Herrmann & Pernul, 1999) within the business process paradigm use conceptual analysis as their research approach. The fair and secure electronic markets approach by Ro¨hm and Pernul (2000) develops conceptually key requirements for electronic markets and proposes a conceptual framework to meet these requirements (conceptual analysis).

Herrmann and Pernul (1999, p. 90) employ conceptual analysis to build their argument that there is a need for a systematic and holistic business process security methods. Furthermore, they use artefact building research to sketch their modeling notation.

## 4.4.4. Applicability to IS development

The approaches within the business process paradigm do not address the issue of how they can be integrated into business process or IS development approaches. Furthermore, as we see no explicit connection with processes or notations of IS or ‘‘normal’’ business process development methods, we conclude that the business process approaches cannot be integrated into IS development.

## 4.5. Viable and survivable IS paradigm

## 4.5.1. Research objectives

The viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) subscribes to interpretive and means-end oriented research objectives, whilst the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) entails all three research objectives.

The main research objective of the viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) is interpretive: by examining diferent threats to IS, practitioners can obtain an understanding of the vulnerability of their IS. This, in turn, helps to develop appropriate countermeasures for these attacks (means-end oriented research objective): ‘‘The Viable System Model. . .provides a simple, functionalistic model to aid in the development of attack and defence strategies’’ (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000).

The survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) primarily encompasses critical and means-end oriented objectives. The survivable IS approach first entails a critical research objective by criticizing the extant ISS methods as functionalistictechnical, lacking a human-oriented social orientation (Karya et al., 2001, p. 454). Second, the survivable IS approach also suggests that the whole concept of ISS is problematic. Instead of focusing on ISS, we should focus on viable IS: ‘‘[we] propose a new framework for building secure information systems, or as we suggest them to be called, viable information systems’’ (Karya et al., 2001, p. 453). According to them the survivability of IS, including the survival of future ISS threats, should be the target of ISS research (critical view). Their new method, which is aimed at achieving a survivable IS, suggests a means-end oriented research objective. Finally, the modeling notation aims at making security issues in IS development understandable by making them explicit (Karya et al., 2001, p. 460); an interpretive research objective.

## 4.5.2. Organizational roles

The viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) involves a technical role, whereas the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) entails the socio-technical role.

At first sight the viable system model seems to hint at socio-technical role: ‘‘The VSM is usually employed in the detection of shortcomings in organizational structure.’’ (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000). We see that by organizational structures, they are, in fact, referring to technical systems. In their article, they discuss technical attacks (denial-of-service and hackers modifying www-pages) and countermeasures in relation to a technical means to protect such attacks. This implies that ISS problems result from flaws in technical systems. We do not find any indication of a role for users in ISS design, which further suggests a technical view.

Hence, we conclude that the viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) is concerned with the technical system and improving its quality. The organizational role in the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) is socio-technical:‘‘In our view, an information system is a human activity system comprising five elements, namely hardware, software, data, procedures and, above all, people. . .in order to support human activities in the context of an organisation.’’ (Karya et al., 2001, p. 454). This implies that their design priority is socio-technical: they equally value the technical and social systems. ISS problems result because only technical means are used in seeking to find solutions to ISS issues, with result being that IS is regarded as ‘‘[. . .] a system that processes data and delivers information.’’ (Karya et al., 2001, p. 454). According to Karyda et al., ISS should cover people aspects, too. Hence, both the attributed cause of ISS problems and success in ISS design accord with a socio-technical view. Although Karya et al. (2001) do not explicitly reveal their view on the impact of users on ISS, we infer it to be socio-technical: users preferences constitute the social environment of IS, which is as important as the technical system.

## 4.5.3. Research approaches used

The viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) applies the idea of Beers viable system model to build a conceptual ISS approach (hence, the research approach is conceptual analysis). Similarly, the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) has also adopted Beers model in developing a conceptual ISS approach (conceptual analysis).

## 4.5.4. Applicability to IS development

The viable system model by Hutchinson and Warren (2000) does not ofer any guidance as to how this approach can be integrated into an IS development process. The viable system model seems to be aimed at exploring existing IS, which is another barrier in integrating the viable system model with the early phases of IS development processes.

The survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) recognizes the need for integrating ISS methods into the IS development process (Karya et al., 2001, p. 458). While demonstrating how this approach can be built into certain business process modeling techniques (Karya et al., 2001, p. 461), no concrete guidance is given as to how the approach can be integrated into methods of IS development. The idea of the ‘‘risk estimation diagram’’ may be added to IS/software development notations, though. This can be done by embedding into the notation risk factors in use cases (e.g., putting a risk factor dimension into the textual description of a use case), abuse cases, or data flow diagrams. Hence, the applicability to IS development methods is deemed potential.

## 5. Discussion of the results

Table 2 summarizes the results of the analysis on the level of individual ISS approaches, while Table 3 presents the overall results and respective implications in the light of the four viewpoints considered in the study.

The most common research objective is means-end oriented (14 out of 17 modern ISS approaches), and is evident in the task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) within the responsibility modeling paradigm, all the approaches within the security-modified IS development, the information modeling, the business process and the viable and survivable IS paradigms. The interpretive objective was the second most common (11 out of 17 modern ISS methods), and was found in three approaches within the responsibility modeling paradigm (the approach by Strens & Dobson, 1993; the semantic responsibility analysis by Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996; and the abuse case by McDermott & Fox, 1999, approaches), all the approaches within the viable and survivable IS paradigm, the DFD and ER modeling approach (Pernul et al., 1998) under the information modeling paradigm, the approach by Herrmann and Pernul (1999) within the business process paradigm, and the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996), the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996), and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) within the security-modified IS development paradigm.

Only four approaches, the logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988) and the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996) within the security-modified IS development paradigm, the task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) within the responsibility modeling paradigm, and the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) under the viable and survivable IS paradigm, evidence a critical research objective. It has been argued that, due to the social dimensions of IS, interpretive approaches are needed (Klein & Lyytinen, 1985; Hirschheim, 1985; Walsham, 1996; Klein & Myers, 1999). We agree, while maintaining that critical approaches are needed as well. First, critical studies help us to recognize possible weaknesses in the existing, perhaps dominant, approaches and also ensure that nothing can be taken for granted or dogmatically adopted. Second, critical approaches, when successful, tend to be the ones that are able to make the most contributions to science (Popper, 1970); critical approaches are the best candidates for bringing about scientific revolutions and paradigm shifts.

The most commonly held view on the organizational role attributed to ISS was the technical view (7 out of 17 modern ISS methods). This view is held by the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) and the object-oriented security semantics (Ellmer et al., 1995) approaches within the information modeling paradigm, the approach by Herrmann and Pernul (1999) within the business process paradigm, the task-based authorization approach (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) within the responsibility modeling paradigm, the logical modeling (Baskerville, 1988) and the spiral (Booysen & Elof, 1995) approaches within the security-modified IS development paradigm, and the viable system model approach (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) within the viable and survivable IS paradigm. The socio-technical view was found in 5 methods: the virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996) within the security-modified IS development paradigm, the responsibility approach (Strens & Dobson, 1993), the semantic responsibility analysis (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) and abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999) approaches within the responsibility modeling paradigm and the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) within the viable and survivable IS paradigm. The soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) within the security-modified IS development paradigm adopted the social view. The view of the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) within the security-modified IS development paradigm, the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) and the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992) approaches within information modeling paradigm, and the fair and secure electronic markets approach (Ro¨hm & Pernul, 2000) within the business process paradigm lie between technical and socio-technical.

The predominance of the technical view means that only technical and some socio-technical approaches are available to practitioners when they are choosing their ISS development method. As a response to the scarcity of socio-technical, and social ISS methods in particular, the need for social ISS approaches and techniques, such as user participation (James, 1996), has been proposed. Since the ISS literature recognizes that users may comply poorly with the security procedures of organizations (e.g., Sasse, Brostof, & Weirich, 2001; Siponen, 2000; Straub, 1990), social considerations are needed in ISS thinking to ensure that designs meets users expectations. Security solutions are of little help, if a systems security features are not properly utilized by its authorized users because the latter find the security solutions cumbersome. The introduction of social techniques may have a positive efect on this behavioral issue.

Conceptual analysis was the most commonly used research approach (13 out of 17 modern ISS approaches). Empirical (theory-testing, theory-creating, artefact evaluating) studies were lacking (Table 3), except for three approaches within the security-modified IS development paradigm: the logical modeling approach (Baskerville, 1989), the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998). The virtual methodology (Hitchings, 1995, 1996) under the security-modified IS development paradigm entailed (empirical) artefact evaluating research, but as no results were given we did not classify this approach as (empirical) artefact evaluating research (Table 3).

Consequently, at present there is a plethora of modern ISS approaches (14 out of 17) at the conceptual-theoretical stage only (with respect to conceptual analysis and/ or artefact building). This means that practitioners do not have empirical evidence on the practical usefulness of the diferent ISS approaches. Hence, we stand in need of rigorous qualitative and quantitative empirical studies exploring the usability of the current methods in the field. Equally, since ISS methods are intended for practical use, any new method should incorporate empirical evidence on its usability, ease of use and relevance in a context of use. Also, there is room for empirically evaluating the existing ISS approaches. In particular, comparative empirical studies comparing or gathering experiences on alternative ISS methods would be welcomed. Both empirical theory-testing and theory-creating, as well as artefact building and evaluating research approaches can be brought to bear on the various ISS approaches. For this purpose, we would particularly welcome interpretive research methodologies, such as case and field studies (Cunningham, 1997) and action research (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998).

## 5.1. Applicability to IS development

Only three approaches (the logical control approach by Baskerville; the spiral approach by Booysen and Elof; and the abuse case by McDermott and Fox) can be smoothly integrated into IS development methods. The logical control approach (Baskerville, 1988, 1989) within the security-modified IS development paradigm can be integrated into structural IS development methods, dataflow and ER-diagrams in particular. Also, the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) within the security-modified IS development paradigm can be integrated into methods that use Boehms spiral model. The abuse case approach by McDermott and Fox (1999) under the information modeling paradigm can be integrated into use case notations in the requirements analysis phase. In addition, it may be possible to integrate four modern ISS approaches with some modification into some parts of certain existing IS development methods. These are: the security constraints modeling (Pernul, 1992), the data and security semantics (Pernul & Quirchmayr, 1994) and the DFD and ER modeling (Pernul et al., 1998) within the information modeling paradigm and the survivable IS (Karya et al., 2001) under the viable and survivable IS paradigm.

Since the extant ISS methods showed poor integration with IS development methods, we argue that future ISS methods should ofer more guidance on the integration of diferent ISS approaches into IS development.

## 6. Towards the next generation of ISS methods

In the previous section we summarized four implications based on the results of the analysis. A critical reader may ask whether there are more than these four general implications, for example a specific research direction that future ISS methods should take. Our answer to this is yes, and we endeavour next to show what path the next generation of ISS methods should follow. However, before any such criteria for next generation ISS methods can be presented, we need to delineate the state of the existing ISS approaches in terms of generations. Baskerville (1988, 1993) proposed a generational classification of ISS. Following his lead, we ofer an updated version of this generational classification, adding in the information security research communities (Fig. 3). We argue that the ISS methods are now in their fourth generation, and thus that future ISS methods should aim to become fifth generation.

The practitioners tradition has had most to do with the development of ISS principles, checklists and most ISS security standards. The research problems they address often arise out of their practical experiences – hence the label practitioners. Formal development has been particularly favored by computer scientists (Denning et al., 1989; Hirschheim et al., 1995).

## 6.1. The third generation: modeling

The third generation approaches focus on diferent means of modeling organizations ISS requirements. Such third generation ISS approaches are logical modeling (Baskerville, 1988, 1989), the spiral approach (Booysen & Elof, 1995) and the ISS planning methodology (Straub & Welke, 1998) within the security-modified IS development paradigm, the responsibility modeling approach by Dobson (1990), the taskbased authorization (Thomas & Sandhu, 1994) and the abuse case (McDermott & Fox, 1999) approaches under the information modeling paradigm, the security semantics (Smith, 1989), all approaches within the information modeling paradigm (Pernul, 1992; Pernul et al., 1998; Ellmer et al., 1995), the fair and secure electronic markets approach (Ro¨hm & Pernul, 2000) and the ISS approach by Herrmann and Pernul (1999) within the business process paradigm and the viable system model (Hutchinson & Warren, 2000) within the viable and survivable IS paradigm.

## 6.2. Fourth generation: socio-technical design

The fourth generation ISS methods add the social and socio-technical design aspects to the third generation approaches. The fourth generation ISS methods have been contributed by the ISS community. The user participation utilized in the soft approach to the planning of ISS (James, 1996; Armstrong, 2000) is an example of a fourth generation socio-technical design approach. Other fourth generation approaches are the semantic responsibility analysis (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1996) within the responsibility modeling paradigm as well as the survivable IS approach (Karya et al., 2001) within the viable and survivable IS paradigm.

## 6.3. Fifth generation: social and adaptable ISS methods

The fifth generation methods should encompass (i) social and (ii) adaptable ISS methods that are (iii) rigorously developed along with practice. Fifth generation methods should incorporate the use of social techniques (i), such as user participation, to ensure social acceptance of security techniques and procedures. Such techniques are also relevant for obtaining users input and knowledge on ISS matters. The need of social techniques should not be interpreted to mean that users decide everything or that deterrents and sanctions cannot be used. However, the use of sanctions and deterrents should be justified (see Siponen & Vartiainen, 2004), and ideally, their use should be accepted by the members of the organization at large.

Adaptability, as an extension of the developmental duality thesis of Baskerville (1993), means that it will be possible to integrate ISS methods with diferent IS development methods (not just one particular IS development method, or a certain set of ISS methods). Ideally, this means that developers will be able to choose the ISS methods they want to use, and that these ISS methods can be integrated into any IS development method (ii). Since adaptability increases developers autonomy to choose the ISS methods they want, the fifth generation of ISS methods will be developer rather than methodology driven. In this way the fifth generation methods will provide developers with freedom of choice over the method they use as the basis for their IS development, and enable them to embed the security brick into their preferred IS development method (Siponen & Baskerville, 2001).

The last requirement of fifth generation, rigorously developed ISS methods closely allied to practice, stresses the need of empirically grounding those methods (iii). Because ISS methods are intended for use in the field, any fifth generation ISS method should incorporate empirical evidence on its usability, ease of use and relevance in practice. In the same vein, because practitioners use ISS methods in organizations, the development of fifth generation methods should take place in close consultation with practitioners, and should be tested in practice.

## 7. Conclusions

Alternative modern ISS development approaches classified as paradigms – viable and survivable IS, security-modified IS development, responsibility modeling, business process, information modeling – exist. First, we identified the underlying disciplines (IS, software engineering, computer science and mathematics) and research communities (ISS, computer security, database security, cryptology) from which modern ISS paradigms and approaches derive. Second, we compared the underlying assumptions of these modern paradigms and approaches. The most common research objective was means-end oriented; consequently, more critical and interpretive studies were called for. The predominant organizational role was the technical view; hence, the need for socio-technical and social methods. Most of the modern ISS approaches are based on conceptual development only. Consequently, rigorous empirical studies are needed to test ISS approaches in practice. Only three ISS methods can be integrated into IS methods; future studies, therefore, should pay more attention to the issue of integration. Third, we classified these ISS approaches into five generations of ISS methods. We argued in the paper that the existing ISS methods belong to the first four generations, and that future work on ISS should move towards a fifth generation of social and adaptable (empirically grounded) ISS methods.
