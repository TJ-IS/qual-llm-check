# Efectiveness of privacy assurance mechanisms in users' privacy protection on social networking sites from the perspective of protection motivation theory

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Efectiveness of privacy assurance mechanisms Privacy customization Social networking sites Protection motivation Privacy concern Self-disclosure

## A B S T R A C T

Along with recent advancement of web technologies, social networking sites (SNSs) afect peoples lifestyles by enabling them to perform so many activities which were not easy to do before. However, protection of users disclosed information becomes an important challenge not only for SNS users but also for governing organizations. This study focuses on SNS users' protection from privacy breaches that are made by or facilitated by SNS vendors and explores the efectiveness of privacy assurance mechanisms on SNS users' privacy preserving actions. This study proposes a conceptual model that explains SNS users' privacy protection behavior based on protection motivation theory. An empirical data was collected to validate the proposed research model. The results suggest that users' coping appraisals of the overall SNS assurance mechanisms, along with their threat appraisals, positively relate to their protection motivation. Further, the results confirm that privacy concern mediates the relationship between threat appraisals and coping appraisals. Finally, findings suggest that protective motivation discourages one's engagement in risky behavior (disclosing self on SNS), in addition to un dertaking safeguards (customizing privacy settings). Meanwhile, we find that individuals' use of safeguard increases personal engagement in risky behaviors (self-disclosure).

## 1. Introduction

Social networking sites (SNSs) engender the types of communication that were not easy or possible before. Accessing to personal opinions and even personal information is now possible with or without the knowledge of SNS users [63], making the protection of users' disclosed information an important challenge not only for SNS users but also for governing organizations [18]. In this study, we focus on SNS users' protection from privacy breaches through SNS. These breaches may involve a SNS purposefully misuses user data for profit, or its security flaws that inadvertently enable third-parties to gain access to such data [94]. For example, Facebook has been accused of releasing user information to the third parties without user permission [35]. Incidents of SNS breaches, including the recent Cambridge Analytica scandal, clearly shows that SNS provider-related SNS breaches is a real threat and it reminds SNS users to take protective actions against such looming threats.

SNSs have gradually increased their oferings of privacy assurance mechanisms to protect user privacy and fulfill government obligations. Among them are privacy assurance statements where SNSs explain their commitment to protect user privacy as well as the actual policies in enhancing user privacy protection. SNSs have also adopted third-party privacy protection seals such as TRUSTe, BBBOnline, and WebTrust. These seals act as a self-regulation enforced by industry to maintain a satisfactory level of standards when it comes to privacy preserving [9]. Finally, many SNSs provide granular privacy customization features [121] that were not available in traditional e-commerce web sites. Through these features, users may alter the way how their data is collected, stored, and distributed, or delete their accounts in extreme cases. Each of these privacy assurance mechanisms control and protect users' privacy diferently. Therefore, this study classifies these privacy assurance mechanisms by linking them with diferent types of control agencies. According to control agency theory two control agents are employed to increase perceived privacy control toward information disclosure: personal and proxy controls [132]. Personal controls (such as privacy customization) allow SNS users as the control agents to protect their privacy. This form of control requires users' efort and activation. Proxy controls (such as privacy statement and assurance seal), on the other hand, enable powerful others (e.g., SNS platform providers, industry regulators, and government) to act as the control agents and ensure SNS users that their information is safe and will be released to other parties only by their consent [132].

In this study, we explore privacy assurance mechanisms for their efects on SNS users' privacy protection. We conduct the current research through the risk perspective, as it seamlessly integrates privacy threats, risks and mitigation. Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) guides our theoretical development because of its proven ability to model interest in protective behaviors in various contexts [7]. PMT has also been applied in the IS literature to study protection attitudes and behavior [e.g., [52,64]].

The present research addresses several important research gaps in the literature of SNS privacy protection. First, prior studies have not examined the proxy privacy control mechanisms (privacy policies and privacy seals) and the user customizable privacy mechanisms together. These two assurance mechanisms act diferently. On the one hand, proxy privacy control mechanisms are analogous to a ‘black box’ approach for privacy protection. Despite that they function without needing the involvement of end-users, the extent of how such selfregulations are enforced is not visible to the general public. On the other hand, privacy customization options put privacy control in the hands of users and require manual eforts from users. The efect of considering both mechanisms on protection motivation and behavior is quite diferent from studying them individually like many studies in the past [e.g., [87,99]]. For example, Facebook's privacy policy concerns about the company's data collection practice and secondary use of the collected information. Neither document covers protection of one's in formation to peer Facebook users, which is a feature only available in an individual's privacy settings. The co-presence of these diferent assurance mechanisms introduces unique issues afecting user perceptions and decisions on how much privacy protection they are able to exercise, how efective are such protection, and whether enforcing privacy control is efortless. Therefore, our approach of taking both forms of assurance mechanism to study their role in the fear appeal process creates a unique contribution.

Next, prior SNS privacy literature has not explored the likely association between privacy threat appraisal (risks of privacy breach) and privacy coping appraisal (privacy preserving controls). In non-SNS context, few studies, such as Johnston and Warkentin [64], reported that some elements of the two appraisals may be related. Tanner, Hunt and Eppright [122] contended that emotions might play a mediating role between threat appraisals and coping appraisals. They proposed the existence of “intermediate emotional state”: threat appraisals induce emotions, which subsequently result in coping appraisals. They described this sequential protection motivation schema as Ordered Protection Motivation Theory (OPMT). Unfortunately, empirical validation on the mediating role of emotion is scanty. To date, prior online privacy studies have not explicitly considered threat emotions except Boss, Galletta, Lowry, Moody and Polak [17], which is within the security context on desktop anti-malware, but not privacy on SNS. Few studies suggested that threat emotions might afect one's information processing and decision making [14,21]. This is because threat appraisal is frequently assessed either concurrently together with threat mitigation or as the direct source to induce the mitigation action. Without this intermediate state of threat emotion being in efect, results of threat appraisal may not directly lead to coping intentions. Our empirical assessment linking threat appraisals and resulting coping intentions through a well-defined intermediate emotional state helps shed some light on the applicability of OPMT to SNS.

Finally, prior SNS privacy research has largely ignored whether users' protection motivation will afect their engagement in risky behaviors on SNS platforms – the behavior that puts the person under risks in the first place (e.g., sharing personal information on SNS). Prior studies are also silent on how one's use of the privacy assurance mechanisms will change his or her risky behavior. On the one hand, it is plausible that the feeling of increased safety due to the use of privacy assurance mechanisms, may fuel an individual to engage in more unsafe actions such as sharing more private information on SNS. This is known as the phenomenon of “risk compensation,” Individuals become less careful to perform a risky behavior when they perceive they are more protected [85]. On the other hand, it is possible that the same person will attempt every possible venue, utilizing privacy assurance mechanisms as well as reducing self-exposure on SNS, in protecting personal privacy. Therefore, the net result is the combined efect of the above two forces, one pulling the person away from engaging in risky online behavior, and the other one drawing the person back in. An empirical assessment from our work not only delineates the two efects, but also the strengths of the efects in the context of SNS.

To address these existing gaps, we focus on common privacy assurance mechanisms ofered by most SNSs and propose a conceptual model that explains SNS users' privacy protection behavior. The main objectives of this research are: 1) to study how SNS users' perceived efectiveness of privacy assurance mechanisms associate with users' privacy preserving actions by applying PMT as the theoretical lens, 2) to investigate the potential efect of privacy concern, as an example of threat emotion, in regulating the relationships between core PMT constructs, and 3) to explore if SNS users' use of personal and proxy privacy assurance mechanisms will afect risky behavior such as sharing private information.

We collect survey data to empirically validate our conceptual model. The results reveal three intriguing findings. First, users' coping appraisals of the overall SNS assurance mechanisms, along with their threat appraisals, positively relate to their protection motivation. That is, Internet users are promoted to take protections, although they have already been protected by an existing portion of the SNS features, such as privacy seals or statements that automatically protect user privacy. This finding rejects the postulation that users will reduce their protec tion intentions at the presence of available safeguards, adding new knowledge to the literature on PMT and SNS. Second, analysis results confirm that privacy concern mediates the relationship between threat appraisals and coping appraisals. Concern reflects a troubled or anxious state of mind and a concern of privacy indicates the level of anxiety or fear over their control of personal privacy. We are among the first to report this mediation efect, which contributes to the PMT literature (more specifically the OPMT) by advocating the important role of emotions in linking the two types of appraisals. Our paper, therefore, joins the scare prior studies [115,122] to uncover the relationship be tween the two appraisals. Finally, we find that protective motivation discourages one's engagement in risky behavior (disclosing self on SNS), in addition to undertaking safeguards (customizing privacy settings). Meanwhile, we find that individuals' use of safeguards (privacy customization) increase personal engagement in risky behaviors (self-dis closure). When combined, these findings reveal two conflicting roles of protective motivation: while it directly discourages risky behavior such as self-disclosure, it also indirectly encourages self-disclosure because it reduces the vulnerabilities of SNS users when they increase their personal control (privacy customization) over their information. Findings as such add new knowledge to the literature on PMT as they uncover the intricate, underlying paths, through which protective motivation afect one's engagement in risky behavior.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Privacy assurance mechanisms

Information privacy refers to “an individual's right to determine how, when, and to what extent information about the self will be released to another person or to an organization” [20]. Boston consulting group predicts that the number of people who perceive data misuse will dramatically increase in near future because of increased SNS use [8]. Threats to SNS users' privacy are caused by diferent sources. Peer SNS members with a malicious intent could pose privacy threats in several ways. They can steal personal information and impersonate a victim on the same SNS, other SNSs, or even any online or ofline environment [51]. Peers can use a fake identity by masquerading as a trustworthy third party [5].

Another source of threat for SNS privacy is SNS platform providers. They could intentionally trade user data with unauthorized parties without any user consent. Researches show that many protection mechanisms, such as tagging restrictions or wall post access controls, have received little interest from platform providers or their approved third parties [12]. For example, Facebook has been accused at several courts in the US and Europe for releasing user information to the third parties without consent [35]. SNSs could also unintentionally leak user data because of a lack of oversight over third-party activities (e.g., advertisers or marketing partners) on their platforms [76].

In recent years, SNS providers have started ofering a variety of privacy assurance mechanisms to help users reduce the amount of personal data that may be collected by SNS providers or the user's peers on the same SNS platform. Online privacy assurance refers to “mechanisms that directly or indirectly provide customers with assurances and guarantees that their private information will be protected and kept private by the website” [9]. For example, Facebook users may use “View Activity Log” to edit published contents, such as profile, posts, and likes, and reduce what Facebook knows about them. In “Ad Setting,” users may also toggle of the ability for Facebook to collect user data on third-party websites or apps (e.g., Amazon notifying Facebook a recent visit or purchase of a Facebook user). To limit data dissemina tion, users may also customize SNSs to limit data sharing to third-party apps or how they want their own data to be visible to others, such as friends and third-party advertisers.

Privacy assurance mechanisms have been studied by many researchers in diferent contexts. Appendix A summarizes recent studies on relevant assurance mechanisms in e-commerce and SNS contexts. According to control agency theory two control agents are employed to increase perceived privacy control toward information disclosure: personal (such as privacy customization) and proxy controls (such as privacy statement and assurance seal) [132]. Based on this classification of privacy assurance mechanisms, there are two key areas in SNS privacy assurance context that require more attention.

First, a variety of privacy customization options on SNS put the direct control of one's privacy in the user's hands, which forms a direct user privacy experience afecting their sense of privacy protection [132]. The efect of this customizable privacy protection in formulating user's sense of privacy has received relatively little attention in the literature. In the example of popular SNSs such as Facebook, there exist many customization options that allow users to control the types and amounts of personal data to be collected, and maybe later misused or distributed without user consent, by Facebook and by the user's peers. For instance, a user can turn of “Location History”, which stops other interested parties from tapping into the user's discrete location records, travel history, and personal connections with other Facebook users. When personal data is withheld through privacy customization features on SNS, a user reduces the possibility for others to peek into traces of his or her demographic, behavioral and preference patterns. To put it in a diferent way, this form of privacy protection is user controllable that targets protection at the stage of data collection and improper use. Its efect on one's sense of privacy and how that sense of privacy drives one's intention to engage in risky actions have received little attention.

Second, most studies do not distinguish between the role of personal and proxy controls in fear appeal process. Proxy control mechanisms, such as privacy policy statements and assurance seals are used by SNSs to safeguard consumer privacy. Privacy policy statements act as a contract between consumers and the companies, whereas the privacy assurance seals serve as a third-party endorsement of a company's privacy protection practice to deter breaches and other illegal forms of data use. These privacy assurance mechanisms are SNS providers' selfregulations that do not require involvement of users [124]. On the other hand, personal control mechanisms, such as privacy customization features, require users' efort to become activated and protect users' information. Because of this requirement, personal controls could not only be considered as a coping mechanism but also, could become a protection response that users can take against privacy threats. In contrast, proxy control mechanisms are not considered as a protection response by users. Therefore, these two forms of assurances could play diferent roles in fear appeal process and should be studied with attention to their diferences. To the best of our knowledge, the diferent roles of the two forms of assurance mechanisms have not been studied in the past.

## 2.2. Self-disclosure

In this study, self-disclosure refers to the intentional and voluntary release of personal information when using SNSs. A number of factors/ antecedents with theoretical foundations have been reported to afect self-disclosure behavior [135]. Antecedents include perceived benefits [13,74,77], privacy concerns [139], personality traits [97], and risk control/mitigating factors [120,121].

In most previous studies, the cost-benefit perspective based on privacy calculus theory is the mainstream theoretical approach to explain self-disclosure behavior [16,34,136]. Individuals disclose personal information to obtain certain benefits. Thus, antecedents are related to benefits such as relational benefits [13], useful information [74], enjoyment/entertainment [29,77], self-presentation [25,30]. In spite of perceived benefits, individuals who are sensitive toward personal information less likely reveal their personal information because of the greater concerns for privacy invasion [127]. Privacy concerns are considered the major issue in privacy literature [139]. In the SNS context, costs of self-disclosure related to privacy concerns are related to individuals' fears regarding unauthorized access to their personal information including improper data practices by the SNS providers [13,49,116].

Only few studies reported the significant efect of risk mitigating factors such as privacy customization, privacy setting [120,121] and trust [69,75]. For instance, Stutzman, Capra and Thompson [121] report that privacy customization can be used to control privacy-related costs of self-disclosure. Stern and Salb [120] also suggest that privacy settings can be used to decrease user perceptions of privacy risk. Previous studies explored factors afecting self-disclosure behavior mostly from the cost-benefit perspective. Privacy literature has limited number of studies focusing on protection motivation as a main factor influencing personal privacy control and self-disclosure behavior in SNS context.

## 2.3. Protection motivation theory (PMT)

PMT, developed by Rogers [109], explains and predicts protection attitudes and behaviors of an individual who is exposed to a threat. PMT suggests that there are three important component in fear appeal: (1) the severity of the threat’ negative outcomes; (2) The probability that the threat occurs; and (3) the eficacy of protective responses [109]. Maddux and Rogers [84] revised the original version of PMT by adding self-eficacy as the fourth component that afects protection motivation behavior.

PMT suggests two cognitive processes that an individual carry out to cope with a threat: threat appraisal and coping appraisal. Generally, threat is defined as “something that is a source of danger that can bring harm (physical or mental) to an individual” [65]. According to PMT, threat appraisal is a process of estimating the severity and susceptibility of a threat while coping appraisal refers to the process of evaluating the eficacy of protection responses and the perceived self-eficacy of the individual who is exposed to the threat [65]. PMT also addresses that there are two sources of information that influence the threat and coping appraisal: environmental and inter-personal sources of information. Environmental sources of information are verbal persuasion and observation and inter-personal sources consist of personality variables and prior experiences [38].

Beyond cognitive processes, PMT argues for the importance of fear arousal in one's decision making and actions on self-protection against threats. Fear arousal concerns threat emotions [38,84]. In psychology, fear arousal has been well studied since 1950th (see Higbee [53] for a review). Many studies have defined fear arousal primarily in terms of the subjects' reported concern or worry [e.g., [31,102]]. Higbee [53] specifically termed “concern-type” fear as an emotion that responds to realistic or anticipatory threat, in contrast to other types of emotions such as “nausea-type fear” (e.g., being neurotic). Following Xu, Dinev, Smith and Hart [131], we define privacy concern as a context-specific (i.e., SNS context) feeling that reflects individuals' inherent worries about possible loss of information privacy. Folkman and Lazarus [39] contended that emotions that convey worry, anxious, and fear fall in the same cluster known as threat emotions. Further, IS literature on security and privacy have a tradition of using feeling such as being worry and anxious in measuring fear [17,89].

PMT literature has limited insights on two issues. First, it remains largely unknown as to the relationship between the two appraisals. While the original and the revised versions of the PMT suggest that the threat and coping appraisals are parallel processes that happen concurrently [84,109], a number of studies argue that these two processes could be sequential [115,122]. Scherer [114] and Scherer [115] argued that information processing in appraisal is most likely to take place in a sequential fashion: certain evaluation checks such as threat appraisal must occur prior to other evaluation checks such as coping appraisal. This argument is in consistence with Lazarus [79]: “once threat appraisal takes place, information about possible lines of coping (secondary appraisal) is given urgency, or search processes relevant to coping are activated” (p. 197). In their Ordered Protection Motivation Model, Tanner, Hunt and Eppright [122] argued that threat appraisal must occur prior to other evaluations such as coping appraisal.

A second weakness of the existing PMT literature is that it has largely ignored the role of emotions. Emotion is argued to afect cognition as it alters attention and beliefs [42]. Prior studies, however, showed that emotion such as fear aroused by threats does not have a consistent direct efect on decision makers' attitudes or behaviors [17,107]. Some studies argued for the potential mediating efect of threat emotion on the relationship between threat appraisal and coping appraisal but offered little empirical evidence. Tanner et al. contended that emotions mediates between threat appraisals and coping appraisals [122].

Compared with alternatives such as health belief model [112], technology threat avoidance theory [80], and theory of planned behavior [3], PMT better suits for online social-technical platforms such as SNS, since the Internet has made it faster to launch threats in larger scales and higher frequency. As a result, one is alarmed for the con sequence of privacy threats, which motivates the person to evaluate their privacy protection options. PMT ofers an opportunity to simultaneously study threat appraisal and coping mechanisms in presence of concern over threats on one's own privacy.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Considering PMT as our theoretical lens, we propose our research model in Fig. 1.

We follow PMT to expect that threat appraisal and coping appraisal jointly afect one's motivation for SNS privacy protection. During the threat appraisal stage, the appraisal process leads to an intermediate state called protection motivation [110] that causes adaptive or maladaptive behaviors. Adaptive behaviors aim to decrease the threat and control the danger (e.g., privacy customization), while maladaptive behaviors refer to reduction of undesired behaviors (e.g., not to share information) [17,122]. Therefore, users motivated to protect their information have two options: (1) maladaptive response to disclose less information [28], and (2) adaptive response to customize privacy protection features. The second option may result in more self-disclosure behavior. Users appraise privacy threats and coping strategies knowing the existence of such protection from the personal and proxy control assurance mechanism. Our model is based on this conception to study how the user customizable privacy mechanism functions when the two forms of protection are concurrently in place. As a result, our model shows that both forms of privacy protection mechanisms are in efect.

When online privacy protection is concerned, Raman and Pashupati [105] reported transference and avoidance as the two popular coping strategies that online users employ. Transference coping refers to one's acquisition of privacy protection from other parties. As aforementioned, there exist a range of privacy customization options on SNS [60]. These customization features allow users to adjust the types and amounts of personal data to be collected, and subsequently reduce the likelihood and potential impact of future data misuse or unauthorized distribution. Controls as such also reduce the residual risks that are not addressed by industry or SNS provider self-regulation (e.g., privacy seals and privacy statements).To measure one's extent of exercising SNS privacy customization features (e.g., learning the features, configuring the settings, and monitoring the performance), we use the term of privacy customization [132]. PMT suggests that individuals who are motivated are more likely to engage in protective actions. Following PMT, we expect motivated SNS users to increase the use of transference coping strategy by showing a greater level of privacy customization. Those who are motivated in privacy protection will appreciate the utility that customization features ofer, and subsequently engage in a greater level of privacy customization [96]. In contrast, individuals who are less interested in protecting privacy will find less value in exercising the customization features, and subsequently display a less amount of privacy customization. Consequently, we hypothesize that:

H1 (SNS users' protection motivation positively afects their privacy customization). Unlike the transference coping strategy, the avoidance coping refers to one's withdrawing from privacy-inducing activities [105]. Within the context of SNS use, members' self-disclosure directly induces privacy risks, as personal information exposed may be subject to future exploitation. In line with PMT [84,128], we expect motivated SNS users to be more likely to adopt the transference coping strategy by reducing their privacy disclosure. It is worth nothing that one's engagement in the transference coping and avoidance coping is not conflicting, as motivated online users were found to employ multiple coping strategies [105]. This is because no single coping strategy fully eliminates the threat to privacy, calling for a user to adopt multiple approaches. By reducing self-disclosed data, SNS users minimize both the odds and the likely impact of future privacy breach. In contrast, those who are less interested in privacy protection are likely to continue UGCs creation, because they do not see the utility in disclosure reduction actions. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2a (SNS users' protection motivation negatively afects their self-disclosure behavior). While Raman and Pashupati [105] underscored the importance of transference and avoidance coping strategies, they failed to acknowledge the intricate relationship between the two. When individuals become able to transfer the privacy threat, they may be enticed to engage in more privacy-inducing activities. This is known as the phenomenon of “risk compensation,” where individuals become more careful where they perceive greater risks and behave less careful when they feel more protected [85]. For example, drivers were found to engage in more risk-inducing activities (e.g., driving faster and less carefully) when belted [58]. In the current context, SNS members who find protection in the use of privacy customization features may conceive an increased sense of security, which is likely to promote members to disclosure more for two reasons. On the one hand, members anticipate the disclosure-induced privacy loss to reduce, because privacy customization decreases one's vulnerability to privacy breach. On the other hand, they anticipate the benefits that are associated with an increased self-disclosure. According to the Social Penetration Theory, personal relationships evolve as a result of information sharing and they improve when the exchange of information grows [6]. As a result, SNS members may engage in more self-disclosure in exchange of anticipated social relationship improvement. Because of this “privacy calculus” [34], we expect members to increase selfdisclosure when they use privacy customization features. Considering the above, we expect:

H2b (SNS users' privacy customization eforts positively afects their self disclosure behavior). We expect the protection motivation of a SNS member to be driven by his or her threat appraisal. Threat appraisal refers to the assessment of possible threats which exist in the relationship between users and the potential sources of harm or loss [119]. It is a cognitive process in which an individual assesses the risks of performing a specific behavior. Threat appraisal is the result of an assessment of both threat severity and susceptibility [110]. Threat severity refers to the significance of personal loss while threat susceptibility refers to the probability of one falling to the threat [64]. The PMT literature suggests that decision makers will increase their protection motivation, when their threat appraisals return a high level of risk. In consistent with PMT, we argue that members will develop a higher level of protection motivation when they judge the SNS threat high. Because a high level of appraised threat implies a greater amount of anticipated loss of privacy, members will elevate their motivation in self-protection in order to prevent their personal loss while using the SNS. H3 (SNS users' threat appraisal positively afects their protection motivation). In addition, we argue that the protection motivation of a SNS member to be driven by his or her coping appraisal. Coping appraisal refers to someone's assessment of own ability in coping with a threat [110]. It consists of two separate processes: (1) assessment of the efectiveness of coping mechanisms that someone can use against a threat (response eficacy), and (2) evaluation of one's ability in applying coping mechanisms against a threat (self-eficacy) [84,110]. PMT suggests that coping appraisal encourages one's protection motivation. Following PMT, we expect members to become more motivated in privacy protection when they apprise the threat coping options to be efective and easy to use. This is because the presence of efective and easy to use privacy controls instill confidence in SNS members with regard to safeguarding their privacy. These controls make the members feel in control of the situation and, subsequently members become motivated to protect their privacy. Otherwise, members may grow uninterested in privacy protection since they sense no help in sight. We hypothesize that:

H4 (SNS users' coping appraisal positively afects their protection motivation). Finally, we contend that SNS members' appraisals are connected in such a way that threat appraisal afects coping appraisal. This is consistent with OPMT, which suggested that the appraisal process is an ordered process where threat appraisal precedes coping appraisal [79,114]. That is, individuals assess the amount of threats they confront and then appraise their abilities to cope with the situation. Tanner, Hunt and Eppright [122] theorized that fearful emotions may connect these two appraisal processes. Specifically, the growth of intermediate fearful emotional response afects how decision makers attend to coping appraisal information (i.e., attention allocation). A fearful emotion also alters one's cognitive assessment of response eficacy performance and self-eficacy – information comprehension.We contend that this may hold within the current context with privacy concern (fear of privacy loss) acting as the fearful emotion. Privacy concern in online communications refers to fear of being monitored, losing anonymity, identity theft, and so forth [19,88,90,134]. Individuals who are concerned about their privacy show fear, worry, and anxious [36,37]. Threat appraisal may fuel privacy concern: when SNS users believe that privacy threat is probable to happen and that the consequence is significant, they become concerned about sharing their information. Subsequently, privacy concern may afect one's evaluation of coping options. Studies in psychology and neurosciences found that a high level of fear and anxiety introduced bias to individuals' cognition process [108,123]. For example, SNS users may feel overwhelmed at the presence of high privacy concern and, subsequently lose confidence in the use of technical solutions for protection purpose (i.e., perceived response eficacy). Therefore, we contend that privacy concern may link threat appraisal and coping appraisal, acting as a mediator.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Instrument development and data collection

Most of the items in our measurement model were adopted from prior studies. We defined some new items to measure a number of new constructs we proposed in this study (See Appendix B). We measured all items by using 7-points Likert scale. The data was collected from 342 currently active users of SNSs. After removing incomplete and invalid responses, we ended up with a sample of 315 respondents indicating a usable sample size rate of 92.1%. Table 1 represents a summary of demographics of the respondents. Survey participants were assured confidentiality and were informed that the purpose of the study was to investigate the antecedents of self-disclosure on SNSs. Before partici pants answer the survey questions, one of the researchers explained the concept of privacy assurance mechanisms on SNSs and provided them with some examples of these mechanisms. Because some SNS users are using multiple SNSs, we asked them to choose one SNS they use most frequently and think about their ongoing experiences with that SNS. Then, they were asked to complete questions that measure their selfdisclosure, followed by those measured the other constructs and demographics.

Survey responses were collected from college students who took undergraduate or graduate courses at a large institution in the United States. According to a study done by Pew Research Center in 2019, 18–24-year-olds presented the largest user group on Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, and Snapchat [98]. Several recent SNS [e.g., [4,46,81]] and cybersecurity studies [50,61,126] also collected data from college student population. Therefore, the student sample is ap propriate for the current research.

Through their analysis of the estimation issues with PLS-SEM and

CB-SEM, Sarstedt, Hair, Ringle, Thiele and Gudergan [113] suggested PLS, as opposed to CB-SEM or consistent PLS (PLS), as the recommended data method for conceptual models with both reflective constructs and formative constructs (pp. 4007). PLS analysis was suggested as a robust method against lack of normality [44]. Because normality is an assumption of SEM and the variables we are exploring are not normally distributed (See Appendix C), PLS analysis is chosen for the analysis. To test our research model, we used Smart PLS 3.2.8. PLS measures the direction of relationships and those strengths by using metric properties of the measurement scale [10]. This study applied three steps of analysis: (1) An assessment of measurement model by evaluating item reliability and validity, (2) a check for the presence of common method bias, and (3) a structural model assessment to evaluate the model's predictive power.

## 4.2. Measurement model assessment

To check the adequacy of the measurement model, this study examined items reliability and validity [57]. The reliability of each construct is assessed by analyzing the Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability. Values above 0.7 typically indicate acceptable reliability of the measurement model [95]. Average variance extracted (AVE) is also used to test for the convergent validity. AVE values above the benchmark of 0.7 are generally deemed as adequate and show that the latent variable explains more than half of the variation in the indicators [41]. The diagonal values on Table 2 represent the square root of AVE. These are measures for the variance shared between a construct and its indicators and explain the convergent validity of the measurement model. The values of Cronbach's alpha, composite reliability, and AVE, demonstrate the internal consistency and convergent validity of the measurement model.

To test for the discriminant validity of the measurement model, this study applied two methods. First, AVE values are supposed to be greater than the of-diagonal correlations which is true in our case (see Table 2). Second, the related items of each construct are supposed to load highly on the factor the construct measures and cross-loadings are supposed to be lower than the within construct loadings (See Appendix D) [70]. This study used Varimax rotation in SPSS 24 to do factor analysis.

Perceived threat and coping appraisal were modeled as secondorder formative constructs. Both constructs modeled as second-order formative constructs with two first-order components (See Fig. 2). All the weights from the first order components to the second order constructs were highly significant (p < .001). This study also tested potential multicollinearity among the first order components by performing variance inflated factor (VIF) test. The results showed that all the VIFs were below 3.3, indicating that multicollinearity is not a concern in measuring threat and coping appraisal [100].

## 4.3. Common method bia

Common method bias (CMB) could be an important source of measurement error [101]. Presence of CMB may result in erroneous conclusions of construct qualities and construct relationships [22]. To evaluate the presence of the CMB in our measurement model we applied three diferent methods. First, this study applied Harman's single factor test. Recent studies confirmed that contrary to the conceptual criticisms that Harman's test lacks the sensitivity to detect common method bias, it adequately detects common method bias under conditions commonly found in survey-based research [43]. The results of unrotated factor analysis for all 34 indicators show that 8 factors account for 79.1% of the variance and the first factor accounts for less than 50% of the total variance (23.2%). Based on these results, we conclude that CMB is not a serious issue in the measurement model.

This study also applied a full collinearity assessment approach suggested by Kock [71], Kock and Lynn [72] and followed by Guo, Bao, Stuart and Le-Nguyen [47]. The results of the assessment, summarized in Appendix E, suggest that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values are below the recommended cut off value of 3.3 [48,93.100]. Thus. this also suggests that CMB is not an issue in the measurement model.

Finally, We applied the marker variable approach proposed by Lindell and Whitney [82]. We followed the guidelines suggested by Rönkkö and Ylitalo [111] to use this approach in PLS. We chose a marker variable that (a) is measured using Likert scale similar to the other variables. (b) is not theoretically related to other constructs in our study, (c) is not highly correlated with other measurement items. Based on these criteria we chose perceived benefit item - “I benefit others when I share my information on this SNS” – as the marker variable. The results of the analysis (See Appendix F) does not show any significant change in the coeficients and R-squares, suggesting that CMB is not a major concern.

This study examined the path coeficients and R-squares to assess the structural model. Path coeficient indicates the strength of the relationship between constructs and R-square shows the predictive power of the model. The results of the structural model assessment revealed that most of the hypothesized relationships are significant (See Fig. 3).

The results also revealed that the control variables were included in this study were non-significant $\textstyle { \left( p \ > \ . 0 5 \right) }$ . This study also examined the mediation efect of privacy concern on the relationship between threat appraisal and coping appraisal. Based upon recent critiques by Zhao, Lynch and Chen [140] regarding the reliability of mediation test approach suggested by Baron and Kenny [11], this study followed the approach developed by Preacher and Hayes [103] to test the mediation efect of privacy concern. This method has been used widely in information systems research [e.g., [1,55,59]]. The traditional Sobel mediation test was critiqued [11] and therefore is not popular anymore. ## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Contributions to theory

This study makes several contributions to theory building. First, it contributes to the SNS literature by exploring and validating the role of user-controllable privacy customization (personal control) together with protection motivation. The inter-relationships between the two have received limited attention in previous research, even in the traditional e-commerce literature [83,129] where privacy concern is frequently constructed as the direct antecedent of use intention or selfdisclosure. Our work provides the beginning evidence that privacy concern does not necessarily lead to an action (adaptive or maladaptive) unless appraisal of coping strategies and motivation to protect one's privacy are both in place. This finding is especially useful, since SNSs encourage self-expression and self-presentation through many venues on the platforms (e.g., geographic location, group afiliation and user preference). Each of these venues leaves ample opportunities for user tracking, data collection and user profiling that appeal to busi nesses, interested parties and even the platform providers. Making these opportunities viable while still assuring users a sense of privacy protection through platform providers' self-proclaimed measures (such as privacy statement or policy) will unlikely to go far. Our work shows that putting granular privacy controls in the hands of the users encourages self-disclosure, especially when there is a protection motiva tion.

Second, our study extends the information assurance literature by shedding new light on PMT. We find evidence that threat appraisal indirectly relates to coping appraisal, through privacy concern as a mediator. In doing so, we join the scarce prior studies, especially ordered PMT [115,122], to further uncover the relationship between the two appraisals. More specifically, our findings highlight the importance of threat emotions such as privacy concern in PMT nomological network. Prior studies have reported limited findings on the direct efects of threat emotions in end-users' decision making and actions [17]. Our study is among the first to reveal an intriguing mediator role of privacy concern in regulating user appraisals, showing how cognition may be biased by afect in the context of SNS privacy protection. This also adds to the prior research that study privacy concern primarily through the threat appraisal perspective [e.g., [133]].

Third, this study contributes to the SNS literature by reporting two conflicting, but interesting mechanisms through which protection motivation afects risk-taking behavior such as self-disclosure. Self-dis closure, especially when sensitive information is involved, can be risky, since unauthorized sharing, duplication and modification of this information are often bevond the control of the original authors once the information leaves them. Such risk often creeps up even when nonsensitive information is disclosed. For example, concerns over the consequence of unwanted exposure to authority figures [e.g., [62]] or peers (e.g., bullying reported in Xie and Kang [130]) are also associated with self-disclosure for certain user demographics or contexts. Our work, however, addresses multiple efects of how protection motivation manifests in the presence of SNS risks. On the one hand, we find that protection motivation directly discourages users from engaging in selfdisclosure that potentially exposes users to privacy predators and identity crimes. On the other hand, our empirical results also confirm that protection motivation encourages users to engage in privacy customization and protection, making it less risky to disclose one's private information. As the risk decreases, individuals may be more comfortable with sharing personal information to grow their social networks on the SNS. This is known as the “risk compensation” phenomenon. Risk compensation view suggests that individuals become less careful to perform a risky behavior when they perceive more protection [85]. Our work also partially adds to the privacy trade-of literature [e.g., [63]], which suggests that users often face the trade-of between potential privacy risks of SNS and benefits made available through it (such as opportunities for interactions, comfort-seeking, and knowledgesharing). Our results highlight that privacy customization has an efect of lowering the barrier for self-disclosure, but this efect is still not stronger than fear induced protection motivation that deters further self-disclosure. Furthermore, the overall relationship between protection motivation and self-disclosure is −0.187 as shown in Fig. 3, suggesting that individuals who are motivated ultimately reduce their disclosure of private information. Still, the discovery of the two competing paths of efect reveals the intricate associations between pro tection motivation and self-disclosure.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

This study provides important implications for practitioners. First, findings of our study show that procedures for SNS privacy protection are a welcomed gesture. While SNS privacy assurance features through proxy controls (e.g., privacy policies and seals) are common, they are usually limited due to the language written not being approachable by the general public, and self-proclaimed privacy statements are subject to change without notice. Therefore, this partial privacy protection through SNS platform provider or third parties rarely satisfy the general users. Inviting users to a variety of user controllable privacy customi zation options puts users into the driver seat, which engenders a better sense of privacy. SNS platforms are recommended to continue or even enhance their existing oferings on user controllable privacy customization.

Second, our findings show that coping appraisals are indirectly affected by perceived threat, through privacy concern. However, the privacy literature [e.g., [133]] reminds us that women perceive more risk from information disclosure, while men perceive more benefit from it. As self-disclosure comes with benefits (e.g., self-expression, relational maintenance, etc.) and associated risks (e.g., privacy risks and vulnerability), one engages in self-disclosure when its benefits outweigh the risks or when one is aware of ways to control the associated privacy vulnerability. As a result of this gender variation, coping appraisal and protection motivation may be afected. Therefore, making privacy customization procedure known, and easy to use is the beginning step to shrink this gender diference in privacy concerns during self-disclosure. Many SNS platforms are beginning to allow users to tune their privacy exposures and review their activity logs, but they generally lack the procedure to inform users of their privacy exposures in real time. Coupled with the general business practice of turning of certain privacy controls by default, it may be a little too late when one realizes that their privacy were not protected the way they prefer or when they are not even aware of the existence of such specific privacy protection options. As a result, privacy concerns elevate and options known to the user for coping appraisal is limited, which eventually afects self-disclosure intention. Promoting user awareness of privacy control mechanisms and the privacy implications at the time of user activity will provide a lasting efect on key constructs and their relationships from privacy concern all the way to self-disclosure in our model.

Third, while users are cautious about the likely threats to privacy, the current study finds that users will engage in more self-disclosure, when they increase the utilization of privacy customization features. Self-disclosure bears importance to SNS platform providers for disclosed member data (i.e., user generated contents) helps SNS platform providers to provide customized services, attract new users, and secure adverting revenues [27]. To further promote user self-disclosure, making privacy customization available is a required, but insuficient step. Promoting user awareness of available privacy controls and user's ability to comprehend the scope of each control will help reduce privacy concern and the efort needed for appraising coping strategies.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Similar to other researches, this study is not free from limitations. Although our conceptual model successfully explained self-disclosure, there are still other possible factors that have not been investigated as the antecedents of self-disclosure behavior [34]. This could afect our model's explanatory power (R-square) since our focus was only risks of sharing personal information on the SNS. Future research may look deeper into the interwoven relationships among risks, benefits and available privacy controls. For example, as one of the early studies on the privacy trade-of on social media, Jiang, Heng & Choi's work highlights the two consequents of the trade-of as self-disclosure and misrepresentation. Although our present study delves only into SNS providers supplied options for privacy control, another line of privacy literature has also stressed the importance of user-exercised privacy controls. For example, falsification of personal information (similar to Jiang, Heng & Choi ‘s misrepresentation construct), passive reaction through blocking, ignoring or deleting unwanted information, and identity modification as explored in Chen and Rea [26] are viable techniques for user-exercised privacy controls.

Similarly, an expansion of privacy trade-of by looking at user interactions and efect assumptions could shed some light on the validity of construct relationships. For example, not all SNS platforms have a web interface. Snapchat and Instagram are among the examples of apps available only on smartphones. As these apps are constantly active when one carries the phone, privacy implications may be diferent between the mobile and web interfaces. For example, in order to exercise certain privacy controls (such as passive reaction through hiding presence information and ignoring disruption from notifications), a user of mobile apps has to specifically turn of app features or shut-down the app service. When using the web interface, simply walking away or closing the browser would accomplish a similar efect. Thus, the cost for privacy protection may be comparatively higher for mobile apps. It will be interesting to see if the aforementioned privacy trade-of varies across web versus mobile user interfaces. Additionally, the assumption of privacy trade-of being static is increasingly questioned. Looking into how the protection motivation and privacy controls (both personal and proxy controls) change across time will likely uncover patterns of construct relationships.

Furthermore, our study lacks cultural diversity. We collected data in the United States. Diferent cultures may care about privacy diferently [129]. Finally, this study does not capture the actual self-disclosure behavior of SNS users. As argued by Smith, Dinev and Xu [118], the actual disclosure behavior of SNS users is a more direct predictive measure than the intention to disclosure. In this study we address Smith et al.'s argument by measuring the disclosure behavior instead of the intention to disclosure. This study borrowed almost all of the measures from Koohikamali, Gerhart and Mousavizadeh [73]. They applied these items to measure actual behavior.
