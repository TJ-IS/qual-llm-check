ETHNOGRAPHY/NARRATIVE

# ‘‘What else is there…?’’: reporting meditations in experiential computing

Mads Bødker

Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, 5, Frederiksberg, 2000 Copenhagen, Denmark

Correspondence: Mads Bødker, Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, 5, Frederiksberg, 2000 Copenhagen, Denmark. Tel: +45 2670 3115; E-mail: mb.itm@cbs.dk

## Abstract

Compelled by Yoo’s (MIS Q, 34:213–231, 2010) call for research on experiential computing, the paper suggests meditations as a genre for both doing and reporting on fieldwork. Meditations are used to engender passionate renderings of research encounters that are part introspective and reflective and part causative and instructive. The meditations weave together everyday experiences with IT with theoretical reflections on embodiment and affect, and suggest the potential for a new scholarly sensuousness. The paper suggests that paying attention to feelings related to technology in everyday life and the use of alternative representational tactics and theoretical motifs can be generative of new matters for empirical research. Emphasizing the senses and the body and their importance in developing a sensory apprenticeship in IS, the paper suggests alternative routes to knowledge and representation.

Keywords: affect; embodiment; experience; methods

## Exposition

Information technologies are increasingly a fundamental part of everyday life. Digital networks, embedded digital devices, mobiles, wearables and a range of digital devices seem to form a vast backdrop for mundane activities and experience. Questioning what living with ubiquitous (information) technology means, or indeed what it feels like, has a range of antecedents in fields such as philosophy of technology (Heidegger, 1953/ 1993; Merleau-Ponty, 1945/2002; Ihde, 1990, 2002) as well as the interdisciplinary research fields of information systems research (IS) and human–computer interaction (HCI). IS and HCI have included particular versions of phenomenology (as the study of experience), design-led studies as well as pragmatist philosophy to better understand experiential, embodied and ‘‘felt’’ aspects of human relations to technology (Boehner et al, 2008; Ciborra, 2004, 2006; Dourish, 2001; McCarthy & Wright, 2004; Wright & McCarthy, 2010).

In IS research, Yoo (2010) has articulated how IT as experiential might be conceptualized. He argues that ‘‘experiential computing calls for a reorientation of our focus from task performance and information processing to lived experiences of everyday life activities that are digitally mediated’’. User needs are much broader than informational needs or increasing their work performance. Articulating people’s needs in the context of IT should begin to reflect ‘‘deeper basic human needs and values’’ (Yoo, 2010: p. 217). As computers are moving deeper into the life worlds of people, and as the role of computers change from supporting formal tasks towards supporting a wider and more heterogeneous set of mundane activities, research in IS must similarly move towards a more general concern with how ubiquitous and pervasive computing is entangled with mundane ‘‘being’’.

According to Yoo, a specific aim for experiential computing is to guide IS research away from an exclusive perspective that prioritizes humans as organizational members and IT as fundamentally instrumental, serving discrete organizational or operational purposes. Taken broadly, experiential perspectives on IT ask how everyday experiences and performances are mediated through IT and how human conditions, felt lives, experienced realities and intentional relations to the world are entangled with computing technologies.

An emerging experiential computing perspective in IS offers a range of important directions and opportunities for enacting social worlds differently by expanding the theoretical sensibilities we bring to bear on the realities we investigate. However, on suggesting the turn towards the experiential in IS, Yoo does not indicate any possible or appropriate methodological implications. Methods and approaches, like the theories and conceptual vocabularies they are shaped by, enact the social in very tangible ways (Law & Urry, 2004). So, in moving towards a concern for mundane computing and a concern for understanding and describing the nature of digital experiences in everyday life afforded by ubiquitous information technology, what advances and innovations, in terms of methods and approaches, might be appropriate and generative? What kinds of worlds are revealed in the process?

In the paper I explore a method that I describe using the terms meditation and meditative. I am interested in how this approach may help reveal possible new concerns for IS and experiential computing in particular. As a method, meditations are meant as an opening towards ‘‘new kinds of researchable entities’’ (Bu¨scher et al, 2011; 99, see also Lury & Wakeford, 2012). In the paper I consider the dual and overlapping commitments to meditations as a scholarly genre in research on experiential computing: (1) as a transformative, mindful and sensory/bodily acute engagement in everyday encounters with IT, and (2) for reporting the dense affective/embodied textures and immediacies of situations in ways that allows an audience to reflect along with the author and to co-experience mundane situations as sensory, affective events.

Has information (systems research) lost track of a rather unruly body (Hayles, 1999) by persistently abstracting information and systems from the intimate-yet-social spaces of feelings, affects, moods, ambiences and atmospheres that compose the felt, embodied shapes of everyday life? Resting on the primary concerns of experiential computing outlined above, the goal of the paper is to open up a space in IS for alternative understandings of – and a different form of participation in – felt, embodied spaces of everyday life as it is increasingly entangled with digital technologies and devices. How might IS researchers, tasked with understanding and informing present and future technological lives, become better at appreciating the felt qualities of life with ubiquitous, pervasive and mobile computing technologies? How might they begin to shape representations of these lives into scholarly forms that strives to maintain an experiential resonance with body-subjects affected by and through technologies? How might they attain a position that enables a tuning-into a ‘‘Stimmung’’ – literally a tuning or a mood (Heidegger, 1962) – and to the often inexplicable ways an event or a situation ‘‘[vibrates] with surface tensions spied or sensed’’ (Stewart, 2007: p. 45)?

## Theory

A key proposition of this paper is that getting closer to felt life with IT involves a move beyond the practice perspective and beyond its questions of ‘‘what can/do/will we do with technologies’’, towards an exploration of felt experiential perspectives that reflects on corporeal and felt or experiential entanglements across assemblages of digital technologies and humans.

A key concern for the paper is the development of an approach for considering and relating everyday entanglements of humans and technology beyond the organizational practice perspective that otherwise dominates research in IS. Seeing IS beyond practice neither suggests that practices with IT are unimportant, nor that there are no associations between practices/doing and feeling/affect and experience. However, by asking ‘‘what else is there…’’, I propose that a purely practicebased view, one that primarily sees and describes technology as entangled or imbricated within human practices of organizing, might obscure some less conspicuous aspects of what Yoo (2010) has described as the lived experience of everyday life with technology. Among these are the aspects of human experience perhaps best described as affectively felt, as well as the materialities of moods and atmospheres that both shape and emerge from felt life.

Feelings and affect are, if we contrast them somewhat carelessly with practice, excessive and seem to reside largely outside of the discursive/interpretational frameworks of social research. ‘‘How are you feeling?’’ is an altogether more challenging question to answer unambiguously in a text or in a narrative than ‘‘what are you doing?’’. The idea that feelings and affects reside largely outside of language has been pursued conceptually by Massumi (1995, 2002) and Thrift (2004, 2008), among others, under the broad agenda of affect theory (Gregg & Seigworth, 2010).

The simple implication is that ‘‘it is difficult to develop an account of felt experience with technology’’ (McCarthy & Wright, 2004: p. 15, emphasis added) since accounts necessarily rely on words and language that may not sufficiently capture the rich, elusive nature of felt experience. This, of course, is particularly the case when words, language and narrative must conform to academic genres typically denied of passionate, felt and personal engagements. The approach suggested in the paper thus works as a kind of ‘‘bracketing’’ representational strategy (Michael, 2004) that makes a number of deliberate cuts in order to focus on aspects of experiential computing typically overlooked.

Any continuity of practice/discourse and feeling/affect notwithstanding (see Wetherell, 2013), I am proposing that IS benefits from a remedial engagement with feeling and affect as part of the suggested turn towards an experiential computing agenda. The paper therefore emphasizes feelings, vague moods, sensing bodies and affective life as it plays out in quite ordinary and unremarkable settings, possibly at the cost of downplaying the continuities with a practice- or organizational perspective.

It does so with the intention of highlighting the plausibility of a scholarly engagement with feeling and affect in IS, and to develop a style of working with such issues that may eventually come to complement other more well-known ways of working. At this point, a relevant step is to indicate some theoretical vectors that consider the excessive nature of feelings, moods or ambiences, and how they tend to fall below the radar of scholarly inquiry.

## Affect, feeling, embodiment

It seems IS research rarely takes emotions seriously: ‘‘Only marginally the emotional and almost never the aesthetic dimensions of human existence are acknowledged’’ (Avgerou & McGrath, 2005: p. 300). A number of different approaches have been pursued in order to explore emotions and felt experience. Stein et al, (2014) have introduced emotions and affect to sociomaterial theorizing, complementing an organizational case on IT implementation with the involved actors’ emotional responses. Using pragmatist philosophy, McCarthy and Wright (2004) discuss ‘‘felt life’’ and indicate the need to go beyond life as a form of practice to life as felt, stressing the entanglement of technology and human experience. Ciborra (2006) also consistently warns us of the lack of emotions in IS research which otherwise prides itself with a concern for understanding human–technology relations as situational.

In this paper, affect is considered an appropriate indication of situated, embodied feelings, moods and other forms of transitory experience. Distinguishing emotion clearly from affect, Anderson (2014: pp. 763–764) argues that where emotions are ‘‘a nonconscious background […] named, interpreted and reflected on by people […] affect refers to the felt quality of an experience: a quality that provides something close to the background sense of an event or practice or space’’. In this sense, affect or feelings that settle in the body do not necessarily emerge from the body; instead, they can be understood in terms of pressures or intensities that afford certain structures of feelings through social–material events and interactions. They can also be perceived as an emergent backdrop of moods and ambiences, or as particular energies that modulate the body into a feelings or a certain disposition.

A number of recent philosophical efforts ‘‘have called for a reconceptualization of the body as a subject, experienced from within rather than from without, arguing for the recognition (‘‘re-cognition’’) of the fact that the human body is the grounds from which one needs to explore experience’’ (Bhatt, 2013; 7). Theories of embodiment and ‘‘affect’’ have consistently sought to deconstruct onedimensional models of subjectivity. Broadly speaking, theories of embodiment shift the challenge of understanding feelings from the realm of the purely cognitive towards a fuller appreciation of the role a situated body-mind plays for feelings and for felt life.

Noticeable proponents of more corporeal theories of feeling and affect include Damasio’s (1994) embodied cognitivist theory of the ‘‘somatic marker’’. Philosophers and interdisciplinary social scientists inspired by the work of Deleuze such as Massumi (2002), Thrift (2008), Blackman (2012) and Anderson (2014) have proposed theories concerned with what they term the ‘‘autonomy of affect’’. In a necessarily abbreviated form, the autonomy of affect suggests that feeling takes place ‘‘under the radar’’ of the conscious individual and outside of signification or discourse. Affect in the form of moods and ambiences as well as shock, trauma or pleasure acts autonomously on or within bodies, only occasionally finding an outlet in recognizable signification and discursive meaning through emotion. On this rendering, emotions, rather than being a primordial feeling of ‘‘something’’, are a socially and culturally shaped narrative and reflections on feeling or sensing.

Drawing on affect theoretical literature, Bødker and Chamberlain (2016: p. 2) have suggested that ‘‘‘feelings’ – before they become manifest as emotional narratives or expressions to be ‘read’ or invoked through an interpretation of data – have fluid, precarious, and mobile forms that can register in bodies as trauma, surges of pleasure, visceral shock, embarrassment, pains, moods, tones, resonance, ‘gut feelings’, incontrollable laughter and so on’’. ‘‘Rejecting standard modernist blueprints that suggest the existence of a self-governing, psychological subject – a subject essentially assumed to be autonomous and informationally closed to the outside world – affect theories suggest instead that humans can be understood as intimately (and messily, unpredictably) entangled in continuous vital and felt exchanges with the world’’. Thus, rather than casting subjects as rational, autonomous and self-contained, affect theories tend towards understanding humans as body-subjects (Jackson, 1989; Blackman, 2012), emphasizing the entanglement of mind-bodies over the standard Cartesian account of subjectivity that considers thinking purely as a process of the mind separated from a sensing body.

Body-subjects are informationally permeable, emergent and fundamentally relational things (Blackman, 2012; Massumi, 2002). They are non-consciously responsive to a wide range of often barely felt, but nevertheless significant, sensory modulations set in motion by a situation. Affect can be said to involve a pre-cognitive and embodied attunement or porosity of the embodied subject. Such an attunement is always nascent and prone to modulations. Put simply, people are affected; moved, tickled or stirred, as they continuously and non-consciously register atmospheres, ambiences, premonitions or slight stirrings of the senses. Latour, emphasizing the relational possibility of body-subjects, goes so far as to claim that ‘‘to have a body is to learn to be affected, meaning ‘effectuated’, moved, put into motion by other entities, humans or non-humans’’ (Latour, 2004, p. 205, emphasis in original). Below, I occasionally use the terms ‘‘modulate’’ and ‘‘modulations’’ to indicate how technologies may stir, tickle or move the body-subject and how an emerging sense of things (often vague, incoherent and confusing) may linger and vibrate within bodies.

In subscribing to this version of subjecthood; ‘‘[t]he solidity of the subject has dissolved into a concern with those processes, practices, sensations and affects that move through bodies in ways that are difficult to see, understand and investigate’’ (Blackman, 2012, p. ix). The body is moved or suggestively and contagiously pressed into mood by a range of things and relations rather than consciously ‘‘processing’’ situations that in turn become manifest as emotional cues and narratives. Pursuing the idea of embodied subjecthood aims to avoid the sacrifice of visceral, passionate and sensuous aspects of the lives that are studied to neither typical methods of interpretive research that search for representable meaning or rationality ‘‘inside’’ the psychological subject(s), nor to the restraints of particular academic methods, ethos, genres or styles.

## Affect-oriented methods

Thus, part of a methodological challenge for an articulation of IS as experiential is to experiment with ways (and words) that may suitably shape research into forms that are suggestive of experienced or ‘‘felt’’ life itself; that attempt to invoke and represent energies and the vibrancy of experiences before these are translated to conform to the presumed authority/impartiality of traditional scholarly formulae.

The living, mobile, experiential, affective body seems, with a few exceptions (e.g. Ciborra, 2006; McGrath, 2006; Thompson, 2012; Stein et al, 2014), under-articulated in IS research. The methods typically employed tend to keep ‘‘the heart […] out of our head’’ as they consistently ‘‘bend to the dictates of the scientific method: they strive to keep the observer separate from the situation to be studied’’ (Ciborra, 2006, p. 138).

If we want to think and talk about the embodied, affective or sensory in ways that refuse to reduce embodied sensation and dispositions to social constructions, discourse or text, those forms of representations and theoretical apparatuses that IS has typically inherited from discursive turns in the social sciences and the humanities, new genres, representations and new forms of scholarship may provide new openings. We cannot simply add sensory or affective modes of ‘‘being-with’ technology to the dominant representations, methods, genres or discourses in IS.

Research fields such as communications and media studies maintain a long tradition of conceptualizing affected bodies and embodied subjectivity alongside apparently ‘‘disembodied’’ technological practices such as online communications. Some notable work includes the exploration of boundaries, tensions and transitions of embodiment in cyberspace (Stone, 1992), embodied performances and participation in online interaction, e.g. body identities and fantasies in chat rooms (Campbell, 2004), Turkle’s (1994) work on off- and online gender identities in multi-user dungeons (MUDs), as well as Paasonen’s work on ‘‘networked affect’’ and online pornography (Paasonen, 2011). Critical renderings of the cybernetics legacy in the conceptualization of a disembodied, informational self include Hayles (1999) and more recently the phenomenology of body performances, dance and digital arts in Kozel’s work ‘‘Closer’’ (2007) that attempts a practice-oriented exploration of the relation between digital devices and ‘‘the thickness of our own sensory experience’’ (p. 11).

Studies in the area of practice-, arts- and design-based research have been instrumental in foregrounding embodiment in artistic and designerly work as well as in the research process (e.g. Scrivener & Zheng, 2012; Stolterman, 2008; Stolterman et al, 2008). Studies such as these have engaged in practices without the presumption of verbal reports as somehow more reliable or consistent than embodied practice and doing.

Methods such as autoethnography (Denzin, 2014, Ellis et al, 2011) have provided an opening towards rich representations of embodied subjectivities, suggesting the possibility of narratives of the self as a way to engage with culturally, socially, politically and indeed materially inscribed bodies through reflective accounts of the ethnographers own life and experience (Spry, 2001). Alongside such concept as ‘‘messy texts’’ (Marcus, 1998) in anthropology and arguably also displaying some affinities with the notion of ‘‘impressionistic tales’’ (Van Maanen, 1988), theoretical orientations such as nonrepresentational theory (e.g. Thrift, 2008; Lorimer, 2005; Anderson & Harrison, 2010) have involved drawing inspiration from the arts in order to break the spell of transparency in the way researchers reason and talk about the world out (and in-) there.

In the following, I describe meditations as a genre. I suggest how the method I call meditations might work as an approach to research on everyday IT phenomena, as well as a representational tactic that seeks to transform the reader.

## Meditations

In the previous sections I provided justifications for why an experiential computing research agenda needs to include a deeper engagement with affect and feltness to complement a focus on practice. I have suggested that Yoo’s (2010) version of experiential computing is programmatic and how it generously embraces new possibilities for research in IS, but also noted how it is lacking in terms of methodological consequences. Methodological aspirations for experience-centric IS should include the attempt to capture felt-ness and affect, those things often deemed excessive, irrelevant, hard to capture or indeed so subtle that they seem to be beyond (or below) proper scholarly interest.

Following Buddhist traditions, a meditation is usually understood as a practice that requires concentration and calm introspection leading to insight and absorption. Going about our day-to-day practices often relies on analysing, planning and thinking ahead of here-and-now situation. Beyond the spiritual aspirations that are typically encountered in traditional Buddhism, meditations might simply offer an escape from the practical urgencies and headlong practices of everyday life. To phrase it somewhat simplistically, rather than thinking about ‘‘what to do’’, the practice of meditations urges us to consider, with our full sensorium, ‘‘how do I feel’’ at a given point in time.

I suggest that a meditational stance and the ensuing attentiveness that comes from writing up events can be achieved as a part of ‘‘everyday research’’ (Brinkmann, 2012). Meditations as an everyday research approach can help researchers become attentive to new research opportunities inside the setting of their own lives. In such situations, deeply personal and often incidental or serendipitous experiences may become visceral, sensed and reflective through introspection and writing as a form of inquiry (Richardson, 2000). A meditational approach can also transpire as part of more formal research settings, where relations to informants are transformed by feeling or imagining along with them. While meditations can be autobiographic and involve a reflective position on the self, they are not to be approached as proper autoethnographic work. Rather than a self-reflective method for understanding and representing broader cultural phenomena (Ellis, 1999; Ellis et al, 2011; Denzin, 2014), meditations are specifically intended as a narrower and arguably more experimental approach to the discovery and exploration of affective felt-ness such as moods or ambiences involved in everyday relations to technology.

I tentatively call meditations a form of affective introspection that transforms or re-positions the scholar from the conventional figure of a detached observer to a feeling, sensuous ‘‘scholar-with-a-body’’. They are attempts at cultivating a particular response to situations in the here-and-now of a constant on-flow of feelings, moods and somatic sensation.

A meditative approach compels the person meditating to attempt a situated and sensuous form of knowing; to try to observe and recall how her/his senses are at work, assess what senses are active or dominant in a situation and what feelings emerge in the situation at hand. It should compel the scholar to try to feel what stands out from an episode, to attempt an affective inspection of the body and to grasp at the dense forms of moods or atmospheres that fleetingly tinges and shapes a situation.

Meditations as a form of sensuous scholarship ‘‘is ultimately a mixing of head and heart. It is an opening of one’s being to the world – a welcoming. Such embodied hospitality is the secret of the great scholars, painters, poets, and filmmakers whose images and words resensualize us’’ (Stoller, 1997: p. xviii). Meditations aspire to (re-)sensualize research practices carried out under the experiential computing agenda, by encouraging the researcher to engage more openly with the senses, to welcome rather than reject those feelings, moods and sensory modulations of the body that may overshadow (if perhaps briefly) interpretation and rationalizations.

We turn now to the representational aspect of meditations, meditation as a form of writing and reporting. Within philosophy, a well-known example of meditations is Descartes ‘‘Meditations on First Philosophy’’ from 1641. His work is exemplary of a highly personal and selfreflective scholarly modality in which the author struggles with an intellectual and spiritual exercise (Lavery, 2007). At the same time, Descartes attempts to encourage the reader to think along rather than accept the authors’ deliberations at first appearance. Meditations, in this sense, are thus partly ‘‘instructive’’, inviting the reader to meditate on challenges and questions along with the author, without confining such reflections to a particular doctrine or strict axioms.

In her work on mediaeval religious texts, McNamer (2010) has shown how piety texts and depictions of the suffering (i.e. the ‘‘passion’’) of Christ were in essence ‘‘affective meditations’’ inviting and instructing people how to feel, i.e. how to empathize with suffering. Discussing Descartes’ choice of meditations as a medium, Hatfield (1986) has further argued that Descartes’ choice of meditations over more analytical approaches or formal logic as a philosophical medium was controversial: ‘‘Although works of religious meditation may make use of argument, their purpose is not to present a continuous argument that compels by force of logic; they serve as guidebooks to prepare the soul for illumination from above or within’’ (p. 47).

Drawing on the piety tradition, as well as a Cartesian appropriation of the meditational genre, meditations are vehicles for my own trying out of different affective and embodied modalities of everyday sensing in relation to IT. This experimentation entails being attentive in specific situations and in the recollection of sensory modulations through the process of writing. They try to represent a particular attentiveness that I struggled to preserve in the turmoil of an eventful everyday without interpreting the experience I had and reducing the report to accounts of practice. As a style of writing about sensations and affect, the purpose of meditations reported in this paper is not unlike the mediaeval piety texts in their attempts to invoke an affective and compassionate response in the reader. These are stories that aim to pull or invite the reader into a world of ephemeral sensation and moods that come and go.

My challenge is to create empathetic accounts about life wherein annoying, blocky phones, the ghostly interferences of smartphone connectivity as a (somehow) felt presence or an interrupted walk that turned into an attempt at registering my body’s modulation by my mobile phone is not downplayed as secondary qualities to delineable and instrumental registers of cognition, sense-making or practices of organizing. However, as texts I acknowledge that, short of experiencing such things ourselves, I will never arrive at wholly satisfying representations of lived, felt experience; texts are always barriers to, as well as media for relating rich accounts of experience.

We turn now to the meditations. The first meditation is a mundane episode of walking in a city that gradually transforms into an introspective exercise. In dealing with my phone as a foreign body I gradually begin to cultivate an awareness of moods and sensory modulations, only to discover a strange sense of care and compassion for the device.

The second meditation involves a chance encounter with jarring interferences from a mobile phone that provided an opening for a dialogue with a research participant on how mobile connectivity can be felt in corporeal ways.

In the third meditation, a walk with a friend motivates the spinning out of a story about phantom vibrations; a mobile device felt to be vibrating when it is not, into a story about bodies that become expectant and charged (and potentially incapacitated) by potential energies from ‘‘lightning clouds’’ of suspended phone notifications.

## Foreign bodies

Arriving in a big city by airplane. Imagine your head buzzing slightly with jetlag. 10 h of flying. Layover, 15 h. What to do? The particular airline keenly and authoritatively pointed out that ‘‘passengers are not allowed at any time to switch on any electronic devices, not even those in flight mode…’’. This meant that I had no way to rely on my treasured phone and the trusty old playlist of ‘‘Music for Long Haul’’ for sleeping. Now, finally I leave my luggage in the hands of the airline, turn on my rather large smartphone, push the headphones deep into my ears and jump on a metro for some hours of walking. As the train closes in on the city, synthesized ambient soundscapes play a fitting tribute to the passing vistas. I get off at a central station. Outside it is hot and muggy and I wear clothes fit for early Scandinavian autumn more so than humid Asian summer. I keep the headphones in and the music on. A quick look around confirms that it’s normal to carry a phone in your hand, so I set off on my walk, smartphone in hand, headphones blocking out the drone of the cars. My head still buzzes. I sorely need sleep, but I am also curious to see the city.

After a pleasant enough kilometre, my right hand hurts, and my body feels blocked and sore from holding the phone (in the firm grip of the visitor, the stranger unaccustomed to the rules and risks of this foreign city) while walking. Annoyed and tired, I insist on listening to music, so I put the phone in my front right trouser pocket, and I carry on. Another kilometre or two. Instead of a sore arm, my phone now grates on my thigh through pants that are increasingly damp with sweat. I shift the phone to my left pocket. After a while the same result; an awkward sense that my upper leg is somewhat inflexible, that I move differently to accommodate a blocky device in my pocket. I worry about the health of my phone. Will it get bent? Can it deal with the increasing dampness? Will it suffer?

This device doesn’t belong here, now. It is a foreign body on my body. I feel as if my body too feels foreign to the phone. That thing which normally lives peacefully on my desk, in my jacket or in my gentle hands as I do everyday communication and coordination or casual browsing, has taken on a more menacing but at the same time more fragile personality. I would need to adapt my clothing, my ways of walking or my travel gear to carry the phone as a respectful companion rather than as a constant reminder of its gross materiality.

The phone takes over, even as I fight it. As I try to ignore the device, the stream of things that are happening in my environment is lost to a flickering attention that is now primarily diverted towards figuring out how to walk gracefully with the phone. I sit down for a while to rest. I need to rest my feet and my body needs to rid itself, somehow, of this insensitive device. At the same time, I find myself feeling sorry for the phone, caring for its health, checking the well-being of the headphone jack and worrying that it has become too hot, scratched, bent or otherwise damaged.

## Spectrums

Can you sense the invisible, spectral landscapes of digital connections and infrastructures? Somehow this became an issue in a conversation with a participant in a research project. Together with a colleague I am studying how musicians use electronic instruments and software in their creative work.

We are visiting a basement studio, learning about the different machines and the processes of creating music with switches and knobs. As we look at some of the contraptions in the studio, a ticking and humming noise washes briefly over our conversation. And again. It’s a harsh, annoying kind of noise. First ghostly, undefined, then quickly becoming more violent, more like screeching chalk on a blackboard. It makes my teeth itch. We recognize it as interference from a mobile phone. The sensitive leads pick up a radio frequency signal as one of our phones tries to connect to a cell tower. On certain occasions these interferences seem to share some qualities with the music performed. We move our phones away from the table where the instruments are placed. To be sure not to disturb, I switch my phone to flight mode.

The situation with the interference lingers almost imperceptibly in our conversation after the session, and my informant jokes about how he imagines radio waves, what do they look like? In the basement studio there are shadow areas without cell connectivity and hot spots where cell connectivity is good, but the hot spots potentially introduce hum, clicks and hiss to the sound signal from the instruments. We pace around the basement for a while, identifying good and bad spots, placing ourselves in different positions to escape the signal shadow that our water-filled bodies seem to introduce. He says, It’s like you develop this sense of where they are, the good spots and bad spots. For instance, at the door, that’s a good place if you want to check Facebook.

I stand there for a while, trying to imagine what it might feel like if you navigate the room by connectivity, and I remember that I sometimes develop this slight sense of the electromagnetic architecture of certain places. Sometimes walking to the window of my apartment, sometimes knowing instinctively where I can go to get bars on my phone.

We exit the basement and chat in the doorway. The courtyard here is one big signal shadow…at least sometimes. It’s unpredictable, he says. Can you feel the signals? Sometimes you can, kind of when your phone vibrates and you realize that an email rolled in, then you’d think that here’s a hot spot – that’s sort of like feeling the signal.

Of course our bodies play tricks on us. You’ll think the phone is ringing or a message just come in, you look at it and there’s nothing. It’s probably just a twitching nerve or something, he says. We stand there for a while, trying to figure out how to talk about radio frequencies and mobile phone connectivity. The click and buzz from the loudspeakers reappears in our conversation. I ask what electromagnetic signals might look like? I imagine it a bit like the Wi-fi signal icon, you know, spreading out like a fan, he says. He looks across the courtyard. On the other hand, it’s probably more like a ball maybe, a bubble. Are we in a bubble now? I ask. Yeah, they are almost all over, sometimes they overlap, they move around too. Wind and weather, perhaps? We laugh a bit about imagining this invisible spectrum-stuff, but as I leave the studio, I retain in my guts reminiscences of the harsh sounding interference as something that grates, something that cuts and scratches my nerves through my skin, something ablative that yanks at cells and molecules. I can feel my hairs stand on end.

## Lightning rods

I am walking along with a friend in the city. I’m not doing research; we are chatting about very non-researcherly and normal things. We both hear the familiar ‘‘incoming message’’ ping from a phone. We jerk slightly at the sound, maybe also at what we think is a slight vibration from our phones? He first looks around, at me, then finally he halts right there on the sidewalk to (slightly annoyed) inspect his phone…no messages! I need to get my phone out too, just to be sure. A few metres down the street, another guy has stopped to check his phone. We manage a slight smile, considering the silly situation. Then we carry on.

Getting back into a shared rhythm is now a bit of a challenge: Where were we…? Instead of continuing the conversation where it was disrupted, I ask my friend instead to think back to the feeling of ghostly vibrations we both probably felt – or did not feel. It’s stress, he says. You are too vigilant about things when you carry your phone. It’s like this low-level constant disruption; you become a kind of lightning rod. Yeah, I nod. We walk along. How do you ‘‘feel’’ your phone, I ask. It’s a bit like a lightning rod, you know, it’s maybe like a shock or a little jolt, something that comes unexpectedly, there’s no system to it, it’s random. As we part, I try to imagine how it feels being a lightning rod, a conductive, grounded protrusion that reroutes and renders an electrical strike harmless.

I try to imagine people on the sidewalk as walking lightning rods, each with their own thunderous cloud of potential ‘‘stuff’’ suspended above them. Unfinished business, unanswered messages, persistent questions and forgotten appointments. I suddenly get a sense of urgency, a feeling of the same charged black cloud lingering over me, potentially bursting at the seams with messages and mails for my phone. I walk on, anxiously expecting an informational lightning to strike at any moment. My muscles tense a bit, and I feel my attention turning increasingly towards my body, a body that has become expectant, that begins to feel rigid and sore from waiting for something to happen. My feet walk along. I feel a bit withdrawn and I walk an arbitrary route, not really walking anywhere. I am trying to listen to my body, see if I can get that sense of a vibration I have experienced so often. I am not hearing much more than the occasional ringing ear and a murmuring stomach. I soon realize that I have put my phone in ‘‘do not disturb’’ mode; that it had become a rather inert piece of hardware tucked away in my jacket. Holding it in my hand and turning it back to ‘‘disturb’’ mode unleashes a passing blizzard of sounds and vibration, and my phone goes quiet again. I feel like I have emptied the cloud and that it now begins to build up a charge again, waiting to connect and vibrate again in my body. I walk along, feeling my own body beginning to build up a charge too, becoming tense with anticipation.

## Discussion

In the following I attempt to provide an analytical perspective on the three episodes related above. The intention is first to examine what, and how, things were brought to light in using the meditational approach as a guide. This part of the analysis is written in third person, referring to the ‘‘I’’ from the meditations as ‘‘the researcher’’. The change in perspective is meant to indicate the more general possibilities of the meditational approach.

The analysis is not concerned with an extraction of ‘‘meaning’’ from the meditation or how the matter of the meditations may be spun into various discursive trajectories. It does not take the form of an interpretive narrative that outlines a set of discrete implications or establishes a resolved meaning of the worlds under scrutiny. Instead, it emphasizes the genre appropriate undertaking of a meditational approach; not to confirm or convince, but to facilitate or intensify a particular affective disposition in the reader. The analysis is therefore concerned with the positioning of the researcher in the setting, and they also attempt to clarify what the meditations are illustrations of. The aim is to show how things such as affect, feeling or moods were learned about and articulated and how affect seemed transformative of the embodied researcher.

Secondly, I address more directly the process of writing meditations. This is done from a more personal position to indicate some of the practical struggles of writing, and it considers how the pedagogical/instructive features of the meditations are (hopefully) achieved.

Thirdly, I argue how the meditational approach can be included in a broader experiential computing research agenda that aims to participate in the sensory, affective and experiential worlds of others.

## Analysis of meditations

For all three episodes, the breakdown of an activity seems to have created the conditions for meditation. Breakdowns transform a situation from being taken-forgranted to being an opportunity for reflection (see e.g. Orlikowski, 1996; Weick & Quinn, 1999). The breakdowns in the three meditations are all subtle and rather familiar, arguable merely sufficient for disrupting a sense of the calm surface tensions that keep the senses from being acutely overloaded.

Taking a meditational approach, the question that guides the response to a breakdown is ‘‘what else is there… (beyond practice)’’. The meditational researcher does not ask ‘‘how do people carry their phones’’, ‘‘how do people avoid interference from invisible electro-magnetic radiation’’ or ‘‘how do people fix the flow of a conversation after a mobile phone interruption?’’ Rather, the breakdown is seen as an invitation to consult the senses and any felt aspects of the event that stand out in the form of embodied affect; this can be something subtle but it can also be something palpable and resonant.

In ‘‘Foreign Bodies’’, the researcher’s exhausted body is attracted to, and at the same time obstructed by, the mobile phone. The phone provides a chance to escape and unwind, to create a private bubble of calming sounds. But the phone slowly begins to intrude on the body; it becomes a burden. ‘‘Foreign bodies’’ suggests that beyond the mundane situation of walking with a mobile device, where the device is of purely practical or instrumental concern, lies the opportunity for appreciating the tensions between frustration and care that settles in the body in various forms. The rendering of a walk in a big city explores the unstable, shifting mindfulness that alternates between an immediate care for walking – that is, setting one foot in front of the other – and the increasing need to care for the kinds of mobile yet typically not ‘‘walk-with-able’’ (Marshall & Tennent, 2013) devices we carry with us through everyday life.

The researchers’ mood shifts back and forth between one of pleasure and relaxation and one of annoyance, following from a feeling of being incapacitated by physical discomfort where his ‘‘upper leg is somewhat inflexible, that I move differently to accommodate a blocky device in my pocket. I worry about the health of my phone. Will it get bent? Can it deal with the increasing dampness? Will it suffer?’’. The mood, towards the end, gradually shifts towards a sense of affection for the mobile device. As the meditation comes to a close, the researcher sits down and gives up. The phone has become too present and too intrusive, rendering his body too blocked and too rigid. And yet, in this developing sense of presence, in his troubled relationship with this thing that has become intrusive, the device nevertheless retains a more complex personality as an object for which he unexpectedly begins to feel a duty of care.

Mobilities such as walking ‘‘engenders new kinds of researchable entities and a new or rediscovered realm of the empirical’’ (Bu¨scher et al, 2011: p. 99). ‘‘Foreign bodies’’ features a snapshot of the subtle, surprising and intricate tensions between an increasing dependence on digital devices and the way these devices can impinge on mundane embodied performances. Walking can sensitize us to how our bodies are engaged in and affected by motions through a dense landscape of potentialities and obstacles (Kusenbach, 2003; Anderson, 2004; Edensor, 2010), but also how we increasingly carry obstacles (painfully blocky phones) and potentialities (empathy, care for a treasured device) around with us. Walking with a mobile technology subtly disrupts as well as facilitates mobility. In the meditation, the phone provides a particular (and paradoxical) shape to the moods and feelings involved in the overall experience of moving the body around on foot (see also Lee & Ingold, 2006; Eslambolchilar et al, 2016).

The next two meditations are examples of trying to cultivate and retain a conscious openness to feelings and moods in encounters with others. They entail a willingness ‘‘[to] see what will happen. To let the event sing to you’’ (Thrift, 2008: p. 12). In both ‘‘Spectrums’’ and ‘‘Lightning Rods’’, the researcher insists on trailing more faint feelings, intuitions and moods and the ways they are articulated and reflected on in a situated dialogue with a research informant and a friend. They confront the phantasmagorical imaginaries of particular dispositions of feelings developed in a dialogical form. These are fantasies that nevertheless seem to affect the body. Both meditations mobilize the idea that affect ‘‘not only traverses the opposition of the organic and the nonorganic; it also inserts the technical into felt vitality, the felt aliveness given in the pre-individual bodily capacities to act, engage, and connect – to affect and be affected’ (Clough, 2007, p. 2).

In these two meditations, the intensifying senses and feelings in the body of the researcher provide some initial shapes of new researchable entities: How does infrastructure actually feel? Do interruptions from mobile phones do something (subtle) to the body as well as to the mind? Beyond practices, beyond organizing with technology and perhaps slightly beyond the strictly ‘‘logical’’, how might a vague sense of electromagnetic energy as well as imaginary clouds of information settle in the body as uncanny vibrations, goose bumps or the feeling of a tense body that is gradually knotting up? How may the insubstantial be substantial?

Both of the meditations stage the scholar-with-a-body as a body-subject, porous to what some theorists have described as the contagious properties of feelings. The meditations stage affects, moods and feelings, as ‘‘transmissive’’. The researcher thus invites (or accepts) affective attunement (Blackman, 2012) and entrainment (Game, 2001) as part of an animated, vital relation to a social materiality.

In ‘‘Spectrums’’, the jarring sounds of interference from a mobile phone reveal an opportunity to learn about how digital infrastructures or connectivity can be felt, even if the suggestion is slightly playful and speculative. It tells the story of how a certain felt-ness of unseen ‘‘things’ translates into an ambience and into hairs standing on end. In following the informant’s off-hand remark about being able to imagine or see radio waves, the researcher remains open to the suggestive imagination proposed by the informant. Imagining and sensing radio waves and connectivity is a notion that does not necessarily translate into a practical concern. In the meditation, sensing radio waves emerges as a mundane, affective entanglement that generates a slight whisper in the researcher’s body; ‘‘I retain in my guts reminiscences of the harsh sounding interference as something that grates, something that cuts and scratches my nerves through my skin, something ablative that also yanks cells and molecules’’. Some form of energy manifests as a tingling or an intuition. Literally a gut feeling, invisible radio waves may be more subtly persistent in the everyday lives lived within the connection-scapes of a pervasive digital infrastructure.

Indeed, a sense of unseen energies might engender awe and fascination as well as become the source of tensions, anxiety and very real trauma. This, for instance, in the form of the felt (and yet likely imagined) associations between electromagnetic radiation from Wi-fi routers and constant, enervating headaches described in Bødker and Chamberlain (2016). Rather than trying to uncover the meaning of practices in the field, a new set of concerns emerges as the researcher attends to the invisible/imagined matter of electromagnetic spectra and begins to sense the outlines of a ghostly, energetic ‘‘Hertzian space’’ (Dunne, 1999). The situation that begins as an electronic noise that discharges into the room later comes to settle in the form of a slight nervousness or a tingling on his skin.

In ‘‘Lightning Rods’’ a mobile phone message and the way it interrupts a casual walk with a friend becomes the conduit for a dialogue on what it feels like when life involves an incessant flow of notifications and other interruptions from digital devices. The dialogue gradually turns away from an initially rationalizing, explanatory mode that compels the researchers friend to try to explain why we feel phantom vibrations (‘‘It’s stress, he says. You are too vigilant about things when you carry your phone…’’), into more imagined territory by way of a metaphor suggested by the research informant (‘‘…and you become a kind of lightning rod’’). The researcher then leaves the interaction with the image of the living lightning rods haunting him, trying ‘‘to imagine how it feels being a lightning rod, a conductive, grounded protrusion that reroutes and renders an electrical strike harmless’’. The mood, the sense of being a conducive opening for an ambient world of information, gradually becomes rooted in the researcher; it transpires through his body to become a way of relating to the world. He carries with him a mood that originated in the dialogue, not only as a sense of gloom or frustration, but as a gradually developing fantasy that connects to the mounting anticipatory tension in his body.

The researcher embraces and develops a benign hallucination, trying to sense the world as somehow electrified, buzzing with static, and potential energies ready to discharge. In that process, his body reacts by becoming slightly rigid and alert: ‘‘Like a live wire, the subject channels what’s going on around it in the process of its own self-composition. Formed by the coagulation of intensities, surfaces, sensations, perceptions, and expressions, it’s a thing composed of encounters and the spaces and events it traverses or inhabits’’ (Stewart, 2007: p. 79). His researcherly attention becomes jumpy, like a sore nerve exposed to the elements, a soreness that temporarily moulds his mind and his body into a new shape.

## On writing meditations

What does it mean to write from an affective and embodied perspective? I suggest that the writing up and ‘‘re-collection’’ of episodes of felt life must be approached as a process that is integral to the meditational method. Writing the meditations is indeed a form of inquiry (Richardson, 2000). It is a process of struggling to enrol an audience into felt worlds of the self and others (Ellis, 1999), as well as a generative training ground for inquiries that are transformative of both the author and the discipline.

The renderings become meditational by drawing on the genre conventions and pragmatics characteristic of the meditational piety texts, which urge the reader to feel along with the author. The text should invite the reader to learn about the possible and sometimes puzzling sensate worlds of others. It must seek the pragmatic aim to somehow transform the reader.

Meditations always come from a subjective and fragile position, thereby raising fundamental questions about the author’s reliability. Rather than relying on an inherent or objective measure of the truthfulness of the event, the meditational approach is partly successful by being transformative of the audience. This, of course, requires an attention to the craft of writing, which is often purposefully aesthetic and evocative. The challenge is to build a scene of life that feels alive, engaging and believable to the reader.

Since the kind of attentiveness that I encourage obviously comes and goes in the thick everyday life, the meditations above are necessarily selected, edited and condensed in the process of writing. While all three situations involved an effort to maintain an affective introspection, the process and the outcome is accentuated and condensed in the narrative’s written after the fact. The actual situations were certainly messier, more chaotic and less linear than they appear in writing.

Not all situations of encountering IT in everyday life develop into meditational research opportunities. Some situations stand out as useful, insightful and interesting; others – materialized as stacks of scattered notes and short, struggling vignettes that were less convincingly concerned with paying attention to feelings – have been abandoned or consigned to the drawer. After several attempts suddenly discovering a compelling and engaging event and developing a suitable account, the phenomenon – e.g. walking, interference and felt connectivity, or information overload – seems to open up with a renewed energy and with unexpected nuances that were not necessarily apparent as the event emerged. When I begin writing, it feels as though I am confronting the phenomena for the first time. Later in the process, the feelings I had during the event often become familiar and recognizable again. Writing thus becomes a tool with which to reencounter or re-collect an affective event.

Writing meditations is therefore also transformative of the author. Writing up each episode germinates a heightening awareness of the richness of an event and the potential that lies at the core of asking ‘‘what else is there…?’’ The feelings and moods of a situation are always in some way underspecified and often devoid of semantics and meaning that can be grasped. They nevertheless do something unintended or surprising to the body; they ‘‘stand out’’ to punctuate the attempt at grasping the event intellectually or reflectively. Consider how certain details in a photograph may unexpectedly stand out to ‘‘prick’’ the viewer and attain the almost viscerally bruising sense that Barthes described as punctum (Barthes, 1981: p. 47). This notion of puncturing has encouraged me to develop further a sense of tuning-into unanticipated happenings and events that tickle and stir me.

In developing and presenting my meditations, I have been iterating between a conscious effort of affective (self-) introspection, writing messy notes and writing up narratives that condense and emphasize aspects of felt experiences that I felt compelled to explore. Often, in sitting down to write up a meditation, it came out trivial and uninteresting, prompting me to try to attend once more to my own feelings involved in some mundane interaction with a technology. This has included impromptu re-enactments of parts of an event (e.g. trying to walk with a bulky phone in a trouser pocket) or attempts to recall vague sensory impressions I have written about in my notes (e.g. closing my eyes to try to re-imagine the walking lightning rods).

Writing becomes part of the meditational exercise by providing a reflective space. In the struggle to write, I am increasingly aware of how developing the meditations relates to subsequent events and nascent embodied sensibilities. The re-encountering of feelings provides a guidance for subsequent attending. When something stands out, and when I allow myself a brief introspective moment, a situation now tends to leave me with a palpable impression, a tinge or the indistinct shape of a mood. The mood holds a promise to reveal some surprising way in which feelings briefly modulate the body and thus leave a mark on everyday life with IT. I find myself sitting in my office or walking my department listening to sounds from digital infrastructures and devices. I search for connections between sounds and levels of activity, and for the way ambient sounds of IT create transitory places of casual interaction (e.g. people showing YouTube videos to each other in the hallway), studious concentration (e.g. click-click-clacking laptop keyboards) or the condition of an invisible device (e.g. I can tell that I have turned on the ceiling-mounted projector when I hear a slight hum from the fan) … which then leads me back to my keyboard, to writing and exploring … and so on.

The writing of meditations, in this way, is a vehicle for (re-)learning how to sense. It guides the researcher in cultivating further a sensory awareness in those situations that may otherwise transpire without attempting to grasp what living with technologies might feel like.

Drawing on the genre conventions inherited from the meditational piety text, the success of meditations rests to a large degree on the ability to evoke a sense of empathy or sensation in the reader. Any approach to representing felt life – in text or otherwise – is likely to engender widely different kinds of responses: various degrees of empathy and different levels of recognition and confusion. They offer considerable interpretational flexibility. If a rendering is successful, it can begin to nurture new dialogues, new curiosities and new appreciations of human–technological entanglements. It may also solicit new ways of framing what counts as experiential.

The written word certainly remains the primary modality for reporting academic work. Textual representations of felt life, however, can quickly become rather stale artefacts that do not live up to the promise of tickling the reader. Learning from the arts, visual, acoustic, olfactory, gustatory and even palpable representations may significantly expand the representational registers of experiential computing research. Using other forms of media in the representation of felt life can open up new avenues for embodied and participatory research.

The transformational effect that a meditational approach may have on the discipline of IS and the notion of experiential computing is developed in the following section. I emphasize how the meditations, even if they rely on what I called ‘‘affective introspection’’ and renderings of the researcher’s own felt experience, can also be a way to stimulate a different approach to participation in IS and experiential computing. Meditations, so I argue, are also a way of immersing oneself as a researcher into the felt worlds of the people we do research on or with.

## Towards sensory/affective participation

I situated meditations within an ‘‘everyday research’ agenda as suggested by Brinkmann (2012). Broadly, this implies that researchers use their own lived experience as a springboard for exploring the wider implications of a phenomenon. The meditational approach suggests that researchers can cultivate a sensory attentiveness in personal, as well as social and collective research encounters that are centred on the variety and richness of everyday felt experiences with IT.

The turn to practice and the associated turn towards user participation in design have supported a broad and critica understanding of everyday technology users as agential and empowered rather than passive receivers of technology (see, e.g. Dahlbom & Matthiasen, 1993). This paper set out to go ‘‘beyond practice’’. If digital technologies are everywhere, a meditational response to experiential computing asks how these technologies create particular conditions for feeling and sensing, and how these might extend and complement our understanding of how technologies create new ways of organizing or acting.

Based in part on Stoller’s (1997) work on sensory scholarship and Lave’s (1988) work on situated learning, Pink (2009) has proposed the idea of the ethnographer as a sensory apprentice. She argues that ‘‘often moments of sensory learning are not necessarily planned processes through which a particular research question is pursued in a structured way as it might be in the context of a survey or even a semi-structured interview’’ (Pink, 2009, p. 65). Learning about how other people sense and feel their world is rarely deliberate. Through cultivating a mindful openness towards the possibility that experiential computing has a felt component, something that settles in and modulates the body, insights can emerge serendipitously and in unexpected places.

Importantly, performing a sensory scholarship in IS does not have to take place in the workplace or in situations where IT is conspicuously present. Indeed, it does not have to take place in the confined conceptual space of a research setting. If we pay sufficient attention, everyday life in a world of ubiquitous IT turns out to be vital and animated, abounding with affective forces and fleeting moods. Developing sensory apprenticeship and participation entails ‘‘taking a series of conceptual and practical steps that allow the researcher to rethink both established and new participatory and collaborative ethnographic research techniques in terms of sensory perception, categories, meanings and values, ways of knowing and practices’’ (Pink, 2009: p. 9). In attempting to extend the methodological foundations for doing research on experiential computing in IS, a meditational approach acknowledges that there is a possibility to learn more about ephemeral, corporeal sensations and those issues that would perhaps otherwise be lost in the headlong business of doing work in the field.

Meditations encourage scholars to consult their bodies as they continuously try to understand ‘‘what else is there…’’ Meditations also urge scholars to acknowledge that their own bodies, as well as excessive and contagiously surprising tales, metaphors and stories co-articulated with research informants or even close companions, may become useful guides that can render felt life with technology accessible and relatable. Senses, moods and feelings are not strictly private. Working from an attentiveness to the self (i.e. ‘‘how do I feel?’’) and writing about the body supports an ongoing (if always incomplete) awareness of a porous self, that is, the self as a body-subject readily exposed to the potential contagion of countless affective materialities.

Consider, for instance, how walking with others works as a means for investigating mobile technologies from the vantage point of moving bodies immersed in assemblages of fleeting sensory modulations. Retaining an open channel to a collective sense that emerges from a shared rhythm of walking with a mobile or wearable technology, can become a conduit for a more deliberate curiosity about moods or the shifting engagement of sensory modalities. Physical performances such as walking might indeed contribute to a ‘‘sensory embodied experience as a basis from which to empathise with others’’ (Pink, 2007: p. 243). Or consider how paying attention to the (designed as well as incidental) sounds of IT in your own life might become a way to explore how soundscapes are part of publicly circulating and fluid textures that affect bodies and shape the ‘‘Stimmung’’ (i.e. tuning) of events; how the feeling of things vibrates below a surprisingly unremarkable surface of technological entanglements.

## Concluding remarks

I began this paper by noticing a range of theorists who lamented the lack of emotions, reflections on embodiment, affect, movement and sensuality in the current academic landscape. This concern is particularly relevant in the context of experiential computing, which Yoo (2010) advances as a new IS research agenda. This paper illustrates how meditations, as both a practice and a genre of representation, afford the exploration of moods and feelings of felt experience that are rarely considered in our investigations of human–technology relations.

In foregrounding affect, embodiment, fleeting sensations and moods as important leitmotifs, meditations as affective introspection and as a style of reporting that aims to invoke, on the part of the reader, a sense of an affectively charged situation, might help IS discover and develop new researchable phenomena that have hitherto
