## Abstract

Timely reporting of rare infectious disease cases to the public health system, especially after identification at laboratories, is essential to initiate quick and effective public health response. To ensure that the public health reporting system is appropriately monitoring the rare infectious diseases under surveillance, it is recommended to have a regular assessment of timeliness, especially after the rare infectious case is confirmed. This study aimed to evaluate the timeliness of data reported to the Ohio Disease Reporting System (ODRS), a public health reporting system in Ohio, for managing rare infectious diseases. In a cross-sectional analysis of rare infectious disease reporting data in four local health jurisdictions (LHJs) in the state of Ohio, wide delays were found between various reporting steps, particularly when the laboratories were not using the electronic method of reporting, and the delay observed was mainly at the hospital level and at the LHJ level. This study highlights the supply chain nature of information transfer and calculates the delay at various interacting points of the information supply chain system. The results establish that a centralized approach with an electronic disease reporting system conveys information faster than traditional reporting channels (decentralized approach). Delays of the decentralized approach are isolated at various stakeholder levels and with respect to various

types of rare infectious diseases for better understanding of the information supply chain system for managing rare infectious diseases.

Keywords: Information supply chain system, coordination theory, health care reporting, lead time in reporting

## 1. Introduction

Reporting cases of rare infectious diseases is important for the detection of common-source outbreaks, the assurance of appropriate medical therapy, and the planning and evaluation of disease prevention and control programs by the public health system (Chorba et al., 1989). The timely transfer of information on rare infectious disease incidences to the public health authorities is necessary to ensure effective public health response (Reijn et al., 2011). A good public health information system (HIS) like any health information technology has great potential to improve quality and reduce the cost of healthcare (Khatri & Gupta, 2014). Healthcare organizations are investing and participating in a growing number of interorganizational healthcare systems such as community health information networks and integrated delivery systems (Payton, 2000). In a specially convened expert workshop on the systematic evaluation of HISs, the participants identified the importance of design, planning, execution, and on-time reporting in building a proper evidence base that can be used for making informed decisions and subsequent interventions by using health informatics (Talmon et al., 2009). Haux (2006) envisioned the shift from institution-centered departmental and later hospital information systems toward regional and global HISs and the use of the collected data for epidemiological

research. The author encouraged the development and investigation of appropriate transinstitutional information system architectures for better disease reporting, adequate methods for strategic information management, and advocated methods for modeling and evaluating HISs in the healthcare sector. Haux also recommended changes focused on sharing of information between various stakeholders in the system and ensuring timeliness in reporting of infectious diseases.

Delays in reporting of rare infectious diseases can cause a danger to public safety. The United States (US) experienced a nationwide outbreak of EV-D68 in 2014. State public health laboratories confirmed 1153 cases of EV-D68 in 49 states and the District of Columbia with respiratory illness caused by EV-D68, from mid-August 2014 to January 15, 2015. Most of the confirmed cases were children. The Center of Disease Control (CDC) received approximately 2,600 specimens for enterovirus laboratory testing during 2014, of which 36% tested positive for EV-D68 and another 33% for an enterovirus or rhinovirus other than EV-D68. There were likely millions of people mildly infected with EV-D68 who did not seek medical treatment and/or get tested, and 14 patients were detected with EV-D68 after they died. This example shows the serious consequence of delays in reporting rare infectious diseases both at the laboratory level and at the hospital level for treatment and prevention purposes. State public health authorities have the authority and responsibility to determine and release information about the incidence of rare infectious diseases to contain the spread of the disease. Naturally, any reduction in delay times in the information supply chain assists in achieving this goal (“Enterovirus D68,” 2015).

To evaluate the timeliness of reporting to the public health system, we looked at lead times in the reporting of rare infectious diseases after its confirmation at various stakeholder levels such as laboratories, hospitals, and local health departments until it reaches the public health reporting system. Timeliness is the key construct (Ward et al., 2005; Birkhead et al., 1991) because as time passes, it would be more difficult to contain the spread of these rare infectious diseases (Reijn et al., 2011). Jajosky and Groseclose (2004) reviewed studies involving the timeliness of the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS) data to understand and evaluate whether the existing reporting systems could support timely notification and state response to multistate outbreaks and suggested further research on timeliness in reporting of diseases in the US.

A study conducted by Ward et al. (2005) on the infectious disease reporting system in the Netherlands evaluated the timeliness of surveillance data on infectious diseases reported from the regional to the national level. They compared a “paper-based reporting system” with an Internet reporting system known as the Online System for Infectious Disease Reporting within ISIS (OSIRIS). ISIS refers to the Infectious Disease Surveillance Information System. The researchers assessed the delay at two distinct levels: (1) total delay (defined as the time between symptom onset and reporting at the national level) and (2) central delay (defined as the time between regional and national reporting). The research found that the median central delay was significantly lower for all diseases in OSIRIS than the conventional reporting system. This USbased study evaluated the differences in lead times at various stakeholder levels between a traditional reporting system and an online reporting system after confirmation of rare infectious diseases.

At the local public health level, timeliness can be achieved by minimizing delays at various levels of reporting including hospitals, laboratories, and local health departments (LHDs)

##

or LHJs. Similar to the inter-organizational product information supply chain (Legner & Schemm, 2008), information on rare infectious diseases is being passed from one stakeholder to another in county public health systems. Hence, the whole process from the identification of disease to reporting to the public health system for preventive actions can be captured in the form of an information supply chain system for managing rare infectious diseases (Marinos, 2005; Sun & Yen, 2005). A public health department would benefit from timeliness in reporting rare infectious diseases as it improves the responsiveness to any occurrence of rare infectious diseases through control and preventive measures (Reijn et al., 2011). In the information supply chain view for managing rare infectious diseases discussed in this research, we focused on the delay in reporting of rare infectious diseases at each stakeholder level and the importance of coordination between the stakeholders to ensure timeliness at the local health level.

Scholars and practitioners have long advocated the benefits of reduced lead times in reporting infectious diseases including rare and emerging infectious diseases (Jajosky & Groseclose 2004; Milinovich et al., 2014; Harvey et al., 2013; Magill et al., 2014). Jajosky and Groseclose (2004) investigated the median reporting delay for various notifiable infectious diseases from the date of disease onset to the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS) and suggested that “further study is needed to identify the major sources of reporting delay” and to assess “how reporting timeliness may be improved for the timely detection of cases and clusters” (p. 9).

Even though the public health system would gain from reduced lead times in the information supply chain system, particularly in the case of infectious diseases and more specifically rare infectious diseases, the system has been slow to radically reduce lead time. This

##

research applies coordination theory to answer the following research question. Which actors are responsible for delays in the information supply chain for managing rare infectious diseases and how can timeliness be improved? Data were collected from four LHJs in Ohio for 2 years. The information supply chain system was described with the help of detailed process maps and performance measures. Simulation was applied to gain further understanding of the information supply chain system behavior. The data demonstrated that current collection, identification, and data transfer practices have a major impact on the lead time in the information supply chain of rare infectious diseases. This study discusses the role electronic laboratory reporting (ELR) and non-electronic laboratory reporting (non-ELR) may have on the information delivery process. It argues that the disadvantages of the non-ELR method are poorly understood from an information supply chain perspective. The non-ELR method generates longer lead times not only at the laboratory level but also at the hospital and LHJ levels. Knowing the key actors responsible for the delay provides a tool to use in managing major resources to avoid reporting delay and improve timely detection of rare infectious diseases.

## 1.2 Rare Infectious Diseases- Definition and Classification

“The definition of a rare disease is based on disease prevalence, which is defined as the total number of cases of the disease in the population at a given time. This means that at any point of time, there must be fewer than 200,000 people affected with a condition in order for it to be considered a rare disease. The definition for a rare disease is not based on incidence, which is a measure of the risk of developing a disease within a specified period of time.” (M. Greenwood, Information Specialist, Genetic and Rare Diseases Information Center, personal communication, April 12, 2012).

##

Rare diseases encompass genetic diseases, rare cancers, auto-immune disorders, congenital anomalies, toxin-induced diseases, infectious diseases, and other diseases such as drug-resistant communicable diseases. Among the rare diseases, infectious diseases need immediate attention (Brooks & Hoberg, 2006). However, the US, Australia, and Canada do not have national or provincial plans for managing these diseases (Elger, 2011). This study limits the discussion to rare infectious diseases in the US alone because the healthcare management systems in the US, European countries, and other parts of the world vary considerably (CSL Behring, 2011). The very fact that the infectious disease is rare makes its diagnosis difficult (Kirchmayr et al., 2008), and many rare infectious diseases are not diagnosed properly or are diagnosed only after several hospital visits or after consultations with multiple doctors (Al-Eissa et al., 1991). In many cases of rare infectious diseases, the symptoms are nonspecific and the doctors will simply be treating the patient’s symptoms. If the symptoms are unusual, the doctor may be motivated to refer the patient to a specialist. At other times, the motivation for referral is nonresponsiveness to treatments (Storla et al., 2008).

For the purpose of parsimony and understanding the rare infectious disease reporting in detail, we have classified the rare infectious diseases according to the CDC classification. They are classified into Class A, Class B1, Class B2, and Class C diseases depending on the severity of the public health concern. According to the Infectious Disease Control Manual, Class A diseases are defined as “diseases of major public health concern because of the severity of disease or potential for epidemic spread.” Class B (includes B1 and B2) diseases are “disease of public health concern needing timely response because of potential for epidemic spread.” Class C diseases are “an outbreak, unusual incident or epidemic of other diseases (e.g., histoplasmosis, pediculosis, scabies, staphylococcal infections)” (ICDM, May, 2015). Although the reporting

time varies, reporting authorities are encouraged to report the incident quickly (“Ohio’s Communicable Disease Reporting Requirements,” 2015). For Class A diseases, immediate reporting is mandatory after a case, suspected case, or a positive laboratory result. For Class B1 diseases, reporting can be done by the end of the next business day after the existence of a case, suspected case, or a positive laboratory result. For class B2 diseases, reporting can be done at the end of the work week after the existence of a case, suspected case, or a positive laboratory result. For Class C diseases (including an outbreak, unusual incidence, or epidemic), it should be reported by the end of the next business day (“Ohio’s Communicable Disease Reporting Requirements,” 2015).

## 1.3 Theoretical Background

King et al. (1989) argued that specific organizational factors may facilitate an organization’s ability to integrate and use information resources such as the existence of extensive computing facilities and strong technical support staff, which are crucial in implementing the information system. Their main contribution was that “supplier relations” and coordination stand out as a specific area of opportunity for the successful implementation of information management systems.

Building on the overarching framework of coordination theory (March & Simon, 1958; Malone, 1988; Malone & Crowston, 1994; Crowston, 1997), the interorganizational information supply chain system for managing rare infectious diseases is characterized by a set of dependencies between various stakeholders, which needs to be explicitly managed. Malone and Crowston (1994) defined coordination as the process of managing dependencies between activities. To understand the coordination process in detail, organizations can be viewed as being composed of actors, goals, and resources. The actors in this case are various stakeholders including laboratories, hospitals, and LHJs. The goal is to reduce lead time. Resources include communication medium and facilities involved in the collection and transformation of information. Coordination theory provides the theoretical background for the study, which focuses on the problems dependencies create and how improved coordination can address those problems. Malone and Crowston (1994) mostly focused on dependencies as arising between tasks, which is applicable to this study as we closely analyze the process design. In addition, coordination theory has the advantage of making it easier to model the effects of reassignments of activities to different actors, which can help in process redesign efforts. The coordination theory constructs, description, relevance to an information supply chain for rare infectious diseases, and the case study design and data collection are given in Table 1.

The Ohio Disease Reporting System (ODRS) was developed as a web-based system to make disease reporting more timely and efficient for disease reporters (e.g., hospitals, laboratories, and physicians) and to improve communication about infectious diseases among disease reporters, LHJs, and ODRS, with only a few authorized persons allowed to enter the details into the system (“Annual Summary of Reportable Diseases,” 2009). Coordination problems, a consequence of dependencies in the organization, constrain the efficiency of performing tasks such as preventive and control measures. To achieve the common desired outcomes, the multiple actors and interactions, resources, and goals need to be coordinated, and hence coordination theory is well suited for addressing this research (Kishore et al., 2006). Instead of focusing on one problem as a separate entity from the other, coordination theory focuses on how components of a system under study interact (e.g., how lead time from

laboratories to hospitals interacts with lead time from LHJs and its final entry into ODRS) (Crowston, 1997).

Information supply chains for managing rare infectious diseases are systems in the sense that they are composed of elements that interact to produce a predetermined behavior or output (e.g., the action to be taken by a public health department to control the spread of diseases). Traditional analysis focuses on isolating individual parts. Instead we are using a systems thinking approach in the information supply chain system for managing rare infectious diseases. This approach expands the analytical spectrum and examines how the constituent parts including hospitals, laboratories, LHJs, and the ODRS interact with one another resulting in the coordination outcome (i.e., timeliness).

## 2. Materials and Methods

We selected rare infectious diseases according to the list of rare diseases provided by the National Institute of Health. Notifications on rare infectious diseases from January 1, 2010 to March 31, 2012 were retrieved from the ODRS, including the date of symptom onset, date of laboratory-confirmed diagnosis, date at which it is reported to local hospitals, date of reporting to LHJs, and date of reporting to the ODRS. Because the reporting is extremely important after laboratory confirmation, we focused on two different types of reporting: (1) the centralized reporting system where laboratories directly report the cases online to ODRS and (2) the decentralized approach where the laboratory sends the report back to the hospitals. For each case, we determined the time intervals between the confirmatory laboratory report (zero time point) from the laboratory to the hospital and from hospitals to the LHJs and finally the time taken at LHJ to enter into ODRS.

## 2.1 Methods

In accordance with the CDC classification of diseases, we classified the rare infectious diseases selected for the study into Type A, B1, B2, and C. Because there was no outbreak or food-borne diseases in the county for the period of study, there were no Type C rare infectious diseases included in this study. From the empirical analysis of the collected data, this study has identified three major delays: (1) delay at the laboratory level, (2) delay of reporting at the hospital to the LHJs, and (3) delay in entering data at the LHJs to ODRS. When the laboratory adopts the ELR method for reporting of rare infectious diseases, it actually bypasses hospitals and LHJs and reports confirmatory results directly to the ODRS. For the purpose of parsimony, this research refers to such reporting directly into the ODRS as a centralized reporting system, and it is already considered to be the best method of reporting. Because fewer people are authorized to report directly into the system and many stakeholders follow traditional methods of reporting such as fax and telephone, this study focused on the nonelectronic reporting (decentralized reporting system) of rare infectious diseases as there are delays at various stakeholder levels in the decentralized traditional reporting system. This study also examined the delay in the centralized reporting system, even though the main focus was on the decentralized reporting system after the disease confirmation at the laboratories.

## 4. A Simulation Modeling Approach

Simulation-based methods are used to develop frameworks to evaluate and improve the performance of supply chain structures (Swaminathan et al., 1998; Valluri & Croson, 2005; Zeng & Sycara, 1999). We employed simulation with Arena to conduct the research on interorganizational dependencies and lead time issues associated with the information supply chain for managing rare infectious diseases. The process of modeling the system with simulation can help in observing the operation of the system over time, considering the influence of internal and external factors, and helping forecast the system’s behavior in different circumstances (Teilans et al., 2008). Because of the modeling flexibility, simulation is often regarded as the proper means for supporting decision-making on supply chain designs and “the ultimate success of supply chain simulation is determined by a combination of the analyst's skills, the chain members' involvement, and the modeling capabilities of the simulation tool” (Van Der Zee & Van Der Vorst, 2005).

We selected Arena for its modeling capabilities to provide a realistic simulation model, which is both transparent and complete. We strongly focused on all inter-dependencies and key attributes of different lead times in the information supply chain of rare infectious diseases and focused in developing a realistic simulation model for our study. For achieving this, we assessed the simulation model attributes that are essential for supporting a successful design of an

information supply chain, which can be generalizable and replicable. Then we matched the nonelectronic method to the electronic method to compare the lead time in both methods.

## 4.1 Simulation Model Development

The static information supply chain model for managing rare infectious diseases was transitioned into a dynamic simulation model with the addition of fitted distributions to represent entity flow and the logic within the process steps. Features such as delay and record blocks were added for statistics collection. These statistics are used to capture the performance measures of interest, including the key performance indicator of timeliness, i.e., the lead times at various stakeholder layers.

## 4.2 Context and Scope

The subject organization for this study is a county public health system that provides control and preventive services for a large population (approximately 380,000) distributed across four LHJs. For this study, we built and studied a simulation model based on the delay and lead time in processing or transferring information with respect to several rare infectious diseases over 2 years.

Each activity is defined as a process in the simulation model with the associated fitted statistical distributions for the activity times (i.e., lead times or delay at various stages). Source blocks were created to model the arrival of entities (i.e., patients) into the hospital, followed by decision blocks coded with logic to implement the decisions. The data collected provided the distributions for the percentage of entities in and out of each decision block. The inputs and outputs of each activity represent the potential paths that an entity can take through the system. We recorded lead times at stakeholder levels including at the laboratory, at the hospital before it is transferred to different LHJs, and at the different LHJs before it is entered into ODH/ODRS using the Record features in Arena. The key performance indicators (delays or lead times) were collected with respect to various factors including disease types, laboratory types, and jurisdiction types. Input Analyzer was used to find the fitted distribution of various lead times to ensure model accuracy and validation (Altiok & Melamed, 2001).

## 4.6 Validation

Trace-driven and self-driven are two types of simulation models categorized based on the way that they are driven. “A self-driven (distribution-driven or probabilistic) simulation model is the one which is driven by input values obtained via sampling from probability distributions using random numbers. A trace-driven (or retrospective) simulation model, on the other hand, is driven by input sequences derived from trace data obtained through measurement of the real system” (Balci, 1989, p. 64). In the trace-driven simulation model, the trace data become the input data model, which should be validated against the actual system input process (Balci, & Sargent, 1983). The simulation model employed in this research is trace-driven and hence was validated by comparing the means of the real life data with the simulated averages (Balci, 1990; Balci, 1995). The sufficiently large data sample, correct formulation of the problem, and tracedriven validation make this simulation model sufficiently credible and acceptable for decision makers (Balci, 1995).

## 5. Discussion

Similar to the study conducted by M’ikanatha et al. (2003), we found that electronic reporting systems hold promise for modernizing rare infectious disease surveillance by making reporting more timely. The study by Bellika et al. (2007) found that “up-to-date epidemiological data from the local population may be provided directly from the source EHR (Electronic Health Record) system within 4 seconds,” provided the system architecture is proper and general practitioners have full access to electronic health record systems. Implementing EHR can help improve public health preparedness. While implementing information supply chain systems in healthcare, special attention needs to be given to the systems’ graphical user interface, design quality, feature functionality, project management, procurement, and how previous experience affects implementation outcomes. Privacy, efficiency, liability, and adequate training on the system are also required for the successful implementation of information supply chain systems (Ludwick & Doucette, 2009). Haux (1997) recommended the need for further development of methods and

tools of information processing for early recognition and prevention, health reporting, healthcare information systems, and knowledge-based decision support.

One may argue that digitization can solve the problem permanently. However, looking at the scenario, many of the local hospitals are still not equipped to enter the details digitally into ODRS. ODRS access is available only to epidemiologists, limited laboratories, and LHJ officials. Glouberman and Mintzberg (2001) commented that hospitals are “extraordinary complex organizations… a good deal of the technology of medicine is ‘high.’ But most of that is delivered in small, disconnected applications.” Wang et al. (2005) found that “hospital organizational and financial factors influence hospitals' strategic adoption of clinical, administrative, and managerial information systems.” Those hospitals that initiate the process by reporting electronically have the details of the cases already in the ODRS are likely to obtain the information back electronically from laboratories after disease confirmation. However, because such entries are not widespread, complete digitization may not be feasible at this point of time.

Another issue is the level of partnership between various actors in the information supply chain system for managing rare infectious diseases. Each organization involved in this information supply chain system plays a unique role or delivers a valuable service that the partnership requires. The truth is that public health partnerships “often cannot afford to have participating organizations forgo joining the partnership, nor can they afford to have them drop out or drag their heels. Nonetheless, partnership governing bodies have few levers to pull to stimulate participation” (Weiner & Alexander, 1998). Thus coordination becomes integral to the success of the information supply chain system and is unfortunately lacking as revealed in this study.

According to the American Hospital Association of Ohio, there are 183 hospitals in Ohio (Ohio Hospital Facts, 2011). Of the 183 hospitals, only 71 (38.8%) hospitals are currently using the ODRS system for reporting rare infectious diseases. The plausible reasons include inadequate staff, lack of accessibility to ODRS, IP address conflict, lack of proper encouragement from LHJ to report rare infectious diseases electronically, and system incompatibility existing between various actors involved in the information supply chain system for reporting infectious diseases.

State and LHJs benefit from timeliness in reporting of rare infectious diseases, which allows timely reaction to contain the spread of rare infectious diseases and take crucial preventive measures. Ensuring timeliness in the information supply chain for rare infectious diseases is particularly challenging with the number of stakeholders involved in identification and confirmation followed by treatment, control, and preventive measures. The ODH and LHJs depend on stakeholders not only to provide information in time but also in an accurate manner. One way of addressing this challenge is to analyze how stakeholders respond (with regard to timeliness) to different situations (with respect to type of disease, the laboratory locations, reporting method, etc.), which we have performed in this research to understand the sources of delay in detail. This allows measurement of lead times at various stages of reporting and how much each stakeholder or layer contributes to the overall delay. The ability to measure the lead times in reporting allows us to explore whether any change in the reporting process will result in a possible reduction in lead time in the reporting of rare infectious diseases.

## 6. Conclusions

Evaluation of the existing information supply chain system is extremely important to further develop the methods and tools for rare infectious disease reporting. Evaluation is the act of measuring or exploring properties of an existing information supply chain system (in planning, development, implementation, or operation) to assess the quality, value, effects, and impacts. The result can help in making informed decisions in a specific context as suggested by Ammenwerth et al. (2004). Our study also supports the assertion by Austin et al. (1995) that the management of healthcare information systems “can no longer be left to system professionals if strategic benefit is to be realized.” In this study, we proposed the passing of information from one stakeholder to another to be viewed as an information supply chain system where several stakeholders are involved and coordinated.

The development of an efficient public health information supply chain management system with all stakeholders’ full participation can provide a collaborative advantage and longterm economic benefits. Collaborative advantage is defined as the “benefit gained by a group of participants as the result of their cooperation” (Ferrat et al., 2008). The information on rare infectious diseases becomes extremely important once the laboratory confirms the disease. When it is reported by ELR, it is entered directly into ODRS, which minimizes delay at the hospital or at the various LHJs in ELR. However, we have observed that a considerable number of confirmed cases are still reported through nonelectronic reporting, which increases the delay and hinders the timeliness of important actions by the public health authorities.

Lead time is a critical measure of a rare infectious disease information supply chain’s performance. It affects responsiveness and can ultimately lead to economic loss from an increasing spread of diseases. This paper presents results of a study on the analysis of an

information supply chain’s lead time for rare infectious diseases. The main contribution of the study is understanding the root cause of the delay in reporting and where the delay occurs. Any further action can be taken only if we can identify the origin of lead limes. Lead time reduction is a great way to improve responsiveness to contain the spread of infectious diseases through streamlining resources and operations. This study focused on the actors that extend lead times and thus negatively affect responsiveness. It is impossible to streamline the process, allocate resources, or take measures to correct delay when you are missing the root causes or sources of delay.

## 7. Limitations and Future Research

These findings highlight the challenges confronting the integration of an information supply chain characterized by reporting delays, lack of data sharing, and limited use of the World Wide Web in rare infectious disease reporting. Despite the contributions of this research, there are certain limitations worth noting. This study, conducted in northeast Ohio, is geographically limited. This study specifically focused on the timeliness aspect, which may be influenced by many quality factors from a technology standpoint, user training, etc., which can be considered in future research. Other issues that need immediate research attention are the privacy, efficiency, and liability on the existing information supply chain system for managing rare infectious diseases. In such cases, there is further research required on the governance issue of integrated information systems as suggested by Alexander et al. (1995). It is interesting to note that there is a high variation in the accuracy and completeness in reporting of notifiable infectious disease in the US (Doyle et al., 2002), and an interesting study would be to assess

accuracy in reporting at various stakeholder levels to obtain a better picture about the completeness in reporting.

Understanding the reasons for delay is another important consideration. For example, part of the delay at LHJs may be attributed to determining the “right jurisdiction” before entering the report into the ODRS. Often, LHJs find the laboratory report sent to them does not belong to them and have to redirect it to the correct jurisdiction before being entered into the system, and this causes delay. Future research could focus on the root causes of delay to minimize the delay at various stakeholder levels in the system.
