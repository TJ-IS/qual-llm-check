## ABSTRACT

This study measures individual resilience to cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems and explores the antecedents of resilience. The research model integrates the resilience literature with the fear appraisal literature to address individuals’ fears of cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems. The model is tested using data collected through an online survey. The results show that (1) community support and family financial management significantly increase resilience toward cyberterrorist attacks, and (2) resilience to and fear of cyberterrorist attacks significantly influence individuals’ behaviors in handling their finances. This research helps IT practitioners to develop strategies to build cyber resilience capacities among individuals and communities.

Keywords: information security, cyber security, cyberterrorist, cyberterrorism, fear appeals, resilience

## 1. Introduction

Cyberterrorism is the ability of terrorists to conduct terrorist actions in cyberspace with the intent of creating violence and destruction on or even death of its target. It plays with the joint force of psychology, ideology, and economy to promote great fear in society (Gable, 2009). In essence, cyberterrorists might target critical infrastructure systems (e.g., power grids, water supplies, and financial and banking systems) in cyberspace to create disastrous societal and economic impacts. Recent studies in information systems (IS) suggest that more research on cyberterrorism is urgently needed (Hua & Bapna, 2012). Industries also made a similar call for building an ecosystem resilient to cyber threats (Ayoub, Firth, & Nayaz, 2016).

Financial systems and markets have been specially and routinely targeted by attackers from a plethora of terrorist groups because these systems are heavily reliant on the Internet and telecommunications technologies. The threat to these systems is salient also because potential gains from a successful attack for terrorists could be substantial, ranging from stealing money to fund terrorist actions to crashing national, even global financial markets (Gable, 2009). Many experts have projected that massive cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems and institutions are possible and might result in catastrophic pitfalls that could spark domino impacts on the national economy and personal finance (Gable, 2009; SEC, 2002). Our entire financial system and market are built upon trust and confidence in a reliable accounting system. Therefore, a worst-case scenario could be that cyberterrorist attacks destroy records and drain accounts, thus causing disastrous consequences on financial institutions, global and national economies, and general populations. On the other hand, presently consumers rely heavily on credit cards or debit cards daily for financial transactions, whereas businesses count on core financial systems for their operations. In 2014, 471 million Visa debit cards and 183 million Master debit cards in the U.S. made \$35 billion and \$13.6 billion, respectively, in debit card transactions (Holmes, 2015). As of May 2016, credit card debt was estimated at \$953.3 billion (Holmes, 2016). Given these facts and considering the possibility of the worst-case scenario, security researchers and practitioners would be extremely interested in finding the answers to the following questions: (1) What would happen if credit/debit cards and ATMs cannot be used following a cyberterrorist attack? (2) How long can Americans survive without payment instruments? (3) Would Americans be resilient to such terrorist attacks and avoid chaos in their lives and communities?

A theme that runs through the above questions is how resilient citizens are to such attacks. “Resilience” is defined by the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) as “ability to resist, absorb, recover from or successfully adapt to adversity or a change in conditions” (DHS, 2011). Reliance is a salient concept in the disaster recovery literature because research and practice have shown that building resilience in communities and citizens is a way to reduce the impacts that disasters can have on the nation and its communities. Research has also shown that individual resilience is integral to community and national resilience. It is a critical capacity to ensure people can cope with disastrous situations and bounce back strong from such situations (Dumont & Provost, 1999; LaFromboise, Hoyt, Oliver, & Whitbeck, 2006; Lyon & Parkins, 2013). For example, prior research found resilient people can better cope with loss, trauma, and life stress (Bonanno, 2004; Bonanno, Galea, Bucciarelli, & Vlahov, 2007). Numerous studies also found that resilience is an essential factor for individuals and families to cope with mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in facing war or terror (MacDermid Wadsworth, 2010). Therefore, we believe that individual resilience is an essential factor for individuals to remain confident and rational when dealing with financial chaos due to a massive cyberterrorist attack on financial systems.

However, very few studies have been conducted in the IS field on individual resilience to massive cyber attacks, especially cyberterrorist attacks. In essence, we know relatively little about individual resilience regarding its role in an organization’s or a nation’s cyber resilience. Most of the existing resilience studies focus on business-side issues (Rose & Krausmann, 2013). For example, many studies recommend the establishment of system redundancy for cyber resilience (Bodeau, Graubart, Heinbockel, & Laderman, 2016). In practice, cyber resilience is presently a critical survival strategy for business continuity. While financial service providers may build redundancy or cyber resilience to make customers more confident in the availability of financial services, the behavioral consequences of a massive disruption in financial services to civilians are still unknown. If all protective mechanisms fail and massive cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems happen, what role would individual resilience play in the recovery process? Would individuals have to adapt their economic behaviors such as keeping more cash at home or using multiple financial systems? Understanding such adaptive, coping behaviors in the context of cyberterrorism may be crucial for cyber disaster planning and recovery and for establishing a nation’s or an organization’s cyber resilience. This study defines such behaviors as “economic resilient behavior,” that is, an adaptive behavior that an individual develops to deal with the disruption of the critical financial resources due to an adversity, in our case, financial adversity caused by cyberterrorist attacks.

Furthermore, one main intent of cyberterrorist attacks on the financial systems is to destabilize civilian life and create mass paranoia, mistrust, and panic toward the national economy and financial systems and markets. Past research found that strong fear as the negative effect of terror due to a terrorist attack is contagious in a community (Masten & Obradović, 2008). The terrorist propagation of fear could significantly affect the ability to execute contingency plans and make rational decisions among the target population and its communities (Masten & Obradović, 2008). However, it is unclear how civilians’ fear plays a role in a chaotic situation due to a massive cyberterrorist attack and how fear impacts their economic resilience behavior. It is also unclear how fear appraisal plays a role in fear appeals when fear due to such an attack could be much more extreme than general security threats (e.g., a phishing) despite past IS research having made significant contributions to the knowledge of fear appeals in the context of general security threats (Boss, Galletta, Lowry, Moody, & Polak, 2015; Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Johnston, Warkentin, &

Siponen, 2015). Nevertheless, understanding individual resilience and economic resilient behavior is critical to the development of cyber security strategies on preparedness in the area of financial assistance, and understanding the role of fear may help develop such strategies to mitigate chaos and terror caused by cyberterrorist attacks.

To address the void in IS security research, this study aims to explore the antecedents of individual resilience and its consequent economic resilient behavior. Essentially, this study examines the relationship between individual resilience and their economic resilient behavior, discovers the role of fear in individual economic resilient behavior, and explores the antecedents of individual resilience. By empirically validating the proposed research model, this study develops the individual economic resilient behavior model to predict individual behavior after cyberterrorist attacks by integrating the literature on resilience and fear appraisal. The primary research questions in this study are as follows: (1) How does individual resilience influence individual economic resilient behavior? (2) What are the antecedents of individual resilience? (3) What role does fear play in individual economic resilient behavior? The findings of this study are expected to provide recommendations for cyber disaster planning and recovery regarding how to create resilient communities and encourage civilian resilience after cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems. These findings may help IT practitioners develop strategies to build cyber resilience capacities among individuals and communities. These findings may also be generalized to other cyberterrorist attacks and help understand the impact of individual resilience. The study contributes to our knowledge of citizens’ postcyber-attack adaptive and recovery behaviors.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: first, the theoretical background relating to resilience is presented; the research model is proposed along with hypotheses; the methodology is discussed in detail; research findings are presented; and implications are discussed, along with research limitations and prospects for future research.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Extant cyberterrorism research has focused on four orientations: technological, legal, economic, and behavioral. All these orientations have piqued increasing attention. The technological-oriented research stream focuses on the technical means to prevent cyber attacks (Hansen, Lowry, Meservy, & McDonald, 2005; Griffith, 1999). The legal-oriented research stream examines the legal perspectives as they relate to the prosecution of cybercrime and cyberterrorism (Gable, 2009; Trachtman, 2004). The economic-oriented research stream develops and analyzes economic models to determine the level of investment necessary to safeguard information assets from cyber attacks (Hua & Bapna, 2013). The behavioral-oriented research is built upon the disciplines of psychology, criminology, and sociology and focuses on ascertaining the behavioral profiles of hackers and insiders (Fagnot, 2007; Kjaerland, 2005; Rogers, 2001; Workman & Gathegi, 2007). In general, much of the attention has been paid to deterrence, prevention, and detection in the security action cycle (Straub & Welke, 1998). As such, Willison and Warkentin (2013, p. 1) called for more research on phenomena which occur before deterrence, especially research on “the thought processes of the potential offender and how these are influenced by the organizational context, prior to deterrence.” Extending their call, this study calls for more research on phenomena which occur after deterrence and detection and during recovery, given that very little research attention has been paid to recovery. Recovery is a crucial part of business contingency planning for critical infrastructures as well as business continuity. Yet, research on citizens’ postcyber-attack adaptive and recovery behaviors is still lacking.

Additionally, many prior studies have examined general hacking and cyber security issues are not specifically related to cyberterrorism. Table 1 shows the main differences of cyberterrorism from hacktivism and cybercrime with regard to the type of targets and the intended level of disruption. Cyberterrorism targets computer systems that operate central national infrastructures to create mass disruption and destruction to a nation (Baldi, Gelbstein, & Kurbalija, 2003; Vegh, 2002). As such, examining recovery is even more critical in the context of cyberterrorist attacks given hypothetical mass disruption and destruction if such an attack succeeded and the essential role of individuals in building an ecosystem resilient to cyber threats (Ayoub, Firth & Nayaz, 2016).

## 2.1 Psychological Resilience

## 2.1.1 Theory of Social-Ecological Resilience

The term “resilience” originated in physics and mathematics and described a springing back to the previous, steady state (or equilibrium) after disruption (Holling, 1996). This view of resilience has been challenged when applied to social psychological contexts because the view assumes that there is a “steady state” that people want to return to after disturbances or disasters. However, social systems and human beings are more complex. When the equilibrium is disrupted by a disaster or an adversity, do people want to just bounce back to the previous state or adapt to a new, better state? Is there a steady state people can bounce back to given that human society consists of “built, natural, social, and economic environments that influence one another in complex ways” (Norris, Stevens, Pfefferbaum, Wyche, & Pfefferbaum, 2008)? When applied to human society, resilience is more than a physical property; it is a type of social-ecological capacity rooted in complex amalgams of institutions and communities (Norris, Stevens, Pfefferbaum, Wyche, & Pfefferbaum, 2008). From this perspective, the theory of social-ecological resilience defines resilience as the capability of a complex system to respond to adversity by trying to maintain system functions while recognizing changes, adapting to changes, and thus creating a new equilibrium for better system functions (Holling, 1996; LaFromboise, Hoyt, Oliver, & Whitbeck, 2006). This theory presents a holistic view of resilience, explains resilience in a complex dynamic system composed of a myriad of subsystems, and emphasizes the adaptive nature of the system as people in the system cope with and adapt to adversity. In other words, in such a system, individuals’ interactions with subsystems help build their resilient capacity. More specifically, while managing dramatic changes caused by adversity, individuals in the system attempt to adapt. Such adaptation creates resilient outcomes (Holling, 1996; Lyon & Parkins, 2013).

## 2.1.2 Psychological Resilience

Based on the theory of social-ecological resilience, this study defines psychological resilience as an individual’s capacity to remain functional by absorbing, recovering from, and adapting to adversity. As shown in Figure 1, psychological resilience is critical for an individual to recover from an adversity and adapt to more resilient behavior (i.e., resilient outcomes). Prior research found that the psychological capacity of resilience enables an individual to properly adapt to stress and adversity and that psychological resilience is not a trait and can be developed in processes (Lyon & Parkins, 2013). In general, people with positive emotionality are more likely to demonstrate resilience because they know how to balance negative and positive emotions (Frederickson, Tugade, Waugh, & Larkin, 2003). People with higher resilience can more easily navigate themselves around stress and adversity, stay positive, and pursue resilient outcomes.

Figure 1. Social-Ecological View of Psychological Resilience

Furthermore, under the social-ecological view of resilience, as shown in Figure 1, psychological resilience is supported by primary protective factors from a myriad of subsystems of an individual when an adversity causes disruption, destruction, and panic to the individual’s life (Holling, 1996; Lyon & Parkins, 2013). Psychological resilience is the capacity that people demonstrate under the stress of disruptive situations and devastating life events. Specifically, resilience is a natural and normal part of the innate health built into all humans through adaptation and evolution (Bonanno, 2004). Therefore, psychological resilience comes from both people’s inner systems and external environments with which people interact (e.g., families, communities) (Masten, 2001). As such, protective factors for psychological resilience come from a network of social support including family, community, and society (Pinkerton & Dolan, 2007). This study focuses on the direct social network of individuals — their communities and their families.

Community support refers to resources that a community can provide with its members to respond to and recover from an adversity (LaFromboise, Hoyt, Oliver, & Whitbeck, 2006; Pfefferbaum, Pfefferbaum, & Van Horn, 2011). A community that carries resources, skills, and knowledge of coping can provide social support to its members and help them to build resilience and to take necessary actions to remedy the impact of adversity (Norris, Stevens, Pfefferbaum, Wyche, & Pfefferbaum, 2008). “People in communities are resilient together, not merely in similar ways” (Brown & Kulig, 1996). Thus, people in a supportive community can get important guidance and support to be resilient. In the context of cyberterrorist attacks, although conserving sufficient resources can increase national and community resilience, this conservation cannot always protect communities completely. Some communities could still face the shock from cyberterrorist attacks and lack resources, knowledge, and skills to help community members. On the other hand, it is critical for governments to know whether the individuals in those unprotected or unprepared communities can be resilient to the situations and how long they can be resilient when developing a national plan.

In addition to community support, family characteristics also play an important role in the development of individual resilience (LaFromboise, Hoyt, Oliver, & Whitbeck, 2006). Family resources, structures, and interactions influence family members and support their action more rapidly. Family-level resources vis-à-vis financial resources and psychological, cognitive, and emotional supports are strongly associated with an individual’s resilience (Ali, Dwyer, Vanner, & Lopez, 2010). With family support, people are, in general, more resilient and able to better cope with stress and adversity in their life (Pinkerton & Dolan, 2007). In the context of a postterroristattack environment, individuals may suffer trauma and demonstrate symptoms of depression or PTSD due to injury, significant financial loss, or other disastrous circumstances. Families with resources can often act rapidly and robustly and serve as a type of redundancy, or a “backstop,” to family members in the event of disruption and disturbance. Individuals in such family normally demonstrate more resilience. Prior research further indicates that resilience leads to positive, adaptive outcomes and that resilient behaviors are acts taken by people in a complex system to respond to changes in an attempt to create a new, possibly better, equilibrium in the system (Lyon & Parkins, 2013).

When individuals go back to their family for support, family financial resources are crucial for financial and material support. Healthy family financial management is a foundation for such support. Family financial management is defined as how an individual allocates his/her family’s financial resources to achieve the family financial goals (Dew & Xiao, 2011). Well-managed family finance helps develop resilience among family members because they can cope with an adversity without too many financial constraints (Ali, Dwyer, Vanner, & Lopez, 2010).

National preparedness and capability are crucial for community support and resilience building among citizens. For example, in the context of a postcyberterrorist-attack environment, both individuals and communities may need federal support for talent and knowledge given the complex nature of such attack. However, how to build national preparedness and capability for cyberterrorist attacks is still a challenge to a nation (Ayoub, Firth & Nayaz, 2016). For this reason, this study only examines protective factors related to family and community.

## 2.2 Fear Appeals

Theories on fear appeals form the major theoretical foundations for studying security behavior concerning security threats targeting individuals and organizations (Chen, & Zehadi, 2016; Johnston, Warkentin, & Siponen, 2015). Fear appeals use persuasive messages to arouse fear in people. These messages describe the terrible consequences of a threat to convince people that they will face those consequences if they do not take actions to combat the threat. The outcomes of fear appeals are attitudinal change and intention or behavioral change. Fear appeals have three main components: fear, threats, and perceived efficacy. Fear is an emotion provoked by a feeling of being threatened (Maren, 2008). A threat is a person or thing likely to cause danger. Perceived threat is a perception of the extent of the vulnerability and severity of the threat. If someone does not perceive the threat, he would not be afraid. Perceived efficacy is a person’s belief that the actions recommended in the message are effective and can decrease the effect of the threat.

The fear appeal literature also points out that the process of fear appraisal is critical for end users to have protective behavior in facing cyber security threats in both work and nonwork settings (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Posey, Roberts, & Lowry, 2015). Past studies showed that proper concerns due to fear of the negative consequences of the threat can trigger problem-solving behaviors (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). Fear is an outcome of fear appraisal based on the cost–benefit analysis of the threat and an essential feeling that triggers protection motivations and behaviors (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Posey, Roberts, & Lowry, 2015). Thus, fear appeals are a proactive method for motivating people to act. Fear appeals are also vital in prompting people’s resilient behaviors, which could proactively minimize the impact of cyberterrorist attacks. Prior research has shown that the capacity to assess and reorganize threats is another essential factor in taking adaptive, resilient actions (Norris, Stevens, Pfefferbaum, Wyche, & Pfefferbaum, 2008). Such capability enables individuals to correctly judge the situation, raise necessary concerns toward the situation, and take meaningful, deliberate action accordingly. Necessary concerns also trigger an individual’s inner system to be resistant and adaptive (Lyon & Parkins, 2013). In the context of disaster planning and recovery, fear is also an emotion in response to a threat. Fear and uncertainty often motivate individuals to be prepared by taking preparedness actions such as buying emergency preparedness kits and creating family emergency plans. In the current research context, we define fear of cyberterrorist attack as the extent of worry a person has in response to the threat of cyberterrorist attack (Allen, 1993; Goodwin et al. 2005). However, the role of fear appraisal in the context of cyberterrorism is unclear and warrants further research effort.

The other pillar of fear appeals is coping appraisal. Past IS security research found that security coping efficacy and response efficacy are the two main factors in coping appraisal to motivate end-users to take recommended security countermeasures such as install antivirus software and firewall to cope with security threats (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). However, as shown in Table 1, cyberterrorism aims at systems operating critical national infrastructures instead of end-user systems. Under this context, end-users have no or very limited coping efficacy in dealing with those systems.

## 2.3 Economic Resilient Behavior Model

Drawing from the theory of social-ecological resilience and the literature on individual psychological resilience and fear appeals, this study proposes an economic resilient behavior model (see Figure 2) to study individual psychological resilience in the context of cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems. Adopting the theory of social-ecological resilience, the model suggests that to build resilience to cyber attacks on financial systems, individuals must interact with their environments — in this case, their community and family. Those who get more support from their community and have more resources in their family are more likely to absorb the shock of a cyber attack and show resilience. Moreover, resilience leads to positive outcomes, which are adaptive, resilient behavior — in this case, economic resilient behavior. Economic resilient behavior refers to adaptive behavior that resilient people have to regain financial functioning and well-being. In our case, the adaptation shows as alertness and watchfulness about money (Klontz, Britt, Mentzer, & Klontz, 2011) and reflects in how people change their routine way of saving money. Furthermore, by adopting the fear appeal literature, the model also posits that fear is an influential factor in economic resilient behaviors. Individuals who can recognize the threat are more likely to keep their internal structures intact. Consequently, they are more likely to exhibit economic resilient behaviors.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

Community support can take several forms, including financial assistance and personal advice from one’s local community. The community can influence people’s reactions to crises as well as help them better cope with and adjust to the situation (Braun-Lewensohn & Rubin, 2014). Community support is very likely to contribute to people’s psychological resilience when they face difficult and disastrous situations such as natural disasters, terrorism attacks, or personal tragedies (LaFromboise, Hoyt, Oliver, & Whitbeck, 2006). LaFromboise et al. (2006) found that if youth perceived that there was community support, they had higher chances of being resilient in the face of adversity and demonstrating prosocial behaviors. Prior studies also indicated that those who relied on other people in the community as sources of emotional support demonstrated a strong tendency toward resilience (Werner, 1995). Community support can be considered as a protective factor for people’s psychological health, a critical factor in psychological resilience (Dumont & Provost, 1999). Past research also found that people’s resilience can be predicted by community support along with some demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age, education, and income) (Bonanno, Galea, Bucciarelli, & Vlahov, 2007). In a massive cyber attack on a financial system, victims of such attack may suffer not only a great financial loss but also an emotional and psychological shock. They may even experience nationwide chaotic situations. Strong community support may help them handle their temporary financial difficulty and provide needed emotional support, legal counsel, and other assistance. Such community support helps victims deal with the stress and adversity caused by the attack and better adapt to the situation. With strong community support, people are more likely to demonstrate high psychological resilience. Following this logic, it is hypothesized as follows:

H1. Community support has a positive relationship with civilians’ psychological resilience.

Good family financial management can mitigate a family’s economic stress and, in turn, increase its members’ psychological resilience (Conger & Rueter, 1999). Psychological resilience implies the capacity of people or a family to successfully manage challenging life circumstances (Walsh, 2006). It is believed that with good financial management, civilians and their families tend to have more financial resources at their disposal and, thus, have higher capacities to handle financial stress and be resilient. Good financial management may also help civilians and their families deal with other stressors. A massive cyberterrorist attack on a financial system could create crises on personal finance. Individuals who have their families to collectively cope with the crises have more resources at hand and thus are more resilient. Given the above logic, it is hypothesized as follows:

H2. Family financial management has a positive relationship with civilian’s psychological resilience.

Many studies have been conducted on risky behaviors. Using the data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health, Ali et al. (2010) found that resilient adolescents were less likely to engage in risky behaviors. Another study found that youth with higher personal resilience reported a lower frequency of engaging in risky behaviors (Prince-Embury, 2015). Research further pointed out that psychological resilience helps young adults to more successfully cope with issues in their lives (Pinkerton & Dolan, 2007).

When civilians experience a cyber attack on a financial system, resilience triggers their problem-solving ability and motivation to deal with financial stress. To be resilient and adaptive to their new financial circumstances, civilians must find a solution and adapt. Changing their current way of interacting with the system might be a solution. For example, saving more money and diversifying their savings afford them the opportunity to have long-lasting financial strength. As a result, those who are more resilient are more likely to be able to modify their saving behavior rapidly to adapt to the change. Hence, the third hypothesis is as follows:

H3: Psychological resilience has a positive relationship with economic resilient behavior (i.e., change in saving behavior).

Fear appeals have been studied intensively in the fields of psychology and marketing. A fear appeal uses a persuasive message, which can motivate a person to take the desired action. Many theoretical models of fear appeals have been proposed. The most popular theory is protection motivation theory (PMT) (Rogers, 1975). PMT has been broadly tested and is supported by many empirical studies on cyber security (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). PMT holds that fear appeals increase a person’s fear and concern of a threat, prescribe feasible solutions to avert the threat, and highlight the importance of the recommended solutions. Successful fear appeals will make a person believe that the threat is relevant, harmful, and possible. If the person does not take any action, he will face great risk. By the same token, when financial systems are under attack by cyberterrorists and, consequently, are unavailable to their users for an extended period, fear appeals can arouse fear among users about the safety of their money and even their financial future. For example, one worst-case scenario could be that an online bank loses its records. Although the bank would take actions to remedy the risk, individuals, on the other hand, may have to adapt their current system use behavior (i.e., by saving more cash and diversifying their savings) out of fear. Thus, we argue that fear may trigger users to change their financial behavior to minimize the impact of the attack. Following the above logic, it is hypothesized as follows:

H4: Fear of cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems has a positive relationship with economic resilient behavior (i.e., change in saving behavior).

## 4. Research Methodology

## 4.1 Survey Development

This research used an online survey to collect data. To ensure the validity of the constructs in the research model, we developed the measurement items of the constructs according to the existing literature whenever possible and adapted them to the current research context. Table 2 shows the measurement items, construct definitions, and construct main references. In detail, measurement items for psychological resilience are adapted from Campbell-Sills & Stein (2007) and Connor & Davidson (2003). Items for community support are adapted from LaFromboise, Hoyt, Oliver, & Whitbeck (2006) and Pfefferbaum, Pfefferbaum, & Van Horn (2011). Two items used to measure the fear of cyberterrorist attack are adapted from Allen (1993) and Goodwin et al. (2005). Items for family financial management are self-developed by this study based on the major areas in the family financial management identified by Dew & Xiao (2011) — savings and investment, cash management, and credit management. Finally, items used to measure economic resilient behavior are also self-developed by this study based on the resilience literature related to adaptation in behaviors (Norris et al. 2008). Specifically, items for economic resilient behavior measure hypothetical behavior projected by individuals. Measuring hypothetical behavior is appropriate under the current research context because it is almost infeasible for researchers to simulate a large-scale cyberterrorist attack on financial systems with mass disruption and destruction and then measure actual behavior of the impacted population. Additionally, massive cyberterrorist attacks on critical infrastructures are still theoretical possibilities. As such, individuals have no such actual behavior to report. A five-point Likert scale was used for all measurement items. One-item demographic variables — age, gender, education, race, marital status, and family income — were also included in the survey.

We used a scenario-based survey to investigate consumers’ economic resilient behavior after a cyber attack on financial systems. The scenario-based approach is a commonly used research method in behavioral security research and in other IS research (Johnston, Warkentin, & Siponen, 2015). This approach is appropriate for this study because, as just mentioned, massive cyberterrorist attacks on critical infrastructures are not yet materialized.

## 4.2 Data Collection

This study developed the survey instrument using established development guidelines (e.g., Churchill, 1979) and hosted the survey on SurveyMonkey. The survey instrument was pretested and refined based on guidelines. In detail, the survey questionnaire was developed based on an intensive literature review. The initial items were reviewed by two research experts on cyberterrorism and were refined based on their feedback. The pilot test was conducted in a class of 13 graduate students in a university in the east coast of the United States (U.S).

To reach the general public in U.S., Amazon Mechanical Turk was employed to recruit the respondents from the public. All respondents were given a small incentive. In the survey, all respondents were presented with a scenario that simulates a massive cyberterrorist attack on a financial system. The respondents then answered questions regarding resilience, family financial management, community support, fear of cyberterrorist attacks, economic resilient behavior, and demography. In total, 263 adults participated in the survey. After deleting five incomplete records from the survey, 258 usable records were included in the subsequent data analysis. This study used the partial least squares (PLS) method for the data analysis. PLS is a component-based technique for structural equation modeling (SEM); it is suitable for this study because PLS is less restricted by sample size, measurement scales, and residual distributions. Moreover, this study introduces the theory of social-ecological resilience to the IS field. However, the theory is not only new to the IS field but also primitive in sociopsychology, its original field. As such, our model is not filled with well-established nomologies in the IS field. Our study attempts to explore the new theory and stimulate more theory building upon it. As our study is testing a new theory with an exploratory objective, PLS is deemed a more suitable technique (Gefen, Rigdon, & Straub, 2011) because it is more appropriate for preliminary model development and for testing not well-established nomologies (Boss, Galletta, Lowry, Moody, & Polak, 2015). Johnston et al. (2010; 2015) used PLS to test their preliminary fear appeal models. Lowry and Gaskin (2014) contended that PLS should be selected for exploratory work. Moreover, another objective of our model is to predict behavioral change; hence, PLS is a better choice than a covariance-based SEM technique for our inquiry (Chin, 2010; Gefen, Rigdon, & Straub, 2011). The bootstrapping resampling procedure in PLS was used to estimate the research model. Figure 3 shows the results of the hypothesis test. The results confirmed all the four proposed hypotheses with all the p-values less than 0.001, thus strongly supporting the research model. As hypothesized, the positive association between community support and resilience was confirmed with a path coefficient of 0.290 (H1), thus indicating that people with a strong community support tend to have a high level of resilience that enables them to cope with difficult circumstances. The results also confirmed H2 with a path coefficient of 0.315. This finding indicates that people with good family financial management have a greater ability to be resilient in disastrous situations — in this case, the massive cyber attack on the financial system they are associated with. H3, stating the positive association between resilience and economic resilient behavior, was confirmed with a path coefficient of 0.137. This finding supported the argument that resilient people are, in fact, better able to deal with financial stress and strain brought by the massive cyber attack on financial systems and bounce back from difficulty. Finally, the positive relationship between fear of cyberterrorist attack and economic resilient behavior was also confirmed with a path coefficient of 0.636 (H4). This result indicates that reasonable concerns and risk perceptions enable rational behavioral change in personal finance in the face of a massive cyber attack on financial systems. Meanwhile, community support and family financial management together explained 24.4 percent variance of resilience. Resilience and fear of cyberterrorist attack together explained 43.3 percent variance of economic resilient behavior. In sum, the results support the research model with good explanatory power.

## 6. Discussion

The findings from H1 show that more community support can help civilians become more resilient. These findings also support the argument that resilience can be developed and improved. When civilians face cyberterrorist attacks on the financial system associated with their financial accounts, they will worry about their savings and daily routines. If their financial institutions are attacked and, thus, inaccessible, people cannot withdraw money from ATMs or use credit cards to buy anything. As a result, people will have to resort to using cash. Usually, 69% of Americans are unlikely to have more than \$50 on hand, and about 10% of them carry no cash at all (Ross, 2014). Hence, how can they survive on little to no cash for seven days? They will need to borrow cash or get resources from their communities (e.g., friends, neighbors, and relatives). If they cannot get support from their community, they must rely on the government. The government should be able to provide emergency food and cash assistance in such a dire situation. These community supports should help increase individual resiliency. Governments need to advertise their ability to provide emergency assistance through mass media so that people will know whether and how they are supported. Without community supports, civilians are unlikely to be resilient, and chaos might ensue. In addition, massive cyber attacks on financial systems may also cause disastrous domino effects in the business world. For example, such attacks may cause nationwide financial shortfalls and cash-flow issues, which dramatically impact daily business operations. When this happens, local supermarkets and grocery stores may not be able to stay open because there will be no in-flow of cash revenue to sustain normal operations. Such a devastating situation may trigger other terrorist attacks or cause societal chaos. Our findings emphasize that providing extra resources and supports to civilians through their communities is essential to their resilience in coping with massive cyber attacks.

Findings through H2 indicate that people can boost their resilience through good family financial management. This can boost people’s confidence, engender calmness, and improve people’s capacity to overcome unexpected financial disturbance. If people can maintain a certain level of financial capacity through their family, they can be resilient when there are cyber attacks on their financial institutions. The findings suggest some strategies that may help civilians maintain that level of capacity, for example, diversifying their saving across multiple financial institutions; this improves their chances of accessing a surviving bank system while others are disabled by attacks. Moreover, if people have a family financial emergency plan, they are more likely to have access to cash, which will likely help them survive — perhaps even without external help.

Findings through H3 confirm the link between psychological resilience and economic resilient behavior change in saving behavior. Previous studies found that resilient adolescents were less likely to engage in risky behavior (Ali, Dwyer, Vanner, & Lopez, 2010; Prince-Embury, 2015). In a similar vein, this study concludes that if people are highly resilient, they take precautions to deal with risk and are more likely to adapt to making some positive behavioral change after a cyberterrorist attack. They cannot bear the thought that they are caught off-guard in the event of a subsequent attack. Borrowing money from friends, neighbors, and relatives and waiting in a long line for government assistance may not be viable. Hence, resilient people are likely to be proactive to prevent significant impacts from such a chaotic and risky reoccurrence.

Findings in H4 support the role of fear appeals in behavioral change in the context of cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems. The findings point out that fear of cyberterrorist attacks stemming from fear appeals can increase a person’s positive behavioral change in that he/she adopts a feasible solution to avert the threat. Concerns about cyberterrorist attacks against financial institutions make people believe that the threat is relevant, harmful, and possible, and they could face a great risk if they do not take action. The findings indicate that sometimes it is necessary to raise fear and concerns among civilians. Such fear and concerns may trigger people’s inner strength to take positive actions and avert the disastrous situation.

In short, the findings demonstrate the importance of community support and family financial management when civilians experience the devastating impacts of a cyberterrorist attack. Thus, it may be beneficial that communities and governments provide civilians with education and training on family financial management for cyber attacks. A nation may benefit from such endeavor and develop a resilient civilian population and resilient communities against terrorist attacks of all kinds. At the same time, it is also wise to trigger civilians’ psychological resilience by arousing a certain amount of fear and raising concerns about cyber attacks.

The proposed model can help practitioners predict individual postattack behaviors in the context of cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems. This model also helps us understand how to improve individual psychological resilience that may help improve community resilience during a cyberterrorist attack. This study explores the antecedents that explain why individuals behave in a resilient manner when they are impacted by cyber attacks. Additionally, including fear appraisal in the study helps us understand the role of fear stemming from citizens assessment and recognition of massive cyberterrorist attacks in motivating their resilient behaviors (Norris, Stevens, Pfefferbaum, Wyche, & Pfefferbaum, 2008).

## 7.1 Implication for IS research

Although many studies have focused on deterrence, prevention, and detection in the security action cycle (Straub & Welke, 1998), recent research called for more research on phenomena which occur before deterrence (Willison & Warkentin 2013). This study further extends IS literature by making an endeavor to study a phenomenon of recovery, and calls for more research on after-deterrence phenomena. Specifically, this study contributes to research by developing a research model that integrates the resilience literature with the fear appeal literature to examine individual resilience to cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems and explore the antecedents of resilience. Moreover, this study brings the concept of individual resilience into the context of cyber security. Although the concept has been intensively studied in other fields such as psychology and disaster recovery, it is a novel, salient concept in the current research context because individual resilience is a critical strength of an individual, a community, and even a nation to recover and adapt when facing devastation — in this case, massive cyberterrorist attacks. Furthermore, this study examines economic resilient behavior from an adaptive,

changing perspective. This is an important perspective as IS research moves to postadoption information technology use. In many circumstances, we need to understand how end-users change their information system use behavior after triggering events such as security incidents and attacks. Thus, this study brings insights into individuals’ postcyber-attack adaptive and recovery behaviors.

## 7.2 Implication for Practice

This study explores how people change their behavior following cyberterrorist attacks. Improving community support and family financial management can improve individuals resilience, meaning that they would take precautions to avoid experiencing the negative effects of subsequent cyberterrorist attacks. Although cyber security is certainly a popular topic in the mass media, most people are still unaware that massive cyber attacks on critical infrastructure including financial systems — are possible and could have disastrous consequences for organizations and individuals. In general, people lack a sufficient understanding of how to protect themselves under such circumstances. This study provides insights into individual emergency preparedness for cyber attacks with massive impact on civilians. In addition, this study may also help businesses understand their involvement in educating and training customers about cyber attacks and develop cyber resilience plans with considerations given to individual cyber resilience. For example, an online bank may advise its customers about financial diversity (e.g., having an emergency fund) and access channel diversity (e.g., having alternative access to funds, such as mobile access). IT practitioners may facilitate businesses to develop strategies to build cyber resilience capacities among individuals and communities (e.g., developing technology and strategy that enable emergency fund access during the postattack time).

On the other hand, businesses must be cautious about individual economic resilient behavior. This study shows that after cyberterrorist attacks against a financial system, individuals are more likely to hold cash at home than to save it with institutions. It is also possible that they may spread their money across multiple banks to have greater financial diversity. Such actions may influence the cash flow of some financial institutions and, subsequently, public confidence in financial markets. A worst-case scenario could occur when a large number of customers stop using online banking systems and choose to withdraw cash from their accounts. We may be able to avoid such a worst-case scenario if individuals are resilient and have a reasonable amount of emergency funds in cash. Additionally, both businesses and governments should realize that building national and organizational cyber resilience are the ultimate solution for business continuity. Businesses also need to be cautious about customers’ fear of cyber attacks on financial systems. While reasonable fear is a product of the rational cost–benefit analysis which could trigger people’s inner strength in dealing with the consequences of a cyberterrorist attack, extreme fear may cause emotional turmoil and irrational behavior. When a massive cyberterrorist attack on financial systems takes place and crowds line up at banks to withdraw cash, it is not a sign of resilience but chaos. In other words, resilience building takes place before the disaster and reinforced after the disaster, but not during the disaster.

From the national security perspective, policymakers need to make their citizens more aware of cyber resilience and educate citizens on how to be more resilient to cyberterrorist attacks. This study provides insights into how such education can be conducted. Apparently, educating citizens about improving their family financial management in preparation for “rainy days” is applicable in the context of cyberterrorist attacks. Moreover, policymakers can help build community support and let people know what support exists. For individuals, improved community support is an external change, and improved family financial management is an internal change; both can enhance individual resilience.

From the perspective of financial market stability, financial institutions must be aware of the potentially devastating impact of a cyberterrorist attack on their systems. This study demonstrates that their customers may change their saving routines by pursuing financial diversity. Obviously, this could trigger unforeseen crises in their business operations, cash flow, and reputation. Nevertheless, these institutions need to see the crisis as an opportunity. While investing more money to improve their cyber security and cyber resilience, they could provide more diverse products and services. As such, their customers have alternatives when dealing with the consequences of cyber attacks.

## 7.3 Limitation and Future Research

There are several limitations that need to be considered when researchers interpret our research results and plan for future research. First, although this study tried to recruit research participants from the general public in the U.S. using Amazon Mechanical Turk, the sample pool is not a perfect representation of the current population of the U.S. For example, this study may not represent those with limited computer literacy. This study also could not reach the population with an annual income of more than \$120,000. Future research needs to access those populations to examine their cyber resilience and the uniqueness of it. Second, this study has a strong U.S. background. Therefore, cautions should be taken when researchers apply our findings to other cultures and nations. Future cross-cultural or -national studies that investigate the role of cultural or national specific factors in citizens’ resilience for cyberterrorist attacks can help address this limitation. The third limitation is the context of this study: bank systems failure and a cyberterrorist scenario. Future studies may investigate other systems that operate critical infrastructures and multiple scenarios of cyberterrorist attacks with different levels of disruption to further explore the research model with more contextual relevant factors and resilient outcomes. Fourth, this study examines individuals’ psychological resilience under cyberterrorist attacks in their social system — namely their community and their family — and only includes fear in the research model. Future research may explore the role of other social systems and other emotions in individuals’ resilience. Additionally, future research may also investigate community- and industrial-level cyber resilience and how IT preparedness helps build such resilience. Finally, this study only explores economic resilient behavior. Future research may investigate other consequent behaviors of resilience. For example, future research may further explore the relationships between individual, community, and national resilience under cyberterrorist attacks. Future research may also extend the current model to include more factors related to social support, community support, and family support and to study their respective influences on resilience.

## 7.4 Conclusion

As many experts project that cyberterrorist attacks could be real (Baldi, Gelbstein & Kurbalija, 2003; Gable, 2009), this study examines how individuals can be economically resilient to cyberterrorist attacks on financial systems. It contributes to IS research by investigating a postdeterrence phenomenon in the current research context and calls for more research on the postdeterrence phenomenon. It is a timely endeavor given that individuals and organizations grow more reliant on information technology for daily activities and operations while moving more information and systems including System Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems for critical infrastructures to cloud. This practice opens theoretical possibilities of mass cyber attacks, especially cyberterrorist attacks. Industries and governments have called that “[g]overnments, organizations in the private and public sectors and individuals must all play their part in building an ecosystem that is resilient to cyber threats” (Ayoub, Firth & Nayaz, 2016, p. 3). This study contributes to this call by developing and empirically testing the research model to examine economic resilient behavior in the context of cyberterrorist attacks on finance systems.
