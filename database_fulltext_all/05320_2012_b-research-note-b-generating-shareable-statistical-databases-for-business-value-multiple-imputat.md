## 1. Introduction

Organizations are generating growing volumes of data about their employees, customers, and trading partners. While the potential value of these data is generally understood, privacy and confidentiality concerns limit its use. For example, although supermarket scanner data matched with medical records would be invaluable to health researchers, such a linked database creates serious privacy concerns (Davenport et al. 2007, Perloff and Denbaly 2007). Other examples include the Netflix prize, which involved distribution of altered customer data that in some cases were later identified by researchers (Narayanan and Shmatikov 2006), and the AOL data scandal, in which that company divulged personal information, leading to class action lawsuits.<sup>1</sup> One approach to resolving this dilemma is to change or mask the data in some way to retain statistical properties but limit the identification of individuals.

A scholarly literature in statistics and computer science focuses on the development of masking algorithms and methods for assessing their validity (Domingo-Ferrer and Mateo-Sanz 2002). However, this literature typically is aimed at the needs of national statistical agencies. Business organizations and others have not substantially benefited from the proliferation of approaches developed in recent years to mask data sets. Our principal objective is to address this gap between research and practice. We extend the growing literature on data masking by providing three contributions to the scholarly literature and to management practice: (1) develop a usable approach to masking data sets by focusing on the requirements of business organizations; (2) demonstrate the validity of the approach in simulation studies and with actual business data; and (3) discuss application guidelines.

We develop the multiple imputation with multimodal perturbation (MIMP) approach to sharing sensitive business databases that minimizes the risk that sensitive data about specific individuals will be disclosed while preserving important statistical information contained in the data. The technique is a hybrid approach that employs multiple imputation (replacing a data point or data record with a draw from a probability distribution) and multimodal perturbation (add perturbation with multiple modes to decrease the risk that an individual can be reidentified). MIMP adds to a growing stream of literature in information systems research addressing database utility and privacy (Garfinkel et al. 2007, Lee et al. 2010, Li and Sarkar 2006, Menon et al. 2005, Saar-Tsechansky and Provost 2007, Zheng and Padmanabhan 2006). Specifically, MIMP provides a combination of three substantial enhancements over existing approaches to data masking. First, MIMP provides asymptotically valid point estimates and statistical inference not only for univariate relationships but also for bivariate relationships such as correlation coefficients and multivariate relationships such as regression coefficients. This characteristic overcomes a limitation of prior methods such as data swapping that provide valid estimates of univariate relationships only. Second, the developed approach meets a key business requirement: it is applicable to various types of statistical databases, whether normally distributed or not. This property overcomes a limitation of existing techniques such as statistical obfuscation that require multivariate-normal data—rarely if ever encountered in practice (Burridge 2003).<sup>2</sup> Third, while statistical agencies have fairly uniform requirements regarding risk and utility (often specified in regulations), business organizations differ in what they consider “good enough” along each dimension. MIMP enables the data owner to explicitly trade off risk for utility via a noise parameter, which is not possible with several existing techniques. The analyst employing MIMP has the flexibility to adjust this trade-off to her specific requirements.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In §2 we motivate the problem, develop solution objectives, and review prior research. In §3 we develop the new MIMP approach to data masking. In §4 we present Monte Carlo simulation results to evaluate the efficacy of MIMP. In §5 we illustrate application of the MIMP approach to actual business data, summarize the contribution to the literature, and suggest future research avenues.

## 2. Motivation and Prior Research

## 2.1. Background

Supermarket scanner data are used by marketers to understand consumer trends and to develop advertising campaigns. Compiled by such companies as Information Resources (IRI) and Nielsen, scanner data are also used by researchers, for example, to understand the association between consumption patterns and health (NRC 2002). However, such data are of limited use to researchers because they are collected for commercial purposes and do not contain enough variables to examine important research questions, such as those analyzing the connection between fatty foods and cardiovascular health. One way to overcome this limitation is to link scanner data to detailed health data. Linked data might comprise direct identifiers (name and address), demographic data (age and income), behavioral data (purchase of ice-cream and cookies), and health data (weight and cholesterol).

While valuable, such a linked database raises privacy concerns. One approach to addressing privacy issues is to delete direct identifiers (DDI) such as name and address. Despite its widespread use, DDI is insufficient given the widespread availability of public databases that enable linkages to be drawn between seemingly innocuous variables such as age and income and identifying variables (Sweeney 2001). A recent example of this problem is when Netflix released “de-identified” movie histories of its customers that used the DDI strategy. Researchers were able to match these data with the Internet movie database (IMDB) to identify a person’s entire movie viewing history prior to 2005 (Narayanan and Shmatikov 2006). This has been followed by more substantial cases, such as the linking of social security numbers with birth places (Acquisti and Gross 2009), and identification of trace amounts of an individual’s DNA in complex DNA mixtures (Homer et al. 2008). Clearly, the DDI approach, although widespread, raises serious privacy issues: “De-identification provides only a weak form of privacy” (Narayanan and Shmatikov 2010, p. 26)

Now consider the possibility of transforming the linked supermarket–health care database so that individuals cannot be identified but statistical analysis remains valid. For example, one might add a random perturbation drawn from the normal distribution to blur each data record. Data resulting from such a procedure would be beneficial in a variety of contexts in which privacy issues impede data sharing, for example, home energy use data and online shopping behavior.

## 2.2. Requirements of a Data Generation Method for Business Organizations

For a masking method to be feasible for business organizations, several objectives would have to be met from a data generation and a data analysis perspective (Table 1). First, real statistical databases do not follow particular analytic distribution functions, so the generation method would need to be compatible with any type of data. The masking transformation could not be limited to data sets with known distributions such as the normal distribution. Second, business organizations do not have the resources of national statistical agencies to carefully model the original data sample to ensure that the masked data are valid. This means that the generation method would need to be automated within software modules, rather than ad hoc—no special statistical data analysis capabilities would be required of the data generator. Third, as privacy requirements differ across organizations (e.g., health care versus social media), the data owner would have the ability to set and assess the trade-off between statistical utility and risk so that both are compatible with her particular requirements. Fourth, the method must be low-cost because today’s business managers increasingly use free open-source software such as mySQL and Google Docs and have grown to expect free or low-cost software to be high quality and reliable. Thus, protecting the method within costly software is incompatible with business requirements. Fifth, the method must have a robust statistical foundation with predictable asymptotic properties.

## 2.3. Existing Methods for Generating Shareable Statistical Databases

2.3.1. Random Perturbation. This method involves perturbing the data by adding a normally distributed random variable with zero mean (Fuller 1993, Paass 1988, Spruill 1992, Tendick and Matloff 1994) and subsequent scaling to preserve the covariance structure of the original variables (Kim 1986). The method is easy to implement but provides only modest levels of statistical utility and reidentification risk protection. In a simulation study comparing various masking techniques according to utility and risk, perturbation ranks 68 out of 109 in terms of statistical utility and 56 in terms of disclosure risk (Domingo-Ferrer et al. 2001).

2.3.2. Statistical Obfuscation. Information preserving statistical obfuscation (IPSO) is a model-based approach for generating a new data set that exactly preserves its sufficient statistics (e.g., regression coefficients and covariance) (Burridge 2003). The original data set is split into confidential and nonconfidential variables, and a sample from the conditional distribution of the confidential variable ${ \cal Y } \mid ( T , x )$ maintains the sufficient statistic T , where the conditional distribution might be based on the regression model $Y = \beta X$ . Statistical obfuscation preserves the sufficient statistic precisely but has two key drawbacks. First, data are assumed to be multivariate normal: “This can be handled in the usual way by transforming the data first—in which case the information that is preserved would be the means and variances of the transformed data.” (Burridge 2003, p. 326.) In practice it might be difficult or impossible to find such a transformation, such as for logit models used to analyze survival data. Second, the data owner must determine the best underlying model a priori, which could be linear, nonlinear, etc.: “We do not have specific advice on this except to note that simpler models give better protection so there must be a trade-off between the two aims [of statistical utility and identification risk]” (Burridge 2003, p. 323). One problem with model selection is that if the analyst model does not nest within the generation model, inferences might not be valid. Thus, while IPSO performs well in terms of utility and moderately well in terms of protecting confidentiality of individuals, several business requirements are not met (Table 2).

2.3.3. Rank Swapping. In rank swapping, two data cells that are similar across two different records are swapped (Reiss 1980). No data are actually changed, so univariate statistics such as the mean will be equivalent. Disclosure risk can be low, depending on the proximity of swaps (Moore 1996). However, the robustness of rank swapping to bivariate and multivariate relationships is unclear. Refinements to the basic technique include swapping two rows only if they are within a specified distance of one another (Carlson and Salabasis 2002, Dalenius and Reiss 1982). Prior research suggests low disclosure risk protection and moderate statistical utility (Moore 1996, Muralidhar and Sarathy 2006).

2.3.4. Data Shuffling. Related to rank swapping is data shuffling, in which a random distribution determines how to replace one value with another in a chain-like sequence within a given field (Muralidhar and Sarathy 2006). Data shuffling provides low disclosure risk and low estimation bias. However, analysis scope is limited to monotonic interrelationships: “If maintaining rank order correlation is used as the criteria for assessing data utility of the masked data, then the results of this experiment indicate that, in every case, data shuffling performs better than all three versions of the swapped data” (Muralidhar et al. 2006, p. 169). To emphasize, data swapping and data shuffling provide valid point estimates of pairwise monotonic relationships such as rank-order correlation, but their robustness to other analysis methods commonly used by business organizations is unclear, and they have unknown asymptotic properties.

2.3.5. Multiple Imputation. Multiple imputation is a technique originally proposed to improve the validity of handling nonresponse in surveys by filling in data that are missing at random (Rubin and Schenker 1986). The intuition is that single imputation does not account for the error associated with not knowing the values of the missing observations.

By multiply imputing several observations for each missing observation, the variability of not knowing is captured by the variability between imputations. Conceptually, multiple imputation preserves the correct conditional framework in the face of incomplete variables. Empirically, a substantial improvement in data utility results from using even two imputations rather than one, together with appropriate combining rules (Rubin and Schenker 1986). Combining rules are statistically derived formulae for computing statistics of interest using multiple imputed samples. For example, the mean of a variable is computed as the average of the mean of each imputed sample. Multiple imputation has been employed in a variety of literatures, including survival analysis of AIDS patients (Hsu et al. 2006), global climate change research (Hopke et al. 2001, Knudsen et al. 2007), and analysis of commodity exports (Fearon 2004). Extending the concept of missing data to all unsampled units in the population, multiple imputation was proposed as a way to generate statistically valid masked data sets (Rubin 1993). Data are generated via several methods, including parametric approaches such as regression and nonparametric approaches such as bootstrapping. The former necessitates that the analyst model nest within the generating model for inferences to be valid. In contrast, bootstrapping provides valid regression coefficient estimation (Raghunathan et al. 2003), though complete records from the original data set are included, raising concern about identification risk. In summary, multiple imputation using Bayesian bootstrapping performs well in terms of value but not in terms of risk, does not enable a user-specified trade-off but has a strong theoretical foundation.

## 3. MIMP: A Hybrid Approach for Generating Shareable Statistical Databases

Multiple imputation with multimodal perturbation combines model-free multiple imputation with random multimodal noise. Adding noise to multiply imputed data maintains the validity of combined statistics, as we show below. At the same time, multimodal noise generated from mixtures of normal densities does not have its density concentrated at zero. This overcomes a key drawback of normally distributed noise that the most frequently occurring value is near zero, meaning that the data are not perturbed much and the confidentiality of respondent individuals and organizations is jeopardized. Combining statistics, such as the mean, of the resulting sum of imputed data and multimodal noise over several imputations averages out the noise, thereby improving statistical properties. We are not aware of any studies that propose adding noise to multiply imputed data of statistical data sets. We now describe the hybrid approach.

## 3.1. Multiple Imputation as a Statistically Valid Approach for Generating Masked Data

Rubin (1993) first proposed that multiple imputation be used to generate “synthetic” U.S. census data in which sensitive fields (industry and occupation codes) are imputed while nonsensitive fields (e.g., gender, weeks worked per year, region of country) remain unchanged. Rubin used a model-based approach (Bayesian logistic regression) to create five imputed data sets in which statistical inference is “valid, and often, perhaps even typically, more precise than the corresponding inferences based on the research file” (Rubin 1993, p. 466). In the model-based approach, there is no risk of disclosing confidential data as sensitive variables from the original data set are not included in the released data. This methodology was employed by Abowd et al. (2006) in a complex setting of cross-sectional time-series data derived from the U.S. Census Bureau’s Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP), the Internal Revenue Service’s (IRS) individual lifetime earnings data, and the Social Security Administration’s (SSA) individual benefit data.<sup>3</sup> In the case of national statistical agencies, extensive resources can be applied to generating valid masked data sets based on modelbased imputation. Appropriate models are developed based on knowledge of the specific data characteristics. For example, in a study of data from the U.S. Current Population Survey, Reiter (2005) employed different imputation models suitable for education (multinomial logit regression), marital status (multinomial logit regression), alimony payments (logistic regression), and social security payments (multistage logistic regression), among others.

In contrast, business organizations manage numerous databases with widely varying characteristics that might be put to use for unanticipated purposes. For example, an electric power company might collect and leverage data on home energy use for the purpose of targeted marketing. In contrast, a researcher might have a different purpose, for example, analyzing the association between electricity usage and home energy monitors controlling for other factors such as household size. If the electricity provider used an imputation model for generating masked data that did not conform to the researcher analysis model, the resulting estimates and inferences would be invalid, a point that is underscored by Raghunathan et al. (2003, p. 13): “The quality of inferences from the synthetic data clearly depends upon the imputation models.” Thus, the challenge for the business organization context is to develop a method for generating shareable statistical databases that does not require detailed knowledge of the data, does not require substantial resources to generate, conforms with a wide range of potential analysis models, and preserves confidentiality. The MIMP approach involving model-free multiple imputation is intended to satisfy these objectives. We now explicate the intuition of the method (technical details are provided in Appendix 1).<sup>4</sup>

## 3.2. MIMP Approach

Given a data sample, we wish to create new samples such that statistics computed based on the new samples are valid and a snooper cannot accurately infer the identity of individuals in the new samples. In most existing methods of data masking, a heuristic is used, such as, “swap values for each field in the data set that are within a certain distance” (data swapping) or “take the average of points in space that are close” (micro-aggregation). There is little or no statistical foundation to such approaches, meaning that statistical statements about, e.g., asymptotic properties, are elusive. In contrast, multiple imputation is based on a well-developed statistical foundation, thereby providing the basis for valid statistical estimation and inference (Rubin 1993).

The BB thus provides the correct interpretation for our purposes: “the BB generates likelihood statements about parameters rather than frequency statements about statistics under assumed values for parameters” (Rubin 1981, p. 131).

## 4. Analysis

## 4.1. Operationalizing Statistical Usefulness and Confidentiality Protection

4.1.1. Statistical Usefulness: Ensuring Results are Valid and Reliable. From a statistical perspective, the degree to which statistical estimation and inference based on the masked data are consistent with population values is an important gauge of statistical usefulness. Several approaches have been employed in the literature, including information loss based on point estimates of univariate statistics (Domingo-Ferrer et al. 2001, Templ 2006), computing the bias and standard error of correlation coefficients (Muralidhar and Sarathy 2006), and statistical inference involving the population value coverage rate of confidence intervals (Raghunathan et al. 2003, Reiter 2005). From a business perspective, the masked data must appear similar to the original data sample in terms of means, correlation coefficients, etc. To address both statistical issues and business user requirements, we use boxplots to compare simulation results of statistics of interest (mean, correlation coefficient, regression estimate) with that of the population. We also employ tables to display simulation results for statistical inference in the case of multiple regression.

4.1.2. Disclosure Risk: Protecting the Confidentiality of Respondents. The risk of reidentification is based on the following thought experiment. Suppose an intruder has five MIMP data sets and has also obtained two variables from the actual database. He uses a simple algorithm to match a randomly chosen MIMP data set with the two-variable database: let the closest point (data record) from the MIMP data set to the actual data set be a match. The worst-case scenario from a risk perspective is to assume that the intruder has all the variables in the actual data set as well as the five MIMP data sets. The Euclidean distance between every MIMP data set point and every actual data set point is measured with scaling of each variable by its respective standard deviation. A match is recorded if the first- or second-closest point from each point in the MIMP data set to a point in the actual data set corresponds to the actual point. This procedure is widely used in the literature and is referred to as Euclidean distance-based record linkage (Domingo-Ferrer and Mateo-Sanz 2002, Muralidhar and Sarathy 2006).<sup>6</sup> As emphasized in prior U.S. census data research: “Our disclosure avoidance research uses the principle that a potential intruder would first try to reidentify the source record for a given synthetic data observation 0 0 0 the percentage of true matches relative to the size of the files should not be excessively large” (Abowd et al. 2006, p. 6). The risk metric is defined as the number of nearest neighbors that are matches with the actual data divided by the total number of data points. A risk of 5% means that if a match were assigned to each nearest or second-nearest neighbor, 5% of such matches would be correct (although which ones is unknown).<sup>7</sup> We define safety as 1—risk and use it to preserve a “higher is better” scale property consistent with statistical utility.<sup>8</sup>

## 4.2. Simulation Plan

Consistent with the literature, we use Monte Carlo simulation to assess statistical usefulness and disclosure risk for MIMP data (Table 3). The baseline procedure (S1) is to first generate a $1 , 0 0 0 \times 5$ multivariate normal population with mean vector 0 and correlation coefficients $= 0 . 9 \ ( \rho _ { 1 2 } ) , \ 0 . 7 \ ( \rho _ { 1 3 } )$ , and 0.5 (all others). Second, draw $r = 1 , 0 0 0$ independent samples from this population of size $n = 5 0 0$ . Third, for each sample generate m = 5 MIMP data sets with noise parameter $d = 2$ . Fourth, compute the mean, Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, and regression coefficient (first variable regressed on other four variables: $X _ { 1 } = b _ { 0 } + b _ { 1 } X _ { 2 } + b _ { 2 } X _ { 3 } + \bar { b } _ { 3 } X _ { 4 } + b _ { 4 } X _ { 5 } )$ for the 1,000 MIMP sets using combining rules. Bias is defined as ${ \hat { X } } - \mu ,$ where $\tilde { X }$ is the point estimate for a given simulation run and $\mu$ is the population coefficient. Standard error is defined as the standard deviation of ${ \hat { X } } - \mu$ . For completeness, we plot ${ \hat { X } } - \mu$ over all simulation runs using boxplots to illustrate bias and standard error.

## 4.3. Statistical Utility

Baseline statistical utility is computed as above and displayed in a three-panel boxplot in which panels correspond to the three statistics of interest: mean, correlation, and beta (Figure 3).

Several observations emerge from the baseline statistical utility simulation analysis. First, there is very little bias for both multiple imputation (medium gray bar) and MIMP (dark bar), as indicated by the symmetry of the interquartile range around zero. This holds for all variables for the mean, correlation, and beta. For example, for the mean of $X _ { 1 }$ and $X _ { 2 } , ~ { \hat { X } } - \mu$ is −000011 and −000008 (multiple imputation) and 0.0007 and 0.0003 (MIMP), respectively. Second, the standard error is slightly larger for the MIMP data versus the multiply imputed data in the case of the mean. Third, to check the sensitivity of these results to simulation parameter assumptions, we changed $d , n ,$ and m and reran the simulations. Standard error increases slightly with increasing amount of noise added, $d ,$ though bias remains very small (Appendix 2, Figure A2.1). Similarly, standard error increases with decreasing sample size, $n ,$ and decreasing number of imputations, $m ,$ as evidenced by a wider interquartile range and longer tails of the boxplots, although bias remains very small (Appendix 2, Figures A2.2, A2.3). We also compared model-based imputation with MIMP using the sequential regression multivariate imputation approach (Abowd et al. 2006) and generalized additive modeling (GAM) (Wood 2006), which illustrates the bias introduced by GAM (Figure A2.4).<sup>9</sup> Finally, we examined the sensitivity of multivariate relationships by using different dependent variables (instead of the first variable) and by using a weighted-least-squares regression analysis estimation. The pattern of results from these robustness checks is consistent with baseline simulations. Overall, the MIMP approach yields data with a very low statistical bias over a range of simulation parameters for all three statistics of interest (mean, correlation coefficient, regression estimate) and compares well to the actual data sample.

We now examine the effectiveness of MIMP compared to another widely used data masking technique: rank swapping. Rank-based proximity swapping has been shown to provide reasonable univariate statistics in prior simulation experiments (Domingo-Ferrer et al. 2001). However, unlike MIMP, which has known asymptotic properties for non-univariate statistics, the statistical utility of rank swapping for bivariate and multivariate statistics is unclear (Fienberg and McIntyre 2004). We conduct a Monte Carlo simulation with the same parameters as adopted in the baseline simulation and choose the swapping parameter $p = 2 0$ (values of a variable are swapped if they are within 20% of the total number of records.<sup>10</sup>) We choose this value as it represents a conservative level based on risk and utility considerations (see §2.3). Results are presented in Figure 4.

As expected, MIMP data and rank-swapped data perform well with respect to the mean. However, rank-swapped data exhibit substantial bias for the correlation coefficient. For example, $\rho _ { 1 2 }$ is roughly 28% less on average than the population value. Also, rank-swapped data are erratic with respect to regression coefficients, exhibiting a negative bias for $b _ { 1 }$ and $b _ { 2 }$ and a positive bias for $b _ { 3 }$ and $b _ { 4 } .$ . We conducted additional simulations to examine statistical utility for nonmultivariate normal data (Figure 5).

As with multivariate normal data, MIMP preserves the point estimate of the mean, correlation coefficient, and regression estimate in the case of nonnormal multivariate data.<sup>11</sup> In contrast, rank swapping preserves the mean as well as certain correlation coefficients with lower magnitudes $( \mathrm { e . g . } , \rho _ { 1 4 } ) ,$ , but does not provide valid point estimates for higher correlation coefficients $\left( \mathrm { e . g . , ~ } \rho _ { 1 2 } , \rho _ { 1 3 } \right)$ . As with multivariate normal data, rank swapping is not reliable with respect to regression estimates for nonnormal data— sometimes positive bias and sometimes exhibiting a negative bias. Additional simulation results using a smaller swap parameter p (down to 5%) are consistent with the pattern of these results. Demonstration of robustness to nonmultivariate normal data thus satisfies a key business requirement not satisfied by certain other methods (see §2.3).

In summary, point estimates of the mean, correlation coefficient, and regression coefficient for MIMP data exhibit high precision (spread of the box plot) and high accuracy (centrality of the boxplot with respect to zero) and are very similar to the sample distributions. These results hold for the case of normal and nonnormal multivariate data (which is likely to be how business data are distributed) and demonstrate a slight but predictable precision reduction when varying simulation parameters. In contrast, rank swapped data exhibit significant and unpredictable bias for the correlation coefficient and regression coefficient estimate.

We now examine the inferential validity of MIMP as compared with rank swapping. We generate the same population and perform the same regression as before (first variable on other four). We then compute the reject rate (percentage of simulation runs in which the null hypothesis of no significance is rejected) using the actual sample and masked data (MIMP and rank swapping). We also compute the percentage of simulation runs in which statistical inference of the masked data agrees with that of the sample (i.e., both reject or both retain the null hypothesis of insignificant regression coefficient) and the number of times in which the masked data inference is conservative, i.e., masked data indicate retain while sample indicates reject. When the sum of the latter does not equal 100, this indicates that for some simulation runs, masked data are not conservative.

## 4.4. Statistical Risk

We next compute identity disclosure risk (data safety) as a function of the amount of noise added (d5, counting a point as identified if either of the nearest two points in space is a match (see §4.1). We use Euclidean distance-based disclosure, consistent with the literature and compare multimodal noise $( 2 , \ 4 , \ 6 ,$ and 8 modes) with unimodal noise for the case of $n =$ 200 and $n = 5 0 0$ (Figure 6). Results indicate that multimodal noise provides a significant enhancement in terms of disclosure risk versus unimodal noise. For $n = 5 0 0 ,$ , MIMP safety (1 − risk) rises quickly then levels off above 95% at $d = 1$ , a broad pattern which holds for nonnormal data and for $n = 2 0 0$ , although for the latter case more noise is required to reach 95% (d = 105). In contrast, for unimodal noise data, safety does not exceed 75% and is roughly constant for $d = 1$ to 3: widening the normal distribution does little to shift data points in space and provides empirical support for the validity and benefit of MIMP. Results are insensitive to the number of dimensions in the data set. Finally, additional simulations indicate that when n is very high (5,000), safety is much higher, e.g., 96.5% for d = 1.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Illustrative Application

We have demonstrated the benefits of a combination of multiple imputation and multimodal noise to accomplish data masking for business organizations. Given the benefits of multiple imputation in preserving statistical utility, the challenge was to combine it with a technique that would minimize risk without reducing statistical utility. The choice of multimodal perturbation was not obvious because, on its own, random perturbation provides only moderate risk reduction and utility. We demonstrated the efficacy of the method in enhancing statistical utility and disclosure risk preservation by using simulated data and examining univariate, bivariate, and multivariate statistics. We also demonstrated that MIMP yields enhanced statistical utility relative to rank swapping. We now provide a brief illustration of how the method could be applied in practice in the context of salary data, typically considered sensitive but available for public universities.

We obtained data on all faculty members of a large midwestern U.S. university and two west coast U.S. universities for 2007. We used this data set as if it were private university data, which would not be disclosed in its original form. The database comprises 6,829 faculty members and three variables: CAMPUS, RANK, and SALARY. We simulated an AGE variable by modeling it as correlated with salary conditional on rank and distributed differently by rank. We examine how the business data owner and the data analyst might use this data set and corresponding MIMP data.

The business data owner must first decide which method of data masking to employ. If the technique requires normally distributed data, the data owner must first determine whether an appropriate transformation exists to normalize the data. For model-based methods, such as statistical obfuscation or modelbased multiple imputation, the data owner must also determine how best to model the data (linear, quadratic, nonparametric, etc.). Both of these tasks are complex, involve substantial resource expenditures, and possibly involve the services of statistical experts. In contrast, if the data owner chooses MIMP, no model is required and data of any density are allowed. The process for generating MIMP data could be as easy as employing an R package MIMP implementation to automatically generate the data, given user input of simple parameters such as d.<sup>12</sup> The analyst can assess baseline statistical utility and risk prior to sharing the data using diagnostics provided in the same R program, and vary d to obtain acceptable metrics.<sup>13</sup> Estimation bias is very low for salary (−0012%) and age (0%), while data safety is very high (≥9905%), equivalent to just a few matched points out of 6,829 (Table 5).

Beyond this specific example, prior research on the discrepancies between the imputer’s model and the analyst’s model is summarized by Schafer (1997, p. 139), who describes the issue as composable into four cases. The four cases hinge on two questions:

who makes the more stringent assumptions, and who is right in making those assumptions? If the analyst makes more stringent assumptions than does the imputer, those assumptions cannot invalidate the imputer’s model, whether the analyst is right or wrong. If, on the other hand, the imputer makes more stringent assumptions than does the analyst, the situation is more complex. Schafer (1997) reviews prior work showing that if the imputer’s additional assumptions are true, the variance of estimates might be smaller than the analyst realizes. The fourth case is the only problem from the analyst’s point of view: if the imputer makes additional unjustified assumptions, the analyst might arrive at false conclusions unaware of the unjustified assumptions. Schafer (1997) argues that, if additional imputer assumptions are effectively communicated to the analyst and rejected by the analyst, the situation is no worse than that faced by the analyst with missing data.<sup>15</sup>

## 5.2. Summary

Our analysis provides three principal contributions to the literature on data masking. First, we have demonstrated that data masking techniques developed for statistical agencies such as the U.S. census might have substantial value when adapted for commercial purposes. As organizations seek new sources of revenue from their expanding storehouses of data, they may adopt data masking techniques to preserve confidentiality and statistical information. Organizations are also under pressure to be proactive in stemming the tide of identify theft, which can also be addressed via data masking in conjunction with other methods. Second, we developed a specific technique—MIMP— that is tailored to the needs of business organizations.

Whether for the purposes of database development, data sharing, or data selling for new revenue sources, MIMP is a modest but important step toward development of safe and useful data sets for business use. In particular, the idea of hybrid approaches that combine more than one technique is an important new strategy in data masking. We demonstrated that MIMP offers high statistical utility and high confidentiality protection. Third, we demonstrated the challenges and opportunities in a realistic business scenario from the point of view of both the data owner and the data analyst, which is infrequent in the technical literature that focuses on algorithm development and the business data owner point of view. What decisions are faced by the data owner and analyst? What statistical expertise can be assumed? How can the data owner employ existing methods successfully? The simple faculty salary example revealed several important characteristics of the data masking context that are critical to business adoption of systems employing data masking.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our study did not analyze the impact of outliers and extreme points on statistical utility and disclosure risk. The U.S. Census Bureau uses such methods as top-coding (e.g., salary over \$200,000 is coded as 200,000) to ensure the safety of certain extreme data points. Thus, it would be useful in future research to generate data that contain such extreme points, use various top-coding schemes in conjunction with MIMP, and examine the effects on bias, inference, and disclosure risk.

Another area of future research is systematic analysis of the sensitivity of MIMP to specific data distributions and other analysis techniques. Sensitivity analysis for relevant distributions is a reasonable next step. For example, Berry and Linoff (2004) and Linoff and Berry (2002) examine business data miners using survival analysis techniques to investigate customer defection. They model customer survival as having an exponential or Weibull distribution. Customer data in online logs are likely to be sensitive, subject to usage agreements, and valuable to organizational partners and rivals. A fruitful area of future research is thus to examine the sensitivity of estimation bias and statistical inference to various types of statistical distributions, amount of noise added, and sample size. Other analysis approaches, such as two-sample comparisons, would also extend validity of the MIMP approach.<sup>16</sup>

A third area of research involves leveraging a distinctive property of MIMP: noise with known properties is added to bootstrapped data. This noise could be modeled as measurement error, which would allow the use of techniques such as simulation extrapolation to correct for the error (Carroll et al. 2006). In theory, this could improve statistical estimation and inference, but further research is required to determine the extent of improvement, including for alternative distributional forms of noise added to the multiply imputed data.

Finally, there has been little scholarly attention paid to information systems design issues in the context of data masking. The IS design science community has much to offer, including approaches for developing and analyzing systems embodying data masking in real organizations (Hevner et al. 2004). MIMP can provide as many sets of masked data as required by a decision maker, which must be decided by the business data owner. This and related questions might be embodied in a user interface, and its study among actual users a useful next step for investigation. Different target user audiences have different levels of risk and utility. We can envision a control panel enabling the decision maker to select among MIMP parameters by simply specifying different mixes of priorities for risk and utility among different target user audiences. A clear next step is to evaluate such a control panel in a manner that takes advantage of what is already known about the usability of security. It has been well established that users are often ignorant of the security consequences of their actions and that opaque mechanisms will not be adopted whatever their technical merits (Garfinkel and Miller 2005, Gaw et al. 2006, Whitten and Tygar 1999). As a result, the usability evaluation of an interface for an information system that masks data must investigate the ease with which a user can effectively specify a given set of trade-offs and the congruence between a user’s specification and her understanding of the consequences of that specification.
