# Input information complexity, perceived time pressure, and information processing in GSS-based work groups: An experimental investigation using a decision schema to alleviate information overload conditions

Souren Paul <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Derek L. Nazareth <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business, College of Staten Island, City University of New York, Staten Island, NY 10314, United States

<sup>b</sup> Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin Milwaukee, P.O. Box: 742, Milwaukee, WI: 53201-0742, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Information processing Information complexity Information overload Time pressure Integrative complexity Collaboration technology Group support systems

## a b s t r a c t

Collaboration technology enhances the ability of work groups to acquire and share large volumes of information within a short period. The processing of voluminous information is challenging and may lead to conditions of information overload. The issue of complexity of information processing in collaboration technology supported group work, and the mechanisms to overcome the information overload conditions have not received suf<sup>fi</sup>cient attention in the past. In this paper, we attempt to address this gap by building a theoretical model and validating it through a laboratory experiment. Based on prior research on information processing at individual level, we propose that information processing in groups that use group support systems (GSS) is shaped by input information complexity and time pressure. We examine information processing of GSS-supported groups to perform tasks involving cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict. We demonstrate that information processing has an inverted U-shaped relationship with input information complexity and a positive relationship with time pressure. The study also demonstrates that the inclusion of a decision schema that incorporates aggregate level information gleaned from the work of prior groups engaged in a similar decision situation alleviates the information overload, enabling groups to process larger and more complex information.

## 1. Introduction

With the globalization of business, enhanced market competition, and the need to respond quickly to customer requirements, many organizations have sought to distribute business activities in locations where the requisite expertise is available, increasingly relying on virtual work groups whose members communicate using some form of collaboration systems. These systems facilitate the sharing and <sup>fl</sup>ow of information, allowing members to access large volumes of information within a short period of time. Collaboration technology can boost the productivity of virtual groups provided that the members can effectively process the acquired information. The productivity of individuals and groups depends strongly on their information-processing characteristics [13]. Although information processing is a well established area of research in cognitive science and psychology, and more recently in group support system (GSS), very little research has speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focused on the information characteristics of collaboration technology supported work groups, with the exception of the work by Grise and Gallupe [19]. Rao and

Jarvenpaa suggest that although groups have extended information processing capabilities, the need to communicate among group members detracts the information processing capabilities of the group members [53]. Thus, many questions concerning information processing within the work group need to be answered. What happens when these work groups access a large volume of information within a short period of time? Do they experience information overload? Does diversity of acquired information result in need for increased information processing? How can the adverse effects of potential information overload be overcome? These are some of the critical issues that will determine the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of collaborative systems in a time-critical group process. In addition, it offers an opportunity to shed light on changes or enhancements in collaboration system functionality that may need to be incorporated to facilitate information processing in technology supported work groups. In this paper, we attempt to address some of these research questions through an experiment involving collaboration technology supported work groups. In conducting our research, we rely on prior studies on information processing and extend them in the context of group tasks involving cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

Prior studies suggest that an increase in the number of information cues provided to decision makers can have an adverse impact on their decision making performance [27,46,55,61,62]. Schroder et al. [55] modeled input information cues in terms of information load and information diversity. They posited an inverted U-curve for the relationship between information processing and information complexity, suggesting an adverse effect from information overload. Later empirical studies on information processing of individuals did establish volume (or load) and diversity of information as two major elements of input information complexity [6,26–28]. The effect of information overload and its impact on decision making has been consistently noted in several disciplines, including marketing [33,38,39], and accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance [29,30]. Studies on information diversity have found that increases in information diversity have a negative impact on the decision processes, including decision time [28] and decision quality [26]. We focus on both these dimensions of input information complexity and conduct an experiment to explore their impact on the information processing of GSS-based work groups.

Prior studies also reveal that the task and the manner of information use also affect information processing and cause overload [15]. Factors that contribute to information overload include non-routine tasks, task interdependencies [68], task interruptions [59], and time pressure [54]. We incorporated the task and process characteristics in our study by measuring perceived time pressure of the participants and focusing only on cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict tasks, which are complex and not routine. In addition, we sought to assess if the use of a decision schema that presents aggregate information on the decision situation would enhance information processing capability and enhance the ability of group members to process large volumes of information. We employed a decision schema for a subset of the groups in an attempt to streamline information gathering and processing. We report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of an experiment in which GSS-supported groups accessed a large set of information necessary to complete a group task. Half of these groups were allowed to use a hypermedia-based schema that depicted the relative importance that some input cues had over others. The performance of both groups are compared and reported in this paper.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We start with a review of some relevant studies on information processing and prior GSS research in related areas and propose a set of propositions. Next, we elaborate the design of the research, specifying the group task, and operationalizations of the constructs. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the study are reported. Discussions of these results, <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and their implications for practice round out the paper.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Information processing and integrative complexity

In cognitive psychology, there are multiple views about how humans process information. The generalists adopt a simple view, and posit that all humans are more or less similar in processing information [46,63]. In stark contrast, researchers like Newell and Simon [48] suggest that each individual processes information uniquely. This is termed the “unique” school of thought regarding information processing by Driver and Mock [12]. They also suggest the presence of a middle path, termed the “differential” school of thought, which recognizes the differences in individuals' information processing capacity. It has been suggested that humans process uncertain and complex information differently [12]. Researchers have sought to explain information complexity in terms of two major factors: information load and diversity [13,55]. Iselin [27] further divided the concept of information load into the quantity of different dimensions and the quantity of repeated dimensions of the input information. Schroder et al. [55] proposed a nonlinear (inverted U-shaped) relationship between the complexity of input information and the level of information processing (Fig. 1), drawing from work by Miller on cognitive processing limitations [46]. The level of information processing is the maximum when the input complexity is moderate, i.e., neither too high nor too low. This is referred to as the “optimal point” of input complexity by Schroder et al. [55]. When input complexity increases, individuals increase their information processing up to the “optimal point” beyond which they fail to process all input information and experience information overload [26]. Further, Driver and Streufert [13] suggest that the inverted U-shaped relationship also holds for the behavior, perception, and decision making of the individuals or groups engaged in information processing. They propose the concept of integrative complexity in this context. Integrative complexity has its roots in Kelly's Personal Construct Theory [34], and represents the degree to which thinking and reasoning involve the recognition and integration of multiple perspectives and possibilities and their interrelated contingencies [65]. While processing information, individuals or groups sort the input information into different dimensions or points of view, which is referred to as differentiation. Differentiation represents the perception of different dimensions within a stimulus domain, and the adoption of different perspectives when considering the domain [65]. Integration represents the development of conceptual connections among differentiated domains and perspectives [65]. The extent of differentiation and integration represents the integrative complexity in the information processing. Integrative complexity is typically measured in levels, with individuals at the low end of the scale employing simple decision making rules, and individuals at the high end employing multiple strategies for dealing with complex integration. Integrative complexity is a central tenet in individual information processing and decision making. The greater the complexity of information available for decision making, the greater is the need for integrative complexity. The nature of the relationship between input information complexity and the level of information processing are generally considered to be nonlinear, and the exact nature is determined by the characteristics of the system that is processing the information [13,55].

As shown in Fig. 1, the system (individual or group) with higher information processing capability will be able to process more information than systems with lower capacity. Driver and Struefert [13] also imply that an individual's information seeking behavior is consistent with his or her information processing capability. They suggested that the integrative complexity model is valid for groups as well. Studies on information processing and integrative complexity in groups can be found in the literature on small group research. Gruenfeld and Hollingshead [20] studied 22 work groups over a period of 12 weeks and found that the relationship between integrative complexity and quality of decision outcomes of groups was dependent on the nature of the task. However, neither information processing nor integrative complexity has been studied extensively in the context of collaboration technology supported work groups. We discuss prior research on information processing in the GSS context in section 2.3. Another factor that affects information processing is perceived time pressure. Prior research in this area is discussed in the following section.

## 2.2. Information processing and time pressure

The concept of time pressure in information processing has been discussed in many disciplines, including accounting, marketing, and management [18,21,40,54]. Schick et al. [54] suggest that the time to process information is a combination of the time required to interact with others in organizations and the internal time required to process information. When the time required to process information exceeds the available time, information overload occurs [54]. Decision making situations that involve extreme time pressure are likely to exacerbate conditions of information overload. In an experiment where students had to make choices about higher education and employment, Hahn et al. [21] found that the extent of information overload was dependent on time pressure, exhibiting a non-linear relationship — an inverted U-shaped curve. Other situations involving extreme time pressure include responding to emergencies. Groups responding to emergency conditions often experience overload, primarily due to threat rigidity [67], wherein planned responses are effective for prede<sup>fi</sup>ned threats, but novel threats prove unsettling [51]. In this context, the relationship between stress and performance has been posited to be an inverted U-shaped curve, based on the suggestion that cognitive absorption in the task may result in reduced cognitive overload. Hendy, Liao, and Milgram [22] posit that the load on human information processing results from time pressure. Maule et al. also found that time pressured individuals accelerate processing of information [43]. Likewise, increases in irrelevant information and loss of cognitive attention are posited to contribute to increased information overload [51].

## 2.3. Information processing in GSS-based groups

While information processing of individuals and groups has been studied extensively, the processing of information in GSS-based work groups is relatively thin. One early work is of Grise and Gallupe [19]. In their study, they demonstrate that GSS tools that can regulate the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information can help users process ideas of high levels of complexity. Other researchers have acknowledged the importance of information processing in GSS research. Hiltz and Turoff [23] argue that the users of computer-mediated communication systems (CMCS) experience information overload which peaks at an intermediate level of CMCS use, when users do not have the skill to screen the vast amount of information that has accumulated in the system. Group processes that use computer mediated communication are particularly sensitive to information overload as the group size increases and the level of individual participation becomes more intense. In asynchronous learning through the use of a virtual classroom, information overload has been reported for large groups and high levels of activity when tools to manage information <sup>fl</sup>ows are inadequate [24]. Brainstorming using group support technology is susceptible to the same concern. Through the use of anonymity, parallel idea generation, and multiple dialogues, electronic brainstorming has been shown to generate more ideas, higher quality ideas, and more novel ideas [11], particularly when compared to non-supported groups [10]. However, the proliferation of ideas may lead to information overload [17]. Other researchers have also found that the quantity of ideas generated in a group correlates negatively with perceived intellectual synergy, perceived learning, process satisfaction, and cohesiveness [8], suggesting the presence of information overload.

Researchers acknowledge that sharing and processing of information are important aspects of GSS [7,45]. Cognitive absorption has been proposed as the means of engaging individuals in a task thereby permitting more intense work [1]. However, in situations where this level of intense involvement cannot be guaranteed, mechanisms to support and facilitate processing of information are clearly desired. Jones, Ravad, and Rafaeli [32] found that though there is a dynamic interplay between volume and complexity of information exchanged in on-line groups. They found that users in on-line communities tended to respond to simpler messages in interactions characterized by information overload. Hiltz and Turoff [23] propose several structures that CMCS can impose to minimize information overload. The use of the Delphi technique [36] and other voting structures can help group members reduce the number of communications to perform a group task. Rao and Jarvenpaa suggest that the use of quantitative models can extend the information capabilities of a group [53]. Leaders or moderators can help group members organize information and curtail unnecessary communications. Ferreira and Antunes found that the introduction of a mechanism to regulate the <sup>fl</sup>ow of ideas can attenuate information overload in electronic brainstorming [16]. Schultze and Vandenbosch [56] found that the use of asynchronous communication, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schemes, and search tools prevented the occurrence of overload in a study involving the use of LotusNotes in a company.

## 3. Research framework

Prior research has shown that GSS-based groups can experience information overload as an unintended consequence of the use of technology. Some of this overload may be caused by the application of the technology itself, e.g. excessive discussion in computer mediated learning, free-wheeling in electronic brainstorming, and the like. However, in many decision making situations, a group may have to deal with potentially large amounts of available information, none of which is generated by the group itself. In these cases, the effects of overload may be experienced. Information overload situations can be avoided by operating in decision environments in which information load matches with the processing capacity of information [4]. Individuals assess overload conditions based on their ability to process input information load and their preference for the rate of processing information [50]. Thus, rather than focusing on information overload in GSS-based groups, we seek to determine how information complexity and time pressure relate to the level of information processing in these groups. We further examine if the availability of a decision aid can alleviate the presence of information overload in a group decision process. The basic conceptual framework for this research appears in Fig. 2.

## 3.1. Research propositions

Based on the prior research on information processing and integrative complexity [13,55,64], the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Grise and Gallupe [19], and the research on controlling overload in CMCS [23,56], we argue that GSS-based groups will also deal effectively with increasing information complexity until their processing capability saturates, and thereafter seek to simplify the processing of information. Therefore, we propose:

Proposition 1. In GSS-based work groups, group members' input information complexity is nonlinearly (inverted U-shaped) related to their level of information processing.

Time pressure has been frequently suggested as a contributor to information overload in decision making situations. In experiments with individual decision makers, time pressure has been found to introduce a U-shaped relationship between information overload and decision quality [21] . In other research, time pressure has been shown to affect individual decision makers differently, with some performing better and others considerably worse [42]. A variety of different coping mechanisms have been posited for group decision makers dealing with time pressure. Marsden et al. suggest the use of an alternative communication language, and report some success in a series of experiments [41]. Changes to the group decision making process, employing a leaderfocused process, are reported in [58]. Filtering becomes a more prominent coping mechanism as participants focus more on task relevant activities [3]; [35]. Hiltz and Turoff [23] also suggest that the use of <sup>fi</sup>ltering and scanning features in CMCS can help users to avoid information overload. The use of <sup>fi</sup>ltering mechanisms has also been advocated by Schultze and Vandenbosch [56] who studied users of LotusNotes in a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Verplanken [69] found that under time pressure individuals accelerated the processing of information. In their study on decision-making under uncertainty, Maule, Hockey, and Bdzola also found that time pressured participants used a variety of coping mechanisms to deal with time pressure [43]. Therefore, we propose:

Proposition 2. In GSS-based work, time pressure is positively related to the level of information processing.

The effect of information complexity in GSS-based group work can be overcome, at least to some extent, by providing additional information processing support. Schultze and Vandenbosch suggest that the use of groupware in asynchronous mode increases users' control over information and helps in avoiding adverse effects of information overload [56]. However, in synchronous GSS applications, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to avoid the adverse effect information overload. Rao and Jarvenpaa argue that the communication needs and information processing needs compete for the mental resources of the group members and thus limit the information processing abilities of the group [53]. They recommend the use of quantitative models to stretch the information processing abilities of the groups. Zigurs and Buckland [71] discussed several types of information processing support that a GSS application can utilize, including tools to gather, aggregate, evaluate, and structure information. In some cases, the simple aggregation of information provides decision makers with a broad overview of underlying information that is hidden in the volumes of data presented to the decision makers. Senior level executives almost always analyze the aggregate information first and then drill down the dimensions where further analyses are necessary [52]. A similar approach can be followed in GSS applications. Decision makers can be provided with aggregate level information of prior decisions, which can consist of preferences, preference structures, policies, and norms of groups engaged in similar decisions in the past. The aggregate information could include a drill-down capability that enables the decision makers to conduct in-depth analyses of any speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspect of prior decisions, such as the details of a particular choice made by the individual decision makers in prior decision making situations. This aggregate level information together with the drill-down facility, can serve as a viable decision schema. The utility of a schema in processing decision information has been discussed in the organizational literature [2,60,70]. Decisions that incorporate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of prior decisions can be viewed as memory-based decision. We posit that the use of the schema will help the decision makers to identify and focus on the critical dimensions when input information is voluminous and diverse. The groups that use the decision schema will have enhanced capability to process information and will experience information overload at a higher level than those not using it. Thus, we propose:

Proposition 3. In GSS-based work groups, group members using a decision schema will experience information overload at a higher level of input information complexity than group members that do not use the schema.

## 4. Research methodology

In order to test the propositions, we used the data that was collected from a controlled experiment at a large public university in the mid-western part of the USA. Fifty-four <sup>fi</sup>ve-member groups were drawn from undergraduate business students in introductory information system classes. They used a group decision support system (GDSS) integrated with a web-based intranet application that allowed them to browse through the information relevant to the cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict task that they had to perform. The GDSS tool used in the experiment provided communication support (such as, simultaneous and anonymous inputs, group display, input feedback) and process structuring support (such as, agenda enforcement). The Intranet application that was integrated with the GDSS allowed the participants to gather, share, and evaluate information which are the basic elements of information processing support in GSS-based group work [53,71]. Information relevant to the decision attributes and choices was stored in the Intranet database. The data was available to the decision makers through a hypermedia interface. This allowed individual decision makers to examine the decision situation and rank order different alternatives on any speci<sup>fi</sup>c attribute based on lexicographic rules. Each study participant received a waiver for one assignment in a mandatory introductory information systems course. Participation in the experiment was voluntary. Based on the availability constraints provided by the students, participants were randomly assigned to one of the <sup>fi</sup>fty-four groups in the experiment. One member of each group served as a coordinator, fully participating in the decision making and performing other tasks as well. Face-toface communication among the group members was strongly discouraged. However, communication between the coordinator and other members of the group was allowed at speci<sup>fi</sup>c transition points from one activity to another. The experiments were conducted in a group decision room. Each experiment session could extend up to 2 h. Pilot sessions were held before conducting the actual experiment sessions to ensure that suf<sup>fi</sup>cient time was available to complete the decision process satisfactorily. The participants in the pilot sessions did not report any dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in working with the system.

## 4.1. Decision situation

The decision situation addressed the evaluation of alternative MBA programs. The participants developed a group level preference structure for attributes of MBA programs in order to produce a ranked list of programs. Undergraduate business students have a vested interest in gaining admission to MBA programs and were viewed as being potentially less biased than graduate students, who would have already gone though a similar but albeit constrained version of the decision. Accordingly, they were selected to play the role of decision makers in this experiment. The evaluation of MBA programs is not a

Table 1

trivial task. Each program is described on multiple attributes, including acceptance rates, percentage of admitted students receiving <sup>fi</sup>nancial aid, and so on. Each applicant has his or her own preferences among these attributes to evaluate individual programs. However, the preferences of others, especially those of the peers do in<sup>fl</sup>uence and shape each applicant's individual choice of attributes and schools. Consequently, arriving at a collective preference structure for attributes to evaluate MBA programs involves examining information that is diverse (i.e. multiple and varied attributes) and voluminous (i.e. large data sets to describe each MBA program). It presents a challenging task, more so given the differences in individual preference structure. This type of decision situation is categorized as a cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict task [44]. The task was intentionally chosen to include complexity and involved cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict, in that it would entail greater integrative complexity for the group. Unlike a decision situation where members select directly from the available alternatives, in this case the decision required that the members specify the attribute weights, so as to reduce any intrinsic bias for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c program. The ordering of programs would be an outcome of the selected attribute weights. The group could retain the combination of attribute weights and rank-ordered list of schools, and end the session. Alternatively, attribute weights could be revised to generate a new rank-ordered list of schools. Turban and Aronson [66] discuss a somewhat similar group task where the students were engaged in group work to identify the criteria for college selection.

The meeting consisted of the following activities. The participants completed a consent form. They were shown a video presentation about the experiment. Using a standard browser, each participant browsed the intranet for information about 24 MBA programs as well as attributes for school selection. Material for each page for an MBA program was obtained from the Peterson's Guide to Colleges. Each page contained information on 18 attributes, and depending upon the program, it included between 49 and 102 pieces of information. Each page on attributes for selection presented an ordered set of information for all schools on the selected attribute. In addition, the groups using the decision schema had access to aggregate level information gleaned from the work of prior groups engaged in similar decision situation. This included the weights assigned for individual attributes, averaged across all groups.

Using an allocation tool of the GDSS, each participant assigned a total of 100 points among the available attributes. Once all team members had allocated their points, the coordinator instructed the system to display group results. Each member could view their own allocation as well as the group average. The system also displayed the dispersion in weights submitted by individual group members. After viewing the dispersion and mean weights for each attribute, the member could change their allocation. This process of readjustment of weights would go on till group members agreed upon the group level weights that could be submitted to the system.

After agreeing upon a set of weights among the available attributes, the coordinator exported the average weights to a database and computed the rankings of the schools. Based on the attribute weights allocated by a group, the computer generated a rank-ordered list of schools following the principle of the simple additive weighting method [9,25,37]. The rank ordered list of schools was imported into the meeting management software from the database. Participants viewed the rankings of the schools emerging from their attribute ratings. Participants electronically voted using the GDSS to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the judgment policy expressed in terms of selected attributes and their weights was <sup>fi</sup>nal. If four or more group members voted in favor of the judgment policy, the group moved to end the experiment; otherwise, they reassessed weights till a consensus was reached. In order to prevent groups from thrashing around endlessly, a maximum of seven iterations was permitted. None of the groups ran into this constraint. It is important to acknowledge that in this case, voting represented not just a terminating step, but an opportunity for groups to evaluate the extent of consensus reached. In part, this re<sup>fl</sup>ects the notions advocated in the Delphi method [36].

Participants were not allowed to take the instruction sheet with them after the experiment, were appropriately debriefed, and were advised not to discuss the study with other potential participants. In an effort to keep the data collection process manageable, multiple groups used the facility at the same time, through the simple strategy of inviting only a subset of the workstations to a meeting. The proscription on face-to-face communication enabled parallel group processing.

## 4.2. Operationalization of constructs

The level of information processing, input information complexity, and perceived time pressure represent the major constructs examined in this study. In prior research on integrative complexity, the level of information processing has been operationalized as decision time, time spanned to integrate information, and the number of integrations performed within a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed period [5,28,55,62]. In electronic brainstorming the level of information was measured as ideas organized, categories organized, and ideas repeated [18]. We chose to operationalize the level of information processing as the decision time and <sup>fl</sup>ow of information.

Input complexity has been measured in terms of the number informational inputs [14,26–29,55,57,61], the number of dimensions in input information [26–29], and the number of repeated dimensions considered [26,27]. We chose to operationalize input information complexity in terms of the number of informational inputs, henceforth referred to as input information volume, and the number of dimensions of input information, referred to as information diversity. Since control and treatment groups had access to varying number of pages, the measure of information diversity was normalized to even out the differences. The operationalizations of the variables are presented in Table 1.

Perceived time pressure was measured using 5-point Likert-type scales, which were included in a questionnaire that the participants completed after the experiment. The indicator items of these scales are presented in Table 1, and their reliability and validity are addressed in the next section.

Data for the operationalizations of information processing and input information complexity was culled from the server logs. The logs included information on the requestor IP address, request timestamp, HTML action, and page requested. Using a map of workstations assigned to individual group sessions, we were able to identify all pages, and hence all bits of information, that any participant saw, as well as the time at which they received this information. Pages were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into categories of navigation, task, program, attribute, and schema. Navigation pages allowed the participant to move between content pages. Task pages cued the participants to enter and exit activities. Navigation and task pages contained little information for decision making, and were visited fairly evenly across individual participants within a group. Program pages described individual MBA programs and included a host of information describing the program on a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned set of attributes. Not all program pages were visited by every participant. Attribute pages contained a general description of the attribute and an ordered set of programs on that particular attribute. Once again, not all attribute pages were visited by every participant. Program and attribute page visits varied considerably within and across groups. Schema pages related to group memory on prior decisions involving the task, and included both numerical and graphical displays of prior allocations. This was available to the treatment groups only, and was used by almost all participants in these groups. When measuring the input information complexity, we used the total number of bits of information across all pages that they visited, since this represented the entire information set that they accessed. Each bit of information represented a data value coupled with its meaning in context. Given the large amounts of information accessed by some groups, it is expected that they would have experienced overload, and would not have processed all the information.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Reliability and validity

Reliability assessments were made for the self-reported construct, viz. perceived time pressure. Experts on group decision-making and attitude measurement conducted initial reviews of the measure to establish its face validity. Cronbach Alpha coef<sup>fi</sup>cients were then computed. Since the measurement scale used had not been tested and validated adequately for virtual teams and in view of the exploratory nature of this research, a cut-off value 0.70 was considered acceptable [49]. To examine convergent validity, factor analyses employing VARIMAX orthogonal rotation was carried out. Cronbach alpha was found to be 0.70. Both indicator items loaded on a single factor with factor loadings of 0.875, indicating both reliability and validity of the instrument.

## 5.2. Hypotheses tests

We conducted polynomial regressions to test our hypotheses. Since the relationship hypothesized was that of an inverted U-curve, a quadratic model was selected.

$$
z = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} x + \beta_ {2} x ^ {2} + \beta_ {3} y + \varepsilon\tag{1}
$$

The standard precautions associated with polynomial regression were adopted [47]. These included keeping the order of the model as low as possible, progressive introduction of polynomial terms, and proscriptions about extrapolating beyond the observed data range. SAS was used to develop the General Linear Models (GLM) for the regression analyses. With the exception of perceived time pressure, which employed the average score for all members in a group, all other measures were obtained at the group level. ## 6. Discussion and limitations

The study extends prior research on information processing and integrative complexity to GSS-based groups. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that level of information processing exhibits the inverted U-shaped relationship with the information complexity, even when the unit of analysis is a group of decision makers. This effect was observed for two measures of complexity — information volume and information diversity. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that information overload is encountered by groups in much the same way that it faced by individual decision makers. Group process gains and losses will certainly have an impact on the maximum processing capability, but the overload problem is encountered nonetheless. Use of communication and information retrieval technologies, often an integral part of group systems, will likely exacerbate the overload problem in that massive amounts of information are available at the click of a mouse, or could be instantly sent to individual member unsolicited. One way to limit this in a group setting is to adopt a structured agenda with an effective facilitator, which is meaningful for synchronous centralized processes. Even in these cases, the nature of the decision may entail looking through large volumes of information. In our experiments, groups accessed an average of 3710 bits of information in just under 42 min of processing. Clearly, this represents a massive amount of information, and while not all pieces of information may have been used, members would have to sift through the available information to select the relevant information. Time pressures only add to this sense of overload. The implication for group activity is that simplifying the group information gathering and processing becomes necessary for effective group decision making. This can be accomplished in a variety of ways. Reducing the number and complexity of tasks involved is an obvious option. Reducing the amount of information to be processed at any stage is another possibility. However, both of these entail changes to the group process that may curtail the group's ability to accomplish its objective. Employing an appropriate decision schema represents another possibility when the group process and task complexity cannot be altered.

The study examined the effect of using a decision schema to simplify the decision process, particularly in the context of information overload. In this case, the schema took the form of a set of weights associated with individual attributes used in historical decision making. Groups were under no pressure to use similar weights, but they did access the schema several times — up to 34 times for one group. The use of the schema would simplify some of the decision making, and should delay the onset of information overload. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated that the schema did simplify decision making, in that the treatment groups did not reach the information overload conditions and many of the treatment groups needed to access fewer pages to complete the task. While prior literature has shown that decision aids provide effective support in the decision making process, this study indicates that it has a positive effect in information processing at the group level, and not just at the individual level.

In terms of implications for practice, it is clear that information overload is experienced in the case of synchronous groups even at moderate levels of information complexity. Our study employed 24 schools with 18 attributes each. Many group decision situations involve more attributes and alternatives, putting groups at greater risk. The use of a simple decision schema improved the ability of the group to deal with additional information, providing increased

information processing ability. More involved decision schema could possibly provide additional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in information processing ability of groups. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that in some cases, groups ignored some of the information they accessed, as indicated by the timestamps on the requests. This strategy has been documented in the literature before for individual decision makers, and our study suggests that it applies at the group level too. It permits the group to deal with larger amounts of information and not be overwhelmed by it. However, it is effective only if the group retains relevant information and discards material that is less germane to the overall decision. Making the determination of relevance is not an easy task, and is clearly task and group dependent. In the context of our study, it can be argued prima facie that all the information is relevant, as all alternatives are described by an identical set of attributes. However, not all attributes are weighted equally in the decision context, and an argument can be made that an attribute that is weighted zero or close to zero is less relevant to the decision. Once the determination is made that the attribute has little impact on the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision, discarding this information has the net effect of increasing the group information processing capacity. The use of heuristics like elimination by aspect would also have the effect of discarding information, and would permit the group to address problems of greater complexity.

These results cannot be generalized to alternative group tasks and con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. If a group work on a simple task that involves less cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict and is decomposable, it is likely that the information overload situation may not be encountered. In a similar vein, it is doubtful that the overload problem will apply in the same manner for virtual and asynchronous groups, given that they have more off-line opportunities to process the information and are not constrained quite the same way as same-time same-place groups. Given the trend towards this form of group work, it would be appropriate to extend this study to asynchronous and virtual teams.

The study is subject to several limitations. The use of student subjects is frequently questioned in terms of whether they are effective surrogates in the decision task at hand. Since this research examined information processing, we believed that the use of students would not be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern. The choice of problem, i.e. evaluating MBA programs, we felt would be one that they could relate to. However, we were not able to ascertain if they were truly engaged in the task. An examination of the pages visited, duration of the group process, number of decision iterations, suggest that most groups were engaged in the task, though no de<sup>fi</sup>nitive claim can be made. Using students who were actually in the process of applying to graduate management programs would provide an added touch of realism, though it is not clear that their decision process would mesh with that employed in this study. While there was some time pressure felt by some groups, in that they came close to the allotted time for the experiment, a study employing real decision makers under actual time pressure would clearly be more insightful. Alternative ways of achieving consensus among participants represents another area for further research. We employed a simple voting scheme to measure consensus. Given that the participants may emphasize different attributes in the decision process, achieving consensus might sometimes prove to be contentious. Discouraging face-to-face communication reduces this possibility, but may lead to hardening of positions and subsequent thrashing before eventua adoption of the <sup>fi</sup>nal group decision. A modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Delphi technique may be more effective in this context. The choice of decision schema is also an area for further investigation. Our study employed the aggregate weights from several prior groups engaged in the same decision task. Providing the weights from individual groups separately, along with the overall measure of consensus, and the time it took to achieve consensus, would yield additional insights for the treatment groups. As to whether the additional information would be more effective in countering information overload or contribute to information overload is an open question. Finally, the measures for information processing employed in this study, though consistent with prior research, are not perfect. For example, the operationalization for information diversity assumed a linear relationship between indicator items and construct, though it is arguable that viewing twice the number of pages entails twice the level of information diversity. Other operationalizations are clearly possible, but in an effort to be consistent with prior research, a linear relationship was employed.

## 7. Conclusions

This research has examined the effect of information complexity on information processing in a group decision-making setting. It established that increasing information complexity alters the manner of information processing and has an impact on the group process and outcomes. In this research, we demonstrated that both input information complexity and perceived time pressure increase information processing and may cause overload. Multiple measures for information processing and information complexity were used to establish this. It also demonstrated that in some cases, the use of appropriate decision aids, in this case a schema that synthesized prior decisions, can alleviate the problem of overload. Further research is needed to assess the impact of information overload on group decision quality, and the effectiveness of alternative decision schema in offsetting this.

The interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study provide motivation for pursuing more in depth studies on information processing in collaboration technology supported group work. Replicating the laboratory experiment in the <sup>fi</sup>eld settings can enrich our understanding of the causes and effects of information overload in group work. Field studies can also help us identify the motivators that help group members to stretch their cognitive abilities and withstand the adverse effects of information overload. Future studies should also be conducted on the teams of experts who are engaged in critical group work, such as emergency management. Experts can sense important information, and can quickly discard irrelevant information. Working in a team of acknowledged peers will permit the experts to collaborate effectively and <sup>fi</sup>lter and organize information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. An examination of their coping mechanisms when dealing with information overload may be insightful for groups that deal with less time pressure.

Souren Paul is an Associate Professor of Information Systems at the College of Staten Island of the City University of New York. He holds Bachelor's and Master's degrees in Electronics and Telecommunications Engineering from Jadavpur University, India and a PhD in Management Information Systems from the University of Wisconsin Milwaukee. He has published research articles in Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Information & Management, Information Resources Management Journal, as well as in the refereed proceedings of various national and international conferences. His current research interests are in the areas of collaboration technology, organizational knowledge management systems, and virtual teams.
