## Abstract

Accounting regulatory changes that affect the information technology (IT) infrastructure of firms have prompted Chief Information Officers (CIOs) to take the lead in aligning IT changes with new reporting requirements. This study investigates the associations between the mandatory adoption of International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) and CIO compensation. We find a significant increase in CIO compensation in the post-IFRS period, which is higher in non-IT firms than in IT firms. Moreover, pay is at a premium for CIOs equipped with more educational background in business. We discuss the implications of our study and offer suggestions for future research.

Keywords: Chief Information Officer, Executive Compensation, Regulatory Reform, International Financial Reporting Standards

## 1. Introduction

Technology executives, namely Chief Information Officers (CIOs), face dynamic challenges in a constantly changing accounting environment, triggered by the significant impact of the prominent adoption of information technology (IT) on how companies conduct their business both operationally and strategically [19,26,27]. 1 The design of the information system (IS) contributes to the quality of financial reporting, which in turn affects the usefulness of accounting information for decision-making [35,51]. 2 Companies need to have ad hoc information to promptly respond to internal and external changes [58]. Changes in accounting regulations create challenges for IT departments, as they need to comprehend the new rules and effects of accounting regulatory changes on executive compensation for Chief Executive Officers (CEOs) and Chief Financial Officers (CFOs) [5]. To the best of our knowledge, existing studies on CIOs and executive compensation have not yet investigated how accounting regulatory changes may influence CIO compensation. This is a key issue, as $\mathrm { C I O s } ^ { \mathrm { \ ' } }$ decisions to plan and evaluate necessary IT changes in a timely manner may not only affect the costs of regulatory conversion but also influence the quality of IS [24,42,49]. To fill the gap in the literature, this study investigates the relationship between mandatory accounting reform, namely the enactment of the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), and CIO compensation practice.

The IFRS constitutes one of the most important accounting regulatory changes influencing the global accounting information environment. Within the trend toward increasing globalization in the capital market, the IFRS provides a uniform set of accounting standards adopted by different countries, aiming to greatly improve the international comparability and quality of financial reporting [4,8,75]. However, the IFRS requires greater information disclosure in financial reports to ensure reliability and credibility [3]. Under the IFRS framework, more professional judgments are likely to be made in companies’ accounting processes.3 Practitioners have raised the concern that IFRS adoption will affect the relevancy and reliability of a firm’s IS [59]. This challenges the CIO to plan and implement necessary IS modifications in order to mitigate regulatory risks that may arise from IT changes.

Investments in IT have proven to be an important vehicle to enhance value by mitigating the associated business risks, thus improving corporate governance and reducing regulatory compliance costs [13,23]. However, the evaluation of IT remains perennially challenging, given the lack of direct and objective financial measures of IT value [34,50,62,68]. This is because IT is often considered as a complementary resource to support other investments, which together indirectly contributes to a firm’s core capability and value [60]. Therefore, the valuation challenge of IT also increases the difficulty of evaluating the performance of the CIO [28,64].

The CIO is becoming an important board participant to help the board to take a holistic view of IT for making better corporate decisions [7,21,27,55,65]. The value of the CIO depends on whether he or she can benefit the firm through a better alignment of IT with business strategies [20,29,41]. The literature on IS suggests that a good relationship between CIOs and non-IT executives is critical for deriving value from IT [20,45]. The sensegiving capability of a CIO helps non-IT executives to appreciate the business value of IT and also what IT could do within their respective functional areas [67,68]. Overall, this suggests that the performance of CIOs is conditioned by their competence, as this affects whether CIOs can talk about IT using business

As IT becomes more complex and critical to business operations, one may expect CIOs to be compensated more for the substantive nature of roles, tasks, and responsibilities placed upon them, particularly when considering the value of scarce human capital attributes for the CIO position. Management literature provides a theoretical linkage that lends support to the association between executive pay and the information-processing demands that come with the job [37]. Anecdotal evidence also shows that CIO compensation has been ranked as high for those on the top management team and remains on the rise [10]. Such an upward trend in compensation practices is most likely driven by higher expectations, along with additional responsibilities assigned to the CIO.

Considering the higher level of information disclosure required under IFRS, conversion to this framework will have a significant impact on accounting/finance functions and on the IT function, which has been prevalently used to enable and support the financial reporting process of a business [3,59]. The conversion to IFRS that affects IT functions (e.g., upgrading or reconfiguring the associated transaction systems, reporting tools, and applications) depends on

CIOs’ knowledge and managerial skills, particularly the capability to interpret and translate IFRS requirements into needed IT changes [25,42,44]. Additionally, the theory of information processing suggests that the substantive nature of an executive’s job is associated with executive pay [37]. More responsibilities for CIOs arising from the needs of IT changes may also boost the information processing demands made on them, suggesting in turn an increase in CIO compensation. Moreover, the executive compensation literature indicates that more disclosure may impose greater scrutiny, leading to a higher risk of termination of executives [38]. Executives may thus seek a pay premium in the post-regulatory reform period to offset such higher risk exposures [38]. In sum, firms could compensate CIOs more because of the adoption of IFRS.

By using the data collected from eleven countries between 2002 and 2012, this study finds a significant increase in CIO compensation after the mandatory adoption of IFRS, particularly in the form of cash compensation. Additional analyses show that, on average, the total and cashbased CIO compensation increased from two years prior to IFRS adoption, suggesting that firms plan ahead for the upcoming mandatory reporting changes. In addition, the results show that the increase in CIO compensation is greater in non-IT firms than in IT firms, indicating that the adoption of IFRS could be less of a burden for CIOs in IT industries. This is mainly because employees in IT firms, on average, have more IT-related exposure and experience. Therefore, IT firms are more likely to adapt to the mandatory reporting reform more efficiently. Conversely, non-IT firms that switch from old to new accounting standards may require their CIOs to exert more effort in managing IT resources and strategies. Consequently, the transition costs, including the increased compensation for CIOs, turn out to be higher in non-IT firms. To shed light on the effect of CIOs’ business skills, the study further examines whether educational background in business has an effect on the relationship between IFRS adoption and CIO compensation. The findings show that CIOs with higher levels of educational background in business are compensated more in the post-IFRS adoption period, supporting the notion that firms value CIOs’ ability of using business language to leverage the value of IT in a changing accounting environment.

This study contributes to the CIO literature by examining the impact of IFRS adoption on CIO compensation. It also complements the extant literature on executive compensation by focusing on the CIO position, as well as the accounting literature, which addresses the costs and benefits of IFRS adoption on business practices. The findings of the study shed light on the technological impact of accounting changes by investigating CIO compensation practices. The investigation of industry characteristics and CIO competency has implications for understanding the evolution of CIO compensation practices in a changing accounting environment.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We provide a literature review and develop the hypotheses in Section II, describe the research methodology and the data in Section III, present and discuss the empirical results in Section IV, and draw conclusions in Section V.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

In recent years, one of the most influential changes in the area of financial reporting has been the global adoption of IFRS [14,75]. Aiming to enhance the cross-border comparability and quality of financial reporting, extensive research that weighs the benefits and costs of IFRS adoption has been conducted by many academics, regulators, and practitioners [4,8,11,33,56,75]. Furthermore, prior research has shown that switching to IFRS increases reporting quality, for example, by improving information comparability, increasing the understandability and prediction capabilities of firm value, providing timely information to accounting information users, reducing information asymmetry, and improving capital market efficiency [3,17,70].

However, compliance with IFRS requirements may impose additional costs on firms, as it requires more detailed disclosures [33]. As a result, companies are expected to increase their investments in the existing IT infrastructure and human capital to comply with the more changes to be made in a firm’s IS may include the collection of new data, modification or reconfiguration of existing systems, introduction of new systems, implementation of more personnel training, and employment of additional personnel and assistance [24,49,59]. Specifically, a shortage of IT talents equipped with accounting knowledge and the ability to interpret and translate accounting language into corresponding IT changes compounds the potential IFRS conversion problems [24].

Collectively, IFRS conversion is expected to be of high cost to firms because of the greater demands and spending in relation to implementation efforts, required knowledge of both accounting and IS, and needed system upgrades or modifications mandated by IFRS reporting standards [31,33,43,63]. Furthermore, anecdotal evidence provides supportive observations that IT is an important area where many companies experience the impact of IFRS [25]. Although CEOs and CFOs are held directly accountable for the output of financial reporting, CIOs are also expected to play an important role affected by such regulatory reforms, as the effectiveness of the IS is closely associated with the quality of financial reporting. Several studies have suggested that CIOs should prepare themselves for the foreseeable impacts of IFRS on IT practices [31,42]. Representing the highest level of decision-making for IT initiatives, the CIO is responsible for managing IT budget, overseeing IT infrastructure acquisition and maintenance, investing in IT human capital, and aligning IT strategies with the firm’s business goals [7,15,18,34,64,67].

Drawing upon the theory of human capital, executives are compensated more for their unique and valuable managerial skills [12]. Because the responsibility of CIOs is likely to indirectly affect financial reporting reliability and credibility through the quality improvements in IS, firms might reward CIOs’ efforts and contributions to enhance financial reporting quality. In addition, the theory of information processing suggests that executives are expected to be paid more for the higher level of information processing demands caused by the need to handle more complex managerial issues [37]. Considering that IFRS conversion may require some significant modifications to the existing IT infrastructure, which affect the outputs of the financial reporting systems, the level of a CIO’s effort is expected to increase in the transition and post-IFRS periods. Moreover, IFRS demands more disclosure efforts [48]. Executive compensation literature suggests that mandated disclosure reform, which increases the level of information transparency and monitoring, is expected to affect managers adversely because of the increased risk of termination and decreased information advantage and private benefits [38]. Accordingly, managers may seek a higher pay to offset the increased job responsibility and associated career risks because of the greater level of monitoring generated from/by more information disclosure [39]. Taken together, we hypothesize that the CIO pay increases in the post-IFRS adoption period.

H1. CIO compensation increases in the post-IFRS adoption period.

The level of information processing needs in today’s digitized business environment is closely associated with the degree of technological complexity of a firm, upon which the effect of IFRS adoption on CIO compensation may be contingent. Specifically, what is the impact on the CIO of an IT firm when there is a mandatory adoption of new accounting standards that affect the level of information collection and disclosure? For instance, accounting treatments in research and development (R&D) activities is one significant difference between IFRS and most domestic standards. As a critical investment activity for IT firms, R&D is commonly associated with technological advancements and innovation development [40]. Prior research indicates that the investment level of R&D is associated with executive compensation, according to the information processing theory, which links information processing demands and job complexity with pay level [69]. The International Accounting Standard (IAS) 38 regulates the reporting of intangible assets, including the reporting for R&D expenditures.4 Although IAS 38 generally prescribes the expenses for research, it requires the capitalization of certain development costs if the defined criteria are met. The accounting for the capitalization of certain R&D costs differs from many domestic accounting standards.

Because R&D is recognized as a key driver of innovation capability, IT firms often are expected to spend more resources on their reporting systems to monitor and evaluate the outcome of their R&D projects. It is likely that IT firms are better prepared and positioned to adopt the new IFRS requirements for R&D. As such, the costs of information collection incurred by IT firms for complying with IFRS reporting requirements are expected to be lower. Because the burden on the IS caused by IFRS adoption is expected to be lighter for IT firms, it may have less effect on CIO compensation.

H2. The impact of IFRS adoption on CIO compensation will be higher for non-IT firms.

Because IT is embedded in an organization’s business processes, the management competency of CIOs is a key driver of their performance in managing IT effectively [20]. CIO leadership, an important attribute documented in the CIO literature, is found to be associated with organizational performance through an effective combination of IT competencies with complementary non-IT resources [18]. Anecdotal evidence indicates that successful CIOs focus more on delivering the business value of IT by thoroughly understanding the firm’s business and its priorities [46,47]. To understand how business works, which performance metrics are valued by the management, and how the planning and implementation of IT affect those metrics, IT practitioners suggest that it is important for CIOs to gain and cultivate their business skills, for example, through education [72]. In addition, having sufficient business skills helps CIOs to enhance their interpersonal skills and build an effective relationship with non-IT executives in order to leverage IT value [18,61,68,72]. In other words, a CIO who understands business language, and can effectively communicate technical issues using a language that is familiar to non-IT executives, contributes to effective IT assimilation [2,67].

The use of a pay premium has been suggested in the human capital literature to compensate executives’ superior management competency [9,22]. Executives with scarce human capital attributes have stronger bargaining power for commanding a pay premium. When evaluating human capital, education is a common measure of one’s knowledge and skill base [9,36]. Education builds human capital because it enhances an individual’s learning and information processing ability and helps to impart knowledge needed for performing specific tasks [9,54]. The literature on human capital and signaling theory recognizes that education is crucial to determine the cognitive ability, which is associated with information processing capability and the ability to deal with uncertainty [9,22]. Skills acquired through higher education help an individual to perform higher value-added tasks more efficiently and more quickly [9]. People with higher intelligence were found to opt for and were selected for higher education [66,71]. The positive impact of educational levels on pay and work opportunities has been documented in prior research [52,73]. In the CIO literature, researchers indicate that educational background is a key factor that a firm considers when appointing a new CIO [6].

Taken together, to facilitate and stimulate the cross-functional communications relating to IT transition projects, which are triggered by external changes originating from the accounting regulations, firms may compensate CIOs with a higher level of educational background in business more, as they are expected to better synthesize IT through communications with non-IT executives. In the context of IFRS, which requires more accounting estimates and information disclosures that vary across businesses, it is important for a firm’s IT infrastructure to be able to produce good quality information for decision making. Having a higher degree of business suggests that the person has spent adequate time and effort to learn and obtain them in an indepth way. Accordingly, a firm can benefit more when its CIO has a deep understanding of knowledge from the business discipline in order to leverage IT more effectively when processing complex business activities under IFRS. That is, following changes in accounting regulations, a CIO’s background in business is important as it affects the CIO’s ability to comprehend the accounting impact on the business and IT, to work with non-IT departments, and to lead and implement successful IT changes. Non-IT executives, especially CEOs and CFOs who are held accountable for financial reporting quality, rely on CIOs to ensure that their firms’ systems have a smooth transition and meet the mandatory reporting requirements. ## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Research Model

To investigate the effects of mandatory adoption of IFRS on CIO compensation, we estimated the following model, while controlling other confounding factors that are expected to have an influence on CIO compensation.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C I O P A Y _ {i t} = & \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} I F R S _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} R E T _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} R O A _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} B T M _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} S I Z E _ {i t} + \beta_ {6} T E N U R E _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {7} E D U _ {i t} + \beta_ {8} C E O P A Y _ {i t} + \beta_ {9} C F O P A Y _ {i t} + \beta_ {1 0} \Delta I N D C I O P A Y _ {i t} + v _ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

To adjust for the skewness of executive compensation, prior literature suggests using logarithmic transformation [5,74]. Therefore, $C I O P A Y _ { i t }$ was measured as the natural logarithm of CIO compensation. To investigate the impact of mandatory IFRS adoption on CIO compensation practice, the following alternative measures were used: salary $( S A L A R Y _ { i t } )$ , bonus $( B O N U S _ { i t } )$ equity-based compensation $( E Q U I T Y _ { i t } )$ , cash compensation $\left( \mathrm { C A S H } _ { i t } \right)$ , and total compensation $( T O T A L _ { i t } ) . ^ { 5 }$ Cash compensation is the sum of salary and bonus, while total compensation is equal to the sum of salary, bonus, and equity-based compensation. Our variable of interest, $I F R S _ { i t } ,$ is a dummy variable, which is equal to 1 if firm i is in a country with mandatory IFRS adoption in year t and 0 otherwise. We also included the following control variables in the model: $R E T _ { i t }$ represents the annual buy-and-hold stock returns that measures firm stock performance, while return on assets $( R O A _ { i t } )$ measures firm accounting performance. The book-to-market ratio $( B T M _ { i t } )$ captures a firm’s growth opportunities. Additionally, information needs in larger firms are on average more complicated than those in smaller firms. We thus calculated the logarithm of firm market value at the beginning of year $t \ ( S I Z E _ { i t } )$ as a proxy for the complexity of a firm’s information processing needs. In addition to firm characteristics, executives are remunerated depending on their experience within the firm. A CIO who works longer in a firm is expected to be more familiar with the firm’s operations and thus is expected to have a better understanding of the firm’s IT needs. CIO seniority was measured using the number of years a CIO has worked for the firm i $( T E N U R E _ { i t } )$ , as a proxy for the $\mathrm { C I O ^ { \circ } s }$ entrenchment within the firm. We also controlled for CIO ability by measuring the educational level $( E D U _ { i t } )$ . CIO compensation is expected to be associated with the compensation of other executives, such as the CEO and CFO. We therefore controlled for CEO compensation $( C E O P A Y _ { i t } )$ and CFO compensation $( C F O P A Y _ { i t } )$ $C E O P A Y _ { i t }$ is the natural logarithm of total CEO compensation, while $C F O P A Y _ { i t }$ is the natural logarithm of total CFO compensation. Furthermore, the demand for skilled CIOs in the labor market might also drive up the level of CIO pay. To account for the industrial competition effect on CIO talent, we calculated the percentage change in the median value of industry-level CIO compensation in year t using the two-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code as industry classification (ΔINDCIOPAY<sub>it</sub>).

Prior research suggests that IT jobs are complex, thus requiring specialized knowledge [1]. The CIO’s role in IT firms could be quite different from that in non-IT firms. To investigate the effect of IFRS adoption on CIO compensation in IT industries, we created a dummy variable, TECH<sub>it</sub>, to identify IT firms on the basis of their SIC codes, and then formed the interaction term, $T E C H _ { i t } \ast I F R S _ { i t } ,$ , to examine whether the association between mandatory IFRS adoption and CIO pay may differ between IT firms and non-IT firms.6

Moreover, the educational background of executives is an indicator that firms often use to compensate for the value of human capital. In the context of the CIO’s role, it is expected that a CIO with a business educational background will help to stimulate a good working relationship with non-IT executives. To investigate whether educational background in business influences CIO compensation when the adoption of IFRS affects a firm’s IS, we measured a business education level variable, BUS\_LEVEL<sub>it</sub>, to identify the highest business degree a CIO has (1 for Bachelor’s degree, 2 for Master’s degree, and 3 for Ph.D. degree, and 0 otherwise). The interaction term, IFRS<sub>it</sub>\*BUS\_LEVEL<sub>it</sub>, was employed to examine whether a CIO with a higher business degree receives a higher compensation in the post-IFRS period, considering the CIO’s ability in interpreting the impact of IFRS requirements on data processing and disclosure.

## 3.2. Sample

We first identified CIOs from the Capital IQ database during the period between 2002 and 2012 on the basis of their executive titles, classified as CIO or closely related titles such as Chief

Technology Officer (CTO), as well as job descriptions. Second, we combined names and person identification of CIOs/CTOs with the Capital IQ Compensation Details database to retrieve the compensation data. Third, we searched the corresponding firms’ annual reports and the Mergent Online database to collect information about the CIOs’ educational background. We excluded firms operating in financial industries (SIC6000-6999) from the sample for two main reasons suggested in the literature: (1) firm characteristics of financial firms are fundamentally different from those of non-financial firms and (2) the timing of the mandatory IFRS adoption required for financial industries differs in some IFRS-adopting countries [5,57]. Fourth, we extracted firm financial data from the Compustat Global database. The final sample includes data from eleven countries and economic areas.7 Two countries, namely India and the United States (US), did not require the adoption of IFRS standards during the sample period.<sup>8</sup> The remaining nine countries and economic areas switched to IFRS during the sample period, and most of them adopted IFRS in 2005. All continuous variables were winsorized at 1% and 99% levels.

Table 1 summarizes the definitions of variables employed in the models. Table 2 provides the descriptive statistics of these variables. All compensation components and related control variables were converted into US dollars depending on the average annual exchange rate in respective country-years. In untabulated results, we performed a t-test to compare compensation components of CIOs, CEOs, and CFOs, as well as those of the top management team in the preand post-IFRS periods. We found similar trends in the change of compensation across CIOs, CEOs, and CFOs in the sample period, and a significant growth occurred in the post-IFRS periods. These findings are consistent with those of prior literature, which investigated CEO and CFO compensation change due to IFRS adoption [5]. This supports our expectation that executives are, on average, compensated more because of increased responsibility and associated risks resulting from the adoption of IFRS.

## 4. Results and discussions

## 4.1. Main empirical results

Table 3 presents the results for the main effects of mandatory IFRS adoption on aggregate compensation, and total cash compensation. We found a significant increase in CIO total compensation, as well as in compensation components such as salary, bonus, and total cash compensation, in the post-IFRS period. The results support our first hypothesis, H1. This is consistent with our expectation that CIO compensation increases after IFRS adoption, as more subjective accounting estimates and information disclosures are needed to comply with IFRS requirements. Increased CIO compensation reflects a higher complexity of enterprise systems in the post-IFRS period in order to meet the new reporting quality under IFRS. The coefficients on $R O A _ { i t }$ are negatively associated with CIO compensation in most cases. This can be explained by considering the nature of IT investment. Effective IT investment requires time and resources to plan and implement. Certain IT involves a constant investment effort to realize the value. In addition, investments in IT are often embedded with other investments. The value of IT may not be observed directly or in a timely manner, as the synergy between IT and other investments takes time to realize. Therefore, IT spending may adversely affect firm performance measured in a short time horizon. As such, firms with an emphasis on improving their operating performance will be more conservative in allocating resources to the IT function. Because the performance of a CIO depends on the value that the IT function could offer the firm, the indirect and lagged realization of IT value likely leads to a negative association between CIO compensation and contemporaneous firm performance. $B T M _ { i t }$ is significantly and negatively associated with total compensation, salary, and equity-based compensation. This suggests that firms with a higher growth opportunity may have a higher IT demand for retaining competitive advantages, which in turn drive CIO compensation. The positive coefficients on $S I Z E _ { i t }$ across models suggest that CIOs in larger firms are compensated more because of the higher level of information processing needs. $T E N U R E _ { i t }$ is significantly and positively associated with total compensation, salary, and equity-based compensation, supporting the notion that CIOs are remunerated depending on their tenure and experience. Similarly, the positive coefficients on the educational level of CIOs $( E D U _ { i t } )$ are consistent with the rationale that a firm pays more for valuable human capital characteristics. We also found that both CEO and CFO compensation are positively associated with CIO compensation in most models, suggesting an interdependent compensation structure among top executives.

## 4.2. Additional analyses

The convergence of accounting standards worldwide is truly a long journey. Although the mission of the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) to develop the IFRS in order to bring transparency, accountability, and efficiency to financial markets worldwide is widely accepted, countries may still have different attitudes or considerations that prohibit, allow, or mandate the adoption of IFRS at different time periods. If local firms anticipate that IFRS will be adopted in the near future in their home countries, they may jump on board to develop or modify their systems in advance. We thus investigated CIO compensation prior to IFRS adoption to Table 6 presents the results of this analysis using the components of CIO compensation from one to three years before IFRS adoption as the dependent variables. The findings are consistent with regard to total compensation, salary, and cash compensation. $I F R S _ { i t }$ is positively associated with these compensation components in both one and two years before the adoption of IFRS. Overall, these results support the notion that firms started preparing for IFRS by changing their systems with a leap of at least two years ahead of when the adoption was to become effective, which in turn had a positive impact on CIO compensation because of the increased workload.

## 4.3. Robustness checks

We applied multiple methods to check the robustness of our results and main conclusions.

First, considering the potential endogeneity problem, i.e., the probability that creating a CIO position may be different across companies and/or countries, we employed a “propensity score matched” (PSM) sample to rerun our tests. We estimated the propensity score for each firm-year observation based on firm size, return-on-assets ratio, book-to-market ratio, and two-digit SIC code. The test results based on the PSM sample are presented in Table 9, Panel A. Consistent with the main findings in Table 3, IFRS adoption is positively associated with $\mathrm { C I O s } ^ { \prime }$ compensation, salary, and cash compensation. These results support our expectation that the change in accounting standards increases the demand for and complexity of information, which in turn results in an increase in CIO compensation.

Second, we took time effect and macroeconomic factors into account to address whether the change over time in CIO compensation is a result of time effect, economic fluctuations, change in accounting standards, or other exogenous factors. Our sample period (2000-2012) covers many important economic events, such as the fast growth in IT firms between 2000 and 2005 and the global financial crisis that occurred between 2007 and 2008. The progress or timing of adopting IFRS also varies among countries. Hence, we employed a difference-in-difference (DID) analysis to clarify the effects of IFRS on CIO compensation. The year 2005 was a major moment for the global convergence of accounting standards because the member states of the European Union, as well as Australia, started to mandate the adoption of IFRS. Since then, firms and accounting regulators in the non-IFRS countries have also considered the potential IFRS impact on their reporting practices. For example, firms based in non-IFRS countries need to consider how to reconcile their business activities and/or transactions with their business partners operating in IFRS countries. Accordingly, we created an indicator, $P O S T _ { i t } ,$ to identify years after 2005 in the sample for the global trend of moving from domestic accounting standards to IFRS. Table 9, Panel B presents the results for our DID analysis. The coefficients on $P O S T _ { i t }$ are negative for all types of CIO compensation, except equity-based compensation, suggesting a negative time effect on CIO remuneration. This result could be attributed to the global financial crisis, which occurred soon after IFRS was widely adopted (2007-2008). However, the interaction term, $I F R S _ { i t } { ^ { * } P O S T _ { i t } } ,$ is positively associated with $\mathrm { C I O s } ^ { \prime }$ total compensation, salary, and cash compensation. In the post-IFRS period, even if the economic situation was bad, CIO compensation still increased as a result of the higher demand for information processing arising from the changing accounting environment. We further tested the significance of IFRS adoption by examining the coefficients on $I F R S _ { i t } { + } I F R S _ { i t } { * } P O S T _ { i t }$ . The results show strong support for our main argument that IFRS adoption is associated with higher CIO compensation.<sup>10</sup>

## 4.4. Limitations and future research

In this study, we conducted various data analyses to empirically examine the impact of IFRS adoption on CIO compensation. Nevertheless, the research has some limitations related to the availability of data. First, our examination of the association between IFRS adoption and CIO compensation is based on information retrieved from financial databases and hand-collected CIO profiles. Although this study covers a long-term period from 2002 to 2012, CIOs in some countries were not included in the boardroom, and CIO compensation was not significant as compared to other executives until recent years. Second, considering the nature of IT effects, the use of CIO compensation as a proxy for measuring CIO efforts and the level of information processing may not be a perfect measure. Third, the hand-collected CIOs’ business educational background data are limited by the availability of information voluntarily disclosed by firms. Limited by the data available on educational background, we can only measure the level and number of business degrees and employ them as proxies for the depth and breadth of business skills a CIO gained through schooling. Training through business education provides CIOs with the relevant skills to understand business matters and facilitates their communication with business executives. We believe that our education measures to some extent help to appreciate the value of $\mathrm { C I O s } ^ { \prime }$ business skills. Although fundamental accounting courses are generally required in business degree curricula, referring to $\mathrm { C I O s } '$ business educational background to examine whether they are equipped with sufficient business knowledge might still be a coarse representation of accounting knowledge, particularly in order to appreciate the influence of accounting regulatory changes. Fourth, besides educational background, work experience is an important factor that influences CIO compensation and is expected to affect $\mathrm { C I O ^ { \circ } s }$ skills and ability, particularly when reacting and preparing for changes promptly. On the basis of the available data, we used the number of years a CIO has worked in a firm as the CIO seniority proxy to account for knowledge and understanding of a firm’s operations and information needs. This measure captures the total tenure of a CIO as a firm’s employee, including the time before being promoted to the CIO position.

The use of complementary methods, such as surveys or field studies, that elicit the degree to which the position requires specific business skills and work experience, as well as the ability of the firm to attract the talent it needs, would be fruitful for a better understanding of the value of the CIO position. More detailed information about educational background (e.g., majors, types of degrees, or certificates) and experience (e.g., the length time a CIO has worked on the job or prior experience from working in a similar position in other firms) could be obtained through the aforementioned methods to provide more insights on the value of a CIO’s business skills. Another line of research would be to investigate whether the relationship between executives whose responsibilities are associated with the output of financial reporting, namely the CEO, CFO, and CIO, could mitigate the impact of accounting regulatory reform on CIOs’ jobs and compensation. In this study, we employed multiple econometric methods to control for confounding factors, such as time and industry effect, that have an influence on CIO compensation. With the advancement in technology and intensified industry competition, accounting standards are modified or updated constantly to different extents, and the priority of CIOs’ tasks changes accordingly. For example, CIOs may spend more time on managing risks associated with compliance and security or on proposing data analytics for exploring potential business opportunities. Researchers could also investigate changes in recent CIO compensation practices to explore further the evolution of business models by using new technologies and the significance of different levels of accounting regulatory changes that affect a firm’s IT needs in order to better appreciate the value of CIO position.

## 5. Conclusions

The study empirically examines the association between the mandatory adoption of IFRS and CIO compensation. Although research on executive compensation continues to proliferate in the literature, the ways in which accounting regulation change may influence the compensation of regulatory reform reflects an international move to use a common set of accounting standards aimed at improving financial information quality and international comparability. The benefits and economic consequences of adopting IFRS, such as improvement in comparability and/or the effects on market reaction, are well documented in prior literature. However, the effects of this regulatory reporting reform on stewardship or the costs of converting the related IS to comply with IFRS requirements in a technological manner have not yet been examined. The study fills this gap and echoes practitioners’ concerns about the expected IT costs associated with IFRS adoption. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical study to examine how the mandatory IFRS adoption affects CIO compensation practices.

By using an international dataset of CIO compensation covering eleven countries between adoption and CIO compensation. From further analyses of compensation components, it is notable that this association is more significant in the form of cash-based compensation, including salary and bonus. The results support the notion that executives are, on average, compensated more for their increased workloads because of adopting new accounting standards. Considering the level of IT investment that is associated with the level of information processing needs, we document that in the post-IFRS period, the increase in CIO compensation in IT firms is relatively lower than that in non-IT firms. With better IT investment ex ante, the transition costs of converting systems in accordance with IFRS requirements and the incremental compensation of and efforts spent by CIOs can be lower for IT firms. In addition, we offer evidence of an incremental effect of the levels of business education on CIO compensation in the post-IFRS period, lending support for compensating IT talent equipped with desirable business knowledge and skills to manage IT in a changing accounting environment.

Our additional analyses provide further insights regarding changes in CIO compensation practices because of the adoption of IFRS. First, we document that on average, cash-based CIO compensation begins to increase from two years before mandatory IFRS adoption, reflecting the expectation and preparation of firms and the change in CIOs’ efforts in the pre-IFRS period. Second, our industry analyses show that the increase in CIO compensation for capital-intensive industries, such as telecommunication, is greater with respect to information-intensive industries, including pharmaceuticals and computers. Third, CIOs received extensive business training through education, i.e., the receipt of multiple business degrees, which are also remunerated more in the post-IFRS period.

Given the fact that IT is embedded in all processes in a firm and plays a crucial role in documenting and reporting economic activities of business, the empirical investigation of IFRS adoption on CIO compensation should be of interest to academia and practitioners working in the accounting and IS fields. This research contributes to the managerial question at the intersection of the literature on IS, executive compensation, and financial reporting quality. The findings lend support to practitioners’ notion that changes in accounting regulations affect not only accounting professions but also IT professions. The study helps to explain the increase in CIO compensation following the mandatory adoption of IFRS and sheds light on the role of a CIO in a changing information environment by showing that CIO compensation increases because of more responsibilities and higher information processing needs under the IFRS framework. In the face of the changing accounting information environment, firms rely on and value CIOs more when they are equipped with sufficient business knowledge and skills beyond the IT discipline. The findings suggest that considering the influence of accounting regulatory changes on IT, CIOs business educational background and the level of information processing needs related to industrial characteristics are the two important factors that should be considered when we evaluate the performance of CIOs and their compensation. This empirical study provides insightful implications for practitioners and regulators to appreciate how changes in accounting rules may affect a firm’s IT function and the CIO’s responsibility by examining the CIO compensation practice.

## Biography

Fang-Chun Liu is currently an Assistant professor of Accounting at School of Business, Stevens Institute of Technology. She received her Ph.D. in Business Administration from Temple University. Professor Liu’s research interests include: strategic cost and performance management, corporate governance, human capital, executive compensation, financial reporting quality, and the financial implications of information technology investment. Professor Liu has presented her research actively at the most prestigious international conferences in Accounting and Information Systems areas. Her work has published in International Journal of Operations and Production Management, Information &Management, and the Proceedings of International Conference on Information Systems.

Hsiao-Tang Hsu is an Assistant Professor of Accounting at College of Business, Texas A&M University-Corpus Christi. He received his Ph.D. in Accounting from Temple University and his LL.M. specialized in Taxation from London School of Economics. His dissertation at Temple University received the 2015 AAA Outstanding International Accounting Section Dissertation Award. Before his doctoral studies, he worked as an international tax consultant at PricewaterhouseCoopers. His current research interests include international accounting, financial accounting, executive compensation, corporate governance, and risk management. His publications have appeared in prestigious journals including The Accounting Review.

David C. Yen is currently a Professor of MIS at School of Economic and Business, SUNY-Oneonta. Professor Yen is active in research and has published books and articles which have appeared in ACM Transaction of MIS, Decision Support Systems, Information & Management, IEEE Computer Society: IT Professionals, Information Sciences, Communications of the ACM, Government Information Quarterly, Information Society, Omega, International Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce, and Communications of AIS among others. Professor Yen’s research interests include data communications, electronic/mobile commerce, database, and systems analysis and design.
