# Transactive memory systems as a collective <sup>fi</sup>lter for mitigating information overload in digitally enabled organizational groups

## a b s t r a c t

With the increasing processing power and plummeting costs of information and communication technologies, the ability of employees to ubiquitously access and disseminate information grows. However, emerging research shows that individuals are struggling to process information as fast as it arrives. The problem of information overload is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant one for contemporary knowledge-intensive organizations because it can adversely affect productivity, decision making, and employee morale. To combat this problem, organizations often invest in technical solutions such as business intelligence software or semantic technologies. While such technical approaches can certainly aid in reducing information overload, less attention has been directed at understanding how collective behavior, and in particular transactive memory systems, might enhance the ability of organizations to cope with information overload. In this study, we ask whether (and, if so, how do) transactive memory systems act as a collective <sup>fi</sup>lter to enable organizational groups to mitigate the potential for information overload. We used social network analysis and interview evidence from the R&D departments of two high-technology <sup>fi</sup>rms in the life science industry and found that individuals spontaneously organized without any centralized control to create a collective <sup>fi</sup>lter. For example, we found that one set of individuals specialized in <sup>fi</sup>ltering external information into the group while another set specialized in <sup>fi</sup>ltering that information for internal use. We conclude by discussing the theoretical and practical implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 1. Introduction

Consider the volume of data produced by humankind from the dawn of civilization to the year 2003. According to former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, we are now producing the equivalent of this volume of data every two days (TechCrunch.com, 2010). At the heart of this information explosion is technology. With the increasing power and plummeting costs of information and communication technologies (ICT), our ability to ubiquitously access and disseminate information continues to grow. This ability has facilitated many important business and societal innovations such as the ability to spot business trends, prevent epidemics, and combat crime. Yet, for knowledge-intensive organizations, there are also numerous downsides associated with information abundance, the most notorious being the problem of information overload.

Conventional organizational theory views of information overload suggest that information overload arises from having more relevant information than one can assimilate (Butcher, 1998) or being burdened with a large supply of unsolicited information, only part of which is relevant (Butcher, 1998; Edmunds & Morris, 2000). Contemporary organizations may view information overload as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem because it can adversely affect productivity (Gonzalez & Mark, 2004; Waddington, 1997), decision making (Chewing & Harrell, 1990; O'Reilly, 1980; Pennington & Tuttle, 2007; Rutkowski & Saunders, 2010), employee morale (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011; Waddington, 1997), and even physical health (Lewis, 1996). For example, reports have highlighted the problem of “inbox overload” (i.e., receiving vast numbers of emails), which may cause users to spend hours each day reading and responding to their emails (Davenport, 2005). Furthermore, a study conducted at Intel reports that each knowledge worker loses approximately eight hours per week due to information overload, which equates to a cost of \$1 billion per year (Basex, 2009).

We should note that information overload is not a new phenomenon. The earliest examples can be traced back to the library of Alexandria where archeological evidence shows that there was more information in one place than one human being could assimilate over a lifetime (Shirky, 2008). What is new, however, is the almost-zero cost involved in publishing, disseminating, and accessing information through Internet-enabled platforms such as email, blogs, and social networking sites. No longer restricted by centuries-old production and distribution costs, nearly anyone can be a publisher today (Hemp, 2009). Instead users should be concerned about the accuracy and reliability of that information because the emergence of Internet technologies has somewhat subverted the role of traditional publishing houses. These ‘gatekeepers’ (as they are often termed) not only <sup>fi</sup>lter information by deciding what will be published, but they typically also ensure the quality and veracity of what they publish.

To avoid and reduce the potential effects of information overload, today's organizations have generally resorted to technical solutions such as intelligent agents. semantic technologies. business analytics. or recursive machine-learning to <sup>fi</sup>lter the relevant information from the non-relevant. While such technical approaches are certainly helpful, organizations still rely on individuals to determine what information is relevant. However, little research exists that attempts to understand social solutions for information overload or, in particular, how collective behavior in the form of transactive memory systems (TMS) could deal with the abundance of information. In effect, it is unknown whether such an approach can help to alleviate information overload. Thus, this paper addresses the research question; Whether (and, if so, how do) transactive memory systems (TMS) act as a collective filter to enable organizational groups to mitigate the potential for information overload?

To address our research question, we examine the R&D divisions of two high-technology multinational corporations in the medical devices industry. Preliminary interviews with management and employees at both <sup>fi</sup>rms con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that they were struggling to process the exponentially increasing amount of technological information available online. Findings from our subsequent social network analysis and interviews contribute to the existing literature on information overload and collective behavior in three ways. First, this study explains the social networking dynamics at play in high volume information-processing environments. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of coping with the problem of information overload through social solutions in combination with technical solutions. Second, by exploring the information overload problem from a collective behavior perspective, this study contributes to the psychology literature, which has heretofore primarily considered the information overload phenomenon from an individual perspective. Third, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of transactive memory in explaining how members of a collective rely on each other not just to store memories but also to self-organize so as to <sup>fi</sup>lter out irrelevant information from entering and being absorbed by the collective.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present our theoretical background. In Section 3, we present our research setting and methods. In Section 4, we present our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and, in Section 5, we discuss the implications of those <sup>fi</sup>ndings

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Information overload

Organizational theory has long considered how functional groups can be best organized to process external information. For example, seminal studies have shown that performance is determined by the type and amount of external information with which a team engages (Ancona & Caldwell, 1992), the tradeoff between exploring and exploiting external information (Lazer & Friedman, 2007), and whether specialized information brokers exist in project teams (Allen, 1977; Tushman, 1977; Tushman & Katz, 1980). While a variety of studies in this vein consider how organizational structure, roles, and actions can be understood as solutions to the problems of monitoring, gathering, processing, distributing and using information, there has not been a systemic effort to identify how such collectives organize to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally avoid information overload.

Futurologist Alvin Tof<sup>fl</sup>er <sup>fi</sup>rst coined the term “information overload” in the 1970s when he predicted that the continuing swell of information would eventually cause problems. Since then, researchers found that information overload is a multidisciplinary phenomenon that affects individuals, groups, organizations, and society (Pennington & Tuttle, 2007), and the <sup>fi</sup>eld of psychology has most extensively studied the problem. A central concept linking many information overload studies has been cognitive load theory (see Sweller, 1988, 1989), which suggests that individuals can only process about six or seven chunks of information at one time, and that going beyond this pushes them into the realm of overload—also known as Miller's “magical number seven plus or minus two” (Miller, 1956).

While there are multiple overlapping terms and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions evident in the literature — cognitive overload, sensory overload, communication overload, and information fatigue syndrome (Eppler & Mengis, 2004), organizational researchers have de<sup>fi</sup>ned information overload as a state induced when the amount of input to a system exceeds its processing capacity (Schultze & Vandenbosch, 1998) or when information processing capabilities and the information loads encountered are mismatched (O'Reilly, 1980). Some researchers have extended information overload's de<sup>fi</sup>nition by incorporating time; that is, they have suggested that overload becomes evident when the time needed to process information exceeds that allocated for the activity (Hiltz & Turoff, 1985). Yet, it has also been argued that overload is not an absolute concept but is instead a temporal phenomenon experienced in differing ways by different people. Those who have considered information overload through a sensemaking lens maintain that overload is more an issue of perception and interpretation rather than a direct result of receiving too much information (Sutcliffe & Weick, 2008). Supporting this view, lab experiments conducted by San Miguel (1976) <sup>fi</sup>nd that more cognitively complex people are able to process more information before overload occurs. This indicates that information load and processing capabilities are inextricably linked and may even be inseparable (Schultze & Vandenbosch, 1998).

ICT's rapid advance in organizations in recent years has greatly increased the potential for information overload (Schultze & Vandenbosch, 1998). For example, Internet technologies have increased the ease and speed with which employees can access vast amounts of information from outside the organization, which has resulted in a more complex information processing environment (Whelan et al., 2013). Individuals may choose to disseminate this external information to others and/or store it for current or future use in databases and on hard drives for potential reference, which often leads to what has been referred to as “information junkyards” (Sparrow, 2011). Further, internal and external electronic communication enables people to transmit their ideas and questions by overusing “blanket email” (i.e., needlessly copying people onto messages irrelevant to them) and distributing unfocused information on mailing lists (Bawden, Holtham, & Courtney, 1999). This may lead to conversational overload, which occurs when an individual receives too many messages to be able to respond adequately (Whittaker, Terveen, Hill, & Cherny, 1998), or information entropy (Hiltz & Turoff, 1985), which occurs when an individual cannot easily recognize important incoming messages because they are not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently organized (Jones, Ravid, & Rafaeli, 2004).

## 2.2. Mitigating information overload

Research has found that, when faced with a mass of information that exceeds their cognitive capacity to process it, individuals tend to adopt selective processing or <sup>fi</sup>ltration as a coping mechanism (Eppler & Mengis, 2004; Jones et al., 2004; Swain & Haka, 2000). Filtration involves concentrating attention on the most relevant information while purposefully ignoring or <sup>fi</sup>ltering out the less-relevant information (Ben Zur & Brenzitz, 1981; Payne, Bettman, & Johnson, 1988). While the process is considered bene<sup>fi</sup>cial when individuals need to process large amounts of information in a limited amount of time, the danger with <sup>fi</sup>ltration is that individuals can miss potentially useful information.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the few empirical studies that have investigated whether ICT increases information overload are consistent with the above focus on <sup>fi</sup>ltration: they suggest that information overload may be better explained by “<sup>fi</sup>lter failure” rather than by any sudden surge of digital information. For example, Farhoomand and Drury (2002) report that managers face excessive volumes of information because their IT/IS departments cannot design systems that <sup>fi</sup>lter information to only deliver the speci<sup>fi</sup>c information that the managers need to make business decisions. Instead, system designers tend to enter all the information in the system and let the managers themselves decide what is useful. Additionally, certain studies include users who, after the introduction of email (Hiltz & Turoff, 1985), electronic distribution lists (Finholt & Sproull, 1990), and groupware systems (Schultze & Vandenbosch, 1998), reported no ill effects due to the increased information load caused by these systems because they effectively learned to use the tools to <sup>fi</sup>lter out the discussions in which they wanted to participate.

Many researchers have also looked to intelligent information management systems (despite potentially causing information overload) as a solution to the individual <sup>fi</sup>ltering problem (see Eppler & Mengis, 2004, for a review). For example, organizations with larger group settings tend to implement electronic directories with information about who knows what (e.g., searchable expertise pro<sup>fi</sup>les or, for a more speci<sup>fi</sup>c example, the online yellow pages). Empirical evidence suggests, however, that such systems are rarely used (Eppler, 2003; Sim, Crowder, & Wills, 2006) or are ineffective in correctly identifying expertise (Leonardi & Treem, 2012). Instead. people tend to rely on their personal contact networks to find the information they need (Cross, Borgatti, & Parker, 2002; Teigland & Wasko, 2003, 2009; Zimmer, Henry, & Butler, 2007).

## 2.3. Information overload and collective behavior

One observation from our review is that the vast majority of studies, be they focused on root causes, contextual factors, or coping mechanisms for information overload, concentrate on the individual level when analyzing information overload. However, in recent vears, cognitive science has experienced a resurgence of interest in collective behavior, and some cognitive science researchers have thus questioned the implicit assumption that the individual is the critical unit of cognition. A growing number of researchers are shifting their level of analysis from the individual to that of emergent group-level behavior that results from the interaction of multiple agents and their environment (i.e., collective behavior) (Goldstone & Gureckis, 2009). One primary area of their investigation is the concept of the “extended mind”, which proposes that systems that contain but are larger than an individual human are capable of cognition (Clark, 2003; Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Wilson, 2004). Based on this, several researchers have posited that groups of people (in which individuals have denser links with each other than with those outside the group) working together in an integrated, interactive manner can be interpreted as information processing systems (Goldstone & Gureckis, 2009; Hutchins, 1995; Theiner, 2008).

Using laboratory experiments and formal computational and mathematical modeling, researchers have found that groups of individuals tend to spontaneously organize themselves into an effective problem-solving structure without any or even despite any centralized control (Bettencourt, 2009; Goldstone & Gureckis, 2009). While individuals make their own decisions on how to personally handle information <sup>fl</sup>ow, they make these decisions based on the actions of others. Thus, decisions at the individual level lead to the emergence of a group structure that is more than the unstructured aggregation of individual cognition. Instead, it is the outcome of a division of cognitive labor among the individuals (Goldstone & Gureckis, 2009). Some studies argue that this division of cognitive labor is due to explicit organizational decisions by the involved individuals; however, other studies posit that this structured outcome results from the interactions among individuals without their expressed intent (Theiner &

O'Connor, 2010). Still, as mentioned above, the majority of these studies have been conducted in laboratories or through formal modeling and, as such, we have a limited understanding of collective behavior in real-life settings.

Evidence exists to suggest that the collective behavior lens can help in understanding how to avoid information overload. Researchers have reported evidence of people distributing high information loads among their organizational colleagues (Mackay, 2000; Schultze & Vandenbosch, 1998). That is, they lessen their own burden by spreading out the information to colleagues. However, beyond this initial research, we know little about how individuals in organizations make their information processing decisions in light of the actions of others. In this paper, we use the term “collective <sup>fi</sup>ltration” to describe the collective mechanisms that process high information loads.

## 2.4. Transactive memory systems

One in<sup>fl</sup>uential theory that focuses on collective behavior is transactive memory systems (TMS) (Theiner & O'Connor, 2010; Wegner, 1987). Researchers view TMS as the specialized division of cognitive labor with respect to the encoding, storage, retrieval, and communication of information from different domains that often develops in close relationships (Hollingshead, 1998; Lewis & Herndon, 2011; Wegner, 1987). In essence, the theory suggests that people divide the labor of remembering certain types of shared information. When one person needs information in another's area of expertise, they can simply ask for it rather than spend time and energy searching for and learning it on one's own.

Creating and maintaining transactive memory systems follows three processes 1) information allocation, 2) retrieval coordination, and 3) directory updating (Wegner, 1987). Information allocation, the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, is the process by which new information that enters a group is allocated to the people who the group perceives to be experts on the topic. In two studies, Wegner (1987, 1995) found that this expertise differentiation emerged from the group itself or from management who de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles and allocated responsibilities to individual group members. Retrieval coordination, the second stage, is the process by which group members who want to retrieve some information will <sup>fi</sup>rst assess their own “feeling of knowing” on the topic and—if necessary—will consequently evaluate other group members who may possess this information or know who might possess it (Wegner, 1987, 1995). Retrieval coordination begins when the person who holds the information internally is not the one asked to retrieve it. For instance, Tom asks his friend, Bill, for information that Bill has no idea about, but Tom thinks that Bill might know someone who does. Bill then asks his friend, Helen, who may know. If Helen can produce the information and pass it along to Tom, then information retrieval comes to a successful conclusion. The information may then need to be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed in some way in order to make it more useful. Directory updating, the third stage, is the process by which people update their own personal directory of who knows what in a group. This essentially involves group members creating meta-memory directories of the information held by others. These meta-memories usually include information about the subject and location of the knowledge and may also include perceptions about the credibility of others' expertise on each subject. Information seekers require a measure of the retainer's legitimacy and reliability when retrieving knowledge.

In small groups where everyone knows each other's expertise, the three stages described above are usually a straightforward process. Previous research in non-organizational settings suggests that North Americans maintain an average of about 20 signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships at any given time (Walker, Wasserman, & Wellman, 1994) and that this size limit is dictated by the “psychological preconditions for transactive memory” that have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “knowing someone well enough to know what they know and knowing them well enough so that you can trust them to know things in their specialty” (Gladwell, 2000, p. 190). Thus, as group membership grows beyond 20 members, creating and maintaining transactive memory systems become more complex due to cognitive dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in maintaining awareness of so many people's expertise.

Because ICT and virtual teams have become increasingly prevalent in organizations, IS researchers have recently begun to apply TMS as an enhancer of knowledge transfer within and between work groups (Oshri, Van Fenema, & Kotlarsky, 2008). Several studies have found that transactive memory plays an important role in improving the performance of virtual and co-located teams who use ICT to share knowledge (Choi, Lee, & Yoo, 2010; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Yoo & Kanawattanacha, 2001). More relevantly, researchers have also extended the original TMS theory by demonstrating that knowledge domains are distributed across both human and electric repositories (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th, Sawyer, & Neale, 2003; Nevo & Wand, 2005; Oshri et al., 2008). We suggest that TMS is an appropriate lens with which to investigate the mechanics of collective <sup>fi</sup>ltration. We acknowledge that there are similarities between the two concepts as they both consider the collective processing of information. However, researchers have not yet applied TMS beyond an initial focus on knowledge sharing in teams, thus we know little about how TMS may enhance a group's ability to act as a collective <sup>fi</sup>lter to mitigate information overload. This leads us to our research question: Whether (and, if so, how do) transactive memory systems (TMS) act as a collective filter to enable organizational groups to mitigate the potential for information overload?

## 3. Methodology

To address our research question, we conducted case studies of the R&D divisions of two high-technology multinational corporations in the medical devices industry. We believe case studies are the most appropriate research instrument for two main reasons. First, case methods are particularly advantageous when the research objective is building and extending theory (Walsham, 2006). In addressing the research question, this study contributes to TMS theory by applying it in a new set of circumstances; that is, how such systems act as a collective <sup>fi</sup>lter to enable an organizational group to mitigate information overload. Second, case studies are appropriate when there is a need to focus on contemporary events in their natural settings (Benbasat, Goldstein, & Mead, 1987; Yin, 1994), which our case requires because most of what we know about coping strategies for information overload stem from psychology studies that employ controlled experiments.

Taking on board the recent argument that extant IS research generally fails to specify what is meant by the term “information” (Lee, 2010), we conceptualized this term by focusing on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of information: that is, technological information. In order to remain competitive, R&D functions need to keep abreast of the constant stream of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technological developments in and beyond their industry. This state-of-the-art information, which may be obtained from internal or external sources and be in a codi<sup>fi</sup>ed or non-codi<sup>fi</sup>ed form, is what we conceptualize as technological information. An example of technological information would be a medical device engineer learning of a new technique for bonding plastics from reading an industry online newsletter and using this information to advance his or her own work practices. Furthermore, while previous research in innovation contexts has de<sup>fi</sup>ned “relevant” information as information about new technologies, customer needs, market trends, and market developments (Wallin & von Krogh, 2010), we focused on technologically relevant information and not on information related to customers and markets. This speci<sup>fi</sup>c focus on a particular type of information enabled us to more clearly detail the dynamics of information <sup>fi</sup>ltering. In Section 3.1, we describe our case study sites and then elucidate the different methods used to gather data relevant to our investigation.

## 3.1. Case study settings

One of the traditional criticisms leveled at the case study approach has been the issue of replicability (i.e., that another researcher would not be able to replicate the findings). Researchers can largely reduce a case study's vulnerability to replication by carefully selecting case study sites (Yin, 1994). For this study, we gathered data from two medical device <sup>fi</sup>rms operating in Ireland: MedSci and StentTech.<sup>2</sup> We carefully considered the decision to study two cases and these two speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rms. First, we selected two cases because this would enable comparisons that clarify whether an emergent <sup>fi</sup>nding is simply idiosyncratic to a single case or replicated across cases (Yin, 1994). Second, each site must be selected so that it either: a) predicts similar results (a literal replication) or b) produces contrasting results but for predictable reasons (a theoretical replication) (Yin, 1994). We selected the cases of MedSci and StentTech for literal replication; that is, to identify common patterns in how the dynamics of collective behavior assist employees in avoiding information overload. Third, our cases had to meet certain screening criteria before they could be considered eligible as a potential source of data. We needed to study environments where collectives were exposed to high information processing loads. We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally selected the R&D function for our study because of the intensive information processing requirements needed to keep up-to-date with rapidly emerging scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technological developments (Allen, 1977; Chesbrough, 2003). We also ensured that information <sup>fl</sup>ows within each site relied heavily on ICT. This enabled us to explore the role of ICT in potentially being a cause of, and a solution to information overload. Each R&D division had to have more than 40 R&D group members (twice the 20 SNA number) as groupings of such size require a collective cognition to function effectively (Lewis & Herndon, 2011). Additionally, the management at both <sup>fi</sup>rms was receptive to the study, and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, in preliminary interviews, that overload from technological information abundance was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern in their R&D divisions.

MedSci is an Irish-owned multinational that employs approximately 400 people worldwide and has an annual turnover of €37 million. MedSci designs and develops technologies and products that assist medical device manufacturers in improving outcomes for patients. Its R&D division (referred to in the rest of the paper as Med R&D) primarily provides design and development expertise for other medical device companies that wish to outsource their device design. As such, Med R&D needs to continuously demonstrate that it is at the cutting edge of medical device technology in order to win client contracts. Med R&D employs 42 engineers (most with design and mechanical engineering backgrounds) who are organized into three project teams. Each project team will redesign an initial client concept and bring it through to the stage where it can be manufactured for commercialization and market release.

StentTech is the Irish subsidiary of a U.S. multinational that employs approximately 2500 people in Ireland. The company has advanced the practice of minimal-invasive medicine by providing a broad and deep portfolio of innovative products, technologies, and services across a wide range of medical specialties. To keep StentTech at the forefront of the medical device industry, its R&D operations continuously scan the external environment in order to identify opportunities, address new disease areas, and develop new technologies that can reduce risk, trauma, cost, procedure time, and the need for aftercare. We gathered data from the <sup>fi</sup>rm's Irish R&D unit (referred to as Stent R&D in the rest of the paper). Stent R&D employs 76 R&D professionals (most with medical engineering backgrounds). The group includes the design owners of certain StentTech products, and it is the responsibility of Stent R&D to advance those designs. Stent R&D is organized on a functional basis with four specialist subgroups. Members of each subgroup are specialists in a particular medical technology.

## 3.2. Methods

Our research methods incorporated two major phases. The <sup>fi</sup>rst phase involved analyzing the <sup>fl</sup>ow of technological information into and around each R&D group using, social network analysis (SNA) techniques. SNA is a systematic approach and set of techniques used to study the relationships and <sup>fl</sup>ows of resources between people, teams, departments, and even organizations (Cross & Parker, 2004; Krackhardt & Hanse, 1993; Scott, 2000). With SNA, social relationships are viewed as nodes and ties that can be illustrated visually and mathematically. Building on the TMS literature and previous relevant social network studies, this phase identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the people who played a key role in acquiring information from outside the organization (i.e., “technology scouts”) (Allen, 1977) and disseminating information within each group (i.e., “central connectors”) (Cross & Parker, 2004). To collect the data, we asked all members at both sites to complete a short online questionnaire on their information acquisition and dissemination activities. We achieved at least a 90% response rate in both groups—a level more than satisfactory to conduct SNA (Cross & Prusak, 2002).

To measure external information acquisition activities, we asked respondents to indicate how often they used three different information sources to keep up-to-date with technological developments: 1) personal contacts. 2) Internet sources, and 3) academic publications. An earlier pilot study by one of the authors indicated that these three information sources were the most frequently used by R&D professionals when acquiring technological information from outside the company (Whelan, Ahonen, & Donnellan, 2008). To measure internal communications, we asked, “Please identify which work colleagues you discuss technological issues with at least once a week.” Previous research reports that the choice of once-a-week frequency represents a fairly heavy degree of consistent communication (Allen,

1977) that people are able to remember quite accurately (Freeman, Romney, & Freeman, 1987). We used the SNA software package UCINET v 6.0 (Borgatti, Everett, & Freeman, 2002) to analyze the information network. To increase validity, we included only reciprocated interactions between network members in the analysis. We followed best practice in this regard (Harada, 2003; Krackhardt & Hanse, 1993) to ensure that network members who reported higher-than-actual interactions did not distort the analysis. Indeed, our initial analysis did reveal a small number of individuals who claimed that they were connected to almost everyone; however, few of their colleagues reciprocated these relationships. When two parties agree that frequent interactions take place, one has more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the results.

The second phase involved gathering data through semi-structured interviews across both research sites. In total, we selected and interviewed 21 individuals who were a mix of technology scouts, central connectors, and recipients as revealed through the network analysis in phase 1. In line with the seminal research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Allen, 1977), we operationalized technology scouts as those individuals in the top 10% of the external communication distribution (i.e., the most connected outside the company), central connectors as those in the top 10% of the internal communication distribution (i.e., the most connected within the R&D group) (Cross & Parker, 2004), and recipients as those outside the top 10% in either internal or external communication.

We conducted semi-structured interviews to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the SNA <sup>fi</sup>ndings and to explore in-depth collective <sup>fi</sup>ltration activities in the groups. We also used the interviews to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that those identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the scouts and connectors of the group actually performed these roles. We conducted all interviews face-to-face. and they ranged in length from 30 to 75 min. We generally followed the outline of the interview guide given in Appendix A. Myers and Newman (2007) argue that the qualitative interview has rarely been executed correctly in IS research. In response, they offer their “dramaturgical model” to accentuate the correct procedures the interviewer should follow when the aim is to conduct high-quality interviews. We adopted these procedures for this study. For example, to avoid the problem of “elite bias” (i.e., situations when a researcher may end up interviewing people only of a certain status), we selected employees from all three categories (i.e., scouts, connectors, recipients), and we took care to ensure that the interviewee sample represented all levels of the formal group hierarchy. Myers & Newman further advocate the importance of being a prepared and motivated interviewer. Thus, prior to the main study, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author conducted pilot interviews with eight individuals at a university research lab. The pilot interviews provided opportunities to understand the effectiveness of the questions included in the interview guide. Likewise, to ensure that the interviewer was always fresh enough to engage with the interviewee, a maximum of two interviews were scheduled per day. We analyzed interview data using the NVivo software package and followed established inductive qualitative methods: coding, data categorization, and pattern identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman. 1984: Yin. 1994). We tightly coupled the coding scheme developed for this study to the objectives (i.e., to examine how TMS acts as a collective <sup>fi</sup>lter to enable an organizational group to mitigate information overload). We used the three main constructs of TMS theory to develop the overarching tree node structure: information allocation, retrieval coordination, and directory updating. We then broke down these three master codes into smaller sub-codes of interest. We developed the initial sub-codes from the literature and the contact summary sheets that were made subsequent to each interview. For example, many interviewees explained that a small number of their colleagues actually contribute to the information overload problem by constantly emailing them with irrelevant information on new technologies. Thus, “overload contributors” became a sub-code of the “directory updating” master code. When interviewees discussed such activities, we transferred those speci<sup>fi</sup>c paragraphs to the overload contributor's sub-code. We revised the coding process numerous times because some common sub-codes needed to be broken down further and other unwarranted sub-codes needed to be removed. We grouped the summaries from this round of coding into a smaller number of overarching themes or constructs. For example, the “overload contributors” sub-code merged into the theme “assessing credible interna sources”. We developed the propositions presented in Section 5 from this analysis.

## 4. Findings

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present our initial SNA results and discuss our <sup>fi</sup>ndings organized in three subsections that correspond to the stages of transactive memory systems: 1) information allocation, 2) retrieval coordination, and 3) directory updating. The quotations that we present in this section are those that we consider to best represent our data.

As mentioned in Section 3, we <sup>fi</sup>rst analyzed the social network data at each research site to determine each individual's number of internal and external connections and position in the R&D network. The overarching network structures for both sites were broadly similar and we present the network sociograms for Med R&D (Fig. 1) and Stent R&D (Fig. 2) below. In these <sup>fi</sup>gures, each numbered node corresponds to an individual member of the R&D group, and the lines represent the <sup>fl</sup>ow of technological information between them. The more internally connected nodes tend to gravitate towards the center of the network while those nodes with fewer internal connections tend to be found on the periphery. Our analysis revealed that individuals varied greatly in their number of internal and external connections. At one extreme are those individuals who tend to dominate information <sup>fl</sup>ows either by being well connected outside the company or by keeping in touch with many people internally. At the other extreme are the isolates who have zero reciprocated interactions with other colleagues.

Of interest from the ANOVA is that scouts used the Internet to keep abreast of emerging technological developments much more than connectors (p b 0.05 at Med R&D; p b 0.10 at Stent R&D) and recipients $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 \right.$ at both <sup>fi</sup>rms). ## 4.1. Information allocation

To explore the <sup>fi</sup>rst TMS stage of information allocation and its relevance for avoiding information overload, we focused on searching for and <sup>fi</sup>ltering relevant external technological information into the two R&D groups. While all group members perform this activity to some extent, our interviews con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that each <sup>fi</sup>rm's technology scouts (i.e., nodes 5, 9, 15, 29 in Fig. 1, and nodes 15, 35, 43, 51, 52, 55, 57, 62 in Fig. 2) acted primarily as their <sup>fi</sup>rm's antennae; that is, they scanned the outside world for emerging scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technological information and developments relevant to the work of their R&D colleagues. The technology scouts whom we interviewed all largely agreed that the Internet was the primary medium through which this external information was accessed (e.g., online forums, RSS feeds, industry blogs, search engine enquiries), a <sup>fi</sup>nding that the survey data con<sup>fi</sup>rmed. While access to vast amounts of technological information is incredibly easy in the online world, only a fraction of this is relevant. The following quotation is from one of Stent R&D's technology scouts, who explains how he uses the Internet to keep abreast of external developments. However, he reports that identifying relevant information on the Internet is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult:

What I need to know about is what is new in our field. whether it's new stent devices, or medical devices in general, new drugs, new coating types, new test methods and things like that. So, my window to all of that is through the Internet. OK, sometimes it's information overload and it can be an ordeal…but you need to be able to say, “I want to see this and this and this — but I don't need to know the rest of it”. Finding the gems of information on the Internet is certainly time-consuming… you can do it but you do need to have time and patience.

Our research revealed that management had not formally appointed any of the individuals who performed the technology-scout role to this task. Rather, most of the technology scouts explained that they performed this role largely because they had a genuine interest in keeping themselves and their colleagues abreast of the latest technology developments. Moreover, those categorized as connectors and recipients acknowledged in the interviews that certain colleagues possessed not only the interest but also the analytical skills to sort through this abundance of information, and they were content for technology scouts to take responsibility for that role.

Developing their particular area of specializations is also an emergent process. Technology scouts tended to possess PhDs in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge domain and selected their specialization based on this narrow area of expertise. In some cases, there was competition to be the technology scout in a certain area, and this led to one scout having to <sup>fi</sup>nd an alternative area to establish his expertise. This dynamic is evident in the following quotation from a technology scout in Med R&D, who explains how this form of collective behavior has evolved over time:

I would probably be one of the people here who has a good sense of what the new developments in our industry are. Actually, let me qualify that. When I started working here three years ago, I tried to keep up-to-date with everything…plastics, bonding, cements, new diseases…the lot. That's all available now with a simple Google search. But it's a huge volume, and for me at least, it's impossible to engage with all of it. So over time you learn that [Derek] knows far more about disposable plastics or that [Ann] is the expert in biomedical cement. They are the guys who <sup>fi</sup>gure out what is really new in those areas. I eventually created a little niche for myself with catheter design but that didn't happen overnight. If someone wants to know what the really interesting things happening in catheter design are, I can tell them, “Here is what you need to know, the rest you can forget about.”

## 4.2. Retrieval coordination

Turning to the second stage, retrieval coordination, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that those who <sup>fi</sup>lter and retrieve relevant information from the external environment (i.e., technology scouts) are generally not the ones to then use the information internally. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that, while these technology scouts are strongly connected outside the company and specialize in <sup>fi</sup>ltering relevant external information into the R&D group, they tend to have relatively few connections internally. Instead, they rely on central connectors to distribute the relevant <sup>fi</sup>ltered information they have gathered to others in the group who are best equipped to exploit that information. As Fig. 1 illustrates, each technology scout was linked to a central connector in Med R&D (i.e., nodes 6, 7, 25, 37), a pattern that was largely repeated in Stent R&D. To con<sup>fi</sup>rm that connectors acted as a bridge between the scouts and the recipients, we calculated “betweenness” measures in each R&D group using UCINET. Betweenness refers to the extent to which a person acts as a “broker" between disconnected others in the network. Of the 12 individuals we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as connectors, 10 of these returned the highest betweenness scores, i.e., being a broker between scouts and recipients. The remaining two connectors ranked 13th and 15th in the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed betweenness calculations. This metric supports the view that external information passes through the central connectors before it is distributed for internal use.

Furthermore, these central connectors do not just simply redirect information to all internal colleagues working in a relevant knowledge domain. Rather, they play a central role in further <sup>fi</sup>ltering this valuable information by selecting speci<sup>fi</sup>c individuals as recipients so as to ensure its usefulness to others and to avoid overloading the recipients in the network One scout in Stent R&D explains the importance of this conduit between the scout and connector in aiding the avoidance of information overload:

Friday afternoons can be slack time here usually…people slowing down for the weekend or whatever. That's when I get some time to really go through the listservs I'm signed up to. 90 odd percent is rubbish and doesn't concern us, but occasionally I'd come across something that catches my attention. If it's worth pursuing, I'll direct it to [Paul]. He knows the lay of the land in here better than me. If something has potential, he will know the right people to call on board……It could be because he has interviewed nearly everyone in here for their job [and knows their expertise]…I just don't have that institutional knowledge…..So rather than dumping all the latest developments on <sup>fl</sup>exible wires onto my team, myself and [Paul] can work out what we need to consider.

Moreover, central connectors serve as “translators”, and, as such, they often translate information gathered from an external setting into the local language and setting before disseminating the information to others. The following example of the central connector Conor (node 6 in Fig. 1) is representative of how most central connectors operate within the two groups. If information on an emerging technology is passed to him, Conor uses his extensive knowledge of the internal network to target other R&D colleagues who possess the expertise and interest to potentially exploit that information. This understanding of “who knows what” is the result of his eight-year tenure with Med R&D, in which he has worked closely on projects with almost all other group members. Along with this, he has always been keen to learn about what his colleagues are doing, a fact veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by other interviewees. Because much of the technological information Conor receives is in digital form, he usually uses email to alert targeted individuals of any new developments relevant to them. However, external information of this type often needs to be translated into a form relevant to recipients if they are to consider it. As a result, Conor targets speci<sup>fi</sup>c people as opposed to sending out blanket emails, and carefully constructs his emails so that information relating to new developments outside the company gains the attention of the targeted recipients. An example of this internal <sup>fi</sup>ltering process is provided in Conor's following statement:

You have to realize that most people here are overloaded with the number of emails they receive. If someone sends out a blanket email, it's probably going to be deleted straight away. If I become aware of a new technology we should be interested in, I'll probably know the person who is best placed to examine it. I would probably send them an email <sup>fi</sup>rst, giving my reasons why I think they should be interested in this information. If they are interested, they can come back to me and we'll have a chat about it over a coffee.

As Conor alludes to in his statement, the retrieval co-ordination process occurs when the email recipient seeks out the sender for a discussion on how the group can utilize the new information. In essence, the central connector <sup>fi</sup>lters the information before discussing it further with the domain expert.

Similar to the information allocation process, we found that retrieval coordination emerged over time. None of the central connectors was formally appointed by management; rather, they emerged over time due to personal interests and their development of the skills and knowledge necessary for internal <sup>fi</sup>ltering. The emergence of these central connectors in both R&D groups enabled the avoidance of information overload at the group level because each connector had his/her own specialization area, used his/her own processing skills to <sup>fi</sup>lter the external relevant information to the appropriate people internally, and ensured that the information is in a form that could be easily integrated by the recipients.

## 4.3. Directory updating

The third stage, directory updating, is the process by which people maintain their personal directory of who knows what in the network. Our investigation revealed that this process has important implications for avoiding information overload because individuals used their own directory to help <sup>fi</sup>lter relevant technological information coming from others in the network. As Section 4.2 notes, central connectors in both groups gained a reputation for ensuring that relevant external information reaches the appropriate person, and group members gave careful consideration to the communications from these connectors. In addition to the assessment of their email communications, this reputation was gained informally through “water-cooler conversations” or positive assessments from work colleagues. Connectors also had a tendency to follow-up with recipients to learn if the information they provided was bene<sup>fi</sup>cial, a procedure that further enhanced their reputation.

Not only do individuals update their directories regarding who the most knowledgeable people are in the network, they also update their directories regarding those individuals who contribute to the information overload problem. In both groups, we found evidence of the existence of certain individuals who have earned such a reputation. These individuals tended to frequently use email to “blast” out non-relevant information to the rest of the group. One interviewee from Stent R&D labeled these individuals as “email jockeys”, and explained this in the following way:

…rather than taking ten minutes out to walk over and discuss that new information with someone, these guys constantly FYI [For Your Information] emails around to everyone. That's not really transferring knowledge. These ‘email jockeys’ are useless…nobody ever reads the emails they send around anyway.

Interviewees in both R&D groups indicated that these “email jockeys” do this in an attempt to bolster their own position by intimating that they are more clued into technological developments outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm than others. However, unlike the central connectors who carefully construct and selectively target their emails, these email jockeys do not translate the new information into understandable and relevant terms nor screen out the relevant recipients; thus, people generally do not accept the message. Due to the negative connotations associated with this role, we did not ask interviewees to identify these individuals by name and thus cannot pinpoint them on our sociograms.

To reduce the likelihood of information overload, we found that individuals in both organizations <sup>fi</sup>ltered their emails into two categories: 1) messages they should consider and 2) messages they can simply delete, which is a process similar to how participants in electronic distribution lists avoid unwanted messages (Finholt & Sproull, 1990). The reputation of the information sender plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in determining in which category to place an incoming message. Some network members have gained a reputation for frequently broadcasting online content that has little relevance to the recipient. This is viewed as contributing to the problem of information overload, and the sender's credibility is not viewed positively. As a result, recipients update their personalized directory to essentially <sup>fi</sup>lter out all future technological messages from such individuals. Most of the interviewees reported that they rarely ever read these emails and that these messages are simply deleted, and they even believe that individuals who broadcast emails are aware that no one will read them. The interviewees also explained that network members who translated online information into terms relevant to their recipients and who <sup>fi</sup>ltered this information to the appropriate individuals attained high credibility among their peers. Their communications were usually given full consideration and they were frequently sought out for further exchanges. Moreover, interviewees explained that a person's reputation for distributing irrelevant information was reinforced through the informal conversations that take place throughout the working day, and that this reputation spread quickly throughout the collective. One interviewee described how, on joining Stent R&D, she learned of most people's information distribution credibility over lunchtime discussions, and then used what she learned to inform her own decisions about what information to accept or ignore.

In addition, as management can also implement more formalized initiatives to promote awareness of what others know, we investigated the approaches at the two R&D groups, and here we noted a divergence between the two R&D groups. Med R&D had almost no formal approaches to aid directory updating. As one interviewee explained: “…it all happens through the grapevine. It's sink or swim in here.” Yet, interviewees did not believe this to be a problem. With only 42 members in the group, connectors were very prominent, and the informal approach worked quite well. In contrast, Stent R&D with its much larger employee group used a number of formal activities to promote directory updating that ranged from an enterprise-wide knowledge-management system (KMS) to weekly seminars by group members.

The use of this KMS presents another interesting insight into information overload. To promote knowledge transfer. group members were required to write a detailed technical report documenting the knowledge they used, the procedures they followed, and the resulting outcomes of any project on which they worked. This report was then posted on the searchable KMS. Many of Stent R&D's engineers explained that the KMS was useful in updating their knowledge of who knows what but not in the way management intended. When these engineers found a report on the KMS that was potentially useful for them, very rarely would they actually read the full report. Instead, they were more likely to directly approach the person who created the report and seek a <sup>fi</sup>ltered version of what they precisely needed to know.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The proli<sup>fi</sup>c ability of ICT to access and process information can often result in workers drowning in information, which leads to under-performance, poor decision making, and declining morale. Information overload is a significant problem for the IS community, vet both researchers and practitioners have tended to focus on developing technical solutions to <sup>fi</sup>lter information. This paper takes an alternative approach and considers collective <sup>fi</sup>ltration in combination with technical solutions. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have allowed us to clarify the complex processes that are used in digitally enabled organizational groups to <sup>fi</sup>lter relevant information from the non-relevant information. Using the theoretical lenses of collective behavior and transactive memory systems, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the abundance of information easily accessible through the Internet and other ICTs necessitates that one set of individuals specialize in <sup>fi</sup>ltering external information into the network and that another set specializes in <sup>fi</sup>ltering that information for internal use. Each individual in the network then has his/ her own directory that is used to <sup>fi</sup>lter information from others and that is constantly updated.

The formation of a collective system to avoid information overload is an emergent process through which individuals adopt and grow into specialized information <sup>fi</sup>ltering roles based on their interests and analytical and social skills. Not only does this self-organization at the group level provide a buffer to the vast amount of external information on the Internet, but it also provides a <sup>fi</sup>lter ensuring that only the relevant external information is accessed and that valuable pieces of external information are not missed.

In his seminal 1988 Harvard Business Review article, Peter Drucker rationalized that, due to ICT advances, future organizations will require information specialists to coordinate activities to ensure that organizations do not become swamped in the data they produce (Drucker, 1988). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from these two case studies would suggest that Drucker's vision of the future has become a reality.

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

Here we present the theoretical implications from this study in the form of analytical generalizations (Lee & Baskerville, 2003; Yin, 1994). This approach involves previously developed theory being tested and extended by applying its propositions to a variety of situations. The theory of TMS has generally been used to explain shared memory and how people come to rely on other people and systems as external memory devices. We demonstrate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of TMS in explaining how members of a collective rely on each other not just to store memories but also to self-organize so as to <sup>fi</sup>lter out irrelevant information from entering and being absorbed by the collective in order to avoid information overload. These generalizations are not meant to be treated as exact predictions but can be used to generate testable hypotheses.

Analytical Generalization 1. In knowledge-intensive organizational groups, one type of information specialist (e.g., scouts) emerges to <sup>fi</sup>lter Internet sourced information into the network.

The transactive memory concept explains that certain group members are allocated the task of being the expertise holders in particular knowledge domains (Hollingshead, 1998; Moreland, 1999; Wegner, 1987, 1995). We apply this aspect of the theory to environments characterized by a high requirement for information processing by showing that these information specialists (technology scouts in our study) can also act as a buffer by providing the valuable function of <sup>fi</sup>ltering external information into the organization. This external <sup>fi</sup>ltering function is necessary because so much of the information on new technologies is sourced online, and the accuracy and reliability of this information need to be carefully examined. While all members of each R&D group can easily access information through the Internet, our interview <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the majority of individuals are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed to allocate this task to a small number of specialists who possess the expertise and the personal interest to <sup>fi</sup>lter the relevant from the non-relevant information.

Analytical Generalization 2. In knowledge-intensive organizational groups, one type of information specialist (e.g., connectors) emerges to translate and direct Internet sourced information to other members best suited to exploit that information.

The study of social networks enjoys a long and rich tradition, particularly in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of sociology and anthropology, and more recently in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of management science. This literature has discussed extensively the role of those few individuals who act as the hub of the network, regardless of whether they termed ‘opinion leaders’ (Lazarsfeld, Berelson, & Gaudet, 1948), ‘stars’ (Allen, 1977), ‘centrals’ (Weimann, 1982), or, as in the case of this study, ‘connectors’ (Cross & Parker, 2004). Transactive memory systems somewhat view these individuals as human versions of the yellow pages. Our study suggests that this type of information specialist also acts as a second filter and that they play an integral role in avoiding and dealing with information overload. Not only do they direct information to the relevant information seeker, they also validate the reliability of the information they are sending. Both R&D groups we studied relied heavily on email as a communication system, and almost all the interviewees stated that they often felt overloaded with the amount of messages they received. A particular problem relates to the perceived abuse of email. Many of the emails received by group members were in no way relevant to them. To ensure that their colleagues are kept up-to-date with emerging technologies, connectors carefully construct their emails so as to avoid overloading the recipient. Emails of this type tend to be limited to a few sentences that explain why the information contained is relevant to the recipient. If the information is of interest to the recipient, they then return to the connector and have a discussion about how that information can be exploited

Analytical Generalization 3. In knowledge-intensive organizational groups individuals update their directories of credible information sources based on a) whether the content sent to them by other group members is relevant to their needs or not and b) the prevailing view of the source's credibility throughout the group.

Interviewees at both Med R&D and Stent R&D explained that they receive far more emails than they could possibly fully consider. Consistent with previous transactive-memory studies (Oshri et al., 2008; Wegner, 1987; Yoo & Kanawattanacha, 2001), we found that group members learned “who knows what” through repeated actions and communications over time with their colleagues. However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide the added insight that individuals continuously updated their personalized directory with information about which colleagues were and were not credible information sources by assessing the content of their email communications and, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, whether the sender had the ability to translate the content in the email into terms relevant to the recipient's expertise and interests. This particular <sup>fi</sup>nding is analogous with previous studies of email (Sussman & Siegal, 2003) and online forums (Zhang & Watts, 2008), where the decision to adopt online information is correlated with argument quality. This process also has implications for how the collective organizes to avoid information overload. Workers continuously compare their assessment of technological information distributed by others. These informal discussions reinforce across the collective which colleagues' information should be considered and which colleagues information should be ignored.

In addition to these analytical generalizations, our work contributes to the study of information overload through the application of a social network perspective and a focus on collective behavior. Most IS studies of this phenomenon tend to focus on the technical causes and solutions to information overload (O'Reilly, 1980; Rutkowski & Saunders, 2010; Wolf, Pintner, & Beck, 2011). However, our work suggests that one cause of information overload in organizations may be the lack of an effective collective <sup>fi</sup>lter that is enhanced by technical solutions. An organization's ability to successfully manage information overload may then be related to the emergence of specialized <sup>fi</sup>ltering roles, the abilities of its members, and a pattern of coordinating relationships between these members, all of which ICT supports.

Our study contributes to TMS research because it applies social network analysis to information overload and thus allows for the examination of TMS in the context of nested social interactions (Lewis & Herndon, 2011). Our analysis illuminates how a TMS functions by examining the different roles that speci<sup>fi</sup>c group members in two organizations played and how these different members interacted with the extended memory in their ICT repositories.

This present study also contributes to the research from psychology by examining information overload coping strategies at the group level. Our understanding of the cause and responses to information overload has been generated from an almost exclusive focus on the individual as the unit of analysis (Eppler & Mengis 2004; Jones et al., 2004; Pennington & Tuttle, 2007). Our study's results suggest that individuals, when they face a high information processing load, rely on their network of connections to each adopt specialized behaviors in order to reduce the processing burden on any one individual. Thus, the coping strategies an individual adopts may not be solely for his/her own bene<sup>fi</sup>t (as the extant psychology literature assumes) but rather may be a part of a wider distributed response that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the collective.

Finally, our research also contributes to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of cognitive science and provides tentative support for the collective behavior concept of the “extended mind”. To date, much of this research has been conducted in laboratory settings or formally modeled, but our research conducted in real-life intensive information processing environments reveals that, while individuals make their own personal decisions on how to handle information <sup>fl</sup>ow, they make these decisions based on their knowledge of others. Thus, these individual-level decisions lead to the spontaneous organization of a group structure that represents a division of cognitive labor that results from interactions among individuals without their express intent and without any centralized control.

## 5.2. Practical implications

This study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that information overload is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem in contemporary information intensive organizations. It also highlights the importance of information literacy skills (i.e., the ability to analyze, evaluate, and handle information). Our study suggests that Internet technologies are a vital tool for accessing emerging technological developments and that certain people exist (scouts and connectors) who possess the interest and ability to locate and evaluate this information. In earlier work, we detail the analytical and social skills that enable scouts and connectors to <sup>fi</sup>lter external information into the internal network (see Whelan et al., 2011). This pro<sup>fi</sup>le will enable practitioners to recruit and appoint the right people to these positions. In summary, scouts tend to have a deep and narrow knowledge base of a particular technology space, strong analytical skills, and low-to-medium organizational tenure. In contrast, connectors have a wide-ranging knowledge base that helps them to determine whether new technological developments are relevant to the organization. They have a broad network of contacts in their organizations along with a strong understanding of each contact's real expertise. It takes time to develop such a network, which explains why connectors tend to possess high organizational tenure.

An effective transactive memory system does not leave the responsibility for information to chance (Wegner, 1987). Thus, practitioners should take steps to ensure that the individuals who possess information literacy interest and/or skills are not only able to gain the relevant network positions to allow them to most effectively <sup>fi</sup>lter information but are also are rewarded for their <sup>fi</sup>ltering activities. However, practitioners need to be careful of the degree to which they manage the development of the human <sup>fi</sup>lter at the group level through formal means. As previous research shows (e.g., Krackhardt & Hanse, 1993; von Hippel & Schrader, 1996; Wenger, McDermott, & Snyder, 2002), network roles and relationships at the individual and group levels cannot be mandated; instead, managers should focus on facilitating conditions.

Additionally, practitioners should implement communication etiquette policies/guidelines to help reduce the burden of information overload. For example, we found that many of the emails disseminated internally were in no way relevant to the recipients. A guideline/policy could be implemented whereby people would only be copied on emails if the information contained were directly relevant to them. An interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding in both groups was the existence of individuals who constantly send FYI emails to their R&D colleagues. Rather than enhancing information <sup>fl</sup>ows, the actions of these individuals were seen as a hindrance by their colleagues. The motives of these individuals are self-promotion, similar to those individuals who knowingly enter over-in<sup>fl</sup>ated expertise data on knowledge management systems (Leonardi & Treem, 2012). The FYI phenomenon has the potential to become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem if group members become overloaded with irrelevant messages and end up not considering the important messages.

## 5.3. Limitations and areas for future research

This study is subject to a number of limitations that future studies should aim to address. This study sampled only two cases and focused on information overload experienced by development-focused medical device engineers located in Ireland that were, for the most part, Irish. As Yin (1994) explains, this allows us to make analytical generalizations to previously developed theory, but it would be a mistake to attempt to make generalizations to a wider population. For the purposes of generalizability, future research studies should sample a broader range of cases with differing types of information (e.g., marketing, problem-solving, advice) in a variety of industries, organizational, and cultural settings.

Additionally, the structure of each social network was determined by employee responses to only one survey question, albeit reciprocally answered and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in interviews. While widely used, we acknowledge that this approach may not produce a network structure that fully re<sup>fl</sup>ects reality. Thus, future research should consider analyzing social networks using automated approaches to gather a more comprehensive set of communications data. For example, the freely available NodeXL software constructs network patterns using email traffic

Finally, we gathered no performance data as part of this study. Such data would elucidate the effectiveness of each network structure and the individual positions in it. For example, our network diagrams revealed the existence of a small number of isolated individuals. A future study could examine whether such individuals suffer more or less from information overload and the performance implications of this.

In conclusion, information overload is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem for contemporary organizations. ICT is at the root of the problem, but, if we better understand how these technologies can enable the collective to self-organize, it can also be part of the solution.
