# Cost escalation in information technology outsourcing: A moderated mediation study

Bouchaïb Bahli <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Suzanne Rivard <sup>b,1</sup>

Keywords: Information technology outsourcing Transaction cost economic Cost escalation

## a b s t r a c t

Although the issue of cost escalation has often been mentioned in the literature on information technology outsourcing (ITO), the mechanisms by which cost escalation occurs in the ex-post stage of contracting has received little attention. Evidence suggests that cost escalation is common in ITO engagements. Drawing on transaction cost economics (TCE), this study examines the determinants of cost escalation in ITO. We propose and test a research model by positing that transaction attributes or characteristics (relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, bargaining power and, measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties) do not affect cost escalation directly, but rather through the mediation of the holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties. Furthermore, we examine how the multi-sourcing strategy and clan mechanisms, as a soft contracting mode, moderate this mediation. Data from a survey of 132 ITO decisions were analyzed. The results provide good support for the main hypotheses of the study and yield interesting insights about the determinants of cost escalation in ITO arrangements. Both research and practical implications of the results are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The growth of information technology outsourcing (ITO) market is evident from recent industry statistics. DataMonitor estimated the ITO market to be worth about \$320 billion in 2015 [22]. ITO emerged as an important tool for enabling organizations to improve the quality of services, lower the cost of IT and, obtain access to scarce resources [54]. Notwithstanding these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, unsuccessful ITO experiences in which service providers failed to deliver the expected savings are widely reported [33,35,42,43,45,57,58,64]. Approximately a third of companies studied by Lacity et al. [42] had canceled their ITO contracts and several of 25 world-class organizations recently surveyed by Deloitte Consulting reported many unforeseen problems related to ITO that created additional service costs and generated friction with their service provider, ultimately resulting into higher total costs than anticipated a priori [58]. The problems were important enough that 25% of the IT services outsourced by these <sup>fi</sup>rms were brought back in-house [62].

Recently, some large ITO failures have received wide attention. For example, JP Morgan Chase and Co. ended its outsourcing relationship with IBM Corporation after two years of what was projected to be a seven-year, \$5-billion arrangement. JP Morgan Chase and Co. returned all IT functions back in house [62]. According to transaction cost economics (TCE) perspective [66], a major reason of these failures is that costs escalate in the ex-post stage of the ITO contract. Indeed, cost escalation is a key concern when organizations outsource their IT services [24] and clients are often extremely dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the outcomes of their ITO deals [63]. As Hirschheim and Lacity [35] put it “IT managers commiserate over the challenges of convincing senior executives that, contrary to popular belief, outsourcing isn't always a money-saving option”.

Against this backdrop, this study attempts to advance our knowledge about the mechanisms by which costs escalate in the ex-post stage of ITO contracts. As TCE suggests, a transaction “occurs when a good or service is transferred across a technologically separate interface” [66]. Prior literature has shown that transactions' characteristics or attributes such as relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and bargaining power, and holdup problem form the cornerstone of TCE [38,42]. The proposed model in this study posits that the three major and well-studied transaction attributes, including relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments (or asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city), bargaining power and measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties [42] affect cost escalation via two mediating constructs: the holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties [65,66]. Furthermore, the model also hypothesizes that multisourcing strategy [43] and clan mechanisms [46] play moderating roles in reducing the intensity of the holdup problem and disputes, hence ultimately mitigating cost escalation. Multi-sourcing as a management strategy and clan mechanisms as a soft contracting mechanism are considered widely in TCE literature [42]. Although most of the studies have used these constructs, the present study attempts to integrate them into one comprehensive research model to assess the determinants of cost escalation in ITO arrangement. We focused only on two main scenarios (holdup problem and disputes) or mechanisms by which transaction attributes effect cost escalation. While there are several unwanted scenarios when IT operations are outsourced (e.g., intellectual property, con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, etc.), we limit our study on the most common and cited scenarios in the ITO literature [42] (the holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties). While this conceptualization is based on TCE prescriptions [68], we believe that other lenses may be useful to capture other facets of the studied phenomenon.

This study's contributions to research are twofold. First, this study focuses on the ex-post stage analysis and acknowledges Oliver Williamson's statement [72] “to focus entirely on ex-ante contracting is a truncated way to study organization — especially if all contracts are unavoidably incomplete and if adaptation is the central problem of economic organization. Moving beyond the ex-ante incentive alignment, transaction cost economics turns its attention – additionally and predominately – to the ex post stage of contract”. Furthermore, while contract design, structure, type and characteristics are still important mechanisms in ITO arrangements [15,30,42,52], prior literature argues that ITO contracts are essentially incomplete and the parties cannot credibly commit not to engage in inef<sup>fi</sup>cient bargaining ex-post [9,26,59,66,72], generating a situation characterized by post-contract opportunism even in the presence of mutually agreed-upon contracts [1,66]. Second, the research model proposed in this study takes into account the mediation of two major TCE potential scenarios: the holdup problem and disputes [53,57]. We propose that transaction attributes affect cost escalation through these two mediating constructs. TCE percepts suggest that the mechanisms by which costs escalate are a fundamental issue in de<sup>fi</sup>ning the determinants of cost escalation in ITO arrangement [66]. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we provide the theoretical perspective and hypothesis development. Next, we describe the research methodology, empirical analysis, and results. The paper concludes with a discussion of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the theoretical and practical contributions of our work, its shortcomings, and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical perspective and hypothesis development

Transaction cost economics perspective is based on two key assumptions: bounded rationality and opportunism. Bounded rationality refers to “behavior that is intendedly rational but only limitedly so” [65]. One of the main consequences of bounded rationality is that contracts are incomplete [66]. The second TCE assumption is opportunism which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “self-interest seeking with guile, to include calculated efforts to mislead, deceive, obfuscate, and otherwise confuse” [68]. According to TCE logic, many forms of opportunistic behavior including unreliable reporting of the activities performed, strategic lock-in, price increase, intellectual property, etc. are all potential consequences, or representations, of opportunism [64]. Building upon incomplete contract theory [34] where contracts are distinguished by an incomplete speci<sup>fi</sup>cation at the ex-ante stage (contract terms prepared and negotiated), transition stage (schedules and transition milestones)

and, an execution at the ex-post stage (action stage). Our focus in this study is on the ex-post stage of contracting, that is, the action stage where the contract is executed. The combination of incomplete contracts and representations of opportunism implies that ex-post contracting problems are likely to emerge [71]. In the case of ITO, for instance, an IT service provider may lie about – or exaggerate – its capabilities or use its knowledge advantage to sell services to clients that have little experience and/or awareness of their needs or market prices. IT service providers may also do this because they want to enter a new market, dominate a market segment or lock out competitors [39]. Under these two TCE assumptions, transaction attributes (relationspeci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, bargaining power and, measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties) [4,5,12,51,65] may lead to certain unwanted situations or scenarios (the holdup problem and disputes between parties), resulting in cost escalation [65,66].

Cost escalation refers to the difference between actual costs (all the costs incurred in the completion of the outsourced IT operation) and the contracted costs. Legitimate costs including increased service volumes and added services are not considered part of the cost escalation construct. Costs are not limited to the actual performance of the IT operation; rather, they include costs that are not present when an IT operation is performed in-house, such as maintaining an agreement, monitoring exchange behavior and guarding against opportunism in an exchange situation [70], coordinating with the IT service provider, transferring knowledge, specifying requirements, etc. [24]. Under such circumstances of outsourcing transactions of such attributes, an opportunistic behavior may arise, and an IT service provider may be tempted to increase costs for the client [4]. To counter such situation, two complementary governance mechanisms: a multi-sourcing strategy and clan mechanisms may moderate the relationship between transaction attributes and unwanted potential scenarios [47,70,71]. ## 2.1. The mediating role of the holdup problem

The holdup problem or a lock-in situation refers to a situation where a client cannot get out of a relationship without incurring a loss or sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing part or all of its assets to the IT service provider [28]. As predicted by TCE [66], we posit two antecedents to the likelihood of a holdup problem in an ITO arrangement: relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments and bargaining power. Relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments refer to the degree to which it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to redeploy the assets used to perform an activity to “alternative uses and by alternative users without sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce of productive value” [68]. In other words, it is the extent to which the investments made to support a particular transaction that has a higher value in relation to that transaction that they would have had if they had been used for any other purpose [37,44]. In ITO, relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments stem from highly customized solutions or technologies developed for clients, making their use in alternate contexts dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and suboptimal [15,41]. Examples of speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets include investments in personnel training and learning, investments in de<sup>fi</sup>ning system speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, the purchase of IT assets, and licenses [31].

Speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets, whether physical or intangible, cannot be redeployed to another application or relationship without a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loss in value [69]. TCE asserts that opportunism is relevant to raise the transaction costs in situations where there are speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets and contracts are incomplete. Inasmuch as the value of speci<sup>fi</sup>c asset in other uses is, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, much smaller than the specialized use for which it has been intended, one party is effectively “locked into” the transaction to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degree [68]. In a situation where a transaction is characterized by high relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, TCE posits that if a <sup>fi</sup>rm decides to outsource such a transaction, the redeployability goal is likely to be undermined, increasing dependency and contracting hazards between parties [15,23]. Two situations may result. First, if the investor (the client and/or the IT service provider) makes large investments in speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets (purchasing IT infrastructure, training the other party's personnel, develop proprietary tools, etc.), these relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments make the investor vulnerable to ex-post exploitation due to an unbalanced or asymmetrical investment in the arrangement. If the relationship is to be terminated prematurely, the value of the relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investment would be lost [4,72]. On the other hand, the investor (the client and/or the IT service provider) may use its speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments in the relationship to enhance its bargaining power at the time of contract renewal [32]. To illustrate this point, consider the case of a <sup>fi</sup>rm outsourcing an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system. The IT service provider installs the ERP system for the client, which makes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investment in terms of training and use of the software. Since the system is designed to link together different parts of the organization, an eventual modi<sup>fi</sup>cation is highly probable to both the system itself and the structure of the client's organization so that they <sup>fi</sup>t with the software [47]. Thus, what will emerge is post-contractual holdup problem [57,71]. A holdup problem throughout the contract period is not a problem per se, rather, it represents the opportunism associated with it and is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in cost increases to the locked-in party (the second assumption of TCE). From the service provider's point of view, it might be tempting to make it as dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and expensive as possible for clients to switch to another service provider. Accordingly, we anticipate the holdup problem to mediate these asymmetrical relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments and cost escalation relationship.

Hypothesis 1. The positive effect of asymmetrical relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments on cost escalation is mediated through the holdupproblem.

The second antecedent to the likelihood of the holdup problem is bargaining power, which refers to the degree to which a client has reputable and trustworthy alternative sources of supply to meet its needs [2,66]. TCE predicts that bargaining power imbalance anticipates attempts at expropriation by one contractual partner; hence cost escalation in the exchange. As potential service provider scarcity in marketplace increases, so does the client's dependence on the service provider [69]. When it is possible to choose among many <sup>fi</sup>rms, opportunism is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem. If, however, in the ex-post stage of contracting, there are only a small number of IT service providers in the market specialized in the IT outsourced operation, the client will likely be locked into the relationship with its IT service provider. The ex-ante low-competitive market will shift towards an ex-post dependency situation, in which the IT service provider may extract quasirents because the client has limited options [70]. If the client is made vulnerable by a premature withdrawal from a contract [23], redeployment or switching to another IT service provider will entail considerable costs [37,62]. For instance, at contract renewal time, the IT service provider might exploit the incumbent advantage by bidding marginally lower than the other service providers but much higher than required by its actual costs. This may result in in<sup>fl</sup>ated costs to the client because the incumbent service provider has suf<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge about the ‘true’ cost to be incurred. This form of opportunism, where the number of service providers is small, may leave the client hostage or locked into the relationship with its selected IT service provider [70]. Hence:

Hypothesis 2. The negative effect of bargaining power on cost escalation is mediated through the holdup-problem.

## 2.2. The mediating role of disputes

To Williamson, “all measurement problems are traceable to a condition of information impactedness – which is to say – it is costly to apprise an arbiter of the true information condition should a dispute arise between opportunistic parties who have identical knowledge of the underlying circumstances” [[], pp.31–37]. Accordingly, measurement is costly and subject to error, so contracting parties are not certain how it will fare in their exchanges. This characterizes any dispute, as dispute consist of the competition between parties to capture the difference in their valuation (or quasi rent) of the disputed entity [13]. According to TCE logic [5,66], the likelihood of disputes between parties in ITO arrangements is very common [42]. Disputes refer to issues that have escalated to the executive committee and occur when all other governance processes have been exhausted and when the consequences severely commercially disadvantage one party [5,65,68]. Disputes can be due to measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, which refer to the degree of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in measuring an element of the exchange between trading partners [67]. Measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties may be due to soft outcomes and/or ambiguous links between effort and performance [50]. In the absence of accurate measurements, “buyers must engage in a costly process of monitoring, or sellers must engage in a costly process of signaling” [13]. Prior studies indicate that well-structured SLAs play an important role in cultivating favorable relationships in the course of outsourcing engagements, which, in turn, are related to successful relational outcomes of IT outsourcing [30]. However, SLAs are a form of contract de<sup>fi</sup>nition, and the more the contract is de<sup>fi</sup>ned, the more costly it gets [66]. The process of capturing in detail all elements of an IT service is complex, expensive and time consuming. Articulating, and agreeing with the metrics by which services should be measured are not easy and may require (costly) external assistance. Highly detailed SLAs might lead to information overload, and the detail might obscure critical aspects of the services required [66].

In ITO research, it has been suggested that the more complex, uncertain and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult a task, the more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant the measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties it entails [5,6]. For example, it can be argued that task complexity increases the likelihood of asymmetrically distributed knowledge, which again increases the likelihood of disputes [54], e.g., opposing opinions about requirements due to different knowledge bases rather than different behavioral motivations [24]. Under circumstances characterized by measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, the contracting parties may dig into the contract provisions and clauses to resolve disputes. However, the greater the complexity of the problems encountered measuring the quantity, the quality or even the cost of services, the greater the cost of negotiating, writing and monitoring contracts [70]. When transactions involve considerable measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, contracts between parties tend to be incomplete, thus exposing the client <sup>fi</sup>rm to considerable risk of agency con<sup>fl</sup>icts [61,63,70]. Examining several ITO case studies, Barthélemy and Quélin [12] found that during the contract period, the parties may argue over and dispute the quality of the measurement instruments used and the evaluation criteria chosen. For instance, a response time can be subject to disputes, since it may not be easy to determine why the system is failing to respond in the agreed response time (i.e., due to database design inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, system overload, network saturation, etc.). Any disclaimer concerning negligence of responsibilities and obligations by the IT service provider will bring the contracting parties into disputes. In the absence of an incurred cost due to disputes [12,18], this scenario is not a problem per se, but since costs are incurred by the client as a result, disputes become an apparent problem. Therefore, we hypothesize that disputes mediate the relationship between measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and cost escalation.

Hypothesis 3. The positive effect of measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties on cost escalation is mediated through disputes between the contracting parties.

## 2.3. The moderating role of multi-sourcing

Multi-sourcing refers to outsourcing the same IT operation to more than one IT service provider, a static and structural element that lies ahead of the actual service delivery and impacts a static relational characteristic [10]. Clients can improve their bargaining power by contracting the same IT operation to more than one IT service provider [25]. The multi-sourcing strategy can be traced to Porter's recommendation that “several competing vendors should be used to ensure low costs as well as high performance and quality” [51]. The argument posits that the ever-present threat of losing business to one of the other service providers will induce each service provider to provide a higher level of performance and quality [8,25]. Indeed, just the presence of several service providers can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly curtail the risk of dependency on the IT service provider [7,12] and reduce cost [44]. For instance, a multi-sourcing strategy enabled Fonterra, the world's largest exporter of dairy ingredients, to achieve IT infrastructure cost savings that exceeded expectations and were beyond the savings delivered under the organization's single-sourced arrangement. It has been argued that this outcome cannot be realized when “you are boxed in one single vendor” [10]. We posit that multi-sourcing, moderates the relationship between relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments and the holdup problem. Multi-sourcing does make a difference in the presence of asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments. That is, if asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments level high, multi-sourcing does have a strong impact to reduce the holdup problem, but if asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investment level is low, multi-sourcing is rather irrelevant. Similarly, if there is a bargaining power in the market, multi-sourcing will reduce the intensity of the holdup problem. Therefore:

Hypothesis 4a. Multi-sourcing will moderate the relationship between asymmetrical relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments and the holdup problem.

Hypothesis 4b. Multi-sourcing sourcing will moderate the relationship between bargaining power and the holdup problem.

## 2.4. The moderating role of clan mechanisms

Transaction cost economics and related perspectives in contracting hold that bounded rationality prevent parties from writing detailed and complete contracts that deal with all possible contingencies [34], and the use of social mechanisms can play a role by complementing the adaptive limits of formal contracts [30,49,52,71]. Some of these mechanisms are known as clan mechanisms (anchored in relational exchange theory), which refer to “a situation in which the traditional relationship is replaced by a scenario where the two organizations perceive themselves as having a common, shared goal” [17]. A critical contribution to TCE has been Ouchi's identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of clan mechanisms as a complementary alternative governance device in buyer–vendor exchange relationships [47]. Although clan mechanisms are apparently found in both internalized and externalized exchanges, attempts to apply Ouchi's important contribution to the latter have been scarce to date [40]. Ouchi [47] differentiates between outcome control, behavior control and clan mechanisms. Outcome control and behavior (or process) control are formal controls; this type of control measures, evaluates, and rewards either outcomes or behaviors. In this case, the optimal choice of control mechanisms is determined by the measurability of the task. When there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, outcome control is not effective because clear performance standards cannot be established. Behavioral controls, which involve monitoring the behavior of those who perform the task, are also dif<sup>fi</sup>cult under these conditions. When these control mechanisms fail because of important measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, it has been argued that clan mechanisms – which are characterized by goal congruence among parties, reciprocity, and shared values – are effective control mechanisms [47]. According to TCE, in a situation where it is easy to specify and measure performance, complete contracting is possible [70]. Clearly cases of low Asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments do exist in TCE and contracts can be complete for such instances. However, TCE logic suggests that such situations do not commonly exist. If the parties to an agreement could specify their respective rights and duties for every possible state of the world, their contract would be complete; there would be no gaps in the contract terms. Nevertheless, since transaction costs make completeness unobtainable and it would be prohibitively expensive to write a complete contract, contracts are usually incomplete [66].

Furthermore, both parties have a limited ability to foresee outcomes through formal control mechanisms [17]. In such a case, an informal device — clan mechanisms can be deployed. The term refers to one party relying on normative considerations to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the behavior of the other party. In<sup>fl</sup>uence comes only in the form of shared goals, values and norms. Since there is no explicit restriction on members' behavior, clan mechanisms imply more interpersonal respect and less mistrust than formal control mechanisms. When parties understand the environment and the work to be performed and share common values and beliefs about how to coordinate their efforts, debates and con<sup>fl</sup>icts are less likely to arise [12]. Therefore:

Hypothesis 5. Clan mechanisms will moderate the relationship between measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and disputes between the contracting parties.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Data collection and sample characteristics

Data were gathered through a survey of CIOs (Table 2). Using a database of 3000 Canadian organizations, 1250 CIOs were randomly selected and contacted by phone. They were asked: (1) whether their organization had outsourced some or all of its IT operations; (2) whether they had been involved in the outsourcing decision; and/or (3) whether they had been involved in contract management in the previous <sup>fi</sup>ve years. These constraints were used for temporal stability and in order to avoid response bias, as suggested by Huber [36]. Out of 1053 executives who met these criteria, 390 agreed to participate. A total of 132 executives returned the completed questionnaire, for a response rate of 12.5%. Respondents were mainly in the manufacturing (29.54%), services (21.96%), and insurance, <sup>fi</sup>nance and real estate (18.18%) sectors. The average responding organization employed 3250 people and had \$2.696 billion in assets. The average contract length was 2.75 years, and, at the time of the survey, the average IT operation had been outsourced for three years.

The unit of analysis was the IT operation [3,4]. Eleven IT operations were initially presented to our respondents, including application development, network management, system integration, PC management, hardware maintenance, control of operations, disk space management, production support services, PC installation, Telecom lines maintenance, CPU operations, and operations related to IT applications. Those most frequently outsourced by the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms were application development, system integration, network management, and PC management. Respondents were asked to select only one IT operation.

## 3.2. Control variables

Our choice of controls is guided largely by theoretical considerations. The model incorporates two control variables that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the holdup problem and disputes between parties: the type of IT operation outsourced and the duration of the contract. The type of outsourced IT operation represents an important variable that has an in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on many outcome variables in an outsourcing context, including commitment of the partners to the relationship [30]. In addition, a longer duration of association is generally expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of the relationship between parties [29]. We, therefore, include length of the contract as a control variable. Duration of the contract is also expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence outsourcing outcomes, including the holdup problem and disputes between parties. Firm-level controls are not appropriate in this analysis because the unit of analysis is the IT operation. A t-test was used to check whether the type of IT operation chosen and contract duration affect our model results. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects were observed at the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of 0.05.

## 3.3. Measurement

All constructs in the survey were measured using multi-item scales with seven-point Likert rating system. A conscientious effort was made to adapt existing measures validated from prior studies for the latent constructs in this research. Some of the constructs – relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, bargaining power, measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>- culties, the holdup problem, and disputes – were measured using instruments that had been developed and validated in previous studies. Others – such as multi-sourcing, clan mechanisms and cost escalation – were developed for this study. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step in developing the measures was to conduct a literature review to specify each construct's domain so that each measure would have the desired psychometric properties [19]. The dimensions identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this literature review were veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by IT experts – <sup>fi</sup>ve IT executives, three IT faculty members and four Ph.D. students (IT major) – who were asked to evaluate the dimensions' representativeness, meaning, and relevance to the intended constructs. The proposed dimensions were modi<sup>fi</sup>ed as required based on the experts' feedback.

The measures were further re<sup>fi</sup>ned via a card sorting procedure [56] performed by ten judges — six IT faculty members and four IS doctoral students. To assess the reliability of the judges' sorting, the level of agreement of each pair of judges was measured using Cohen's Kappa [20]. All levels of agreement were high — min 0.79 and max

0.97, where the threshold was 0.65. Before collecting the data, the instrument was pre-tested using four individuals who were familiar with the subject and/or the survey techniques. The survey instrument was modi<sup>fi</sup>ed in accordance with their recommendations.

Non-response bias was assessed using t-tests between early respondents' and late respondents' answers (97 respondents within the <sup>fi</sup>rst 3 weeks and 35 questionnaires received after the 3-week period). T-tests were used to compare the assets, number of employees, annual sales and industry sector of operation of participating and non-participating <sup>fi</sup>rms. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were found at the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of 0.05. Furthermore, we examined the common method bias using Harman's one-factor test [46]. These tests found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant biases in our dataset that would have been due to the survey methodology.

## 4. Results

In an ideal situation, testing the proposed research model would call for establishing – for each transaction in the sample, relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, bargaining power, and measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties – a threshold that would determine whether, according to TCE precepts, the transaction should be outsourced or internalized. In reality, this is not feasible. We believe, however, that the transaction characteristic values of the 132 outsourced transactions in our sample vary enough that we can test the transaction attributes' effects in an ex-post stage of contract implementation.

## 4.1. Measurement model

Measures were validated and the model tested using partial least squares structural (PLS) equation modeling as implemented in PLS-Graph Version 2.91.03.04 [16]. Whereas covariance-based structural equation modeling (SEM) tools such as LISREL and EQS use a maximum likelihood function to obtain parameter estimates, the componentbased PLS-Graph uses a least squares estimation procedure, providing enough <sup>fl</sup>exibility to represent both formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective latent constructs. The advantage of using PLS-Graph is that it estimates a measurement model to ascertain construct validity and measure reliability. Second, using indicators of latent constructs, it yields estimates of the structural model parameters, which test the strength of the hypothesized relationships. Finally, it is not limited by the distribution requirements and sample size limitations of other SEM tools [27]. The measurement model was assessed using PLS-Graph to examine internal consistency reliability and convergent and discriminant validity [16]. The measurement properties are reported in Tables 3, 4 and 5. The measures used in our questionnaire are listed in Appendix A.

Construct reliability pertains to the degree to which items are free from random error and therefore yield consistent results. In our measurement model (Table 3), all constructs had a composite reliability over the cutoff of 0.70, as suggested by Straub [56]. Convergent validity assesses consistency across multiple operationalizations [16]. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, all estimated standard loadings were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p b 0.01), suggesting good convergent validity. To assess discriminant validity (the extent to which different constructs diverge from one another), we used Fornell and Marcker's criterion [27]: the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should be greater than the squared correlation between the constructs. Our results suggest that the items share more variance with their respective constructs than with other constructs. All our constructs met this criterion. AVE values are on the diagonal in Table 4.

## 5. Discussion of <sup>fi</sup>ndings

The objective of the study was to test a model of the determinants of ITO cost escalation. The proposed model contributes to ITO literature in two ways. First, we found support for our Hypotheses 1, 2 and 3 that the holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties mediate transaction attributes (asymmetrical relationshipspeci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, bargaining power and, measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties) and cost escalation. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings address Lacity et al.'s [42] concern about the lack of understanding of the mediating process underlying the TCE logic and con<sup>fi</sup>rm prior theoretical assertion that transaction attributes affect cost escalation through two major mediators: the holdup problem and disputes [7]. Second, our integrated moderated mediation analyses demonstrate general support for our Hypotheses 4a, 4b and 5 and thus address the missing role of two complementary moderators: multi-sourcing (non-exclusivity) strategy and clan mechanisms as sociological responses to safeguard against disputes between the parties. The results of the empirical analysis provide a number of interesting insights and open the way for more research that should re<sup>fi</sup>ne our understanding of ITO cost escalation in the ex-post stage of contracting.

## 5.1. The mediating role of holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that cost escalation is explained, all things being equal, by the effects of transaction attributes through the holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties. Most importantly, this study has shown that the omnipresence of asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments impacts cost escalation through the emergence of the holdup problem created by speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments made in the relationship. Costs are not increased by the presence of speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments per-se; rather they are increased by the mechanism by which these investments create a holdup opportunistic opportunity.

Theoretically, bargaining power of the investor should decrease as termination or switching costs would be higher for the investing party. In classic TCE literature [32,65,66], for example, Fisher (the service provider) refused General Motor's (the client) request to build their plant near GM headquarters. The TCE interpretation of this is that Fischer would have made a tremendous relation speci<sup>fi</sup>c investment that would increase GM's ability to behave opportunistically. Here the service provider, not the client, would suffer because the service provider would have made the relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investment.

In the context of ITO practice, clients and service providers decide which party will make asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments. For example, increasingly, clients will only pay for knowledge transfer (e.g., service provider training, shadowing, and site visits) once. After that, the service provider has to fund knowledge renewal. This contractual practice was prompted by the high turnover in service provider <sup>fi</sup>rms. A TCE interpretation of this practice can be that clients refused to make excessive and recurrent relation speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm these assertions for client investments, the second dimension of relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investment construct. This dimension did not load on its intent construct and was dropped from our analysis. However, our results suggest that the investor (here the IT service provider) may use its speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments in the relationship to enhance its bargaining power [32]. From the service provider's point of view, it might be tempting to make it as dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and expensive as possible for the client to switch to another service provider. These empirical results provide strong support for this hypothesis. The results therefore provide additional empirical support that the view that the holdup problem is strongly affected by variations in the importance of Asymmetrical relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments regardless of whom makes the investment [72].

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also support the idea that the presence of bargaining power plays an important role in the emergence of the holdup problem. Williamson [66,70] (and elsewhere) has popularized the issue regarding the manner in which bargaining power raises transaction cost. A limited number of IT service providers may have been true 25 years ago when IBM, EDS and CSC had 90% of the market share, but in today's world, there are hundreds of thousands of ITO providers worldwide. In fact, a limited number of service providers in the ex-post stage of contracting increases the incumbent IT service provider's bargaining power and, hence, the client may be subjected to opportunistic bargaining that would lead it to considerable costs. In such situations, the IT service provider may be tempted to charge monopoly-like prices, knowing that it can in<sup>fl</sup>ate its costs. Indeed, in traditional industrial economics, this situation is a primary source of <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts [65]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the contention that bargaining power will affect ex-post bargaining and probably result in opportunistic behavior [32]. In the context of ITO, once the client selects an IT service provider and starts implementing the IT service provider's solutions (such as an ERP system), the client may become very vulnerable to service provider's opportunism because the client is ‘stuck with’ the service provider's solution, even if there are many service providers in the market during service provider selection.

The second contribution of this paper is the role played by disputes in mediating the relationship between measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and cost escalation. While TCE acknowledges the role of measurement in determining boundary choice [72], measurement issues have been comparatively ignored in the empirical literature [60]. When contributions from a service provider cannot be accurately assessed, adequate contracts will be costly to craft [48]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties are important antecedents of disputes between parties. The more an IT operation is unpredictable or its measures unveri<sup>fi</sup>able, the more there will be debates and disagreement between the parties and ex-post performance appraisals may be controversial, creating disputes between the contracting parties [32]. This is in line with TCE logic, measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties may damage the performance of markets more than the performance of <sup>fi</sup>rms [66]. Within <sup>fi</sup>rms, when measurement becomes problematic, managers can simply substitute authority and behavioral monitoring [60]. Markets, by contrast, lack the capacity for such managerial intervention: when measurement is highly problematic, markets simply fail increasing the potential for disputes between the contracting parties and, hence, cost to the client [49].

## 5.2. The moderating role of multi-sourcing and clan mechanisms

Moderated mediation is demonstrated when the conditional indirect effect of transaction attributes on cost escalation via the holdup problem, differs in strength across the absence and presence of multi-sourcing. Multi-sourcing is often seen as an appropriate ITO strategy for addressing the issue of holdup because it protects clients from complacency on the part of the single service provider [12,21]. In such circumstances and in order to build protection from service providers and their expected opportunistic behavior, clients split the same IT operation among two or more service providers. Theoretically, this may prevent service providers from opportunistic behavior and ‘kept suppliers on their toes’. Hypotheses 4a and 4b suggest a moderated mediation model [51], whereby the strength of the relationship between absolute levels of relation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments and bargaining power on cost escalation through holdup problem is conditional on the value of a moderator (multi-sourcing). The presence (or the absence) of high level of these two transaction attributes is moderated by the presence (or absence) of the multi-sourcing practice by the client.

Our results also suggest that the integration of soft contracting (in addition to formal contracting), which presumes a much closer identity of interests between the parties, has a role to play in mitigating disputes [46,68]. We do not mean to suggest that clan mechanisms [40] are needed in isolation to mitigate disputes between parties, but rather as a complement to contractual dispute resolution provisions. Prior research shows that a large number of ITO contracts have failed despite the existence of contractual governance processes (operations committees, SLA committees, alliance management committees) [42] and several ITO studies have called for integrating these types of soft contracting as a complement to contractual governance perspective.

Hypothesis 5 suggests a moderated mediation model [51], whereby the strength of the relationship between absolute levels of measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties on cost escalation through disputes is conditional on the value of a moderator (clan mechanisms). The presence (or the absence) of high level of measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties is moderated by the presence (or absence) of clan mechanisms. This study shows that disputes due to measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>culties are mitigated when both parties share an understanding of the environment and the job to be performed, have a common set of values and beliefs about how to coordinate their efforts, and maintain a relationship characterized by a high level of shared principles [47]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings reinforce the suggestions that soft control governance should complement the formal approach to contracting [30,68], suggesting the use of clan mechanisms as one form of social control [17,46].

## 6. Implications for research and practice

The results of this study yield a number of implications for both research and practice. First, the study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that ITO researchers need to pay attention to the importance of the ex-post stage of ITO as an intrinsic component of the phenomenon being studied [56]. Indeed, it is at this stage of contracting execution that costs escalate. Second, the empirical results obtained here provide substantial theoretical and practical insights into the mechanisms by which cost escalate. Results of our moderated mediation analysis have underscored the importance of incorporating 1) what can happen when a client <sup>fi</sup>rm outsourced its IT operations to an external service provider (e.g., holdup and disputes scenarios), 2) how likely these scenarios will happen (e.g., through transactions attributes), 3) if these scenarios happen, what are the consequences (e.g., cost escalation) and <sup>fi</sup>nally, 4) what are the governance mechanisms used (or to be used) to attenuate these scenarios. Researchers may extend this conceptualization and identify other scenarios and mechanisms by which transaction attributes affect cost escalation.

Finally, it was Oliver Williamson [66] who originally provided a framework for the organization of transactions that relies on the fundamental concept of contracts to safeguard against opportunistic behavior, and William Ouchi [47] has since put forth the concept of the clan as a potential complement social response to contracts. Heeding Ouchi, we attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap in ITO literature by integrating a soft governance construct, clan mechanisms into Williamson's TCE framework. Clan mechanisms act as a social safeguard that is relevant in the context of transactions that can be characterized by measurement dif<sup>fi</sup>- culties. We emphasize that managers should not treat clan mechanisms as central governance device but rather as a complement to contracts in an ITO context.

## 7. Limitations and further research

Prior to discussing the implications of the study for research and practice, a number of limitations need to be acknowledged. First, the unit of analysis used in this study is the IT operation rather than the whole contract which may consist of a bundle of IT operations [42]. Second, we focused on only two main scenarios (holdup problem and disputes) or mechanisms through which transaction attribute effect cost escalation. While there are several unwanted scenarios when IT operations are outsourced (e.g., intellectual property, con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, etc.) we limited our study on the most common and cited scenarios in ITO literature [42] (the holdup problem and disputes). Also, we limit our study on two main and complementary moderating constructs (multisourcing and clan mechanisms). While this conceptualization is based on TCE prescriptions [68], we believe that other lenses may be useful to capture other facets of the studied phenomenon. Lacity et al.[42] have suggested to investigate constructs associated with client and service provider capabilities, contractual and relational governance and, decision characteristics. Finally, we have not taken into account contract type and characteristics due to the data collection method used (i.e., a survey) and the fact that the pre-test showed that respondents were reluctant to provide contract details except the contract duration.

## 8. Conclusion

Using a survey approach, this research attempts to <sup>fi</sup>ll a gap in the literature on IT outsourcing research. On a theoretical level, the results offer a plausible explanation for apparently con<sup>fl</sup>icting past results on the determinants of cost escalation. The results of our moderated mediation analysis indicate that transaction attributes impact cost escalation through two main scenarios (holdup problem and disputes between the contracting parties). Our study contributes also to the emerging stream of ITO research by providing a bridge between the two modes of governance: formal contracts and soft contracting that are usually investigated separately. These insights could not be gained with studies that focused on piecemeal approaches that examine direct effects of transaction attributes, mediation or moderation independently [6a,6b,43]. Overall results underline the importance to take into account simultaneously constructs in<sup>fl</sup>uencing cost escalation. We encourage future research toward a more integrative approach of theorizing mediating and moderating effects, so as to offer a more sophisticated understanding of cost escalation in ITO arrangements.

Bouchaib Bahlia is Professor of Information Technology (IT) at ESC-Rennes School of Business, Rennes, France. His research areas are: IT outsourcing, risk management of software development projects and, IT adoption modeling. His publications appeared in Inofration and Management, Journal of Information Technology, OMEGA, Communications of AIS, Requirement Engineering Journal, and others.
