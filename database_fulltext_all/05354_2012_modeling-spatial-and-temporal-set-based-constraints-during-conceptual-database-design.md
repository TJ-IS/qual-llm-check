# Modeling Spatial and Temporal Set-Based Constraints During Conceptual Database Design

Faiz Currim

Tippie College of Business, University of Iowa, Iowa City, Iowa 52242, currim@email.arizona.edu

Sudha Ram

Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85712, ram@eller.arizona.edu

rom a database perspective, business constraints provide an accurate picture of the real world being modeled and help enforce data integrity. Typically, rules are gathered during requirements analysis and embedded in code during the implementation phase. We propose that the rules be explicitly modeled during conceptual design, and develop a framework for understanding and classifying spatiotemporal set-based (cardinality) constraints and an associated syntax. The constraint semantics are formally specified using first-order logic. Modeling rules in conceptual design ensures they are visible to designers and users and not buried in application code. The rules can then be semiautomatically translated into logical design triggers yielding productivity gains. Following the principles of design science research, we evaluate the framework’s expressiveness and utility with a case study.

Key words: data modeling; conceptual design; spatiotemporal cardinality constraints; data management spatiotemporal databases; design science research

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) is critical to the success of organizations today, and a well-managed design and development process plays an important role in system effectiveness. Within the IT infrastructure, databases provide back-end support for both operational and strategic systems. Although the idea of investing in good design has widespread acceptance, there may be some issue as to whether a conceptual schema should go beyond capturing the structure and relationships among data objects to include the business constraints affecting the data.

A number of arguments have been provided in support of modeling rules<sup>1</sup> during conceptual design. Having a visible rule repository is important for managerial decision making (Von Halle 2002) and researchers and practitioners have recommended that user-specified business rules applicable to data should be documented in the database schema itself (ISO 1987, Simsion 2001). Understanding and managing business constraints help organizations have a better sense of their own operating environment (Rouvellou et al. 2000) and improves efficiency and competitiveness (Shao and Pound 1999). Given the importance of conceptual design in database development (Dey et al. 1995), and because a large percentage (estimated over 40%) of systems development failures occur because of faulty requirements analysis and design (Ramamoorthy et al. 1984, RTI 2002), we advocate taking steps to ensure that policies have been correctly understood and validated early in design. Because the cost of correcting errors increases significantly with each stage in development (Boehm 1976, Moody 1998), a schema that facilitates early detection of errors (including those in constraints) is superior to one that does not. Further, visibility of modeled business rules in the conceptual schema is crucial because if rules are missed at this stage, they may never get enforced when the database gets implemented or be inconsistently applied (Shao and Pound 1999). Another reason in favor of modeling rules during conceptual design comes from the adoption of model-driven architecture (OMG 2001) because automatic code generation requires standardized constraint representation (Warmer and Kleppe 2003).

Given the need to represent rules explicitly, questions arise as to the kinds of abstraction required, particularly for complex constraints involving spatial and temporal semantics. We consider a franchising example (Figure 1)<sup>2</sup> to illustrate. To sustain a healthy relationship among involved parties and safeguard substantial investments in the business, many franchising laws and trade practices have evolved over time. These agreements contain crucial clauses regarding the franchisee’s market and the number of current and future franchises that may be granted in that territory (Dailey 1998). Other covenants may restrict the nature and number of competing stores the franchisee can own and whether the franchisor is allowed to solicit orders within franchisee territories. As an example, the agreement may state that up to twentyfive competing restaurants may be permitted in the city at any point in time (Policy 1), with two in the same business district (Policy 2). Although managing and enforcing these rules, which could number in the hundreds and vary nationwide based on sales volumes, customer density, etc., can be quite a task in itself, we would like to focus on the constraint-modeling aspect.

Examining the two policies, we see both speak of constraint limits as evaluated within some spatial and temporal range. Naturally, one would like a formal and standardized abstraction of the rule’s components. The goal of such abstraction, a recognized problem-solving technique (Wing 2006), is to understand the underlying concepts. Subsequently, one can develop a generalizable mechanism to represent similar rules in the future. Without abstraction and a classification scheme, there would be repeated effort involved in constraint specification (i.e., writing out some form of pseudocode each time).

The questions this research addresses are

How can we standardize modeling the effects of space and time on database constraints?

How is the resultant framework valuable?

We choose to examine the effect of space and time on constraints because of the large number of systems that use spatial and temporal data (Khatri et al. 2004), including applications in logistics, remote sensing, urban planning, inventory management, real estate, financial markets, and accounting.

After presenting a literature review in §2, we focus our attention on the spatial and temporal characteristics of set-based rules (cardinality constraints) and illustrate relevant spatiotemporal abstractions in a framework presented in §3. Our view is that a formal classification of the underlying rule category (cardinality) and the spatiotemporal aspects allows the database analyst to better understand, represent, and elicit relevant business policies. We support this view by an evaluation of the framework in §4. Our conclusions and future work are summarized in §5. ## 2. Literature Review

Past work has suggested that explicit modeling of rules allows for better expression of real-world semantics during conceptual modeling, for simplification of developed conceptual schemas (Rauh and Stickel 1992) and for rule visibility. Some of the other advantages of modeling constraints and consequent inclusion in the database system are facilitating query optimization techniques (Thalheim 1996), aiding in semantic query processing (Elmasri and Navathe 2006), and aiding heterogeneous database integration (Ramesh and Ram 1997). Documentation of rules leads to improved application understanding and specification. This in turn results in decreased costs, increased consistency, and easier maintenance (Rouvellou et al. 2000). Additionally, visible rules are easier to modify in a dynamic environment and can allow for semiautomated translation into code using CASE tools.

A number of general-purpose and domain-specific conceptual modeling languages have been developed (with and without spatiotemporal support) since the original entity relationship (ER) Model (Chen 1976) was proposed. These are described in various survey papers on the topic (Gregersen and Jensen 1999, Hull and King 1987, Tryfona and Jensen 1999). The types of constraints commonly modeled are class identifiers, attribute-value multiplicity, attribute domain restrictions, and interaction relationship cardinality. The first two types are built into the semantic model constructs, whereas the latter two are typically specified by annotations either on the schema being developed or as part of the data dictionary. We envision the rules in our framework being incorporated into the conceptual schema via data dictionary annotations and subsequently translated semiautomatically (i.e., with some analyst input) into DBMS constraints.

The term cardinality in most semantic modeling grammars is synonymous with interaction relationship cardinality. Authors have also proposed modeling cardinality for attributes (i.e., the minimum and maximum number of values an attribute can have) and entity classes (Elmasri et al. 1990, Lenzerini and Santucci 1983). There are many types of relationship cardinality constraints as well. Liddle et al. (1993) formally define and compare the semantics of interaction relationship cardinality in 13 different conceptual modeling languages and come up with a distinction between participation, co-occurrence, and projection cardinalities (an explanation of these cardinality types is in §3.2). These three types have been generalized (McAllister 1998, Thalheim 2000) to cover participation, co-occurrence, and projection constraints with one or more associated entity classes. Appearance cardinalities, and cardinalities on other higher-order modeling constructs have been proposed (Ram and Khatri 2005), where the authors suggest using the term set-based rules to avoid the limited scope “cardinality” connotes in most existing conceptual modeling grammars. Work has also been done on the distinction between set- and instance-level cardinality (Currim and Ram 2006). Our framework takes all these rule aspects into consideration, although this paper is not just a discussion of cardinality but rather an illustration of the value of using a framework to capture temporal and spatial constraints during conceptual data modeling. We pick cardinality because of its formal structure and universal familiarity.

Whereas snapshot (non-spatiotemporal) conceptual languages usually deal with constraints applicable “on the current database state,” the incorporation of “begin” and “end” time stamps to record “entity and transaction histories” (Batra 2005) leads to interpreting cardinality across multiple time units. Temporal modeling grammars differentiate between snapshot and lifetime cardinality (Tauzovich 1991). This concept is refined to distinguish between sequenced and nonsequenced constraints (Snodgrass 1999). The snapshot and lifetime divisions can be adopted for spatial dimensions as well (Parent et al. 2006). There has been passing reference about taking into account the potentially multiple granularity levels between the endpoints of snapshot and lifetime (Wijsen and Ng 1999), but no further work on the idea has been done until ours. To summarize, although most spatiotemporal semantic modeling languages have incorporated cardinality, there is no work detailing a classification of how space and time augment constraint semantics (cardinality included). We feel it is important to isolate the components of spatial and temporal constraints to allow for structured representation and translation. In an implemented system, each of these dimensions will be separately represented by (relational) attributes and likewise be independently checked by different pieces of constraint logic.

Work has been done in the area of spatial data integrity constraints (Borges et al. 2002). However, these focus on spatial interactions like topological relationships of adjacency, containment (Egenhofer and Franzosa 1991), and their implications. When semantic integrity constraints are discussed (Cockroft 1997), it is usually in regards to geographic features; e.g., roads and rivers cannot overlap.

Another area of related work is the development of various types of constraint definition languages (e.g., OCL (OMG 2012) for UML) and description logics. The technology is suited for analysts who wish to use a language that can represent the pseudocode or logic of rules and make axiom-based inferences about constraints (Calvanese et al. 1998, Urban and Lim 1993). However, they do not incorporate spatiotemporal semantics and are not targeted as a means to classify constraints. Similarly, the work in active database systems and related areas such as rule models, languages, constraint execution, and implementation (Paton and Díaz 1999, Widom and Ceri 1996) focuses on logical specification and execution. An active database system could be used to implement the rules we describe.

## 3. Framework for Spatial and Temporal Constraint Extensions

Assuming the use of an ER-based conceptual design formalism, we follow previous work in using the underlying semantic modeling constructs as a natural way to think about the constraints and classify them (Ram and Khatri 2005). While the analyst develops the conceptual schema, she can simultaneously consider the relevant rules for each entity class or relationship. Model constructs (i.e., entity classes, attributes, interaction relationships, generalizations/specializations, aggregations, and composites) form the first dimension of classification in our framework. The second dimension for constraint classification is that of the spatial and temporal windows and boundaries.

A variety of different kinds of cardinality constraints may be defined on entity classes, attributes, interaction relationships, inclusion relationships, and so on. This characterizes the core “constraint type” (Box 1, Figure 2). Any constraint (e.g., up to 25 franchises are permitted) is an instantiation of a given constraint type (in this case, entity class cardinality). When time and space are in the picture, two new aspects arise: (i) an evaluation window (Box 2), and (ii) an applicability bound (Boxes 3a, 3b). The evaluation window provides the defining interval (or area)over which the constraint should be checked (e.g., up to 25 franchisees are permitted per city, at any point in time). The applicability bound delineates when and where the constraint was valid. It defines the begin and end points of the “frame” the evaluation window slides along (e.g., the limits for the previous constraint could be applicable during 2004–2010, in the Midwest region of the United States). We discuss these concepts in more depth shortly.

In the rest of this section we discuss the concepts of spatiotemporal windows of evaluation and applicability bounds and how they affect cardinality constraints on interaction relationships.

3.1. Windows of Evaluation for Time and Space The span (or window) of time during which a constraint is evaluated classifies it into one of two types, sequenced or nonsequenced. Similarly, the spatial extent (or window) over which a constraint is evaluated classifies it into either unit-spaced or collective-spaced. The latter notions are similar to sequenced and nonsequenced for time, but we adopt a new term for ease of distinction. Also, the term “sequenced” is more suitable for time where the domain is a totally ordered set. We assume that the reader is familiar with basic temporal and spatial terminology, and we refer to detailed work in the temporal (Bettini et al. 2000, Jensen et al.

1998) and spatial (Frank 1992, Rigaux et al. 2002) areas for more information.

3.1.1. Temporal: Sequenced. Borrowing from temporal query language terminology, we define a temporal constraint as sequenced with respect to a snapshot constraint if (and only if) the temporal constraint can be expressed as a snapshot constraint evaluated independently at each point in time. Given a snapshot constraint, “A franchise should have exactly one value for its status attribute,” the corresponding sequenced constraint is: “A franchise should have exactly one value for its status attribute at each point in time.” The granularity of the underlying construct determines the interpretation of a point in time. So, if the granularity of status is day, the franchising project should have only one value for status on a given day.

Granularities are the unit of measure, or resolution, for a temporal data point (Dyreson et al. 2000). A franchise’s start date is typically measured in a granularity of days, whereas the granularity for when an order was placed is often known in minutes. The granularity may be specified in terms of a universally accepted notion, such as day/hour/minute/second, or a userdefined one. A sequenced constraint is specified at the granularity level of the data it constrains.<sup>3</sup> Thus, if a franchise’s start and end dates are known to the granularity of days, it is not meaningful to specify a constraint at the level of minutes.

A current constraint can be defined as a special kind of sequenced constraint whose period of applicability is the present or current time. The time instant corresponding to current time continuously changes. From an implementation perspective, it would be captured in a database system by the temporal variable now (Torp et al. 2004) and evaluated against the data at the current time. The granularity measure for the current time depends on the underlying construct data. The implementation of this constraint is similar to that of a snapshot constraint in a nontemporal database.

A sequenced constraint can also be implemented as a current constraint if three conditions are met. The first is: the constraint is implemented on or before the beginning of the applicability bounds. The second is: no changes are made to data retroactively, i.e., no change is made to data in the past. This is because past data will not be checked in an implementation that only considers current time. The third is: data is not stored about future valid time (including forever). These conditions must hold for both valid time and transaction time (Snodgrass 1999).

3.1.2. Temporal: Nonsequenced. A nonsequenced constraint is one that is evaluated over a set of granularity instants (the lifetime of the data entity is one such set). For example, if we consider a snapshot constraint: “A franchise should have exactly one value for its status attribute,” a nonsequenced variant could state, “No more than two different values for status should exist during any week.” The assumption here is that the granularity for status is day. Within nonsequenced constraints, two types of evaluation frames appear. These are fixed window and sliding window. The time window is the temporal element specifying the duration over which the constraint is evaluated. A temporal element is defined as a finite union of time intervals (Jensen et al. 1998); i.e., it is a set of intervals.

With a fixed window, the constraint is evaluated once over the duration specified by the window. An example is: “No more than three different values for status should exist between Jan-01-2010 and Jan-31-2010.” The temporal element specifying the window size and the temporal element specifying the constraint applicability are identical for this type of constraint.

In contrast, a sliding-window constraint is (conceptually) evaluated multiple times during the constraint’s applicability. The size of the time window (for evaluation) is strictly smaller than that of the constraint applicability bounds. An equal length for the evaluation window and applicability bound would imply a fixed-window constraint in effect. The final parameter for this kind of constraint is the size (or granularity) of each slide. We infer the following relationship between the constraint components: granularity of the construct ≤ size of the slide ≤ size of the evaluation window ≤ size of the constraint applicability. An example of a sliding-window constraint is “A franchise should have at least one inspection every quarter (3-month interval). The constraint should be evaluated each month for the preceding quarter, and is applicable between Jan-01-2005 and Dec-31-2010.” Assuming inspections had a granularity of day, we come up with the following relevant temporal information: The previously mentioned relationship among rule parameters will continue to hold, i.e., the granularity of inspections ≤ the slide size ≤ the window size ≤ the applicability bound size.

Comparing temporal constraint types, we observe that sequenced constraints are applicable for data classified as states, and nonsequenced constraints are applicable to both events and states. Events have no duration, and so we cannot associate sequenced semantics with them. Sequenced constraints are relevant for data with point-based semantics (atelic data) and not interval-based semantics (telic data) (Terenziani and Snodgrass 2004), although nonsequenced constraints can be applied to both. The reason is that point-based data (e.g., a franchise status being active) can have the property of being true at each point between the begin and end of the associated valid time. Therefore, it is meaningful to check how many franchises are active at each point in time. This is not the case with interval-based semantics (e.g., asking how many franchise inspections were completed at each point in time; completion is telic and must be looked at as a whole rather than as an ongoing sequence of states). Nonsequenced constraints are meaningfully applied only to constructs (relationships, entity classes, etc.) that are time varying, whereas sequenced constraints may be applied to a snapshot construct at each point during its existence.

There is a similarity between sliding-window and sequenced constraints (analogously, we can draw a parallel between current constraints and fixed-window nonsequenced constraints). Both are evaluated multiple times within the applicability bounds. The difference between them is that for a sequenced constraint the evaluation window and slide size are both a single granule of time (corresponding to the granularity of the underlying construct).

3.1.3. Spatial: Unit-Spaced. Analogous to sequenced constraints in the time dimension, unit-spaced constraints are evaluated at each point in space. The semantics of a point in space depends on the granularity of the underlying construct. Using a nonspatial constraint as an example, “There should be no more than five franchises,” a corresponding unit-spaced constraint is “There should be no more than five franchises at each point in space.” Thus, if the granularity of the franchises class is a city subdivision, then there can be up to five franchises in any city subdivision. To elaborate further, the term unit could mean a point in the Cartesian plane, a town block, or an entire city. The unit of evaluation is generally referred to as the resolution or granularity, which depends on the application. As with time, granularities for space may be expressed at standard resolutions (e.g., dms-degree, dms-minute, and dms-second)<sup>4</sup> or user-defined ones. Unit-spaced constraints are always evaluated at the same granularity as the underlying construct.

3.1.4. Spatial: Collective-Spaced. A collectivespaced constraint is one that is evaluated over multiple spatial granularity units. As with temporal nonsequenced constraints, collective-spaced constraints may be classified into two types, those evaluated over a fixed window of space, or those evaluated over a sliding window (region) of space. Corresponding to “There should be no more than twenty-five franchises,” a collective-spaced constraint could be, “There should be no more than twenty-five franchises in each city.”

For fixed-window constraints, the spatial element specifying the window size is identical to the spatial element for the constraint applicability. The constraint is only evaluated within the window specified. An example of this type of constraint is: “There should be no more than ten franchises in downtown Phoenix.” In contrast, a sliding-window constraint is evaluated multiple times within the constraint applicability. The window of evaluation slides according to the granularity specified. We have the following relationship between the various constraint aspects: granularity of the construct ≤ size of the slide ≤ size of the evaluation window ≤ size of the constraint applicability. To illustrate, we use an example: “There should be no more than twenty-five franchises in any county in Arizona.” Assuming franchises had a granularity of city, we have the following spatial information related to this constraint. The size of the window is a county, the granularity of each slide is also at the county level, and the constraint applicability is the state of Arizona.

3.1.5. Differences Between Temporal and Spatial Semantics. Often, an implicit understanding is that the semantics of space and time are very similar, with the main difference being that space may have more dimensions. So, previous authors have extended the semantics of temporal dependencies and constraints into spatial ones in a direct manner by simply increasing the number of dimensions to be considered (Khatri et al. 2004, Wijsen and Ng 1999). There are definitely a number of similarities between time and space, and one can, for example, draw parallels between time intervals and lines in geometry. However, in the context of constraints, there are some key differences in the spatial and temporal semantics that we highlight.

The standard extension of a nontemporal cardinality constraint to incorporate temporal semantics is by the use of a sequenced constraint (i.e., one applicable at each point in time). Compared to this, the extension of a nonspatial constraint to include spatial semantics is not by the use of a unit-spaced (i.e., one applicable at each unit of space) constraint as one might expect. Instead, it is the special case of a collective-spaced constraint that is applicable over all space (relevant to the database). For a similar reason, a current temporal constraint is more akin to a snapshot restriction (compared to one applicable to the present point in space). This is because, in general, a snapshot (nonspatiotemporal) database contains data that is true for a particular point in time and across all regions of space. For example, if we have a snapshot constraint that states, “a franchise-chain may have up to five hundred employees,” this is naturally understood to refer to five hundred employees at any point in time, but across all space (country or world; as the case may be). The implication for relational design is that a sequenced and all-space constraint could be maintained in a conventional database system without the storage of additional temporal and spatial information if certain conditions were satisfied.

Another difference in the semantics of time and space is in the nature of the dimensions. The two dimensions typically considered for time are valid time and transaction time. The former describes when the data was valid in the real world, and the latter describes when it was captured in the database.

On the other hand, the two (or three) dimensions usually captured for space relate to the horizontal geometry (or horizontal and vertical geometry) of the data. These are more closely related to valid time and not transaction time (because they do not deal with where the data was captured in the database). A concept akin to transaction time may prove useful in a spatial context for a distributed database environment. Here, changes are made to multiple local copies of the database and then propagated to the rest of the system. We can then define the begin point of transaction space as the location where the data was entered and the end point where it was deleted. Once again, however, we notice a difference with time in that there is no linear order between the begin point and the end point for data. As such, the region where we believed the fact to be true would depend on the propagation policy of the distributed database management system. Also, as is evident from the description, transaction space is not a purely spatial concept, rather it is a time-varying spatial concept.

## 3.2. Spatiotemporal Interaction Relationships

An interaction relationship (IR) relates members of one entity class to members of one or more entity classes. The spatiotemporal equivalent of an interaction relationship is defined as: $R \subseteq I R \times T \times S ,$ where T and S represent the domains of time and space. Let $M =$ $\{ C _ { 1 } , { \overset { \cdot } { C } } _ { 2 } , \dots , C _ { n } \}$ be the set of entity classes participating in the relationship. This assumes that an entity class plays a single role in R. If not, we replace M with $\bar { L } \doteq \{ L _ { 1 } , \ldots , \bar { L } _ { k } \}$ , where L denotes roles played by each entity class. While the interaction relationship exists at all times and places relevant to the database, a given relationship instance r will be associated with a temporal element $t ^ { r } \in \mathbf { P } ( T )$ and spatial element $s ^ { r } \in$ P4S5, describing when and where it exists in modeled reality (note: $\bar { \bf P ( } T )$ denotes the power set of $T ,$ and a temporal element is a set itself).

The symbol $R ^ { \prime }$ stands for a set of one or more spatiotemporal interaction relationship tuples, $R ^ { \prime } \subseteq R$ The letter t stands for a time frame of interest. For a sequenced constraint (evaluated at each point in time), we let $t \in T$ . For a nonsequenced constraint (evaluated over a window of time), we set $t \in \mathbf { P } ( T ) ,$ which allows t to map to a time window (collection of points). Correspondingly, for space we will use s for the spatial window of interest. The degree of the relationship R is the number of participating entity classes and is denoted by the symbol n. A participating entity class in R is referred to by: $C _ { i } ; 1 \overset { - } { \le } i \overset { - } { \le } n .$

The temporal element and spatial element corresponding to the subtuple will be the same as that for the tuple. Role projection $\pi _ { L } ^ { R } ~ ( r , c , t , s )$ is defined as: $R \times \bar { C } \times T \times S \bar { \to } \bar { \mathbf { P } ( L ) }$ . It takes as input a relationship instance $r ,$ an entity $c ,$ a time window $t ,$ a space window $s ,$ and returns the set of roles that the entity c plays in the instance r during the temporal and spatial window of interest.

## 3.3. Implementation Considerations for the Framework Artifact

The implementation of constraints can be done via database-level triggers and procedures to allow for cross-application enforcement (although in practice these are sometimes written into the application layer). A classification of constraint types gives the database administrator an a priori sense of the corresponding efficiency of the constraint categories (inferred based on the nature of spatiotemporal aggregation that needs to be performed, López et al. 2005). Furthermore, if translation logic is built into CASE tools, we gain productivity (by automating trigger writing) and reduce the likelihood of errors in generated code.

Earlier in this section we described six temporal and spatial evaluation windows and four kinds of relationship cardinality. Based on the interaction between these two aspects of the framework, the translation of each kind of constraint into database triggers is different but follows the same basic approach. We describe the general strategy for the translation and illustrate with an example. A database trigger is usually fired by a database event (insert, update, or delete). To prevent constraint violations, we need triggers fired before the database event takes place. A minimum cardinality specification of >0 implies the need for a delete trigger on the corresponding relation, and a maximum cardinality of <M requires an insert trigger. If the constraints contain predicates or if primary key values can change, then an update trigger must be used. A standard approach can be used when generating SQL code to check the constraint based on (a) the constraint type (e.g., participation or co-occurrence) and (b) the windows of evaluation and applicability bounds. For example, a participation constraint (maximum) of the form CARD-R-PT (rel, EClass) with a temporal window of a week converts to: SELECT count(<sup>∗</sup>) from rel table where EClass key-column = new-tuple · EClass key-column and date column >= systemdate - 7. If the count( ) is over the cardinality limit, the trigger performs actions to manage the violation.<sup>5</sup>

We recognize that some translation aspects may continue to require human inputs such as managing how spatial and temporal data is stored, deciding locking granularities for tables, and what action to take upon violation of the constraint. The action usually takes the form of preventing the database operation that violates the constraint (hard or restrictive constraints), or permitting the operation but taking other actions to address it (soft or prescriptive constraints). Implementing hard constraints is usually straightforward, but for soft constraints two different kinds of actions can be taken: issuing warnings or generating further database updates to redress the problem (corrective action). Administrator intervention is usually needed because clients may want warnings in the form of error messages in the user interface, or entries in a log, or some other option. These issues are discussed in much more depth in active database literature. Research also exists in modeling the effect of constraint violations at the conceptual design phase (Balaban and Shoval 2002). We leave the development of this matter for future work.

## 4. Evaluation of the Framework

Artifact evaluation is an integral part of the design science methodology (Hevner et al. 2004). Our efforts to maintain research rigor include formal definition of the constraint semantics, careful design of the case study, and theoretical support for the completeness of the cardinality component of the framework (relative to relational algebra; described in previous research in Currim and Ram 2006). Additional details of our adherence to design science guidelines are in Online Appendix 1. A good conceptual grammar is said to possesses properties of simplicity, minimality, formality, and expressiveness (Batini et al. 1992). Constraint types contained in a conceptual grammar should also satisfy these properties. Another dimension of interest is that of usefulness. Simplicity is satisfied when a schema annotated with the framework-defined constraints is understandable to database analysts (the users of the framework). We conducted a laboratory study on novice database designers (graduate students) expected to have the most difficulty with a new framework. The study involved 55 subjects and used a between-subjects two-level quasiexperimental design. The results showed that performance improved (in terms of time taken and number of correctly identified rules) when using the framework, but there was a small but statistically significant adverse effect on perceived ease of use. However, this is to be expected because the framework adds a layer of complexity over a modeling grammar. Novice subjects also found the framework a useful aid in coming up with questions (to ask the client) while gathering constraints. Additional details are available in the dissertation work by one of the authors (Currim 2004). We achieve formality (i.e., a precise and well-defined meaning for each constraint type) by specifying constraint types using first-order logic (FOL), in §3. Minimality, ensuring that each constraint type is different and nonredundant, was tested by examining the semantics expressed in FOL and verifying that each constraint type had a distinct definition. We analyze expressiveness and usefulness in more detail with a case study presented in the remainder of this section.

## 4.1. Background and Objective

Expressiveness of conceptual modeling languages is described by Batini et al. (1992) as the ability to comprehensively represent the real world. Chaves and Carvalho (1996) define expressiveness as the extent to which a conceptual modeling language can represent reality. Other authors term this as completeness, or the capacity of a data model to meet user information and functional requirements (Moody and Shanks 1994). In other words, it is the degree to which a modeling language contains the elements (constructs) needed to represent the system(s) it wishes to support (Mantha 1987). We prefer the term expressiveness to completeness, because the latter suggests a sense of absoluteness. These descriptions by authors refer to the expressiveness of a conceptual language as a whole, but we feel the same principles are relevant to a constraints framework based on a conceptual modeling language. We do not capture every possible type of rule and confine ourselves to spatiotemporal cardinality constraints (STCCs). Accordingly, we operationalize expressiveness as the ratio of STCCs expressible by our framework/STCCs in case requirements. Interestingly, for conceptual grammars, Moody and Shanks (Moody and Shanks 1994) state, “An important aspect of completeness is the representation of business rules in the data model”

(p. 103). To that end, our framework augments the expressiveness of existing snapshot and spatiotemporal modeling grammars (which adds to framework usefulness). Our objective in the case study is to test if our framework was sufficiently expressive to capture spatiotemporal constraints and also examine its usefulness.

## 4.2. Case Study: Institutional Review Board

Case study as a research approach is well-established in the management information systems (MIS) research community (Benbasat et al. 1987, Cavaye 1996, Lee 1989). A number of prior papers evaluating conceptual grammars advocate moving beyond the laboratory setting to field studies and real-world testing. For example, both Batra et al. (1990) and Kim and March (1995) recommend that future work should include field studies and actual applications. A field setting can improve the external validity of studies evaluating conceptual modeling languages (Palvia et al. 1992). Strong recommendations have been made (Bajaj 1997, Khatri 2002) for case research in the context of measuring expressiveness. Because ours is a new framework and we wanted to evaluate it in a real-world setting, we felt that a case study examining a database development effort was appropriate.

Establishing the unit of analysis is an important step in designing the case study. Benbasat et al. (1987) recommend it be done prior to searching for sites. We set the target size as an organizational unit, because that was sufficiently large to provide complex constraints, yet not so large that the magnitude of the modeling overshadowed the research goal of testing the framework. Prior to seeking a case, we also considered what other properties besides scale would help to make a good field setting for us. In our situation, we thought the ideal organization would be one for which policies and rules were critical and one that was engaged in developing a new information system (and be willing to let us perform the study). We chose the institutional review board (IRB) at a major public university. Given its role in assessing university research projects involving human subjects, it provided a good setting because the failure of controls leads to serious consequences for research practice (e.g., the suspension of all federally supported medical research at Johns Hopkins University in 2001, Steinbrook 2002). It was also a suitable opportunity for a study because the IRB was developing a new system to support their operations and was open to our conducting a study. This gave us access to existing documents and staff member time and also allowed us first-hand observation of the database design efforts. Overall, we felt the IRB satisfied the requirement of providing sufficient breadth and depth of data (Darke et al. 1998) to answer our research questions.

The systems analysis and design effort underway at the IRB was conducted by a team of five doctoral- and masters-level MIS students (with prior modeling experience) under the supervision of a faculty member. To avoid influencing their design, we did not train the analysts in modeling spatiotemporal cardinality constraints. Instead, one of the authors joined them as an observer. In keeping with case study methodology recommendations, multiple data collection sources were used, such as the IRB website and sample reports for the different IRB project categories. Documents included review forms, investigator guidelines, problem-reporting documents, deviation and noncompliance forms, and subject consent forms for behavioral and biomedical studies. The main policy manual was more than 500 pages long, so we spent more time on the sections that affected the system and constraints. We also looked at documentation generated by the analysts. The purpose behind using multiple sources is to achieve triangulation and improve construct validity because multiple sources provide multiple measures of the same phenomenon (Yin 2002). About 12 hours of interviews were conducted by the analysts. Questions included a description of the interviewee’s responsibilities, the roles of people who interacted with them, how the process of reviewing projects worked, and the data captured for projects under review. At the end of the data collection process, the analysts came up with a natural language description of the requirements (recapped in Online Appendix 2). This was developed into a conceptual schema with snapshot cardinalities listed on the diagram (Figure 3; significant attributes are in Appendix B).

After the schema was developed, the authors took a more active role in the next stage where validation interviews were conducted to test the correctness and completeness of the requirements description. We used this time to discuss our project with the analysts and gather additional information about spatiotemporal rules from the organization. The design team had discovered some part of 27 spatial and temporal cardinality rules during requirements analysis, but fewer than half were discussed in their data dictionary and implementation notes. In several cases, only the snapshot version of the constraint was mentioned in the dictionary. In other instances, some rules were limited to a passing reference in the interview notes. Furthermore, a few constraints were left out of the conceptual modeling script although the analysts were aware of their existence. We tried to understand why this happened, and responses from the analysts included “I didn’t know what to do with that” [in ER] and “it does not belong in the ER, I will use Java” [to handle it]. Without a framework, the analysts did not understand the constraints and were not able to explicitly elicit and represent them, nor were they able to group them into categories of any kind. Instead, most of the constraints were postponed to be dealt with later as ad hoc programming tasks.

Once we presented our framework, the team members felt that they were able to make the connection between the policy and corresponding constraint components (evaluation window, applicability bound), as well as to understand what semantics needed to be sought out when augmenting a conventional constraint: “We were mostly looking at the main ER [earlier]. All of the gathered constraints could be modeled using our framework. Further, we came up with additional rules based on notes on hand as extensions of rules gathered in the previous stage, e.g., how many values a project’s status attribute could take on over a period of time as opposed to at a point in time. We also gathered completely new rules (e.g., flags to be triggered when the number of violations from lowrisk projects exceeded a certain amount) that had not been written down in the first stage. At the end of this phase, we added constraint annotations to the data dictionary that were included in the final version. An independent reviewer (not involved in the analysis, but knowledgeable about database design) was requested to verify that the natural language requirements gathered by the completed design effort were fully represented by the schema and annotations.

## 4.4. Further Discussion on Usefulness

As mentioned earlier, we found (through a lab study) that the framework increased the number of rules discovered by novice analysts. Many organizations cannot afford the expertise of elite-level consultants or developers, yet have rules that play an important part of their operational success. Over the course of time, additional constraints may mean the difference between smooth operation of an organization and sanctions due to oversight or human error. Building these constraints into an operational system is superior to relying on a system of human checks and balances. To this end, using a framework during requirements analysis improves the likelihood of surfacing constraints (e.g., the rules uncovered in the second phase of the case). Similar to a best-practices list, it provides a reference point against which to check for constraints and thus serves as a preventive measure against possible missed policies. In a business context where rules are critical, any mechanism that reduces chance omissions is a better option in the long run than relying on transient organizational memory. We argue that such frameworks that combine an expressive set of rules and allow for a straightforward translation mechanism into triggers help analysts who wish to model constraints in early design stages and save on repeating pseudocode by providing standard abstractions for the rules.

## 4.5. Assumptions and Limitations

A limitation of the case study was that a large number of policies (and consequently constraints) were temporal in nature. Although we initially felt there would be a higher number of spatial rules (e.g., with respect to project sites), we could not predict the actual number in advance. We were pleased to find that the case contained rules on five of the six construct types (with composite classes being the only category not present in the study). Future case studies with more constraint diversity would need to be conducted to improve external validity. Another suggestion for future cases is to conduct pilot studies as recommended by Dubé and Paré (2003).

We observe in §4 that adding a layer over the ER grammar to capture spatiotemporal constraints leads to additional complexity and a consequent drop in the perceived ease of use. Prior research has shown that a significant reason why analysts do not use available modeling techniques (e.g., class diagrams) is that grammar components are not well understood (Dobing and Parsons 2006) by them. This indicates a need for appropriate framework training prior to analyst use. We leave for future research the empirical evaluation of training levels, usability, perceived usefulness, and framework adoption.

Our framework is based on the widely used entityrelationship approach. A number of competing standards exist, including UML and object-role modeling (ORM). We feel the classification framework for constraints can be extended to the other grammars with appropriate modifications but have not actually implemented or evaluated this and leave such an effort for future work.

## 4.6. Summary

To summarize, our framework makes it possible to elicit and explicitly model rules in a standardized manner leading to visibility of representation and guiding the implementation of constraints. Although temporal rules formed the majority of the nonsnapshot policies in our case (29 of the 42), we feel that is probably representative of the real world where temporal data is more prevalent than spatial data. We found the framework to be expressive in the context of a university IRB and useful to explicitly specify rules. The standardized SQL translation for the logical design phase helped improve productivity. Although we did not have a CASE tool to automatically generate the code, the presence of such a tool would reduce human programming errors. Thus, we feel that using the framework was an effective way to identify rules with spatiotemporal characteristics.

## 5. Conclusions and Future Work

It is important to encapsulate business rules at the conceptual design phase. Nonetheless, currently only a subset of these constraints are captured at this stage. Part of the reason is that no standardized method of annotating conceptual models with business rules exists. Our work makes a contribution in this area by providing a framework for understanding and capturing spatiotemporal set-based constraints. Previously, we briefly introduced the notions of evaluation windows and applicability bounds with intuitive examples (Currim and Ram 2008). This paper discussed the concepts in detail, including how they can be applied to cardinality to create a spatiotemporal constraint framework. We formally specified the semantics of each type of constraint using first-order logic (§3) and highlighted differences in how time and space extend snapshot constraints. We evaluated the framework for expressiveness and usefulness with a case study and communicated our findings in terms of improvement in design and operating procedures for the organization (§4). Using the classification scheme presented by the framework, a database analyst can elicit requirements in a structured manner (reducing the likelihood of missed constraints; significant any time a policy is critical to the business function or integrity of the system), improve her understanding of the nature of the constraints, and specify precise rules that can then be translated into logical design constraints in an automated fashion for productivity gains. A side effect of our framework in the area of cardinality is in providing a reference model to resolve the differing semantics of constraints presented in earlier work—particularly for ternary and higher-order relationships—via mapping to our framework. Our paper thus provides a structured way to classify constraints with spatial and temporal components. Although we demonstrate space and time solely with respect to ER cardinality, the formalisms of evaluation windows and applicability bounds and their properties presented here can be used for different kinds of constraints and also with conventional, temporal, spatial, and spatiotemporal grammars.

We feel that a useful direction of future work involves testing spatiotemporal concepts of windows and bounds on other types of rules. Various semantic constraint types have been identified in addition to cardinality, for example, repetition and ordering (Rundensteiner et al. 1991) and attribute value domains (Hammer and McLeod 1981). Repetition and ordering are relevant for conceptual models that consider bag semantics and list-based semantics, respectively. Although not applicable in the ER context, these are interesting for object-oriented approaches. Attribute value domains have previously been studied for conventional semantic modeling techniques (Halpin 2001, Hammer and McLeod 1981, Ram 1995). A detailed examination of these constraints for spatiotemporal grammars is likely to be fruitful. Other kinds of constraints that were beyond the scope of our current work included transition constraints (those that affect changes from one database state to another) and constraints that spanned multiple constructs (e.g., cross-relationship 9different domains, experiments to determine the impact of alternative constraint representation methods, a comparison of analyst perceptions before and after using the framework, efforts in constraint conflict detection, and the development of a prototype incorporating the framework within a CASE tool.
