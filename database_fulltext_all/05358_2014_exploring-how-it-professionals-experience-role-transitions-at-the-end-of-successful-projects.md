# Exploring How IT Professionals Experience Role Transitions at the End of Successful Projects

Line Dubé

Line Dubé is a professor of management information systems at HEC Montréal. She holds a Ph.D. in business administration from Florida International University. Her current research focuses on different facets of IT project management and mainly exploits the richness of qualitative methods. Her work has been published in many journals, such as Information Technology & People, MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Organizational Change Management, Information Systems Journal, Gestion, and Journal of Information Science. She is the author of many IT teaching cases published in the International Journal of Case Studies in Management, three of which have won the annual best case award. Dr. Dubé is a member of the editorial board of Information and Organization.

Abstra ct: In an increasingly fluid working environment, workers often find themselves in a state of transition and must be capable of adapting to rapid changes. This study focuses on intrafirm temporary role transitions, and more specifically on the case of information technology (IT) professionals transitioning out of a successful project and returning to their functional unit. In-depth interviews were conducted with 19 IT professionals. All of the respondents reported that they had experienced an adaptation period, albeit minor for some. Others, however, felt an important re-entry shock. A shock of high magnitude is mainly experienced when the successful project becomes a referent to which all other work assignments are compared. This idealized project work environment is the result of the decisions top management makes about the project structure, management, and governance. The results show that all people do not react the same way to a shock of high magnitude: some either adapt or change their new role, but others resist. This study highlights the need to better understand role transitions by further investigating the mod erating variables at play in the relationships between actor, experience, magnitude of shock, and reaction. The study contributes to practice by questioning the widely shared assumption that IT professionals effortlessly navigate between project and functional work environments, and by highlighting the need to consider successful projects as a potential source of turmoil for team members. Finally, it raises the question of where the responsibility of managing transitions lies in the organization.

Key word s a nd phra ses: IT professionals, IT projects, job satisfaction, project governance, project management, qualitative study, role theory, role transition, socialization.

A fa st-cha nging a nd highl y competitive environment forces organizations to reinvent themselves and often to add more flexible execution arrangements to their existing hierarchical, multilayer, and functionally organized structure. They now tend to adopt a portfolio of structural configurations (e.g., project, strong and weak matrices) and functional and governance structures (e.g., authority structure and autonomy) that are adapted to the objectives of the situation at hand. This increasingly fluid working environment requires a workforce that is highly agile and efficient, as workers who often find themselves in a state of transition (from one project to another, from a functional unit to a project, or from a project back to a functional unit) must be capable of adapting to rapid changes. Because challenging transitions can lead to counterproductive outcomes, such as job dissatisfaction, anxiety, and frustration, building and retaining such a workforce requires an increased understanding of how these transitions are experienced. This is especially true of skilled information technology (IT) professionals where demands for flexibility and agility are especially high [27]. The widely adopted matrix organization, in all its variants, also forces workers to navigate between functional units and projects, with varying degrees of coupling and coordination. This dynamic is further complicated by projects with high levels of organizational and IT complexity [48], shorter project durations, constant technological changes, unrealistic deadlines, and long hours, all of which exert considerable pressure [45] and further increasing the demands of the working environment of IT professionals.

These transitions can be advantageously conceived as work role transitions. Role theory describes the organization as a set of patterned activities performed by individuals [22]. A work role is a combination of tasks, activities, perceived expectations, and preferences [8]. A transition is better conceived as a boundary-crossing activity in which one exits and enters roles by surmounting boundaries [3, 39]. When IT professionals go from functional unit work environments to project work environments, they need to exit their functional unit role to adopt a project work role and vice versa.

While macro role transitions—entering the workforce, job loss due to downsizing, changing employer, retirement, returning after an illness or parental leave, and so on (e.g., [13])—have received a lot of attention in the literature, intrafirm lateral moves and transitions, even permanent ones such as a promotion, have received less attention [44], likely because they are considered less difficult since they happen within the same organizational context [2]. The end result is that little is known about how employees navigate intraorganizational role transitions and the challenges they face, especially when one considers transitions within the context of temporary assignments such as those related to project work.

Researchers have barely started to look at the impact of the agile work environment on project members. A few studies have shown that project failure may have lasting effects on team members (e.g., [4, 20, 42]). Feelings of inadequacy, self-doubt, resentment, even grief and anger are only a few of the emotions sometimes experienced by team members. Most of the research has strived to identify the factors that explain the magnitude of the negative responses or those that affect the possibility to draw lessons from the experience. In an IT project context, Iacovou and Dexter’s [20] findings suggest that team members be offered meaningful reintegration assignments while noting the importance for project leaders to provide members with a means to express their feelings and access to counseling, if needed. It is doubtful that these recommendations are followed. Indeed, as discussed by Asquin et al. [4] and Turner et al. [45], project-oriented organizations are generally not dedicated to caring for their employees’ well-being.

While there is a clear need for a better understanding of how to manage project aftermath, these research streams’ first steps highlight the completely overlooked opposite issue of how team members react to the successful completion of a project and to the transition that follows. In light of the above, the study offer preliminary answers to the three following research questions:

RQ1: Are transitions out of successful projects effortless?

RQ2: Who is likely to experience challenging transitions?

RQ3: How do organizational and/or project characteristics impact transition experiences?

To answer these questions, we adopted an inductive qualitative approach. In-depth interviews were conducted with 19 IT professionals that had just transitioned out of a large, successful IT project. While it may be counterintuitive to think that project success may have intense effects on team members, the results of this study show that a successful project becomes a referent to which all other work assignments are then compared. A shock of high magnitude is then experienced if the project work environment and the functional unit work environment are significantly different.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The next section presents the relevant literature that can help us understand work transitions. The research method is then presented, followed by a presentation and a discussion of the results. Finally, a conclusion wraps up the paper with a discussion of the implications of the results for research and for practice. A presentation of the limitations of the study and a discussion of some avenues for future research are also included.

## Understanding Temporary Work Role Transitions

## Role Transitions as Part of the Project Life Cycle

With the project ma na gement l itera ture as a vast repertoire of best practices, transitions can be seen as part of the project management life cycle. There are countless books and articles that offer advice on team building and socialization, but the project management literature offers less guidance on the management of the last phase of the project—project closure. Despite the fact that most project management textbooks (e.g., [31]) and some more focused articles (e.g., [16, 19, 35]) all recognize the existence and the importance of this project phase, the advice offered is quite generic. As an example, the latest version of the Project Management Institute’s Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge says only that a staff release plan should be integrated into the staffing management plan [36].

While the issue of transferring the team members back to their original unit is sometimes raised and the anxiety that it may create is occasionally acknowledged (e.g., [16, 31]), nothing, or very little, is said about how it should be managed or about the specific challenges transitioning people may encounter. For example, the anxiety it might create is seen as potentially leading to dysfunctional project behaviors such as conflicts, loss of interest, retirement while still on the job, or lifetime tenure [31].

Overall, strictly from a project management perspective, the focus is on how a timely staff release contributes to reduced project costs and how a smooth transition reduces the risks associated with human resources (HR) at the end of the project.

## Role Transitions as the Responsibility of the Functional Unit

If the project as a unit of production is more preoccupied by its own boundary and short-term objectives, the resulting transitions can be viewed as the responsibility of the functional unit. The socialization process—or how people acquire new skills, beliefs systems, patterns of action, and sometimes personal identities—has been extensively studied [5]. Orientation programs, training, informal activities, mentoring, shadowing, and so on, are all practices used by organizations to ignite performance. This stream of research and the resulting socialization practices along with their relevance or level of performance are by and large essentially geared toward new employees entering a new organizational setting [11, 38]. Overall, the literature shows that organizations are not well equipped to manage transitions, especially when it comes to socializing people that are already members of the organization, which can be conceived as a resocialization process [38].

The literature on expatriation is one clear example. Quite a lot is known about international assignments, their challenges, and how organizations can effectively manage expatriation. However, much less is known about managing a successful repatriation, which often routinely culminates with repatriates leaving their organizations [23, 34]. While the context is different, the passage from a project to a functional unit can be conceived as an employee returning home after a temporary outside assignment. Even if the organization is theoretically the same, the functional unit could have changed significantly during the course of the temporary assignment and the now “changed” employee is returning to a “changed” functional unit. Overall, the literature shows that practitioners and researchers alike show great interest in the need to socially integrate new employees, whereas the case of transitioning employees does not seem to be high on the list of priorities. As Feldman and Brett [12] noted, job changers within organizations are expected to “hit the floor running” without much support.

## Changes in Temporary Job Assignments as Work Role Transitions

Work role transition theories provide some basic concepts for understanding the type of transition in which we are interested. Roles are different because they have different identities that rely on differing sets of goals, values, beliefs, norms, interaction styles, and time horizons [2]. Enacting a role in a project environment may be quite different from carrying out a role in a functional unit. In the case of an IT professional, even if the role may require a basic set of technical or managerial competences, this set may be enacted differently in diverse work environments, because it may be ingrained in a larger context where goals, values, beliefs, norms, interaction styles, time horizons, and even expectations are different.

When an individual goes from one role to another, a role transition takes place. A transition requires a disengagement from one role (role exit) and an engagement into another role (role entry) [6]. Two types of role transitions are usually acknowledged: the macro role transition and the micro role transition [2]. A macro role transition entails infrequent and permanent change; it often refers to important changes such as a promotion or changing employers. Micro role transitions are defined as the frequent and recurring transitions an individual experiences many times on a daily basis: the individual plays the role of boss when talking to an employee, that of an employee when talking with his or her own boss, and becomes a parent when the phone rings and his or her child is on the other end of the line, all within the same half-hour.

Navigating from project to project and from project to functional unit and vice versa is neither a micro role nor a macro role transition.<sup>1</sup> There is no expectation of permanence in project work, as is the case with macro transitions. All team members know that they are going back to their functional unit upon project completion. Once there, they know that they may stay in the functional unit for a while or be assigned to another project right away, even if the time period is not clearly determined. These transitions do not meet the criteria of micro role transitions either. While micro role transitions are recurring, the transitions we are interested in do not occur many times daily or even weekly.<sup>2</sup> There is no expectation of simultaneous role playing. This is why we have defined this type of transition as an intrafirm temporary work role transition.

While the existence of temporary roles has been acknowledged, little research has focused on such transitions [2]. Ashforth [2] hypothesizes that people are not likely to identify much with a temporary role because it is time-limited, but he goes on to explain that some conditions such as valence, salience, and opportunities for social validation may cause a different outcome. With regard to valence, if gains and social desirability are high, a person is thought to be more likely to adopt the temporary role identity. Or if the group treats the person as if he or she is the legitimate holder of the new identity (social validation), the person will also be more likely to adopt the temporary role. However, Ashforth [2] argues that organizations’ members tend to invest little or no time or effort in people in temporary positions, which will deny the temporary holder of any type of social validation.

Role transitions, whether macro or micro roles, may be difficult. Nicholson [33] proposes that negative emotions may influence the selected mode of adjustment and the adjustment outcome. Ashforth [2] ascertains that the magnitude of the transition is defined by role segmentation, as defined by the level of flexibility, permeability, and contrast between role identities. Since flexibility (the ability to easily go from one role to another and vice versa) and permeability (the state of being in two roles at the same time) are impossible in the context of the type of transition under study here, contrast needs to be closely monitored. If the contrast (differences in goals, values, norms, etc.) between two roles is high, chances are that the magnitude of the transition will be high.

The literature seems to imply that intrafirm transitions are less difficult because of the minimal role contrast created by a homogeneous organizational context [2, 26]. However, some authors acknowledge that organizations, especially large ones, can be highly differentiated [30, 39]. This may be more evident in the case of large projects where members of many units and even external partners need to merge into a coherent subculture to work effectively. Even if the magnitude of the transition is lower than one involving a transition from one organization to another, this does not mean that they do not bring about challenges that should be ignored. Dunford et al. [9] found that internal job changers are good candidates for burnout interventions after about one year into their new job, the moment when they are the most at risk, and suggest that individuals need about two years to reach equilibrium after a job change. Dunford et al. recommend that “promotions or transfers within the organization should be timed so employees have time to adjust before experiencing additional change” [9, p. 647]. Such observations raise serious concerns about the effects project work transitions may have on IT professionals who experience a series of changes in relatively short periods of time.

In accordance with our inductive approach where literature is first and foremost an orienting process [46], the literature on role and work role transitions offers interesting concepts, such as role entry, role exit, contrast, and magnitude of transition that will be used to examine transitions.

## Research Method

## General Research Context

The resea rch process d id not util ize a trad itional a pproa ch, but rather grasped an opportunity to study an emergent phenomenon. Originally, the research project aimed to understand how a very large, time-crunched, multipartnered, and operation-critical IT project had achieved success despite important challenges and risks. We were intrigued and motivated by the large consensus that existed in the business community that this project had been a total success. The initial research design called for a qualitative case study approach where extensive interviews would be conducted with key players and a sample of team members. We wanted to explore in detail what had made this project so successful and sought a rich telling of the participants’ experiences. The participants’ interview guide covered the entire project life cycle: How had they been approached to participate in the project (former unit and role)? At what point had they joined? What were their responsibilities? What were their perceptions of the project’s outcomes? What were the challenges and success factors from their perspective? At what date had they left the project, and what had they done since? The overall objective was to allow the respondents to talk freely about their experiences in this specific project from their own unique perspective.

Early in the research process, the cyclical nature of “data collection” and “data analysis” inherent in a qualitative study [32, 43] rapidly led to the realization that the project had varied repercussions for its team members when the project finally ended in all its glory. The process was completely inductive: The interview guide did not include any direct questions about the transition experience, but some participants raised the issue without prompting. After the issue was raised by a third participant, we were taken aback: How can such a success create such turmoil? Although the issue seemed pivotal to the respondents, a literature review showed that we did not know much about the effects of temporary work assignments and transitions on people. It was at this point that the research questions, presented above, became the main focus of this research. We already had an answer to the first research question; the data showed that transitions out of a successful project may be quite challenging. The quest for an understanding of the phenomenon was launched. The research approach was adjusted: The project was not the case anymore; each interviewed participant would be viewed as an individual case studied within the same project and organizational context. At this point, we reflected long and hard on the pertinence of altering the interview guide, but wary of prompting responses or emotions from the respondents (social desirability bias),<sup>3</sup> we decided against it. If the phenomenon was important and pervasive, it would come up during the interviews, as it had earlier. And in many cases, it did. Only when a respondent raised the issue, did we ask questions to obtain his or her perspective in full.

## Research Setting

This large organization had just completed a large and complex 18-month IT project that would set the basis for a new and improved IT infrastructure. The project required the collaboration of many technological partners and, at its peak, required the work of more than 400 IT professionals. Because of its importance, all eyes were on the project.

Studying the experiences of participants transitioning out of a homogeneous project context minimizes the influence of external variables beyond the phenomenon of interest [10]. This allowed an exploration of participants’ variations while controlling for the organization and the project contexts.

## Data Collection

Data collection, which was conducted over a seven-month period, started two months after the project had ended. The organization provided the researcher with a list of all the participants and granted unlimited permission to communicate freely and confidentially with any of the team members. All the members, who were contacted by telephone or e-mail, accepted the invitation to participate. Most had been out of the project for a minimum of two months to a maximum of about nine months (depending on the timing of the interview), a period both short enough so that retrospective accounts were deemed to be exact and long enough for the raw emotions to be diffused and a reflective process to be under way. Considering the initial and emergent objectives of the study, 29 interviews were conducted (with 27 people), and over 2,200 minutes of interviews with stakeholders from all levels directly and indirectly involved in the project led to a rich understanding of the project and its context. We started with the key players, but then expanded into a random and snowball approach. We strove for a balanced representation of hierarchal levels, genders, organizational units, and areas of responsibility in the project. All the interviews were conducted by the researcher.

The analysis of these documents allowed us to better ground the project within its organizational context and was used to corroborate the hard facts about the project.

## Data Analysis

Beca use there is no singl e correct wa y to a nal yze qual ita tive da ta [25, 43, 47], rigorous analysis derives from the “principled” development of a strategy suited for the scenario under study. The data analysis relied on the basic premises of content analysis [47], grounded theory [43], interview analysis [25], and the use of data reduction methods [32].

We began by coding the project context and characteristics. The content of all the interviews was used to make an extensive list of all the project characteristics and, when possible, triangulated the end results with the documents.

The next step was a data reduction process. Following the “role entry” and “role exit” logic (before the project, during the project, after the project), a sequential approach was used to extract the experience of each participant and, from each interview, extracted data about the “before-project experience,” the “during-project experience,” the “transition experience,” and the “after-project experience.” Cut and pasted into a table, the text segments were lengthy and contained sufficient information to include the explanations and the magnitude of the difficulties. We had to go back to the original transcripts only on a few occasions. This table was then the basis for the rest of the analysis.

The first task was to categorize the magnitude of the shock experienced by each respondent. Through a careful analysis of the words used by each respondent, it was possible to rank order them. The two extremes—shock of low and high magnitude— stood out right away. We then carefully analyzed the “in the middle category” (neither low nor high) but determined that no further refinement was necessary. They could all be relevantly categorized as shock of medium magnitude.

In addition to the shock magnitude, the repercussions of the shock were analyzed. Hence, the “transition experience” and the “after-project experience” data were used to classify each respondent’s experience. Three groups quickly emerged: one with few or no repercussions, a second that was clearly in a “wait-and-see” mode hoping something would happen soon, and a third whose members were not able to stay put (were actively looking for new challenges or had already made some changes).

Then we wanted to understand what had caused the observed shock. It was clear during all the interviews that the shock was rooted in the perceived differences between the project environment and the current work environment. But we wanted to deepen the analysis: What was so different? How was the project environment different from the current work environment? So, following the advice to construct a system of categories gradually [25, 43], the differences between the two environments were extracted and grouped into more general categories based on their similarities. For example, many text segments were about the CIO. They clearly conveyed the idea that the respondents felt visible and important while participating in this project and felt their work was appreciated; this seems to be in direct opposition to their situation in their functional unit.

Finally, many respondents discussed what might have helped ease the challenging transition period. We inventoried their comments and have included them in the results section. At each step of the data reduction process and analysis process, we documented the chain of evidence linking data collection to conclusions [32, 49].

## Results

The presenta tion of the resul ts is orga nized a round the three distinct phases designed to understand the participants’ experiences. The data show a clear sequence of unanimous exhilaration (during the project itself), followed by a re-entry shock, and its aftermath. We describe each of these three phases in turn, followed by an analysis of the sources of the shock and a listing of a few elements the respondents thought could have improved their transition.

## A Special Project Environment and a Profound Feeling of Professional Achievement

The respondents all thrived in the project environment. Despite the intensity and long hours, the high uncertainty and the high complexity, and the tremendous pressure, the respondents unanimously talked about the project as the highlight of their professional career. Even if going back to more normal working hours had been a welcome change of pace (mostly because of family obligations), the respondents all said that they would have liked to continue working in such an environment and most wished that the project had never ended. The following are typical responses:

Today, if I had to do it all over again, the chances are 200 percent that I would say “yes.” I enjoyed the experience that much! (Respondent 15)

We [my group] started this project telling ourselves that it was going to be crazy, that it was going to be chaotic, but that we were going to have some fun along the way. And that’s what we did. We had fun, a whole lot of fun! (Respondent 13)

Why was this experience so pleasant and memorable? We made an inventory of all the project characteristics (structural, organizational, and managerial) that explain the high appreciation team members had for their project experience.

At the project’s onset, team members were carefully hand-picked. After an analysis of the tasks to be accomplished, the required specialists from each IT unit were identified and asked to join the project on a full-time basis. A fair image of the work and the challenges to come (as confirmed by the respondents) was offered and people were invited to decline if they could not fit into this demanding environment:

I wasn’t forced to participate, but it was an offer I couldn’t refuse. My interest was just too strong: the opportunity to be part of such a project, to stand out, to show them what we were able to do. It was just too tempting. (Respondent 2)

Following the process of natural selection that occurred at first (a few people left after a couple of weeks), the final team was finally formed. The general rumor was that the best of the best of the organization had been selected (an element that was profusely repeated to the researcher), which flattered the team members’ personal and collective egos, but also created resentment among those left behind.

Decisions that shaped the project structure, management, and culture were made (see Table 2). An external project manager was hired and, because of a lack of adequate square footage, physical space was rented in another building. Together these decisions brought about a physical separation of the project team from the rest of the organization, but also erected a psychological wall between the two. As requested when he accepted the job, complete autonomy was given to the project manager to manage the project’s internal work processes. A very flat project structure was implemented. Close face-to-face collaboration and fast decision making were the order of the day. More importantly, all of the project processes were evaluated in real time. If something was causing a problem or unnecessary delays, it was redesigned on the spot and tested, until satisfactory.

All the project interfaces with standardized organizational processes (planning, reporting, procurement, quality control, communication, HR, etc.) were negotiated with the support unit involved and streamlined for quick information sharing and fast decision making. These decisions resulted in a very strong, efficient, and result-oriented project environment that people identified with and in which they thrived.

## The Reentry Shock

For the majority of the respondents, the end of the project brought both joy and apprehension. On the one hand, team members were proud of their accomplishments and the level of success the project had attained. On the other hand, the last few weeks on the project, especially after one of the most critical implementations had been successfully executed, were important sources of anxiety since the respondents had been away from their functional unit for a long time, for many with little or no contact. Team members had been openly encouraged to stay focused on project activities and to cut any outside distractions or possible interferences from the rest of the organization. The project work had been so intense and required so many long hours that most of the participants had had no inclination to deviate from this directive.

At the end of the project, after a few weeks of vacation, team members physically moved back to their former IT unit. Some members found their unit unchanged, but no large organization can go this long without some significant changes, and other participants learned that their former unit had undergone a major change of vocation or had even been dismantled and that they had been reassigned elsewhere.

All those interviewed experienced some form of adaptation, albeit minor for some. Others, however, felt an important re-entry shock. We categorized the respondents magnitude of shock by carefully inventorying and studying the words they used to describe their experience. As shown in Table 3, seven respondents experienced a mild shock, four respondents experienced a medium shock, and eight experienced a shock of high magnitude.

The first group (shock = low) discussed the transition with no superlatives. Most of the respondents in this group expressed how glad they were to have a few weeks off to rest, usually mentioned something about moving on to a new phase in their professional life, and immediately discussed their next assignment as if the transition had been easy or could be considered in the realm of normalcy. While there was clearly an adaptation phase for all of the participants, it seemed like it had not created any unforeseen or extraordinary challenges for this group. One might think that this is simply because these respondents just went back to their former unit and things had not changed much, but this was not the case for all. Indeed, some of the people in this group had undergone significant changes (moved to new units, for instance).

At the extreme opposite end of the spectrum (shock = high), the last group described a shock of high magnitude, usually with a high level of emotion. They used powerful words such as “mourning,” “survivor,” “depression,” “vanishing,” and “a junkie that was forced to quit cold turkey” to describe their experience. The words they chose and the emotions that transpired during the interview made it clear that it had been a very difficult and challenging personal experience. For many respondents in this group, the emotions were still raw, which made it clear that the transition phase was not over yet.

Finally, we have the group in the middle (shock = medium). This group of respondents acknowledged some levels of difficulty going back to functional life, but used more moderate words such as “quite difficult,” “necessary adaptation,” “quite a change of pace,” and “I was a bit lost” to describe their experience. The challenges were certainly present, but the shock they experienced did not seem to match that experienced by others.

This observation is certainly worthy of future research consideration. However, we also have to keep in mind that this may be also an artifact of the research method. Without being specifically asked, seasoned managers might have been less likely to spontaneously discuss this kind of personal issue with an outsider (the researcher).

## The Aftermath

After a characterization of the shock itself, people’s reactions were analyzed. The inductive analysis led to the identification of three distinct groups. Group A, named the “business as usual group,” is the group whose members went back to work with a short period of adaptation and minimal effort. One member affirms:

I was asked by my new boss what I wanted to do. We discussed a few things, but right away, I was interested in one of the projects that would be starting very soon. (Respondent 4)

Group B is the “wait and see group.” Its members voiced their discomfort and concerns and are now giving the organization a chance to react while they wait. The respondents all mentioned that they hoped that something would come their way, such as a project assignment that would be as stimulating or new responsibilities in their functional unit:

If I am still here after all this time, it’s because I believe in this organization, in its organizational culture and values. There are ups and downs, and even if I’m not really happy about what’s going on right now, I have hope that everything will be okay. I will keep working and will wait for an interesting opportunity. [My organization,] because of its size, faces all the technological challenges one could imagine. (Respondent 11)

Finally, Group C, the “exit strategy group,” exhibited a very different reaction. For this group, the project work environment was a tough act to follow. Their project experience made them react adversely to their new assignment. At times, it affected their work performance, and overall prompted the urge to go elsewhere:

When I came back to my unit, I was almost immediately assigned to a new project. After a couple of weeks, I asked to be released. I had no idea what I was doing there. It was highly political, the objectives were not clear, no decisions were ever made. I told them to call me back if or when they really needed me. Fortunately, my boss was supportive. (Respondent 18)

I wasn’t having fun anymore. So when I came back, the clash was enormous and it was reflected in my job performance. I needed to find a way out. (Respondent 17)

The exit strategy took different forms: One person asked to be removed from a new project he had just been assigned to, while others felt they could not continue to evolve in their functional unit work environment. All had made plans to leave. At the time of the study, one had already transferred to another organizational unit, one was actively seeking a new assignment in the organization, and one was actively seeking a new position in another organizational unit or outside the organization. What distinguishes this group from the “wait and see” group is the degree of proactivity. A thorough analysis of their postproject experiences showed that the members of the “wait and see” group were waiting for the organization to take care of them, while the “exit strategy” group members were actively seeking a new trajectory.

The magnitude of the shock depended on the perceived differences between the work project environment and the new work environment. Overall, a situation once satisfactory (the average tenure within the organization is high at 15.4 years, especially for the IT workforce) is no longer so. Together the structural and organizational decisions that shaped the project work environment (see Table 2) made the respondents feel quite special. The high visibility and the high priority given to the project gave project participants a high level of visibility and importance in the organization and a sense of participating in something important. After being in the spotlight for quite a while and being repeatedly told how good and successful they were, they transferred back to the relative obscurity of a large organization. In the project environment, the level of autonomy was high, the required creativity was high, and each member’s competences were used to their fullest. The autonomy awarded to the project had also resulted in a very agile and efficient project organization that did not seem to be the norm in this organization. All the conditions were created for the project to progress swiftly toward its objective. But these conditions, that had brought them much professional satisfaction, were also diametrically different from the work environment to which the respondents were accustomed.

Overall, the differences originated in the decisions that had been made about how the project would be managed, organized, and structured to meet its objectives. Interestingly, as per the last quotation, the respondents acknowledged that the organization as a whole and a functional unit could not be managed the way the project was, but at the same time wished their functional work could more closely resemble the way the project was handled. For many of the respondents, the project’s success should have paved the way for a reengineering of how the functional work was carried out. An anecdote that was recounted by several respondents is very telling: A few “project” people had to be “reprimanded” and “had to be severely brought back to reality” when, back in their functional unit, confronted with what they perceived as slow decision making, they demanded that the CIO be called on the spot for an expeditious decision. Needless to say, this did not sit well with the organizational hierarchy.

Many of the respondents mentioned how disappointed they were that the project’s successful work environment did not have an impact on the organization as a whole. At this point, our role is not to critique the way the organization performs its daily activities, but rather to draw attention to how distressed the people are in dealing with their “changed” work environment.

The decisions made regarding the project isolation, the project’s autonomy, and the project team also led to some resentment in the rest of the organization, which in turn added to the challenges of reintegrating a functional unit. The project’s autonomy was not viewed positively: “Of course, you on project [X], you don’t follow the rules” and all its variants had been repeatedly heard by many respondents and led to some friction between organizational units and the project team. Because the level of isolation was high, many units also felt that, at the end of the project, they were now in charge of activities they had not been informed of and that they were not prepared to deal with.

The respondents frequently mentioned that the team member selection process also created tensions: Although it was not officially written anywhere, but often used by the project manager as a source of motivation for the hard-working troops, the message that only the best had been chosen to work on the project caught on like wildfire. Many left behind clearly felt some resentment for not being offered a chance to participate (“whether they wanted to or not, they created two classes of citizens in our organization” [Respondent 6]) or for not being considered good enough (“It’s been told to my face: ‘Of course, we’re not on project [X], we’re just not good enough’” [Respondent 6]), but resentment was also felt among the groups whose members were not officially part of the team but also had to do some project work (“I repeatedly had to tell my team back in my unit that I was only the intermediary, that congratulations were for all of us. But it was hard because many worked night and day, were very involved, had to deal with the increased workload and I was the only one who recognized this” [Respondent 10]). They did not receive any acknowledgement or any extra vacation time for the project work accomplished, nor did they have the prestige of those directly involved, and oftentimes, had to deal with difficult situations. Overall, it is clear that the decisions made to lead this project to success did not help the “privileged project people” return to their functional unit easily.

## Easing a Challenging Transition: What Could Have Been Done?

Ashforth [2] affirms that rites of separation and rites of incorporation may play a mediating role and help employees during transitions. We asked the respondents, mostly those who had undergone a challenging transition (magnitude = medium and high), how the organization could have eased the transition. The respondents made two good points: The first was regarding open communication. As was said earlier, all contacts with the functional units were openly discouraged. So, not knowing what was happening caused a lot of uncertainty and stress, made worse once it was confirmed that the project would not be extended and that the end of the project was getting closer.

The second element discussed pertains to the existence of a transition plan. An exploration of the transcripts of the middle managers who experienced a low level of shock and were able to go to their next assignment without much difficulty demonstrated that the middle managers in this category all referred to a discussion with higher-ups in the hierarchy about their next assignment. They seem to have been consulted on what they wanted to do next and most talked about a project assignment, where we might hypothesize that they found conditions that were closer to those of the project environment than of a functional unit. Few people who had experienced medium or high levels of shock seemed to have been asked what they wanted to do next, and while some mentioned such a discussion, the tone seemed to imply that it was not a very satisfying one. Overall, it is clear that a staff transition plan had not been prepared, at least not for the people who ranked lower in the hierarchy. Our data cannot show if these measures, more communication and a transition plan, would have solved or eased the transition challenges, but these are surely strategies worth considering.

## Discussion

This stud y seek s a nswers to three resea rch questions pertaining to the experience of IT professionals transitioning out of successful projects. Accordingly, this discussion is organized into subsections that focus on each of these questions.

## Are Transitions Out of Successful Projects Effortless?

The answer to this first question spontaneously emerged from the data and became this study’s raison d’être. First, the results invalidate the idea that a work role transition is necessarily easy because it occurs in the same organizational context and therefore in the same organizational culture. Temporary organizations, such as a project, are often created so that they take a very different structural and management configuration from that found in the permanent organization so as to be better suited for achieving the objectives at hand [37]. Two very different work environments can lead to difficult transitions.

In light of the results, we formulate this first proposition:

Proposition 1: A work transition experience is not necessarily easy because it occurs within a single organizational context.

Second, while the literature shows the negative effects that project failure may have on project members [20, 42], the results show that a highly successful project can also bring about very negative effects.

Proposition 2: A successful project can bring about difficult transition experiences for team members.

The mechanisms by which effects are created, however, seem to be different. While project failure brings about feelings of loss and shame, a highly successful project creates a standard referent [14] to which next work assignments or roles are compared. This happened despite the fact that the respondents had, on average, a long tenure in the organization and had fulfilled many functional and project assignments in the past. According to Ashforth [2], a person takes on a role identity if the temporary role offers valence, salience, and social validation. Since the project work environment was highly gratifying and satisfying, the ex-role became the standard referent of the work environment to which the person wanted to return.

This shows how what we know about traditional temporary work assignments may not apply to an agile organizational context. As was seen here, a project work environment, by its very own unique characteristics, is very appealing [45]. It does not carry the stigma, discussed by Ashforth [2], of the passerby without any form of engagement.

In the job satisfaction literature, any departure from the standard referent may lead to job dissatisfaction, which is a feeling experienced by many respondents. Notions of autonomy, work progress, and creativity are all related to the skill set utilization and the assessment that the new job assignment did not live up to the standard referent, which led many to a grieving process. Hence, we formulate a third proposition:

Proposition 3: The greater the difference between the standard referent (project work environment) and the required skill set in the following work assignment, the higher the magnitude of the transition and the more intense the feeling of loss and the perceptions of underemployment.

## Who Is Likely to Experience Challenging Transitions?

The group of respondents unanimously loved their project experience. As noted by Turner et al. [45], project work offers greater variety and more interest than functional work. This can be partly explained by the natural selection process that occurred at the beginning of the project, but our discussions all point to the fact that people leaving the project after a couple of weeks were more the exception than the rule. In order to be as flexible as possible, it is interesting to note that organizations expect most IT professionals to achieve high levels of performance in both functional units and project environments despite the important differences between the two.

When exploring transition challenges, two things must be considered. First, the magnitude of the transition itself must be taken into consideration. The respondents all went through a transition, but for some it was much more intense than for others. While it was not statistically significant, future research should consider the possible effects of rank on the magnitude of the transition. This intuitively makes sense since organizations tend to give managers a more important say in their future assignments. Managers also tend to have more room to shape their role as they wish, or to become “role innovators” [33, 39].

Second, the data analysis forced us to differentiate between the magnitude of the transition and the reaction to the transition. This distinction is not always clear in the literature. The results show that all people do not react the same way to similar magnitudes of transitions. For those entering a new work role, the work transition literature usually considers that there are two modes of adjustment: basically the person either adapts to his or her new role (adaptation) or changes his or her work role (role innovation). These are mainly the strategies adopted by the members of the “business as usual” and “wait and see” groups. Even if some were still hoping for a change, somehow they have accepted a new normal.

We believe that it is the idea of permanence behind work role transitions that leads to the consideration of these two possibilities. When the context calls for fluid temporary assignments to units and projects, the notion of time is integrated into the nature of the work role. An expectation of “temporariness” exists. The person may not have the power (or discretion) to change the new role, nor does he or she accept to adapt to the new role, but since job assignments are time-limited, a third possibility is to resist. This is the strategy adopted by the members of the “exit strategy” group. After many months, the transition was not over yet for these people, a new normal had yet to set in; they were actively resisting their new roles. As discussed by Adam [1] and Fine [15], with the inclusion of time multidimensions in our conception of work comes the notion of duration (number of days/months) as well as tempo (speed and intensity of actions). In terms of tempo, Scheuerman [40] shows that we live in an era characterized by an intensification of activities and experiences. It may not intuitively make sense to resist a situation that is likely permanent, but when speed and intensity are expected in work roles, resisting may make sense since it is fueled by the implicit notion that another assignment is possibly just around the corner.

Proposition 4: In a fluid work environment, the greater the magnitude of the transition, the greater the chance the person will resist the new role and look for an exit strategy.

Another interesting observation is that the “wait and see” and “business as usual” groups represented together a large proportion of all the respondents (79 percent). This may be partly explained by the organizational context. The data show that many respondents have a very strong bond with the organization (stability, good benefits, core values aligned with those of the organization, etc.) so the proportions could be highly different in another organization. But as predicted by the job satisfaction literature, people do not react the same way to job dissatisfaction, and this is also observed in our results.

## How Do Organizational or Project Characteristics Affect Transition Experiences?

As discussed by Raab et al. [37], temporary organizations are often set up when the conclusion is reached that the overall organization’s characteristics in terms of culture, processes, and structures conflict with the realization of the new objectives. In order to achieve their goals, temporary organizations are usually given a high level of autonomy and isolation, which generally creates less formalization, less hierarchy, and greater agility [37]. This is what was observed in this organization. Top management thought that it would be impossible to achieve the defined goals if the project organization was submitted to the same culture and rules of the highly bureaucratic permanent organization. Decisions about the structural and management configuration—flat and simple reporting structure, simplified and expeditious organizational processes (interfaces), high level of autonomy, and management and physical isolation (see Table 2)—all contributed to shape the project work environment. In addition, because of the importance of its goals for the organization, top management’s involvement, and its special organizational status, both the project and its members became very visible. All these decisions combined resulted in a very different work environment for the project members.

As the literature on work transitions informs us, the magnitude of the transition depends on the level of contrast between the two roles [2]. A contrast emerges from an objective and subjective comparison between the experiences of the person in the two roles—that is, activities, roles and responsibilities, culture, climate, values, and norms [2, 28]. Our inductive analysis shows that in the case of the group of respondents, the contrast between the project work environment and their current work assignment was more appropriately described in terms of the differences in terms of visibility and appreciation, skill utilization, autonomy, work progress, and job variety and creativity.

The results of this study highlight the tension between project processes and functional processes, that is, the tension between the autonomy the project needs to succeed and the structure needed for efficient routine organizational processes [21]. As people are asked to transition from one work environment to another, this is how structural, managerial, and governance project decisions impact the magnitude of the shock.

The contrast also depends on the context (work environment) of the new role. The literature has long recognized the existence of subcultures in organizations, and these are also shaped by restructuration, changes in vocation and responsibilities, leadership change, new employees, technology, and so forth. So even if a project member returns to his or her previous position in the former unit, the functional unit has most likely evolved and is no longer the same 18 months later.

As supported by work role transition theory, respondents who perceived a great contrast between their current work environment and the project environment in terms of visibility and appreciation, skill utilization, autonomy, work progress, and job variety and creativity tend to experience a transition of higher magnitude.

Proposition 5: The greater the differences between structural, management, and governance configurations (in terms of isolation, visibility, project autonomy, and authority structure) between the project and the functional unit work environment, the higher will be the contrast.

Proposition 6: The greater the perceived contrast between the project and the functional unit work environments, the higher the magnitude of the transition.

The literature on work transition already shows that the higher the magnitude of the transition, the more likely that the transition will be difficult.

Finally, we cannot ignore how the animosity between the project team and the functional units played a role in the transition experiences of many respondents. Asquin et al. [4] noted that the internal labor market (how people were selected and returned to their units) was a source of animosity and even competition in project-based organizations. In our case, the animosity goes even further, and we use the expression “status differentiation” to convey the privileges and visibility of the project:

Proposition 7: The greater the status differentiation between project work members and the functional unit members, the higher will be the magnitude of the transition.

## Conclusion

This expl ora tory stud y positions successful projects as possible sources of turmoil for IT professionals, an aspect largely ignored in the literature. In contrast to the existing literature, in-depth interviews with 19 people just out of a successful IT project in a large organization show that temporary role assignments (such as a project assignment) may provide conditions such as valence, salience, and opportunities for social validation that become a standard referent to which all subsequent assignments are compared, making role transitions potentially difficult. At the core of the transition is the contrast caused by the decisions top management makes about the project structure, management, and governance. The higher the contrast between the functional work environment and the project work environment, the higher will be the magnitude of the transition. All the respondents reported that they had experienced an adaptation period when transitioning out of the project. This was minor for some. Others, however, felt a significant reentry shock. The results of this study show that all people do not react the same way to a shock of high magnitude; some either adapt or change their new role, but others resist.

## Implications for Research

Beyond the unsuccessful project and the macro and micro role transitions, this study points to the need to better understand temporary role transitions and to investigate further how they are affected by the end of successful projects. The results of this study underscore the need for conceptually differentiating between the transition itself, it magnitude, and the individual’s reaction. While the number of observations is modest and the results are not statistically significant, a person’s position within the organization may have an impact not only on the magnitude of the transition but also on the way he or she reacts. This highlights the need for future research to identify the moderating factors that explain transition magnitude and the reaction to a transition. Nicholson [33], for example, proposes that individual differences, notably the desire for control and feedback along with one’s individual anxiety and frustration thresholds, play a role in the selected mode of adjustment to a transition and on the adjustment outcomes. If we know who is more at risk, the organization may better focus its interventions.

The results of this study highlight the need to know which processes and practices are most relevant for the specific case of resocialization. While the importance of resocialization has been acknowledged in the literature [11, 38], it has not been the object of much specific research. We need to better understand what is experienced when an “old new” employee is returning to a functional setting, what the experience is, and what is at the root of this experience. As in the case of newcomers [28], before understanding the process or identifying the right socializing practices, we must first understand these experiences.

The current study has certain limitations that provide opportunities for future research. With a single organizational context, it is possible to bring the existence of a phenomenon to light, but it is impossible to evaluate its pervasiveness. Even if many respondents spoke freely about their transition experience, the choice to not force the topic on the respondents implies that some information may not have been communicated; some interviewees might have decided not to share their personal stories with the researcher. The results show that it is impossible to deny the existence of the phenomenon, but future research should examine the generalizability of the present findings beyond the organizational context. A few contextual factors such as the importance and the length of the project, members’ voluntary participation, a natural bias selection, and so on, may have exacerbated the challenging transition many respondents experienced.

Another important characteristic of the studied context is that people were assigned to the project full-time and were all physically moved to another building. What if the members had been assigned on a full-time basis but had remained physically in their functional unit? Would the transition between roles at the end of the project have been the same? Future research could also look at the phenomenon of concurrent project assignments. What happens in the case where an IT professional juggles multiple project assignments at the same time? As previously stated, perhaps the nature of these assignments bears some resemblance to micro role transitions.

Ashforth [2] discusses the concept of transition identity, a stage in which people are not out of their ex-role, nor do they identify with their new role yet. Future research could adopt a longitudinal design where long-term challenging transition effects could be assessed. The performance of transition practices in different project contexts could also be evaluated.

## Implications for Practice

Knowledge is an important resource for project performance [17], putting the recruitment and retention of IT staff on the IT senior managers’ short list of major concerns [29]. The results of this study suggest that challenging assignment transitions, even out of successful projects, need to be added to the already long list of stressors experienced by IT specialists (see, e.g., [7, 18, 41]). The results show that the contrast is, at least partly, based on the decisions the organization made when shaping the work environment in which the project members were going to evolve. A large part of the contrast is manshaped; therefore, organizations control the level of contrast and have to realize that these choices can lead to a radically different work environment and can put great pressure on team members who are asked to carry out their work in very different environments, and that this may make the transition from one to the other overly difficult. The results show that the effects can be severe. The organization under study benefitted from a strong culture and a group of loyal employees (very low turnover) who were ready to give the organization a chance, but this may not be the case in other organizations, as in the current economy and job market, employee loyalty is generally low [24]. While this needs to be confirmed, the results of this study tend to show that people at the bottom of the pyramid may be more likely to experience challenging transitions than middle managers. Detecting the people who are most at risk and performing targeted interventions may be the best investment for organizations.

The next interesting question for practitioners is where the responsibility of managing transitions lies. While primarily accountable for achieving the project’s objectives, the project manager nonetheless needs to adopt a long-term perspective and to acknowledge that his or her current team members are likely to be the resources for the next project. The project manager should insist on organizational processes that reduce transition anxiety so as to ensure team members’ focus and future availability.

In organizations, the formal socialization process for new employees is often the responsibility of the HR department. However, the resocialization process is often misunderstood and more often overlooked or neglected. As Ashforth [2] discusses, the transition process has already started when one is preparing for role exit, so the process has to be managed even if the next assignment is not yet determined. With the growing tendency for organizations not to manage projects but rather to manage portfolios of projects, the focus may need to go from leaving employees to manage their own careers to a process in which organizations manage a portfolio of resources, including the necessary role transitions. This may highlight the need for an independent unit that would be in charge of transitions. This could take the form of a special “global resource management office” in the IT function, or this responsibility could be added to those of the project management office. It may also imply that HR’s view on career management be broadened; in the realm of macro transitions, promotions and lateral moves are obviously important (career management), but more attention needs to be given to temporary work assignments. For senior managers and HR professionals, the results of this study highlight the importance of project structures and governance decisions for HR. If these vastly different project work environments are unavoidable, the results show the need for a company-wide nonuniform resocialization strategy for current employees that are returning to their former units; the transition can be a highly emotional period that requires a concerted organizational effort that seems to be underestimated.
