# Vendors’ perspectives on trust and control in offshore information systems outsourcing

## A B S T R A C T

Whereas most discussion of outsourcing has concentrated on the clients’ perspective, we investigated the formation of offshore IS vendors’ trust in their client and the client’s control over the vendor; these were assumed to affect the vendor’s performance. Focusing on the Japan–China offshoring context, we conducted a survey of 110 project managers in nine Chinese IS outsourcers. Our findings indicated that trust had a significant effect on project quality but little on cost adherence; the relationship between control and the two vendor performance measures were the opposite of this. Furthermore, information sharing, communication quality, and inter-firm adaptation emerged as three significant contributors to the vendor’s trust in the client; goal setting and cultural blending turned out to be significant in influencing the client’s control over the vendor. One of the key contributions and implications of our research was that certain offshore client behaviors could shape vendor performance by influencing the vendor’s trust in the client and client’s control over the vendor, beyond formal contracts. These findings may be useful for both offshore vendors and clients in developing successful IS outsourcing.

## 1. Introduction

Offshore IS outsourcing is an important topic today (e.g., [28]). According to an IDG report, the global IT outsourcing market will soon grow to US \$17 billion [29]. The clients are concentrated in North America, Western Europe, and Japan, with the U.S. accounting for about 40% of the market, followed by Japan with 10%. Whereas India has been the leading destination, China represents a huge potential vendor that has been mainly ignored.

Outsourcing involves the migration of all or part of an organization’s IS assets, people, and/or activities to vendors located in a different country from that of the client organization. Outsourcing arrangements range from transferring work to the vendor country with lower labor costs and access to specialized personnel to negotiating partnerships in the form of joint ventures or accessing foreign subsidiaries, etc.

Relationship management is a key factor for successful outsourcing. According to the transaction cost theory, tightening control through a well-designed contract is assumed to reduce production and transaction costs, but control via an outsourcing contract is inherently complicated: offshore outsourcing is much riskier than traditional outsourcing. On the other hand, interactions between offshore partners often go beyond the rules, agreements, and exceptions specified in the contract. There is an element of trust, commitment, and mutual interest that is intangible and not easily captured in a contract. The vendor–client relationship rooted in mutual trust gives rise to a strong bond between the participants.

Whereas trust and control are essential to a successful relationship, it is particularly important for clients to exert control over their offshore vendor and to create trust in the relationship while ensuring quality and delivery. Therefore, we investigated the following research questions from a vendor’s viewpoint:

1. What kinds of client behavior contribute to a vendor’s trust in the client and a client’s control over the vendor? and

2. What are the impacts of a vendor’s trust of the client and the client’s control over the vendor on project performance?

In other words, our research investigated what kinds of client behavior influenced a vendor’s perceptions of trust and control and thus the vendor’s performance. To answer the questions, a survey was conducted of outsourcers in China working for Japanese clients.

Our research addressed three gaps in the extant literature. First, very limited research has considered the vendors’ viewpoint mainly due to the difficulty in conducting cross-cultural studies and generating unbiased findings, and yet successful outcome depends on both the client and vendor. Second, although there has been much interest in trust and control in outsourcing, prior studies have reported conflicting results; this deficiency required use of a stronger theoretical basis and good empirical evidence. Third, the literature has generally focused on India-bound offshoring from the U.S. valuable lessons can be learned from looking at the experience of other countries.

## 2. Theoretical framework

Transaction cost and social exchange theories suggest two different sources to maximize the value of outsourcing and increase its success: tighten control through well-designed contracts [14] and developing mutual trust to improve the quality of their relationship [27]. In our study, we were concerned with vendor trust in the client and client control over the vendor.

The difference between a successful and failed outsourcing project may depend on the level of control via well-designed contracts, whose clauses should specify service levels that are sustained during unspecified periods, and limit opportunistic behavior between the client and its vendor. Given possible cultural differences in offshore outsourcing, communication barriers, physical distance, and lack of personal contact, the need for control is strong. In many cases, organizations seek a flexible relationship based on trust after identifying limitations in the legal contract. Trust assumes goodwill and reliability in the working relationship.

## 2.1. Trust building behavior in offshore outsourcing

To investigate factors influencing vendor trust in the client and client control over the vendor, our research drew on work by Das and Teng [8]. Their framework identified various ways of developing trust and control that affected the degree of trust. Prior work on outsourcing has suggested that information sharing is also important. Therefore, three types of vendor behaviors, information sharing, communication quality and inter-firm adaptation, were considered essential in influencing vendors’ trust in the client.

## 2.1.1. Information sharing

This involves the range and depth of information exchanged between partners: a trust-based relationship in which both client and vendor are intimately coupled. Trust results from frequent and relevant information exchange; inter-group exchange generates trust, and eventually facilitates more effective interaction and a better relationship.

In Japan–China offshore outsourcing, the Chinese vendors depend upon the client to provide domain and process management knowledge. However, the Japanese culture is high-context one relying on non-language cues and subtle context hints in communication. It values indirect and holistic communication styles [30], which hinder the free flow of task and process material to vendors. It takes extra goodwill and sincerity on the client side to overcome such cross-cultural barriers. Therefore, the perceived willingness of the client to be open and forthcoming in sharing domain and process information is expected to have a strong impact on the vendor’s trust in a true partnership:

H1. The client’s willingness to share information with the vendor will have a positive impact on the vendor’s trust in the client of an offshored project.

## 2.1.2. Communication quality

This focuses on the process and means of communication. According to social exchange theory, effective communication is essential in achieving intended objectives. Intensive communication keeps partners better informed and this, in turn, makes them more confident in the relationship and more willing to keep it alive [20]. Communication quality is an antecedent of trust and an important trust building mechanism in outsourced IS projects.

Usually, a client sends its experts to introduce a project to the vendor but follow-up communication involves a variety of channels ranging from emails and phone calls to personnel exchange. The client personnel’s other project commitments and the level of importance attached to the project influence the vendors’ perception of the relationship. The vendor team can receive reliable and prompt response or be left alone for weeks without feedback, reflecting the presence or absence of goodwill and reliability of the client. Thus, higher quality communication should enhance the level of vendor’s trust in the client:

H2. Communication quality will have a positive impact on a vendor’s trust in the client of an offshored project.

## 2.1.3. Inter-firm adaptation

An effective way to develop trust between partners is to adapt to meet the needs of the relationship [15]. Inter-firm adaptation refers to the extent to which there is an effort on the part of both organizations to make required modifications in the way that partners collaborate, providing a mechanism for negotiating and agreeing upon mutual benefits, and for creating common goals.

In the context of Japan–China offshoring, inter-firm adaptation is particularly pertinent; a successful relationship usually involves extensive inter-firm adaptation in project schedule, processes, and scope. This is partly due to evolving requirements and the vague style of communication of Japanese clients. The effort in adapting to a vendor’s constraints sends a signal of goodwill and commitment to the vendor. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H3. Inter-firm adaptation will have a positive impact on a vendor’s trust in the client of an offshored project.

## 2.2. Control mechanisms in offshore outsourcing

The level of control is a direct outcome of the controlling process, whereas control mechanisms are the organizational arrangements designed to influence what collaborating partners will do. Based on the specific context of our study, we focused on goal setting and cultural blending in determining client control over the vendor.

## 2.2.1. Goal setting

Understanding a partner’s intention is essential to establishing mutual goals [25]. Through their establishment, partners understand what behaviors and policies are important, appropriate, and correct: clear goals help set the direction of their relationship and help in establishing specific rules and regulations for efficient management and control of their relationship.

In offshore IS outsourcing, the success of IS projects depends heavily on the quality of the requirements; they are difficult to define clearly and completely at the outset, and sometimes must evolve. The clarity of goal setting will exert a strong influence on the vendor. One of the distinct issues in Japan–China offshoring is frequently evolving client requirements, partly due to the service nature of the offshored projects and the Japanese clients mentality. The extent to which the client can clearly establish project objectives is critical and can exert a huge influence on client control over the vendor. Accordingly, we hypothesized:

H4. Goal setting will have a positive impact on a client’s control over the vendor of an offshored project.

## 2.2.2. Cultural blending

This requires an effort to create shared values, norms, and beliefs and is considered a critical element of control in offshore outsourcing. For Japan–China outsourcing, successful projects tend to involve extensive personnel exchange, cultural immersion, language training, bridging engineers on the client site, and client-personnel visits to the vendors [13]. All such activities have an element of cultural blending; for example, an executive commented that even a dining experience in Japan could help his employees appreciate the Japanese customer service culture and lead to willingness to accommodate evolving requirements from a client.

Moreover, the Japanese culture is categorized as collectivist as opposed to individualist, with a propensity of high uncertainty avoidance [12] resulting in group harmony and higher priorities to mutual goals, enforced by rules and regulations: a team manager is expected to issue clear instructions and subordinate initiatives are tightly controlled. In contrast, the Chinese development teams tend to ignore or downplay rules and regulations, exhibiting a more individualist style and idiosyncrasy [18]. Therefore, cultural blending can change the attitude of the Chinese project team, making it collectivist and goal-oriented while conforming to the ways of the ways of the Japanese client. Following this line of reasoning, we hypothesized:

H5. Cultural blending will have a positive impact on a client’s control over the vendor of an offshored project.

## 2.3. Trust and control as contributors to vendor’s performance

Trust is one of the most desired qualities in any close relationship [11]. Since it is not possible to monitor and control every detail in most exchanges, building a minimum level of trust is essential. Consequently, building a level of trust is a probable key predictor of outsourcing success. Many of the practices for mitigating risks [26] were found to occur in the practice of offshoring Japanese clients and Chinese vendors.

There is substantial anecdotal evidence of the relationship between trust and vendor performance. For example, the President of one of the largest and most successful Chinese IS outsourcers to Japan said, ‘‘it is all about trust. When I sign a contract with my client, I don’t need to look at the numbers and I can do it with my eyes closed, ‘cause I know they will treat me fairly.’’ Therefore, he just focused on completing the job with the belief that they would be paid for any extra effort sooner or later. Thus, vendors utilize more and better resources, and take more initiative in dealing with requirement and quality enhancements.

Vendor performance is derived from the three critical objectives of project management: quality, budget, and schedule [9]. However, since schedule is fixed by the client without much flexibility, it is more meaningful to focus on quality and cost control. To the outsourcing vendor, good quality and cost management lead to future and larger projects from the client, and budget control affects its own viability and profitability. Therefore, we proposed the following two hypotheses:

H6. A vendor’s trust in the client will have a positive impact on the quality of the offshored project.

H7. A vendor’s trust in the client will have a positive impact on the cost management of the offshored project.

Organizations are likely to have more confidence in their partners’ cooperation when they feel they have sufficient control over their partners. Effective control over the vendor in offshoring relationships should help ensure more desirable outcomes.

The need for control is likely to be stronger for overseas’ outsourcing than traditional ones for two reasons: (1) the vendors are removed from the requirements setters because of physical and psychological distance and (2) for Japan, Chinese outsourcers usually possess a lower maturity level of project and process management and are not as experienced as their clients [24]. The consensus is that there is a large difference in project management skills of Chinese vendors and Japanese clients. Therefore, strong influence from the client is likely to result in greater benefits, i.e., project quality and cost management. Based on this, two hypotheses were formulated:

H8. A client’s control over the vendor will have a positive impact on the quality of the offshored project.

H9. A client’s control over the vendor will have a positive impact on the cost management of the offshored project.

## 3. Research method

We conducted a survey to test the hypotheses. Our unit of analysis was an offshored IS project and, for each, its project manager was the respondent. The scales were developed in three stages:

(1) The scales were based on those used and reported in prior work. Extensive interviews were conducted in seven companies to help us understand the industry and the general practice of offshore outsourcing in China.

(2) Prior to a pilot test, the scales were pretested by two executives selected from different firms and one project manager.

(3) A pilot test was conducted involving 31 project managers from two IS offshore outsourcing firms, to further refine the scales.

## 3.1. Interviews for scale generation

Interviews were arranged through a senior executive of each target firm; the senior management, including the CEO or President, usually participated in the study. The interviews in each firm were scheduled in a top-down sequence; this assured open and active participation of lower level personnel. The interviews lasted from 45 to 90 min. The interviewees included executives, project managers, and developers from seven Chinese vendors; three of the companies later participated in the survey.

Our interviews showed that Japan–China offshoring had several unique characteristics that had to be considered in our research design. First, generally the client was not the end-user organization but a Japanese IS outsourcer; they were the source of both domain knowledge and process management expertise. Moreover, most of the offshored projects from Japan were tailor-made projects (i.e., service rather than software package development). As such they involved changing and gradually developed requirements. Therefore, it was important to study the control and influence aspects of the Japanese clients. Second, Japan and China have very different customer service cultures, which are complicated by the vagueness of the Japanese communication style. Third, the market structure is one in which many Chinese vendors are vying for a limited number of offshored projects. As a result, there is a large asymmetry in the bargaining power; it heavily favors the clients. This further reinforced the need to study client control over vendors. Fourth, in the relationship between the vendor and client, there is a greater emphasis on trust and mutual adaptation than in formal contracts: indeed, a project may be launched without any formal contract. Lastly, the vendor’s revenue and profit have a nearly linear relationship with contract size with price primarily based on manmonth expended. Therefore, it was critical for the vendor to control cost by completing projects on time and within budget.

The interviews also helped us generate measures for vendor performance (see Appendix A), and other scales. Since most existing measures dealt with clients’ outsourcing success, we took an exploratory approach, based on key indicators of project quality and cost control suggested from interviews with senior management. The project quality was measured as client satisfaction with the deliverable, the fit between it and client standards, and an overall assessment of the project. In contrast, cost control included budget control, overtime work, and percentage overhead for extra labor input, which were key concerns of the vendors.

## 3.2. The initial measurements and pretest

Most of the measures were based on previously validated instruments, while others were developed from prior research but modified for the context of our research and the interviews. Specifically, trust, as one of the central constructs of our research, was operationalized as the vendor’s perception of the client’s goodwill and reliability in their working relationship. An instrument developed by Lee and Kim [16] was adopted and revised for this. Control was measured as the degree to which the client had enough influence so that the vendor did its best to complete the project and make the project outcome predictable through good standards and processes.

Trust involved information sharing, measured as the degree to which useful or critical information was communicated. Communication quality was measured in several ways, including its adequacy, timeliness, frequency, and mode between the participants. Lastly, inter-firm adaptation, as a trusting-building behavior, was measured mostly as contractual flexibility with a questionnaire statement to be evaluated: the client gives us flexibility in adjusting our schedule, work procedures, and process standards.

The two determinants of control were measured similarly. First, goal setting was concerned with the degree of congruence and specificity of project objectives between the client and vendor. A five-item measure was developed for this; it mainly drew from our interviews. Second, the construct of cultural blending drew on both prior studies and our interviews.

To account for extraneous sources of variation in vendor performance, we incorporated task type, relationship type, and age of relationship as control variables. Depending on the project task type (i.e., coding, unit test, analysis, design, system integration, and integration testing) that Japanese clients outsourced, the complexity and importance of the project might differ and so it could influence the degree of vendor performance in offshored IS outsourcing. We also controlled for outsourcing relationship type, one of two categories (direct, with a client who became the direct user of the system, and indirect, where the Japanese firm was working for another company). We believed that the number of layers between the Chinese development team and the Japanese end-user organization could affect the flow of task domain knowledge, client relationship, and project outcome. In addition, since the duration of prior relationship might influence vendor performance, we decided to control for the duration of the relationship with a client to eliminate any spurious effect of time on vendor performance.

A seven-point Likert-style questionnaire was used, with 40 items measuring vendor trust and client control, along with client behaviors that influenced the constructs. In addition, there were three items to measure the cost control aspect of vendor performance, and four items for project quality.

The questionnaire was initially developed in English. We then translated it into Chinese while maximizing the consistency in sentence structure and terminology use. A bilingual IS professor, who was not related to our research, translated the Chinese version back to English and we compared the back-translation version with the original; we found that they were highly consistent.

A pretest was conducted using two executives and one project manager who had expertise in the area of offshore outsourcing. As a result, three of the 47 items were removed to improve the survey’s face validity.

## 3.3. Pilot testing

The pilot test focused on the questionnaire’s internal validity; it was based on responses from 31 project managers in two Chinese companies in order to retain a vendor perspective. Each project manager had supervised at least one offshored project. All had dealt with clients, and were familiar with the key parameters of projects. This role was relatively homogeneous.

The responses were analyzed for discriminant and convergent validity of the target constructs through factor analysis and itemto-total correlation. The levels of internal consistency were all acceptable, ranging from 0.73 to 0.94. Items, whose item-to-total correlation scores and factor loading values were lower than 0.5, were dropped from further analysis. A factor analysis was then performed on all measures except vendor performance; fourteen with factor loading values lower than 0.5 were dropped. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) was also measured in order to assess the reliability of the measurement. The final questionnaire consisted of 30 items for nine constructs, including the two dependent variables measured with project quality and cost control, as shown in Appendix B.

The two phases of instrument validation resulted in a significant degree of refinement of the survey instrument, and proof of initial face validity and internal validity of the measures.

## 3.4. Sample and data collection

For the main survey, a convenience sample was used, through personal networks and referrals. Every effort was made to include companies of various backgrounds, i.e., business, ownership structures, and size. See Appendix C for demographics of the Chinese outsourcers.

Usually an executive in each company was first contacted and a meeting was requested to explain the purpose and significance of the study; then permission was requested to distribute questionnaires to project managers. Participating companies and managers were assured that the questionnaire collected no trade secrets or confidential information. We felt that such an approach would be effective in obtaining access to busy project managers. In one case, after meeting with a CEO, all project managers were called to a conference room to complete the questionnaire; this took 10–15 min.

Project managers were asked to focus on their most recently completed project in answering the questionnaire; it began with three background questions on the project, breakdown of tasks, project types, and client type, to position them on a particular project. Nine companies participated in our study, including one of the largest and most successful Chinese outsourcers to Japan. This resulted in 110 valid questionnaires. In all but one, hardcopies of the questionnaire with a sequential number and company code were used. In the single exception, a manager requested and was allowed to return an electronic copy.

Due to a lack of prior studies of Japan–China offshoring, a comprehensive profile of the Chinese vendors and projects was included in the Appendices, along with the nature of the projects in order to illustrate some unique features of the outsourcing business. On average the companies had been in business for 7.6 years, and had been outsourcers to Japan for 5.6 years. Most had a high percentage of their income from Japan, with the median being 90%. The median number of employees was 110, reflecting the fact that most of the Chinese outsourcers were small. Seventyeight percent of the projects surveyed were between long-term partners, and only 22% were for new clients; thus there was an existing, stable relationship. Only about 30% of the project managers reported that the primary approach to resolving major problem was to negotiate based on contract, while the rest were resolved by creating consensus and mutual compromise due to well-established partner relationships.

Furthermore, about 60% of the tasks performed by the Chinese vendors were coding (32%) and unit test (28%), which were low value-adding work (see Appendix D). Only 19% of the work dealt with detailed design, with virtually no conceptual design and analysis. Moreover, consistent with the common belief in the industry, a high percentage of the projects was customized implementation of IS projects; less than 13% of the clients were end-user companies in Japan but 84% of the Japanese clients were IS outsourcers. With respect to the respondents, three quarters of the project managers had worked in Japan for at least 3 months, and 45% had studied in Japan for at least 1 year. This could be a result of the combination of the vendors’ human resource strategy and frequent travel of the lead developers to the client’s site.

Prior to model testing, we conducted two tests to assess data quality. First, we attempted to detect more than one response on the same project. No multiple respondents were found by comparing the breakdown of reported task details. In addition, since only project managers participated in our study and normally there was only one project manager for any project, the possibility of having multiple responses was minimal. Second, we performed a Harman’s one-factor test for common method variance by running a principal component factor analysis on all of the questionnaire items except those related to cost control and the control variables. ## 4. Analysis and results

The PLS method was chosen to examine our proposed model and its hypotheses: PLS is suitable for assessing theories in the early stages of development and our study may be a first attempt to advance a theoretical model by considering trust and control together from the vendor perspective. Also PLS imposes minimal demands on sample size in order to validate a model compared to other SEM techniques. The size of our sample seemed acceptable at the minimum level, making PLS appropriate for testing the model using the data. We used PLS-Graph version 3.00 for analyzing measurement and structural models.

## 5. Findings and discussion

Our research has offered a new perspective to understanding the contributors to trust and control in offshore outsourcing. A client’s effort in information sharing, communication quality and inter-firm adaptation appear to be important. Moreover, once trust is developed, the focus is on specific actions and the motives and intentions of the partners. As a result, trust increases their confidence in the collaborating partner. Congruence in project goals between outsourcing partners and harmony of shared values and belief are instrumental in creating client control.

It appears that a high level of trust in a client is associated with higher quality standards and level of effort, which can increase cost, although we expected that lower transaction cost would accrue from increased trust. Moreover, the contract practice of outsourcing between Japan and China assumes that quality and the delivery schedule are explicit and strictly followed, though labor input is implicit and managed by the vendor. It is important to note that most of the relationships in our sample were between longterm repetitive partners.

Lastly, as expected, client control over the vendor had a significant impact on cost control, helping the vendor prevent cost overruns, but not on project quality.

## 5.1. Contributions and implications

Our study showed that a client can increase vendor’s trust and thus improve customer relationship and project quality by ensuring effective communication and increasing the range and depth of information transfer. Clients’ effort to maintain flexibility and adaptability toward the vendor also help.

Goal setting and cultural blending can be instrumental in the client gaining influence over the vendor, and thus help in budget adherence. In light of the relatively low software process maturity of Chinese vendors, strong client influence was found to be beneficial for vendors’ budget control and profitability. The practical implications include options for increasing client control over the vendor through cultural blending and goal setting. The former can be done by providing the vendor project team with access to the client culture, social opportunities, and language training; the latter by clear specification of goals and deliverables, and shared understanding that goes beyond the contract.

We have developed a preliminary measure of vendor performance encompassing both subjective measures and perceptions, and it could be useful in the offshore outsourcing context. Prior studies have tried to investigate whether trust and control operate as complement or substitute, but reported conflicting results with no clear explanation. This could be that they generally considered one dependent variable, in spite of the fact that there is more than one outcome. Our study considered two major dependent variables and found that trust had a significant impact on project quality while control was significantly associated with cost management. Thus, trust and control have different roles in improving outsourcing performance and operate as complements.

A study of Vietnamese vendors engaged in offshoring software outsourcing found that vendors’ understanding of the client culture, creditability, capabilities, and personal visits were important in gaining initial trust of a client, whereas maintaining trust required cultural understanding, communication strategies, contract conformance, and timely delivery [23]. However, a study of Indian vendors found that critical factors for maintaining trust in offshoring software outsourcing relationships include transparency, demonstrability, honesty, process followed, and commitment [3]. These studies were conducted with different perspectives from ours, and the factors were different. However, a closer inspection reveals that they are related, e.g., cultural understanding and communication strategies appear to overlap with cultural blending and communication quality in our work. This comparison provides indirect support to our findings.

## 5.2. Limitations

Findings of this research need to be interpreted with the limitations in mind. First, the survey was based on a convenience sample, which was relatively small for survey research despite our effort to obtain more results. As a result, the size poses a threat to external validity of the results: the small sample size limited some data analysis options and reduced the statistical power of the analyses. Moreover, there were several projects from the same company, and the projects might involve the same client, thus the trust and control aspects could have been quite similar, reducing variances. Second, since this research studied Japan–China offshore outsourcing with some unique cultural and cross-cultural influences, the generalizability of the results are likely to be limited.

## 6. Conclusions

Our exploratory research has filled some void in the literature on offshore outsourcing and trust and control impact from the vendors’ perspective. A model of trust-control in offshore outsourcing relationship was proposed; it received some empirical support: Several trust building mechanisms were identified, including the client’s effort in information sharing, communication quality, and inter-firm adaptation. The client should also focus on goal setting and cultural blending with vendors in order to influence and control the project. Furthermore, the vendor’s trust of the client stood out as a key factor affecting project quality, and vendor control, helping prevent cost overrun.
