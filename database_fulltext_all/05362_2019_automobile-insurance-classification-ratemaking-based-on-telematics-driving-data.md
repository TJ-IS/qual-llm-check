# Automobile insurance classification ratemaking based on telematics driving data

Yifan Huang<sup>a</sup>, Shengwang Meng<sup>a,b,⁎</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Statistics, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872, PR China

<sup>b</sup> Center for Applied Statistics, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872, PR China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Usage-based insurance Telematics data Driving behavior Classification ratemaking Machine learning

## A B S T R A C T

Usage-based insurance (UBI), given the development of in-vehicle networking and big data technologies, has received a growing amount of attention in recent years from both insurers and policyholders. The UBI product derives certain driving behavior variables from telematics data, which have stronger causal relationships with accidents and thus efectively improve the pricing accuracy of automobile insurance. This paper mainly investigates the use of extensive driving behavior variables in predicting the risk probability and claim frequency of an insured vehicle. More specifically, logistic regression and four machine learning techniques - support vector machines, random forests, XGBoost, and artificial neural networks - are employed as risk probability models, while Poisson regression as claim frequency model. In addition, aiming at the interpretability requirements of insurance pricing, a data augmentation method of variable binning is adopted to discretize continuous variables and construct tarif classes with significant predictive efects. As a result, our pricing framework can simultaneously improve the interpretability and predictive accuracy of the model, and thus provides a novel solution to implement classification ratemaking for UBI products. The empirical results, based on a dataset from a property and casualty insurance company in China, show the selection of significant variables and the estimation of their specific efects on driving risk, verifying the great potential of driving behavior variables in automobile insurance.

## 1. Introduction

Insurance is the most important potential risk transfer tool. A fair price of insurance should accurately reflect a policyholder's actual risk, otherwise it may cause adverse selection problems (i.e.. those insureds with less expected risk will quit while the more risky insureds will continue to purchase insurance) and damage the interests of insurance companies and other insureds [10]. Therefore, actuaries generally use risk-related information (i.e., risk factors) to classify the risk status of all policyholders and construct an actuarial rating table from calculations based on the expected losses of diferent categories, which reflects the process of classification ratemaking [2]. Traditional risk factors for automobile insurance include personal characteristics, historical claim records of the insured, and natural features of the corresponding vehicle [1,8]. However, premiums are often inaccurate in practice since these factors do not have, or have only indirect causal relationships with the actual driving risk [14].

Developments of in-vehicle networks and telematics technologies have made UBI products (i.e., usage-based insurance or user behavior insurance) appealing to both insurers and consumers [42,43]. The original idea of UBI products was to price insurance based only on each vehicle's usage. However, it now refers to a general kind of insurance, the price of which is determined by each driver's daily driving habits that have been collected from telematics data [41]. Since driving behavior variables have stronger causations with trafic accidents compared with traditional risk factors, they can efectively improve the accuracy of insurance pricing [30]. In addition, the dynamic premiums of UBI products encourage insureds to reduce their premium expenses by driving less or improving their driving habits. Many studies have pointed out that UBI has played a positive role in solving social problems such as trafic congestion, energy consumption, and air pollution [29,34,39], which promotes the level of society's overall welfare. The size of the UBI product, according to a market report published by Allied Market Research, is expected to reach \$123US billion by the year 2022, and as such there is an urgent need for a complete ratemaking framework to help insurers strengthen and improve their decision making.

The core work of UBI pricing is similar to that of other insurance products, which is to predict the future risk of the insured (i.e., the risk probability, claim frequency, and severity of losses caused by accidents) [5,35]. This paper focuses mainly on the prediction of risk probability and claim frequency, and establishes predictive models based on telematics data and driving behavior variables. Firstly, we combine existing studies with several driving behavior theories and expert domain knowledge to extract the most extensive driving behavior variable from the original telematics data. An elaborate process of feature se lection is also conducted to verify the power of driving behavior vari ables and find out those variables with universal importance. Secondly, except for using the traditional method of generalized linear models (GLMs) in both the probability and frequency prediction, several ad vanced techniques are additionally employed for probability modeling. Logistic regression, currently the most widely used model in insurance practice, is used as the benchmark model for probability prediction, and the following four machine learning techniques are also investigated and compared in this paper: support vector machines, random forests, XGBoost $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ an advanced realization of gradient boosting decision trees), and artificial neural networks. Previous studies have shown successful applications of machine learning techniques in the insurance field [18,36]. However, since the “black-box” nature of machine learning is contradictory to the interpretability requirements of in surance pricing, which are necessary for both regulators and con sumers, we further introduce a data-driven binning method and finally propose our UBI ratemaking framework. More specifically, each con tinuous variable will be first divided into certain discrete tarif classes based on the risk homogeneity within classes [26], then used to train the predictive models. Due to the countable number of tarif classes, the risk profiles of all policyholders can be enumerated from limited categories, thus allowing us to conduct detailed analyses to reveal the specific efects of driving behavior variables on both risk probability and claim frequency of the vehicle. Based on these tarif classes, a complete rating table for the UBI product can be constructed straightforwardly. Therefore, this paper manages to make a natural combina tion of data binning and several modeling techniques, and it not only improves the model's interpretability but also the predictive accuracy.

Generally, the proposed framework demonstrates the complete process of insurance pricing, including the preprocessing steps of variable binning and feature selection, as well as the establishment, comparison and interpretation of predictive models. To our knowledge, there has been no research on the elaborate selection of predictor variables and classification ratemaking for UBI, thus the approach and results of this paper will be valuable for improving pricing practices of this novel product. Importantly, our ratemaking framework can also be extended to other business lines of insurance companies.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 gives an overview of related literature on UBI and its pricing models. Section 3 describes the raw telematics data and some driving behavior variables derived in this paper. Our complete ratemaking framework, based on the combination of data binning and several predictive models, is de monstrated in Section 4. Section 5 presents the empirical results, which are based on an actual dataset from a property and casualty insurance company in China. Finally, our conclusions and further discussions are summarized in Section 6.

## 2. Related work

## 2.1. UBI pricing

Table 1 gives an overview of current UBI pricing research. Generally, these studies have mainly been carried out from two perspectives. The first one is the method of risk classification $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the prediction of probability that at least one insurance claim will happen). For example, Guo and Fang [19], using logistic regression analysis, examined the efects of a driver's personality and sudden driving incidents, and verified that driving behavior variables are significant to vehicle collision prediction. However, the lack of driving mileage data in this study limits the application of their finding. Paefgen et al. [32,33] subdivided the amount of a vehicle's mileage according to the specific driving situation (e.g., the time of a day, day of the week, road types, and driving speed), and developed the definition of risk exposure factors. They [33] first attempted to apply machine learning techniques in UBI pricing. Through the comprehensive comparison of predictive powers, the robustness to samples' skewness, and the smoothness of the probability output, they concluded that the traditional model of logistic regression is still the best choice for insurance pricing. Therefore, a further research on logistic regression was then conducted [32], and they discussed the specific impacts on the accident probability of dif ferent driving situation in details. Their definition of risk exposure factors has been widely adopted in subsequent studies. Baecke and Bocca [4] also made a comparison among machine learning techniques. In addition to certain risk exposure factors, they considered the added value of domain knowledge, and introduced nighttime and rush hours as explanatory variables. Diferent from above research on the binary classification, Bian et al. [6] carried out their experiments from the perspective of multi-classification and proposed an innovative ensemble-learning approach for UBI pricing. However, due to the so phisticated form and poor interpretability of ensemble-learning models, it is still dificult to use in insurance practice.

Another type of research mainly follows the traditional actuarial method, and uses GLMs to predict the frequency and loss severity of future claims. Toledo et al. [37] validated the correlation between the “individual risk index” and claim frequency by a Poisson regression, but they didn't show how to calculate the index and developed only a simple prototype system. Ayuso et al. [3] similarly fitted Poisson regression models but considered mileage as the ofset term. Their results also confirmed the important role of driving behavior variables com pared to traditional pricing variables. Ma et al. [31] were the first researchers to examine the use of context-sensitive variables. They believed that the risk of a driving vehicle cannot be quantified by the absolute value of speed alone so that it is necessary to consider real time road congestion and the driving speed of neighboring cars to calculate the relative speed. These variables improved the predictive performance of both logistic and Poisson regressions. However, since they treated each trip as the single observation and implemented the regression on a trip-level basis, a significant imbalance of training samples may possibly exist, which was ignored in their research. Verbelen et al. [40] extended the pricing framework from GLMs to generalized additive models (GAMs) through their introduction of smooth functions to characterize the complex efects of continuous variables. In addition, they provided a novel method to analyze the compositional data (e.g., risk exposure factors) and thus solved the multicollinearity problem between these variables and the driving mileage. At last, Lemaire et al. [27] conducted an empirical study based on a large dataset of 259,029 policies. They used negative binomial regression and linear regression to fit the frequency and severity model, respectively, thereby filling the gap in the loss severity prediction of the UBI product.

In summary, the current research has two main shortcomings. First, as can be seen from Table 1, relatively few driving behavior variables have been used in past studies, which typically focused on only a certain side of driving risks (e.g., mileage and the specific risk exposure factors) and were insuficient to reflect the power of telematics data. In addition, although the application of machine learning techniques has been preliminarily discussed [4,6,33], there are no studies that have considered the interpretability of the models, making them hard to meet the practical requirements of UBI pricing.

## 2.2. Data binning methods

Data binning is a method of dividing the continuous variable into a series of discrete intervals (i.e., bins). Since continuous variables are usually dificult to interpret in insurance pricing, especially in nonlinear models, it is necessary to construct discrete tarif classes before establishing the ratemaking framework. The previous studies of Paefgen et al. [32] and Lemaire et al. [27] have managed to apply the discretized mileage factors, and the latter one has reported an obvious nonlinear efect of mileage. However, the methods they used are relatively simple and their divisions of the tarif classes also lack suficient theoretical bases.

From a statistical point of view, the samples inside each bin should have a high homogeneity. Therefore, the data binning method is similar to cluster analysis. Common methods of data binning include equal binning, quantile binning, complete linkage clustering, and Fisher's natural breaks. The first two methods, respectively, divide the continuous variable into intervals with the same length and the same sample size. The complete linkage is a hierarchical clustering method [24]. In each iteration, only the two nearest bins are merged until the termination condition is met, while the distance is determined by the two points of each bin that are the farthest apart from each other. The last method of Fisher's natural breaks [16] adopts more statistical ideas that can adaptively solve the optimal bins by minimizing the sum of the squared deviations within groups. In addition to the methods discussed above, Dougherty et al. [15] provided an overview of the other binning methods with regard to linear models.

Henckaerts et al. [21], due to the lack of consideration of the de pendent variable in the above binning process, more recently proposed a data-driven method that takes both the efects of the dependent variable and the explanatory variables into account. This method manages to strike a good balance between the risk homogeneity and the continuity of sample values in each bin, thus can more closely match the need of insurance pricing.

## 3. Data description

The dataset we use in this paper is based on a property and casualty insurance company located in mainland China that includes 2151 one year policies and the corresponding telematics driving records during the policies' periods. The time of data collection ranges from April 26, 2015 to July 8, 2017. For policy specific information, 7 traditional pricing variables of automobile insurance will be examined, including driver age, gender, vehicle price, vehicle age, seat amounts, BMS (i.e., bonus-malus systems) coeficients, and the number of claims in the last policy year. Specifically, gender, seat amounts, BMS coeficients and the last claim number are treated as categorical variables in the original data. These traditional variables cover all three aspects of personal characteristics, vehicle features, and historical claim information. For telematics data, each participating vehicle has been loaded with an on board device that contains a GPS as well as speed and acceleration sensors. During the driving of a vehicle, the real-time location information (i.e., longitude and latitude) was recorded on the device, together with the timestamp, direction, speed (both GPS speed and sensor speed), engine speed, and tri-axis accelerations (i.e., the axis of the traveling direction, side direction of the vehicle, and vertical direction of the road). These data were recorded in seconds and then aggregated by trips, while the trip was defined by a switch of the engine ignition.

We conducted a preprocessing to the original telematics data before extracting driving behavior variables. This is a two-step procedure for the deletion and interpolation of outliers. Firstly, abnormal records in which the location goes beyond mainland China and in which the speed is invalid or significantly higher than normal, were deleted from the trip information. If a certain trip contained many more abnormal records and had a poor overall quality, this trip was deleted entirely. Secondly, the driving state was then approximated by two normal records: before and after the abnormal one. On average, about 1.13% of trips were deleted for a vehicle, and 4.36% of abnormal records were approximated in a single trip. After the above steps had been completed, we determined that the cleaned telematics data is able to accurately reflect actual driving situations.

A total of 30 driving behavior variables were then derived from the telematics data, all of which are continuous variables. According to diferent perspectives of the driving risk, we have classified these variables into the following four types: usage, travel habits, driving performance, and critical incidents. Among these, usage is the statistic that reflects how much an insured vehicle was used during the entire policy year, which mainly refers to the driving mileage. The travel habit category quantifies each driver's preferences for driving speed, travel route, and travel time. Driving performance mainly includes certain key indicators of a trip, such as the average speed, the frequency of lane changes, and the stationarity of the driving. Dangerous operations like sudden accelerations, sudden brakes, and sharp turns are summarized by critical incidents. Therefore, the above four types of variables characterize the driving risk of insured vehicles from a more complete perspective. Table 2 presents a brief introduction of all predictor vari ables.

Table 2 shows that the driving behavior variables derived in this paper cover most of those factors that have been used in previous studies, such as mileage, trip distances, driving speeds, risk exposure factors, and critical events [22.38]. Similar methods were used to de: rive the above variables from telematics data, but for critical incidents, we set low/high thresholds to distinguish between normal and dan gerous events, respectively. Therefore, the fraction of sudden incidents (i.e., accelerations exceed the high threshold) to normal ones (i.e., accelerations exceed the low threshold) measures the driving risk from the perspective of relative frequencies.

In addition to the widely used variables described above, this paper focuses mainly on the categories of travel habits and driving performance, and proposes several new driving behavior variables. For example, the range of usual driving regions measures the risk exposure of an insured vehicle in its driving area. To calculate the value of this variable, we first divided the map into grids of $0 . 0 1 \times 0 . 0 1 ^ { \circ }$ , and then assigned the grids to three classes by the two-dimensional kernel density estimation and K-means clustering. Regions within the class of the highest travel density can be represented as the vehicle's usual driving regions. The irregularity of travel routes is a driver's preference for route selection. Generally, a higher irregularity implies a higher risk of trafic accidents because that the driver may not be familiar with the roads' conditions. Since driving trajectory is a set of time series data with dynamic length, we employed the dynamic time warping algorithm to calculate the distance between each trajectory as the measurement of irregularity. In addition, according to domain knowledge, continuous driving for more than 2 h is defined as fatigue driving, which may also reflects a driver's preference for long-distance travel. For the driving performance category, we introduced the concepts of driving instability, comfort score, and bad driving event into UBI pricing. Among these, the driving instability of a trip is determined by the mean absolute value of the speed diferences between each of the adjacent records, measuring the intensity of the speed changes. The comfort score and bad driving event are defined mainly based on the time weighted root mean square of the tri-axis accelerations. The comfort score quantifies the sensory enjoyment of passengers during the entire journey, as defined by Zhao et al. [44]. However, the de tection of a bad driving event is through the instantaneous variation of the weighted accelerations. According to the ISO standard for human exposures to mechanical vibration [23], the crest factor of acceleration was used as the criterion for bad driving event judgements. Last, but not least, this paper also identifies the lane change of a vehicle by analyzing the combination of travelling direction and lateral acceleration. However, due to the complicated modes of lane changes, the simple threshold judgement method may not make accurate identifications, and thus makes this variable less practical.

Since insurance companies in China imposed additional premiums on policies of which the annual mileage is more than 30,000 kilometers, these policies were entirely deleted from our data to maintain high comparability between the samples. A total of 2065 valid policies are remained for analysis, of which 317 polices had one claim, 53 polices had two claims and 6 polices had three claims. Thus 376 polices in all incurred at least one claim during the insurance period, accounting for approximately 18.2% of the total policies. Table 2 also lists the diferences between the sample mean of accident-free and accidents-involved policies. As can be seen from the descriptive statistics, certain variables $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , the annual mileage) have significantly diferent values between the two types of policies. This fact is consistent with previous studies and the intuitive judgments of insurance companies, which implies the important role of driving behavior variables in risk classification and automobile insurance pricing.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Variable binning

Fig. 1 shows the ratemaking process for UBI products. Diferent from previous studies using the continuous form of driving behavior variables directly, a binning process has been first employed to divide the variables into certain discrete factors, which can greatly improve the interpretability of insurance pricing and thus attract more favor from insurance companies [21,33]. There are currently many mature data binning methods. However, considering the special requirement of insurance pricing, which necessitates that consecutive values to be binned within the same group, a novel method for specifically constructing tarif classes must be employed.

Therefore, a regression tree-based binning method proposed by Henckaerts et al. [21], is considered in our paper. The core idea is that the way a regression tree splits ensures the continuity of the samples values in each leaf node; meanwhile, use of information entropy as the splitting criterion also obtains a high risk-homogeneity within each bin. More specifically, they further replaced traditional classification and regression trees (CART) by evolutionary trees [17] to implement bin ning, which uses the genetic algorithm to train the model and thus can search the global optimum more efectively.

For each continuous variable, we first train a generalized additive model by using only this variable as the covariate. The GAM is a flexible regression model proposed by Hastie and Tibshirani [20], which is very similar to the GLM except for using smooth functions to characterize continuous variables. Its regression equation can be summarized as follows:

$$
g (\mu_ {i}) = f _ {j} (x _ {i j} ^ {c})\tag{1}
$$

where $\mu$ is the mean of the dependent variable, $g ( \cdot )$ is the link function, $x _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { { . } } c }$ is the specific continuous variable, and $f _ { j } ( \cdot )$ is the smooth function, which in this paper refers to penalized thin plate regression splines. For risk classification, the dependent variable is binary with 0 representing accident-free and 1 representing accidents-involved, and the logit link function is used; for claim frequency prediction, the dependent variable is the number of claims and the logarithmic link function is applied, while an ofset term should also be contained if necessary (see Section 4.3).

Next, the evolutionary tree is trained, which takes the original variable as the covariate and its smooth efects in GAM as the depen dent variable, and the discrete intervals represented by leaf nodes will be the tarif classes of this variable. The objective of training is to minimize the function as follows:

$$
n \cdot \ln (\mathrm{MSE}) + 4 \cdot \alpha \cdot (m + 1) \cdot \ln (n)\tag{2}
$$

where n is the sample size, MSE is the mean squared error of the model prediction, m is the number of leaf nodes, and α is a tuning parameter, which represents the penalty on model complexity. The penalty parameter controls the balance between the predictive accuracy and model complexity. A large value of α will tend to decrease the number of leaf nodes as well as the binning accuracy, and vice versa. Thus, the key point of our binning process is to choose an appropriate penalty parameter ensuring the homogeneity of the internal samples while reducing the number of bins as much as possible. To ensure the representativeness and simplicity of tarif classes, constraints are further imposed which require that each bin must contain at least 5% of the training samples and the bins for each variable not exceed 10.

## 4.2. Predictive models of risk probability

As aforementioned, a total of five techniques are employed to build the predictive model of risk probability. Among these, logistic regression is the most practical method in the field of insurance pricing, thus it is considered as the benchmark for model comparisons. Logistic regression is a kind of GLM for the binary dependent variable. Given the linear combination of explanatory variables $\mathbf { x } _ { i } ^ { T } { \boldsymbol { \beta } } ,$ , the prediction of (conditional) risk probability is calculated by Eq. (3), and the predictive class of each sample can be further judged by a pre-defined event's threshold.

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (y _ {i} = 1 | \mathbf {x} _ {i}) = \frac {\exp (\mathbf {x} _ {i} ^ {T} \boldsymbol {\beta})}{1 + \exp (\mathbf {x} _ {i} ^ {T} \boldsymbol {\beta})}\tag{3}
$$

The second predictive model is the support vector machine (SVM) [11], which is derived from the maximum margin classifier. Its core idea is to find a hyperplane (or support vectors that describe the hyperplane) in the feature space that divides samples into two classes, so that the distance between the classes reaches the maximum. When the data are linearly inseparable, the SVM can map the sample space to a higher dimensional one by using the kernel function $\kappa ( \cdot , \cdot )$ , thus enhances the predictive power of linear classifiers. Let $\boldsymbol { x } _ { j }$ denote the support vectors and $y _ { j }$ the class labels corresponding to the above support vectors, for a given sample $x ,$ the prediction of its risk probability can be expressed as:

$$
p (\mathrm{x}) = \sum_ {j} \alpha_ {j} \cdot y _ {j} \cdot \kappa (\mathrm{x} _ {j}, \mathrm{x}) + b\tag{4}
$$

where $\alpha _ { j }$ are the Lagrange multipliers, b is the intercept term, and both are model parameters. For generalization, we choose the radial basis function as the kernel of SVMs (i.e., the Gaussian kernel).

The next two models are both tree-based approaches. Random for ests [7] are a kind of ensemble learning algorithm, which improve the predictive power of the overall model by combinations of single decision trees. For classification problems, each decision tree makes in dependent prediction to the sample, and the final label is determined by the majority-vote principle. Unlike common decision trees, the training samples of trees in random forests are generated by bootstrap sampling; meanwhile, the search of optimal splits at each intermediate node is only conducted in a random subset of the feature space. Both improvements reduce the correlation among trees and thus probably avoid overfitting. According to the suggestions by Breiman [7], the size of each subset is set to be the square root of the number of input variables, and 1,000 trees are grown for the random forest in this research.

The XGBoost model [9] is an extension of gradient boosting decision trees (GBDT), of which the core idea is to learn and improve the pre diction error. It takes the prediction residuals of the previous step as the covariate, and retrains a new decision tree on the original samples at each iteration. Therefore, the risk probability will be the sum of the predictive values of all of the decision trees. Let $\widehat { y } _ { i } ^ { ( t ) }$ denotes the prediction to the i-th sample after the t-th iteration and $f _ { t + 1 } ( \mathbf { x } _ { i } )$ denotes the prediction given by the (t+1)-th tree, the objective function of XGBoost has the following form:

$$
O ^ {(t + 1)} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} l (y _ {i}, \widehat {y} _ {i} ^ {(t)} + f _ {t + 1} (\mathrm{x} _ {i})) + \Omega (f _ {t + 1})\tag{5}
$$

where $l ( y _ { i } , \widehat { y _ { i } } )$ is the loss function measuring the cost of the diference between the true and predictive labels, and the second term is the regular term, which imposes a penalty for model complexity. Compared with GBDT, the XGBoost model performs a second-order Taylor expansion on the loss function, enabling us to obtain a more accurate prediction.

Artificial neural network is considered as the last classifier that, in this paper, represents a multi-layer perceptron model with a single and fully connected hidden layer. Neurons in the hidden and output layers will first calculate the weighted sum of all of the input signals from the previous layer, and generate their output signals through an activation function. Specifically, the rectified linear unit (ReLU) is used as the activation for the hidden layer, and the softmax function is used to map the output of the hidden layer into a probability between 0 and 1.

## 4.3. Predictive models of claim frequency

The claim frequency of a policyholder/vehicle is defined as the claim counts during a specific policy period, which refers to one-year in this research. Since the number of claims is a random variable with values of non-negative integers, it does not satisfy the normality assumption of linear regression models. The common method in the in surance field is to establish GLMs by assuming that the number of claims follows Poisson or Negative-binominal distribution.

This paper mainly investigates the Poisson regression model with the utilization of driving behavior variables, and introduces the logarithm of annual mileage as the ofset. That is to say, the regression equation can be described as follows:

$$
\log (\mu_ {i}) = \log (m i l e a g e _ {i}) + \mathrm{x} _ {i} ^ {T} \beta\tag{6}
$$

where $\mu _ { i }$ is the expected claim frequency, and $\mathbf { X } _ { i }$ is the vector of predictor variables except for the annual mileage. Under this setting, the prediction of claim frequency will be proportional to the mileage, which properly reflects the idea of “usage-based” insurance.

## 4.4. Model construction and evaluation

For risk classification problems, we assess the performance of all models based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (i.e., AUC), which is insensitive to the class imbalance and thus outperforms other metrics like accuracy or F-measures. The ROC curve has a horizontal axis of the false positive rates (i.e., the ratio of accident-free samples being erroneously predicted as accidents-in volved) and a vertical axis of the true positive rates (i.e., the ratio of accidents-involved samples being correctly detected). Ideally, the curve should be close to the upper left point, meaning that the classifier with a larger AUC value performs better. For claim frequency prediction problems, the model assessment is based on the root-mean-square error (RMSE). The smaller the value of RMSE, the lower the average errors of the prediction, and the better the performance of the model.

To compare the extrapolation ability of a model, the predictive performance will be determined by a 10-fold cross validation. This technique randomly splits the whole dataset into 10 disjointed subsets. Each time, 9 subsets are selected as the training data, while the remaining one subset is used for prediction and evaluation. Therefore, a total of 10 evaluations will be made and their average value is the final assessment for a single predictive model.

Considering that this paper has derived a wide range of driving behavior variables but only has a limited number of observations, using all the variables directly may probably cause overfitting and thus leading to the misjudgment of real performance of certain variables sets; therefore, we additionally perform a “wrapper” selection process in each fold of the outer 10-fold cross validation. More specifically, an internal 5-fold cross validation is applied on the training data to decide the relatively optimal set of predictor variables. At the very beginning, all variables are used for modeling and then the backward stepwise method is used to eliminate variables, until the evaluation criteria of internal cross validation reaches the maximum. The selected subset of variables will be finally used in the outer evaluation.

During the process of model construction, certain machine learning algorithms have meta-parameters that needed to be tuned, such as the inverse kernel width sigma and the cost parameter C in SVMs, and the number of neurons in neural networks. For these parameters, the grid search method is further applied in the innermost 5-fold cross valida tion, and the parameters with optimal AUC performance will be selected to train the model [28]. In this paper, the grids of the cost parameter range in $\{ 2 ^ { - 2 } , \ 2 ^ { - 1 } , \ \cdots , \ 2 ^ { 6 } , \ 2 ^ { 7 } \}$ while the value of sigma is directly estimated from the training samples, and the grids of the neuron number are multiples of 5 between 5 and 100. The specific technique we use to build and tune these machine learning models is the caret package in the R platform [25].

## 5. Empirical results

## 5.1. Construction of tarif classes

Once the penalty parameter α in Eq. (2) is determined, the process of variables binning and tarif classes construction will be straightforward, as described in Section 4.1. However, due to the diferent importance of variables, an inconsistent magnitude of the smooth efects can be expected, and it is necessary to select the penalty parameter for

each variable respectively.

In order to avoid bias, we first randomly split 30% of the full data to search the optimal penalty parameters. The grid search method is then applied on this segmented data, and in this paper we consider an uneven set of alternative values of the penalty parameter as α = {1, 2, ⋯, 10, 12, ⋯, 20, 25, ⋯,95, 100}. For each individual continuous variable, the process of grid search includes: (i) using GAM to calculate the smooth efects of the variable, (ii) given a specific value of the penalty parameter, training the evolutionary tree to obtain the corresponding binned variable, (iii) applying the 5-fold cross validation to evaluate the predictive performance of the GLM, which uses only this binned variable as the covariate, and (iv) determining the penalty parameter as the value with optimal performance in cross validation. By this way, our tuning method ensures good extrapolation abilities of the binned variables; meanwhile, tuning in the split data will not overestimate their importance in the subsequent evaluation.

Based on the tuned penalty parameter values, we perform our bin ning process on the remaining 70% of the data (i.e., 1445 samples), and construct the binned variables under the context of risk probability prediction and claim frequency prediction, respectively. The binning results are shown in Table 3 and Table 4. As can be seen that, most variables are binned into 2 to 8 classes in the risk probability model, while 2 to 7 classes in the claim frequency model. It is worth mentioning that, although the results we demonstrate are entirely based on the data, insurance companies can properly adjust the number of bins of certain variables in practice to enhance the acceptability of their tarif classes.

## 5.2. Comparison of risk probability models

In this section, we mainly compare the predictive performance of risk probability models, which use diferent predictor variables and diferent modeling techniques. More specifically, comparisons will be conducted based on the following three sets of predictor variables: (i) the traditional pricing variables only, (ii) the driving behavior variables only, and (iii) all variables; meanwhile, the form of the variables is also considered. Table 5 presents the comparative results of all models, and the results of cross validation are all subiect to the feature selection process described in Section 4.4.

Several conclusions can be drawn from the comparison. First, from the perspective of variable importance, the subset of traditional pricing variables has the weakest power in driving risk prediction, and the use of driving behavior variables can almost always improve the predictive ability. However, the role of traditional variables cannot be completely replaced at present, adding these variables also brings improvements in prediction accuracy. Second, regardless of the variables subsets and the models being used, applying the binned variables has generally superior performance than applying the original variables. This result proves the universal applicability of our binned variables and thus, the appropriate preprocessing step of variables binning is worthy of further research. In order to verify the power of binned driving behavior variables, for each of the five techniques, we respectively apply (one-sided) Delong's tests [13] to compare the true diferences among the AUC values on validation samples. The p-value of the test is listed in the superscript of the corresponding model, of which we set the model that uses “traditional variables only/original variables” as benchmark model. As can be seen that, when driving behavior variables or data binning method are used separately, the improvement to the traditional method is sometimes not significant; however, applying both of them together to the model always shows a relatively high significance (i.e., the p-value is less than 0.05). This proves the efectiveness of our framework.

Finally, compared with the benchmark model of logistic regression, the four machine learning models have better performance to some extent. For SVM and NN models, their performance is not so robust as the traditional model on certain variables sets, but performing better in terms of optimal prediction. The performance of RF and XGBoost models is generally superior to logistic regression, reflecting the accu racy and robustness of the tree-based models in risk classification. According to Table 5, we may find that although the application of machine learning algorithms is necessary, the improvements brought by predictor variables, including the introduction of driving behavior variables and the binning process, are stronger than the improvements by modeling techniques. This suggests a closer attention to the use of predictor variables in the insurance practice and future studies.

Table 6 lists the ordering of the importance of each (binned) variable in all the five models. The variable importance is determined by the extent to which the AUC of cross validation drops when removing the variable. For instance, removing the variable “exposure fraction between 60 and 90 km/h” from the logistic regression results in the worst predictive performance of the model, so this variable is regarded as the most important predictor variable in logistic regression. Despite varying with models, certain variables show importance in most of the cases. These variables include the traditional variables such as driver age and BMS coeficients, and the driving behavior variables such as mileage, exposures on 90‐120 km/h speed and in the evenings, average speed, maximum recorded speed, sudden accelerations and sudden left/ right turns. This is basically consistent with previous studies, but with some additional variables proposed in this paper. According to Table 6, most traditional variables are not very important in risk probability models; moreover, the use of driving behavior variables can also replace certain sensitive variables like gender being used in current automobile insurance. Therefore, it may be helpful to better extract, preprocess and analyze these variables with universal importance, both in insurance practice and in actuarial research.

For instance, among all the 2065 samples, the class of which annual mileage was between 4382.549 and 8415.710 km, has the largest sample size with a total of 565 drivers. Therefore, the category [4382.549, 8415.710) is selected as the baseline level for the annual mileage factor. The baseline levels for other factors can be similarly defined. According to the training results of the optimal XGBoost model, the sample that takes the baseline levels in all of the factors has a risk probability of 0.1340, which is defined as the baseline risk probability for subsequent analysis. At the meantime, for a specific factor, changing its value to another level only and keeping other factors at the baseline levels unchanged, the relative efect of this changed level can be calculated as the percentage increment or reduction to the baseline risk probability. Following the above steps, the relative efects of all factors in the XGBoost model can be estimated, which are shown in Table 7.

The understanding of the relative efect in Table 7 is somewhat similar to the coeficient of a factor's level in GLM. It measures the per centage change in risk probability when the level of a factor changes. However, as in the annual mileage or some other factors, the relative efects of some of their levels are equal to 0. This does not necessarily mean that these levels have no efects on the prediction of risk probability; instead, merging these levels will cause a significant decrease in the AUC value. In this regard, we further conduct a sensitivity analysis based on the bootstrap method, to find confidence intervals for the relative efects. More specifically, the relative efects of all levels will be re-estimated 1000 times on the bootstrap samples, and the intervals with 95% confidence can be directly derived from the results of the 1000 estimations. The confidence intervals for each level are also listed in Table 7.

With the range and center of the confidence interval, one can understand the impact of a specific level more accurately. Taking the annual mileage factor as an example, when the mileage of a vehicle is between 0 and 12,122 km, its risk probability will slightly decrease as the mileage increases, while the probability will rise with the mileage when exceeding 12,122 km. Actually, there is not a strictly monotonic but more like a U-shaped relationship between the risk probability and the annual mileage, as once reported by Cripe and Fiete [12]. The reasons are complicated. For instance, when the annual mileage is relatively low, the little risk caused by the increasing usage may be alleviated by the learning efects of driving behaviors; however, after a certain threshold, the mitigation is no longer obvious, and there may also indicate a change in the usage of the vehicle (e.g., from private to commercial use), both resulting in a significant increase in risk probability.

Generally speaking, the overall trend of the efects of each variable is reasonable. However, in the tails of the two variables “fraction of sudden left/right turns”, their specific efects have both dropped abnormally. We attribute this mainly to the reason for the small size of samples in the tail of the variable, thus further research needs to be conducted on larger data. Even so, insurers can additionally merge some of the intervals to strike their own balance between the accuracy and interpretability of pricing. The complete rating table for UBI pro ducts can be further derived by combining all these tarif classes.

## 6. Conclusion and discussion

In recent years, the concept of UBI has been gradually recognized by both insurance companies and policyholders, and the insurance market is calling for a mature UBI product. This paper proposes a ratemaking framework for UBI products, and tests the efectiveness of the framework in two scenarios, i.e., risk classification and claim frequency prediction. We first derive the most extensive driving behavior vari ables from the telematics data and make a full comparison of their applications in insurance pricing. More specifically, for risk probability prediction, the efects of diferent variables have been investigated in logistic regression and several machine learning techniques, while traditional Poisson GLM has been used for claim frequency modeling. Based on the stepwise feature selection, the added values of driving behavior variables are verified, which provides a practical basis for the development of UBI products.

Several conclusions can be drawn from this paper. First, for risk classification problems, advanced machine learning techniques have good performance to some extent, and the XGBoost model has a gen erally highest accuracy of prediction. Second, driving behavior variables play crucial roles in pricing automobile insurance. As discussed in Section 5, the use of driving behavior variables in risk probability models can always improve the prediction accuracy, regardless of their forms and the models being used. The improvements are quite significant, indicating a stronger and more direct causal relationship exists between these variables and the driving risk. However, using driving behavior variables in claim frequency models should be more cautious. Since the claim frequency model has relatively few training samples, using all variables directly may lead to overfitting and a preprocessing step of feature selection is necessary. Similar to risk probability models, the claim frequency models with least prediction errors mainly use the driving behavior variables, which also verifies the superiority of UBI products. Last, but not least, the framework which applies the binning method to construct discrete tarif classes is appreciated in both theoretical and practical aspects. No matter in risk probability or claim frequency models, using binned variables in GLMs will ultimately be better than using original variables. Moreover, the binned variables can be further used to improve the interpretability of machine learning models, making it easier to construct the actuarial rating table while keeping a high prediction accuracy. Although the models and research scenarios are diferent, we are still able to find certain (binned) variables which have universal importance in automobile insurance. Insurance companies may refer to these variables when building their own premium structures for UBI products.

To sum up, our ratemaking framework not only has a high prediction accuracy but also satisfies the interpretability requirements of both regulators and insureds, thus it may have certain application values for practical UBI pricing.

Further research can be conducted from the following aspects. First, the research on UBI may be more closely combined with transportation and engineering fields. This extra knowledge may help insurers identify abnormal data and more deeply understand driving risks by introducing extra environmental and trafic variables. Second, the process of data binning in this paper is only based on the GAM, while the efects of the explanatory variables may difer from other models. Therefore, it is necessary to explore how to quantify these efects in a specific machine learning model, and then solve the optimal construction of tarif classes on this basis. Last, more data needs to be collected to investigate the efects of driving behavior variables and implement our ratemaking framework in loss severity prediction.
