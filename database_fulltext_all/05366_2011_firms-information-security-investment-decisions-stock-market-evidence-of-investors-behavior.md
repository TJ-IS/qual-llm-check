## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Information security investment Market value Event methodology Abnormal returns Investors' behavior Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX)

## a b s t r a c t

In the information society, it is important for <sup>fi</sup>rms to manage their core information resources securely. However, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of measuring the return on an IT security investment is one of the critical obstacles for <sup>fi</sup>rms in making such investment decisions. By utilizing event methodology, this study examines the value of an investment in IT security, based on stock market investors' behavior toward a <sup>fi</sup>rms' IT security investment announcements. Based on a sample of 101 investment announcements of <sup>fi</sup>rms whose stocks are publicly traded in the U.S. stock market between 1997 and 2006, we <sup>fi</sup>nd substantial support for the hypotheses that information security investment leads to positive abnormal returns for <sup>fi</sup>rms. Interestingly, security investments with commercial exploitation tend to result in higher returns than those for IT security improvement. Another interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding is that stock market reaction to security investments shows higher abnormal returns after the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX) than any of those before it.

## 1. Introduction

In examining the recent business environment, the Internet is a critical infrastructure for many organizations. In this electronically networked world, it is more important for organizations to manage various risks on IT components than to apply IT for competitive advantages [14]. As networked computers and connections to the Internet <sup>fl</sup>ourish, the numbers of unsecured and unpatched computers on the networks have also increased substantially in the recent past. These unsecured computers may result in serious threats to organizations and individuals alike. According to Computer Emergency Readiness Team (CERT), the number of computer security incidents has increased dramatically from 1996 to 2003 (Table 1).

For Internet based companies, threats to security or computer incidents can be serious problems which can affect business continuity. The CSI/FBI computer crime and security survey indicated that 95% of 700 organizations from both private and public sectors such as <sup>fi</sup>nance industry, high-tech industry, manufacturing, governments including federal, state and locals and educational institutions had experienced more than 10 website incidents during 2005. The incidents include denial of service, telecom fraud, unauthorized access to information, virus, <sup>fi</sup>nancial fraud, insider abuse of net access, and system penetration [33]. The negative impact of security breaches has been discussed in several studies [15,36,40,41]. For companies, allocating resources to information security is imperative to keep their valuable information secure and to prevent damages stemming from IT vulnerabilities. According to the CIO survey, an average IT security budget in 2003 accounted for 11% of companies' IT budgets, up from 9.5% in 2002 [17].

A rational decision maker will invest further in information security if the cost of the investment is less than the risk of loss or if the investment itself has a positive return for the company. To accurately measure bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of information security investment, it is critical to have information about the likelihood of information security incidents as well as the impact of information security risks, which can be caused by unsecure information systems. However, one signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem for companies in allocating resources for information security comes from the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of measuring the cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t of information security investment. Measuring the exact amount of return on information security investment (ROSI) is always challenging due to limited data for computing the likelihood and cost of information security risk factors. According to the report from the CISSP forum and the ISO27k implementer's forum, information security risk is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a combination of the likelihood of an event, that are incidents or attacks on the information systems, cause negative impact on asset, group of asset or an organization” [18].

The report from the Government Accounting Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (GAO) points out the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties to assess information security risk. Since information security factors are continually changing because of rapidly developing technologies, it is very hard to acquire enough historical data to evaluate the likelihood and cost of information security risk factors. The report also indicates that it is very complicated to try to estimate various indirect costs. For example, implementing new controls for securing information may lead to potential productivity loss in an organization, which is impossible to estimate [31].

While many studies in information system security focus on conceptual development [58] and economic models that suggest an optimal amount of security decision making and measurement [34,53], this study investigates the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of information security based on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's value in the stock market. Our study proposes that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's information security investment activity affects the market value of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. If there is a tangible impact on the value of a <sup>fi</sup>rm when security investment activities are disclosed, it can be interpreted as evidence for the value of the security investment to the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Since the accurate measurement of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from information security investment is not easy, our study suggests another aspect of its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the investment stakeholders by hypothesizing different stock market reactions accompanied by information security investment activities.

Based on an analysis of the relationship between information security investment announcements and the stock price change, this study <sup>fi</sup>nds empirical evidence for the economic value of information security investments.

We investigate the following research questions:

1. Do a <sup>fi</sup>rm's security investment announcements have a positive impact on its market value?

2. Does the stock market react differently to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's intention of investment in information security?

3. What is the effect of information security related laws on the market reaction to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's security investment activities?

To study these questions, we adopted an event methodology to observe investors' reactions to information security investment activities of <sup>fi</sup>rms. We tested our research hypotheses with public announcements of information security investment over a 10 year period from 1997 to 2006. Based on our results, we argue that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for further research on the impact of information security investments and on the measurement of the economic value of information security investments.

## 2. Literature review

Prior research in the study of event methodology tradition in the area of management information systems can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two: the literature that focuses on the market impact of security breaches [13,15,40,41] and the literature that focuses on IT investment [2,16,22,43].

The accurate estimation of security bene<sup>fi</sup>t is a key factor in performing the security economic analysis [8]. For effective security investment decision making, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost estimation for a security investment is necessary. The cost of information security can be calculated by the capital or operating expenditure on hardware, software, and personnel [35]. Managers usually rely on data to support their decision making [4]. However, many security managers make a decision based on their experience, judgment, and their best knowledge because the bene<sup>fi</sup>t estimation for security investment has been dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to determine due to a lack of historical data, a lack of effective metrics, and the complex and sensitive nature of security [12].

Several studies suggest using cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis for effective information security decision making [12]; [29,35,45]. Effective security investment decisions can be made based on the analysis of expected loss from an information security risk and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information security investment which comes from the effectiveness of the countermeasure of security vulnerabilities and breaches as well as preventing future loss by mitigating information security risks. The research of Kim and Lee suggests a methodology can compute ROI of information security investment based on a cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factor analysis [45]. However, the largest body of information security research is mostly focused on the technical aspects of security such as the data encryption methods and access control [35].

Other researchers investigate the economic aspects of information security. Among the studies which use economic approaches to research information security, several use market reactions, based on abnormal returns, to calculate the direct cost of security risks [1,13,15,40,41,60].

The study of Campbell et al. [13] examines the economic effect of information security breaches reported in newspapers on publicly traded US corporations. They found that a highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative market reaction to information security breaches involved unauthorized access to con<sup>fi</sup>dential data. In addition, they discovered differing stock market reactions depended on the degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality of the breached data [13]. The research of Yayla and Hu investigates the impact of security breaches on stock value [64]. Hovav et al. also use market reactions to assess information security risk [40,41]. According to their research, the stock market has penalized Internet speci<sup>fi</sup>c companies more frequently than non-Internet speci<sup>fi</sup>c companies regarding denial-of-service attack announcements. For the breached <sup>fi</sup>rms in their sample set, an average 2.1% loss occurred in their market value within two days of the announcement. However, this security breach announcement positively affected the market value of security developers.

The study of Acquisiti et al. investigated how a company's privacy incidents affect its value in the market by examining stock price change [1]. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated that an event of privacy breach has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on its stock price on the day when the privacy breach announcement was made. The economic implication of software vulnerability was also examined by event methodology. Telang and Wattal explored a stock market reaction to an announcement of software defects. They con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that a software defect announcement caused a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loss of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's market value [60].

As we discussed previously, many studies have generated <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the cost of poor security is very high so that a <sup>fi</sup>rm can face signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic losses due to such information security incidents. The main purpose of this study is to examine the economic bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's information security investment based on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's value on the stock market. Since there are several studies which focused on the economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information security investment by discussing how much such investment would increase returns [39,45,54], this study will contribute to the existing literature on the economic bene<sup>fi</sup>t of information security investment by suggesting a new approach to examine economic bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the investment.

## 3. Theoretical underpinnings and research hypotheses

Event methodology has been utilized in IT literature to examine the relationship between an IT event and its impact on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's value.

There are many prior studies on IT investment announcements which found positive relationships with increases in the market value at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level. The research of Im et al. [43] could <sup>fi</sup>nd positive abnormal returns on small sized <sup>fi</sup>rms' stock market value for innovative IT investment announcements [43]. Chatterjee et al. [16] con<sup>fi</sup>rm the effect of an IT investment announcement on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's value [16]. Their research results show that IT infrastructure investments are associated with signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive abnormal stock market returns and rises in trading volume when <sup>fi</sup>rms announce such investments. IT-based knowledge management and the Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) project investments announcement also brought about signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive market reactions for the <sup>fi</sup>rms [55,56].

## 3.1. Information security investment announcement and abnormal returns

This study is based on ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market hypothesis. The research of Fama et al. describes an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient stock market as one which always fully re<sup>fl</sup>ects any new information to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's stock price [28]. When new information is released in the stock market, a <sup>fi</sup>rm's value is adjusted quickly by market reaction to the new information [27]. The value adjustment process by the stock market would include any expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost related to the new information [11]. This ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market hypothesis has been empirically tested and con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed by many studies in <sup>fi</sup>eld of <sup>fi</sup>nance [50]. Depending on the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market hypothesis which represents that stock price re<sup>fl</sup>ects new information in the stock price, this study utilizes the event methodology to capture the abnormal returns on the stock market price toward information security investments announcements.

The main concept of this study is that an information security investment will bring positive abnormal returns on the stock market price. Thus, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the information security investment as an allocation of resources to improve or protect the information security of the organization, the products, and services. For example, the investment of capital associated with information security activities pertaining to a host of items, including hardware, software, personnel and training, is also a form of an information security investment.

Disruptions in information systems could threaten the survival of organizations [47] because a recent business environment is heavily dependent on information as well as computer systems. It is very critical for organizations to secure information systems from various information security risks. Investments in sound and strong IT security infrastructures lead to the stable protection of the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, integrity, and availability of information resources [47]. The research of Liu et al. con<sup>fi</sup>rms that continuous information security investments contribute to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reducing security incidents of information systems. Based on survey results of 2168 enterprise, they empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rm that information security investments of reinforcing information system defense measure, information security policy and human cultivation reduce computer virus incidents [46]. The research of Bojanc and Jerman-Blazic also asserts that continuous investments in information security measures mitigate losses from cyber threats and information system failures [9,10]. This study suggests that a company's public announcement of information security investment brings abnormal returns on its stock price. To bring positive abnormal returns, a <sup>fi</sup>rm's public announcement regarding an information security investment could capitalize future bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

An organization could generate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by preventing losses caused by information security incidents. According to the research of Kim and Lee, information security investments bring operational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by providing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost-saving for an organization. After information systems are disrupted by information security incidents, a company needs to make extra expenditures for equipment and a workforce (e.g. temporary employment and overtime work) to restore damaged information systems. It is also faced with various direct costs from work loss to possible compensation for customers. However, investing in information security will reduce potential future incidents so that a company could expect bene<sup>fi</sup>t by saving any unexpected cost due to computer incidents [45]. Applying security functions in an earlier stage in the software development process is less expensive than <sup>fi</sup>xing security defects in software products after they are released in the market [39]. Even though a <sup>fi</sup>rm's core product is not an information security product such as anti-virus software and <sup>fi</sup>rewall, making investment in adding information security functions in the products could also bring bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by mitigating risks from cyber-threats.

Since any positive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an event in the stock market can appear as positive abnormal returns on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's stock price on the day of the announcement, this study suggests that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's public announcement about information security investment will cause positive abnormal returns on its stock market price. Therefore, we hypothesize that there will be positive abnormal returns from the stock market upon the <sup>fi</sup>rm's security investment announcement.

H1. On average, the announcements of information security investment will lead to positive abnormal returns for the <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 3.2. Information security investment with commercial exploitation

This study investigates the impact of information security investment as two different types of investments: information security investment for IT security improvement and information security investment with commercial exploitation. For example, if a bank decides to adopt an information security solution or services from an IT security vendor such as Symantec Corp to protect its assets and its customers from various cyber threats, the bank should make an investment in IT security improvement. This study categorizes theses information security investments as information security investments for IT security improvement.

On the other hand, information security investment with exploitation refers to an investment designed to enhance a <sup>fi</sup>rm's business asset over the protection purpose. If a company makes an investment to obtain security solutions and services not only for protection purposes but also to generate tangible returns such as selling more product and increasing the market share, this study categorizes the investment as an information security investment with commercial exploitation. Since this research investigates the impact of an information security investment, we explore the abnormal returns of only the buyer's side. If a company, which has information security business as its core like Network Associate, made an IT security investment such as adding stronger measures to its anti-virus software, this investment decision should be regarded as R&D or product development investment. To make a clear distinction between information security investment with commercial exploitation from the investment for R&D or product development, this study applies information security announcement with commercial exploitation only to companies, which do not have information security as their core business but make an investment decision to obtain IT security elements in order to increase its business assets. For example, if a company, providing software management services, makes an investment to improve its services by using the information security services and products of a security vendor, it can generate more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts by expanding to new customers with its improved services. This investment is categorized as an information security investment with commercial exploitation. The examples of announcements are presented in Table 3. In Table 3, the column named company indicates the target <sup>fi</sup>rm for investigating abnormal return of a stock price.

Regarding the categorization criteria for the investment type, this study refers to DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani [23]. According to their study, an IT outsourcing decision is categorized based on intent. Two main intentions are IT improvement and commercial exploitation [23]. They de<sup>fi</sup>ned IT outsourcing for IT improvement as upgrading existing IT resources and skills. IT outsourcing for exploitation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an investment decision for creating new markets and channels to generate pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

IT improvement leads to upgraded IT capabilities to enhance a <sup>fi</sup>rm's competence [7,30]. However, if an organization attempts to take a further step, it can utilize an IT investment to generate more business, which enables the company to sell enhanced products and services to customers. By IT investment with exploitation, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can have direct and tangible returns on their IT investment as compared to IT investment for IT improvement [2]. In their study, Agarwal et al. discovered that the stock market shows higher cumulative abnormal returns with exploitation than those with IT improvement. The study by Das et al. [21] investigated the effect of commercial exploitation intent on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's market value. According to their study results, the announcement of the commercial exploitation intent generated the greater market value of the <sup>fi</sup>rm than did other strategic alliance announcements such as marketing, distribution, or manufacturing process [21].

In our research, we investigate how the market investors react to each type of information security investment; information security investment for IT security improvement and information security investment with commercial exploitation. We believe that information security investment announcements with commercial exploitation are associated with more positive market reactions than those of IT security improvement The <sup>fi</sup>rm's information security investments add substantial value to its business assets which can favor the <sup>fi</sup>rm's market share in the industry, the market will evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>rm's value more positively as compared to information security investments that have only an improvement intent. We examine the effect of security investment announcements with commercial exploitation on stock market reactions by proposing the following hypothesis.

H2. Announcements of information security investments with commercial exploitation will lead to a greater amount of abnormal return than investments for IT security improvement alone.

## 3.3. Legislation in information security

Legislation on information privacy and computer security, such as the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) has made information security no longer optional for organizations [8]. This legislation places tremendous pressure on organizations to improve their security, reliability and the provable accuracy of corporate <sup>fi</sup>nancial data [42]. In order to comply with these regulations, organizations need to be equipped with well de<sup>fi</sup>ned information privacy policies, plans, and an IT security infrastructure by adding security hardware, software, and personnel. Since government legislation actively responds to public information privacy and security concerns, the legislation of regulations in cybersecurity can re<sup>fl</sup>ect that the government, commercial enterprises and individuals increase in concerns about cyber threats to their privacy [42]. Because of the legislation, top executives need to take more seriously the issue of protecting information, not only to secure their positions in the company but also to avoid violating the law, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences their decision making on security investments [5].

The research of Ghose and Rajan suggests that mandatory investment as a result of regulatory compliance in<sup>fl</sup>uences production output quantities, the extent of market competition, and social welfare [32]. According to them, the legislation generates distortions in IT security investment and internal control investment because organizations need to allocate resources for other activities to IT security. Because of these distortions, the law in information security negatively impacts production output, the extent of market completion, and social welfare. However, the methodology of this research uses an economic modeling approach so that these results may not be supported by empirical data.

H3. Legislation in information security affects the size of abnormal returns toward a <sup>fi</sup>rm's information security investment announcement.

## 3.4. Size of information security solution vendor

As stated above, investment in information security is related to the cost of security software, hardware and personnel. Sometimes strategic investment in information security can be a long term project at the infrastructure level because it requires the vendor's ability to develop security solutions, initial and ongoing technical and business support, as well as constant upgrades with improved technical and business capabilities [55]. In addition, the large vendors always improve their products or services, which would help buyers to make a wise investment [61]. Prior IS studies suggest that the investment in the two top vendors in ERP system projects yields greater market valuations for <sup>fi</sup>rms [37]. Since there are several large vendors in the information security market, we expect that large vendors have an effect on the amount of the market valuation for the information security investment announcement. Therefore, we hypothesize;

H4. Information security solution vendors with large <sup>fi</sup>rm sizes affect the size of abnormal returns.

## 4. Method

This study uses an event methodology approach in order to examine stock price changes corresponding to unanticipated business events in the market. The event methodology is based on the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market theory which states that new information in the market will fully re<sup>fl</sup>ect on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's stock price [27,28]. McWilliams and Siegel point out that the event methodology has the advantage of capturing the relationship between event and market reactions [51]. This methodology is widely applied in accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance literature [26,48,52] as well as in the information systems literature [2,16,22,25,43,55,56,59]. Because the market is not capable of anticipating when <sup>fi</sup>rms will make an information security investment announcement, it is appropriate to use the event methodology to catch unexpected business events in the stock market. Although an event date is uncertain, the event methodology is still effective [38]. Therefore, this study selects the event methodology to investigate whether information security investment announcements would affect the stock price in the market. Using this methodology, the present study investigates the stock price of <sup>fi</sup>rms, which are affected by information security investments. To capture abnormal returns in the stock market, this study only analyzes buyer companies. For example, if M&T Bank adopts information security service from Symantec, this study is able to capture abnormal returns of only M&T Bank's stock price for the data analysis. If Software Spectrum makes an investment to provide customer services and products with information security service working with Network Associates, this study also captures the abnormal returns of only Software Spectrum's stock price for the data analysis.

With these initial search terms, we found approximately two hundred announcements with the term “Information Security”, <sup>fi</sup>fty announcements with “Information Assurance”, two hundred announcements with the term “Data Security” and one hundred announcements with the term “Security Implementation”. In addition, we used the names of information security vendors such as Symantec, Network Associates, VeriSign and IBM in order to collect around three hundreds announcements regarding information security investments. We collected announcements for all available dates which turned out to be a 10 year period from January 1st, 1997 to December 31st, 2006 and generated around500 announcements. After reading the abstract of the articles, we found 155 announcements after removing irrelevant announcements with information security investment from 500 announcements. As we went through the articles, we removed the announcements relating to private <sup>fi</sup>rms because we could not collect the stock price from private equity <sup>fi</sup>rms. We also eliminated any repetitive announcements and keep one announcement. Because the Lexis/Nexis Academic database provides articles and reports from various sources, we happened to <sup>fi</sup>nd the exact same announcement in the same date from two sources, leading us to keep only one announcement by eliminating repetitive announcement. ## 6.7. Computation of cumulative abnormal returns (CAR)

Obtaining the stock prices from the Center for Research in Security Price (CRSP) and <sup>fi</sup>nancial data from COMPUSTAT databases, this methodology establishes the market model for each <sup>fi</sup>rm. The market model is described as:

$$
R _ {i t} = \ref {e q : 1 2 . 3 5}\tag{1}
$$

Where,

$$
\begin{array}{l l} R _ {i t} & \text { Return   for   the   firm } i \text { on   day } t \\ R _ {\mathrm{mt}} & \text { Return   for   the   market   on   day } t \\ \alpha_ {i}, \beta_ {i} & \text { Estimated   parameters } \\ \varepsilon_ {i t} & \text { Error   term   for   firm } i \text { on   day } t. \end{array}
$$

For the market model, we ran the regression between each <sup>fi</sup>rm's stock price and market return in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd the parameters, $\alpha _ { i }$ and $\beta _ { i } .$ The return on the market is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the change of the stock market value as a whole [44]. We used 255 estimation days of the stock price to <sup>fi</sup>nd the parameters in the market model; we started from t=−300 to t=−45 of the event date to ensure that the event returns would not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the market parameters [3]. Ending 45 days before the event date is consistent with the previous studies [16,55,56].

McWilliams and Siegel pointed out that using the longer event window might result in a false inference of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant events [51]. Using short and various event window such as (−1, 1), (−2, 2), (−1, 0) and (0, 1) in the event methodology prevent from catching abnormal returns of confounding events. Armitage suggested that the short event window makes it easy to catch abnormal returns [3]. According to MacKinlay, the event window can extend to multiple days prior to and after the day of the announcement [49]. Additionally, prior studies in information system used short event windows. Table 2 summarizes previous studies on IT investment announcements. Therefore, we looked at event windows of (−1, 1), (−2, 2), (−1, 0) and (0, 1) which the prior studies examined. Event windows represent a period of days in which the impact of the announcement on the stock price is measured [55]. For example, (−1, 1) means stock prices are measured during three days: the day before the event date (−1), the day of the event date (0) and the day after the event date (1).

After determining the market model, we computed the difference between expected returns and actual returns, called abnormal returns. The abnormal return (AR) is computed as $A R _ { i t } = R _ { i t } - ( \alpha _ { i } + \beta _ { i } R _ { \mathrm { m t } } )$ where $R _ { \mathrm { m t } }$ is the market return on day t using the equally weighted CRSP index as a market return. The abnormal returns are cumulated over a speci<sup>fi</sup>c time interval $( t _ { 1 }$ to $t _ { 2 } ) ,$ , event window. Cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) and mean CARs for n stocks are calculated as

$$
C A R = \sum_ {t = t _ {1}} ^ {t _ {2}} A R _ {i t} \quad \text { Mean   } C A R = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} C A R _ {i} / n
$$

Following Im et al. [43], we computed Z-statistics using standardized abnormal returns (SAR) which can be calculated by dividing a <sup>fi</sup>rm's abnormal returns by its standard deviation in order to test the statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance on the abnormal returns.

In order to improve the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we also conducted a nonparametric analysis adding to the already existing parametric tests [49]. According to Corrado and Zivney and Cowan, the sign test of the nonparametric test is considered to be better and more powerful under the null hypothesis where the fraction of positive CARs is the same as the expected return in the estimation period [19,20]. The sign test is a simple nonparametric statistical test which compares one pair of observations by counting the number of positive and negative differences [24]. Based on the ratio of the number of positive signs to the number of negative signs, SPSS 15.0 calculates Z score to test the statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance.

## 9. Conclusion and discussion

In an information society, investments in data and information security are unavoidable expenses for <sup>fi</sup>rms. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to measure the direct return from IT security investments. Based on our main hypothesis, that <sup>fi</sup>rms' IT security investment decisions will cause a positive reaction from investors in the stock market, this study found a meaningful result in that announcements regarding information security investment generate tangible returns from the stock market as an abnormal return. This study result can be interpreted that investors positively react toward a <sup>fi</sup>rm's IT security investment decision.

Our <sup>fi</sup>nding on Hypothesis H1 indicates that the market rewarded an average of 1.46% excess return on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's values with regard to security investment announcements. During the ten year period, security investments were met with a lot of skepticism since the impact of security investment is felt only if things go wrong and IT improvement cannot be measured, unlike other IT investments. Firms were faced with the dilemma that security investments might have no positive return [6]. However, contrary to folklore, investors reacted positively to information security investment announcements, hence; security investments could enhance the <sup>fi</sup>rm's market value. The results of this study indicate that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's information security investment decision could generate abnormal returns on its' stock market price. This <sup>fi</sup>nding supports the fact that stock market investors recognize the importance of information security as well as expect bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from information security investment. On the other hand, we observe some negative market reactions toward information security investment although they are very small to make a huge in<sup>fl</sup>uence on mean CARs. Negative market reactions can be generated by many reasons such as amount of <sup>fi</sup>rm's cash and negative reaction toward <sup>fi</sup>rm's resource allocation or investment priority.

The data analysis results also con<sup>fi</sup>rm that information security investments with commercial exploitation caused more favorable reactions from the stock market as compared to those for IT security improvement. The results of our research indicate that the average CARs for information security investments with commercial exploitation was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than that for IT security improvement. From this result, we could conclude that information security investment for commercial exploitation affects the stock market more positively. If market investors can expect direct and tangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the information security investment, their expectations are re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the value of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in the stock market. This research <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that information security investments need to be considered one step further from a basic purpose, protecting data and information against risks such as cyber threats, privacy incidents, and various natural disasters. For example, Nokia, one of the leading cellular device producers, decides to make an information security investment. Their investment not only provides new service with an information security function on their products but also takes a further step to give current customers con<sup>fi</sup>dence in information security. Their investment could also attract new customers who are aware of the importance of information security and, thus, allow them to expect increasing market shares and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. In this case, their investment announcement could generate larger amounts of CAR than an announcement of an IT security investment with an information security improvement purpose.

Another important hypothesis of this study was to test the impact of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX), which was enacted in July 2002 on investors' reactions toward an information security investment announcement. According to our results regarding Hypothesis H3, investors respond more positively to security investment announcements after the enactment of SOX as compared to the situation before the law. After the SOX enactment, the abnormal returns for security investment announcements were larger than before the enactment. Thus, we can conclude that the SOX is one of the important factors affecting investor behavior in the market as a signal to make companies aware of the importance of IT security investments. The enactment of SOX caused investors to react more positively in the stock market. As a result, we conclude that legislative efforts contribute to increasing awareness of the importance of information security and to arousing attention to information security investment announcements. It is important for shareholders and investors to be aware of the importance of the investment for organizations which plan to do so. The effort of publicizing managers' reasons for making information security investments leads to more favorable reactions from shareholders and investors. In addition, if the IT security investment could generate direct bene<sup>fi</sup>t through an increasing market share or adding new functions to the existing service and product, the company can expect more CAR.

Whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rst 3 hypotheses are supported by our data test results, Hypothesis H4 was not. Our empirical test results reveal that the announcements relating to large vendors like Symantec and Network Associates did not affect the CARs. Among our control variables, industry, <sup>fi</sup>rm size and time were statistically not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, which indicates that abnormal returns corresponding to security investment announcements were not controlled by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's industry, size and time.

The limitation of our study is in the data characteristics. This study used secondary sources such as newspapers and magazines to <sup>fi</sup>nd IT security investment announcements, and these sources of information do not have any details of the investments. The sensitive but important information required for the estimation of the value of IT security investments, such as how much monetary value the <sup>fi</sup>rms spent on the security investment cannot be acquired due to this speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristic of the data source i.e. public announcements on news wires. Compared to previous IT investment studies, the 101 sample size is large enough, due to the fact that we searched for announcements over a long period time (1997–2006) to complement the sample size. However, a larger sample size would have provided this study with richer perspectives regarding information security investment on market reaction. The study will be extended in the future to compare matched pairs of data in order to do a complete investigation of the loss quadrant: stock market investor behavior toward a <sup>fi</sup>rm's information security breach event as well, as the security investment announcements as a result of the breach. Another useful implication for a future study can be considering additional aspects of information security such as interoperability and security standards, which could not be included in this study due to the limitation of the data.

Information security investment is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected by managers' perceptions [63]. For the practitioner, this study provides useful insights on measuring returns on the IT security investment. For <sup>fi</sup>rms, spending resources on information security has been regarded as expenditure rather than an investment because the accurate return on investment is challenging to measure. However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which support the positive value of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's security investment from the market, suggest a new standpoint of information security investment decision making for the management in charge.

Our study shows that the information security investment with commercial exploitation generates more abnormal returns than information security investment for IT security improvement. When a <sup>fi</sup>rm makes a decision about an IT security investment, it should take into account the adding of information security components which helps to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll customer needs with regard to information security. Our research results provide useful cues which can help managers make a decision regarding IT security investment. Now the investors in the stock market are aware of the importance of information security and they also recognize the potential positive outcomes of IT security investments. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to make IT security investments more strategically to satisfy customer expectation regarding information security.
