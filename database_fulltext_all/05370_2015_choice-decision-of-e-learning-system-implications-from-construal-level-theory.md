# Choice decision of e-learning system: Implications from construal level theory

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: E-learning system adoption Construal level theory Technology acceptance model Perceived usefulness Perceived ease of use

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates user acceptance of a new e-learning system when users can choose between the old and the new systems. Drawing upon construal level theory and technology acceptance model, this study proposes that users’ construal level of an e-learning system interacts with their perceptions of the system (i.e., PEoU and PU) and affects their adoption intention. Data collected from 131 participants in a laboratory experiment show that a higher construal level strengthened the effect of PEoU but mitigated the effect of PU on participants’ attitude toward using the system, thus affecting adoption intention. Theoretical contributions and implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Electronic learning (e-learning) systems have attracted considerable attention from researchers and practitioners in recent years [29,49]. Basically, ‘‘e-learning system’’ is an inclusive terminology that describes various information systems that facilitate learning and teaching [20]. It helps organizations reduce costs and increase the availability of knowledge [3]. For example, massive open online courses (MOOC), as a recent innovation in distance education, allow unlimited participation and open access through the Internet, thus making higher education more accessible. Indeed, many universities such as Stanford and MIT are developing MOOC platforms to materialize the benefits of e-learning systems. The global e-learning market is expected to reach \$107.3 billion by 2015 [14]. The decision to adopt a specific e-learning system is typically made by the management of an organization or the instructors who deliver the courses or training. Therefore, the majority of e-learning system users, namely, students and trainees, may not be involved in the adoption decision. The extent to which these users will accept the system and make good use of it determines the extent to which the value of e-learning systems can be materialized [20]. In this regard, investigating the factors that may affect users’ acceptance of a new e-learning system is significant.

A plethora of studies has examined the antecedents of elearning system acceptance. Literature on this topic can be categorized into two: studies that apply the technology acceptance model (TAM) and its extension theories to understand user acceptance (e.g., [20,21,27,29]) as well as studies that examine the acceptance issue by investigating the psychological processes of users (e.g., [49,50]). A review of the literature indicates that scant research has assessed how the constructs suggested by TAM (i.e., perceived ease of use [PEoU] and perceived usefulness [PU]) interact with psychological factors to affect users’ acceptance of elearning systems. Furthermore, limited research has investigated how psychological factors may affect users’ acceptance decision when they have alternative e-learning systems. In view of the fact that it is becoming common for organizations to introduce a new elearning system before they phase out the old one, research on this area will extend our current understanding of the factors that may influence users to embrace a new e-learning system adopted by their organization. Findings of such research will also shed light on how users with different psychological characteristics would accept a specific e-learning system among a number of alternatives.

The current study is aim to address these issues. Specifically, we draw upon TAM and the construal level theory (CLT) to develop our research model. TAM suggests that PEoU and PU are the most critical factors affecting users’ acceptance of a technology. CLT also posits that different individuals can represent an event or object at different levels of concreteness or abstraction [37,39]. Furthermore, the way by which individuals construe an object considerably affects their judgment and decision making in relation to that particular object [21,38]. Building on TAM and CLT, we reason that an e-learning system can be represented at a relatively low, concrete level (e.g., a tool that performs file uploading or downloading) or at a relatively high, abstract level (e.g., a system that facilitates learning and teaching). In addition, we argue that PEoU is a low construal level perception of a system (e.g., how easy performing technical tasks is), whereas PU is a high construal level perception (e.g., how well learning and teaching are facilitated). More importantly, we argue that users’ construal level of a specific e-learning system affects the salience of PEoU and PU in influencing these users’ attitude toward using the system. Our research hypotheses are supported by data collected from a laboratory experiment that involves 131 students from a university where a new e-learning system has recently been adopted before the old one is phased out.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the theoretical underpinnings of the research and the hypotheses. Section 3 describes the methodology and Section 4 reports the data analysis results. The final section discusses the findings and contributions of this research.

## 2. Conceptual background and hypothesis development

This research centers on the idea that individuals who construe an e-learning system at different levels of concreteness or abstraction differ in the way they evaluate the system and make adoption decisions. This section first explicates the concept of construal level in detail. Then we examine the literature on technology acceptance to elaborate how differences in construal level may result in users’ differential evaluation processes in which they formulate their attitude toward using a new e-learning system and its adoption.

## 2.1. Construal level theory

CLT states that the same event or object can be represented at different levels of concreteness or abstraction [37,39]. An event construed at a relatively high level is represented by abstract, holistic, superordinate, and decontextualized features that convey the general core information. By contrast, an event construed at a relatively low level is represented by concrete, piecemeal, subordinate, and contextualized features that convey specific details of information. For example, ‘‘studying’’ can be seen as an act of ‘‘gaining knowledge’’ (a high construal level representation) or an act of ‘‘taking notes’’ (a low construal level representation). Transcending from a concrete representation to a more abstract representation involves retaining the central and core features of an object or event and omitting features that are incidental or irrelevant to the abstraction. For instance, when we represent the act of studying as ‘‘gaining knowledge’’ rather than ‘‘taking notes,’’ we retain the core value of studying (i.e., to gain knowledge) but omit the incidental details of how or the means of achieving it (e.g., taking notes in class). In this sense, a high construal level also focuses on the ‘‘why’’ aspects of an event or the end-state of accomplishing an event, whereas a low construal level focuses on the ‘‘how’’ aspects of an event or the means of accomplishing it. Since high-level representations omit the irrelevant details of an event, they are more abstract, schematic, and goal directed than low-level representations [8,30].

CLT indicates that the extent to which an object is construed at a high or low level can significantly influence what aspects of the object individuals focus on, and this focus can in turn influence individuals’ judgment and evaluation of it. For example, the construal level influences people’s attention to the global versus local features of an object [22]. People generally perceive a global, broad overview of an object (e.g., a forest) when they are far away from the object, whereas they can view the constituent details of the object (e.g., trees) when they are close toit.Therefore, a high construal level drives people to attend to the global features of an object, whereas a low construal level facilitates attention to the local features. In support of this argument, Liberman and Fo¨rster [22] presented participants with global letters that are made of local letters (e.g., a large L made of 20 small Hs). They found that the participants primed with a high construal level identified more global letters than local letters, whereas the participants primed with a low construal level identified more local letters than global letters. To the extent that a high construal level drives participants to focus on the global features, the participants may pay less attention and cognitive resources to process the local features [32], thereby facilitating global processing and impairing local processing, vice versa.

In addition, it has been shown that construal level can influence individuals’ idealistic versus pragmatic concerns related to an object. Kivetz and Tyler [17] argue that a high construal level encourages the expression of an idealistic self and thus increases the attention to identity, intrinsic benefits, whereas a low construal level encourages the expression of a pragmatic self and thus increases the attention to instrumental, extrinsic benefits. In support of this claim, many studies have found that people focus more on the identity-related benefits of an object (e.g., a course in which the professor treats students with respect and dignity) than its instrumental benefits (e.g., a course that looks good on one’s CV) when the object is construed at a high level. This trend is reversed when the object is construed at a low level [11,17].

A high construal level can also direct individuals to focus on the desirability features of an object, whereas a low construal level can direct them to focus on the feasibility features correspondingly. Desirability concerns the core value or benefits of an object or explains the ‘‘why’’ in relation to the purpose of existence of the object, whereas feasibility concerns the costs associated with using the object or the ‘‘how’’ of using the object. Desirability features are related to a high construal level, whereas feasibility features are related to a low construal level. Consistent with this conceptualization, many studies have shown that people focus more on the desirability features than the feasibility features of an object as the construal level increases (e.g., [24,25,34]).

However, recent research has found that the effects of construal level noted above may not always prevail. For example, the effects of construal level on attention to desirability versus feasibility features have been consistently demonstrated in contexts where desirability or feasibility tradeoff information is explicitly given [24,25,34]. In situations where such tradeoff information is not explicitly given, individuals may not be aware of the difficulty of making such comparisons [2] and fail to compare between desirability and feasibility features when they make a choice. Particularly, in their investigation of people’s preference between a small and large choice set, Goodman and Malkoc [12] demonstrate that when individuals are not instructed to focus on the desirability or feasibility tradeoff, priming a high construal level does not result in a preference of a large choice set (a desirable option with more choices) over a small choice set (a feasible option in which decision making is easier). They claim that a high construal level increases the perceived similarity of the options within a set and thus results in a null preference in the size of the choice set. Depending on the situations involved, the construal level may therefore result in different or even opposing effects on evaluation and choice. Hence, investigations on the effect of CLT in the under-researched situations are warranted.

In response to this research gap, the present study presents several distinct differences from prior studies related to CLT. First, while numerous studies have demonstrated that varying the construal level of an object can shift people’s attention to different aspects of the object and thereby influences the evaluation of and behavior toward it, the objects investigated in such studies are primarily student activities, such as academic courses (e.g., [17]), guest lectures (e.g., [24]), and training programs (e.g., [16], study 1), as well as a wide range of consumer products (e.g., DVD player in [11]; hotel and skincare lotion in [16] study 2; radio set in [35]; see also [39] for a review of studies related to consumer behavior). To the best of our knowledge, how construal level may affect the acceptance of a system in general, and e-learning system in particular, has not been investigated. We introduce the concept of construal level to the domain of e-learning system and make predictions that are not readily predicted by prior technology acceptance studies. We argue that construal level can shape how users represent alternative e-learning systems in their minds. These representations in turn influence users’ evaluation of the systems and the extent of their acceptance of such systems.

Second, most of the studies reviewed above focused on a single object at one time. However, in real-life situations, we rarely consider and evaluate only one object in a given situation. Rather, we often face situations in which we are presented with more than one alternative. For example, many organizations introduce a new e-learning system before they phase out the old one. During the transition period, users have the option to use either the new or the old system. As noted above, some studies hint that the effect of construal level on evaluation and choice related to a single object might be different from that related to multiple alternative options (e.g., [12,24]); the latter situation thus warrants more investigation. Therefore, in this research we will focus on situations in which users choose among different alternative systems and examine how construal level may affect the evaluation and adoption of a new e-learning system in the presence of the old one. As detailed below, we base our predictions on technology acceptance research and construal level studies that pertain to the categorization and comparability of alternative options. The reasoning is presented in detail below. Fig. 1 shows the proposed research model that summarizes the research hypotheses.

## 2.2. Technology acceptance model and construal level

TAM has been widely applied in research investigating the factors that affect individuals’ acceptance of a new technology [4,41,44]. Adapted from the theory of reasoned action, TAM identifies the relationship between users’ perceptions of a technology (i.e., PEoU and PU) and attitude and acceptance intention toward the target system [44]. PEoU refers to the degree to which an individual believes that using a specific system is free of effort. PU is defined as the degree to which an individual believes that using the related system can improve his or her performance. According to TAM, PEoU positively affects PU, and both PEoU and PU positively affect an individual’s attitude toward a new technology and thus lead to acceptance intention (e.g., [29,42,43]). These relationships have gained consistent empirical support from previous research on technology acceptance, in general, and e-learning system adoption in particular (e.g., [20,27,29]).

Drawing upon TAM and building upon previous research on elearning system adoption, we posit that the PEoU and PU of an elearning system are the two major factors that influence user acceptance. PEoU affects users’ perception of the usefulness of the e-learning system and users’ attitude toward using it. Attitude is also influenced by users’ PU of the system which in turn influences the strength of intention to adopt the system. Therefore, we present the following hypotheses:

H1. The PEoU of an e-learning system is positively related to the attitude toward using the system.

H2. The PU of an e-learning system is positively related to the attitude toward using the system.

H3. The PEoU of an e-learning system is positively related to the PU of the system.

H4. The attitude toward using an e-learning system is positively related to the intention to adopt the system.

In addition, we argue that the manner in which users construe an e-learning system influences how they represent it, their evaluation and the extent of their acceptance of the system. Specifically, we contend that PEoU and PU are two aspects of user perception of the system that represent an e-learning system at different levels of abstraction, that is, they are of different construal

levels. We find support for this contention from both CLT and information systems (IS) literature. First, according to CLT, two criteria determine which features of an object are of relatively high level and which are of relatively low level [37]. The first criterion is centrality, which means that changing a high-level feature has a greater effect on the meaning of an object than changing a lowlevel feature. If the primary goal of using an e-learning system is to facilitate learning and teaching, then the meaning and purpose of an e-learning system changes fundamentally when the extent to which it can facilitate this (i.e., PU) is altered. By contrast, the PEoU of a system alters how easily and quickly users can pick up the system; it affects the means of achieving the goal of enhanced learning and teaching more than the goal per se. Therefore, PEoU is less central and is of a lower construal level than PU. The second criterion is subordination, which means that low-level features depend on highlevel features rather than the other way round. The PEoU of an elearning system should become important only when the system is useful to users. When an e-learning system is not useful at all, ease of use is of limited relevance. Therefore, PEoU is a system characteristics subordinate to PU and of a lower construal level.

Second, in the IS literature, the Activity Theory provides a framework for understanding human–computer interactions [19]. According to Activity Theory, a human activity can be understood and represented at three different levels. At the first level, elearning can be conceptualized as a human activity, which is a basic unit of analysis in a human–computer interaction context. At the second level, this activity is accomplished through a process consisting of multiple steps or phases called actions (e.g., preparing for classes, communicating with classmates, etc.). To perform each of these actions, in turn, users need to carry out a set of routine steps called operations (e.g., executing the procedures of uploading/downloading a file via the e-learning system). In this threelevel model, the highest level, activity, is driven by an overarching motive, such as to facilitate teaching and learning in the case of elearning. Thus how useful an e-learning system is in facilitating teaching and learning (i.e., PU) becomes a relevant attribute that can represent the system at the high level. Indeed, it is well established that PU, as a high level construct, plays a critical role in affecting system acceptance by users, regardless of their level of experience with the system [31,47]. In contrast, the lowest level of activity, operation, concerns the procedures of performing routine functions in the e-learning system. Through these operation routines, users achieve the goal of supporting their learning and formulate their perception about effort required by using the system. Hence PEoU of the system should be more relevant and representative of the system at the low level, and its effect on user acceptance would diminish as users become more experienced [31,47].

Third, the notion that PEoU and PU are of different levels has gained consistent support from prior empirical studies (e.g., [15,31]). In a study of e-commerce values, Keeney [15] identified and categorized the value of e-commerce shopping to customers into two objectives: means objectives (the means that help achieve other objectives) and fundamental objectives (fundamental reasons for purchasing on the Internet). From the CLT perspective, since means objectives are the ways to achieve the fundamental objectives, they are therefore subordinate to and of a lower level than fundamental objectives. Importantly, Keeney [15] identified PEoU as a means objective, whereas perceptions that relate to the usefulness of e-commerce shopping, such as convenience, enjoyment, cost and time saving, are regarded as fundamental objectives driving consumer e-commerce usage. Similarly, Sun [31] argued that users would quickly become experienced in interacting with a system. Therefore, when users become experienced with interacting with a system, PEoU, a user perception about operating the system, would not appear to have significant effect on experienced users’ continued intention to use the system. In contrast, PU is a high-level construct that plays pivotal role in leading to continued use of the system [31].

In summary, PEoU is related to the effort required to use the system, such as the learning costs associated with adopting a system, whereas PU is related to the core value or benefits that a system offers. Drawing on CLT and studies in the IS discipline, we suggest that PEoU is a relatively low-level, secondary attribute of an e-learning system, whereas PU is a relatively high-level, primary attribute of the system. Importantly, we argue that system users who adopt different construal levels tend to rely on PEoU and PU to different extents when they evaluate a system and make system adoption decisions. We explicate our justifications below.

2.3. Construal level, evaluation of alternative systems, and system adoption

When users are presented with different e-learning systems, why and how does the construal level affect their evaluation and hence their adoption decision? Research on categorization has documented that when people make a choice between alternatives, how high or low level the decision is construed can influence evaluation of the alternative options and thereby the choice. This effect is driven by influencing how people arbitrarily categorize alternative options into groups. Specifically, the higher level a decision is considered to be, the more likely it is that alternative options are represented in abstract forms and in terms of high-level features. Therefore, alternative options tend to be represented similarly and be grouped into large and broad categories rather than be treated as different individual choices [23]. Since the options categorized under the same category are perceived to be similar [28], a high construal level induces people to search for similarities among options [9]. Doing so further increases the perceived similarity among these options.

Applying this logic into the current research context, that is, users considering whether to accept a new e-learning system when they have another choice, we argue that users’ construal levels can affect their focus on different features of the new system. When users construe the adoption decision at the high level of serving the goal of facilitating learning and teaching, e-learning systems become a means to achieve this goal. Different e-learning systems become similar and interchangeable in terms of fulfilling this high-order goal. This perception results in an increased perceived similarity in the high-level, core features of alternative e-learning systems (i.e., PU). Consequently, high-level aspects of user perception of the system, such as PU, become less diagnostic for differentiating between alternative systems and for making the adoption decision.

In this case, we argue that users need to rely more on low-level system attributes, such as PEoU, which is more diagnostic for understanding the differences between alternative systems. Since people commonly rely on factors that are relevant and diagnostic when making a decision [7], at a high construal level e-learning system users tend to rely on PEoU, the more diagnostic attribute, rather than on PU to evaluate alternative systems and to make an adoption decision. Therefore, as users’ construal level increases, the importance of PU in influencing attitude toward using an elearning system decreases, whereas the diagnosticity and hence the importance of PEoU in influencing the attitude toward using the system increases. That is, the relative importance of PU in elearning system adoption decreases and that of PEoU increases when a user construes the e-learning systems at a higher level. Accordingly, we present the following hypotheses on the moderating effects of construal level.

H5. The effect of PEoU on the attitude toward using an e-learning system (H1) is more pronounced when the system is construed at a high level than when it is construed at a low level.

## 3. Method

A laboratory experiment was conducted to examine the moderating role of construal level in influencing the effects of PEoU and PU on users’ responses to a new e-learning system. We collected data from a major university in Asia, which had recently adopted a new e-learning platform. The study was conducted when the new e-learning platform had just been introduced. Specifically, instead of terminating the old e-learning platform, the university has been running both the old and the new platforms concurrently since the new platform was introduced. In particular, for each course offered, the university opened a courseroom in both platforms. Students and instructors were invited to choose between the old and the new platforms for their respective learning and teaching activities. Although the university had the plan of phasing out the old e-learning system eventually, it did not clearly specify the cut-off date. Therefore, the context in which the study was conducted involved a choice between two alternative elearning systems.

## 3.1. Participants

A total of 131 students participated in the study in exchange for approximately US\$4. Specifically, we recruited university students who had used the old e-learning platform to participate in the study, in which they were invited to try the new e-learning platform (details are provided in the next section). Hence, the participants would have information about both the old and the new e-learning platforms when forming their adoption intention. The participants were recruited via various means, such as through e-mail invitations, distributed flyers, and posted notices around the campus. Table 1 shows the demographic information of the participants.

## 3.2. Design and procedures

The experiment was conducted in a computer laboratory. To ensure data quality, we conducted multiple experiment sessions, with each session involving no more than 12 participants. The participants were randomly assigned to a computer workstation. Each workstation was surrounded by cardboards, so that no participant could observe what the other participants were doing during the experiment.

## 3.3. Construal level manipulation

Some prior studies have shown that individuals can have different levels of construal, depending on their trait or chronic characteristics such as age [33], cultural orientation like independent–interdependent self-concept [18], and regulatory focus orientation [5,6]. However, other studies have demonstrated that construal level can also be a state variable in which it can be varied by many contextual factors, such as temporal distance, social distance, special distance, and even hypothetically of events (see [37] for a review). Because construal level can be systematically varied by contextual factors, this study manipulates the construal level of participants by asking them to think about ‘‘why’’ versus ‘‘how’’ they would use the new e-learning system for their study. Specifically, following Freitas et al. [10], we manipulated the construal level of the participants by instructing those in the high construal level condition to think about ‘‘why’’ they would use the new e-learning system for their study and those in the low construal level condition to think about ‘‘how’’ they would use the new e-learning system. The instruction sheet for the participants in the high construal level condition contained the following:

For everything we do, there always is a reason why we do it. Moreover, we often can trace the causes of our behavior back to broad life goals that we have. For example, you are about to learn using a new e-learning platform that supports courses. Why are you doing this? Perhaps to take and complete courses. Why do you take and complete courses? Perhaps because you want to earn a college degree. Why do you want to earn a college degree? Maybe because you want to find a good job or because you want to educate yourself. And perhaps you wish to educate yourself or find a good job because you feel that doing so can bring you happiness in life.

Similarly, in this study, we would like you to think why you would use Blackboard, a new e-learning platform, for your study at the University.

For example, like most people, you probably hope to find happiness in life. How can you do this? Perhaps finding a good job or being educated can help. How can you do these things? Perhaps by earning a college degree. How do you earn a college degree? By taking and completing courses. How do you take and complete courses? In some cases, you need to learn using an e-learning platform that supports courses.

Similarly, in this study, we would like you to think how you would use Blackboard, a new e-learning platform, for your study at the University.

In the same way, those in the low construal level condition were asked to report how they would use Blackboard to achieve the three objectives by completing the sentence ‘‘HOW? I would use Blackboard to do so by . . .’’ for each objective.

## 3.4. Measures

All the participants responded to the questions pertaining to the level at which they construed the e-learning system. Previous studies have demonstrated that construal level affects how abstract or concrete an object is represented by an individual [36]. High construal representations focus on the ‘‘why’’ aspects of an event [40], its desirability [24], and the goals for achieving it [1]. By contrast, low construal representations focus on the ‘‘how’’ aspects of an event, its feasibility, and the means to achieve it [37]. Based on these conceptualizations, as well as prior studies on construal level [16], we developed and adapted three items to measure high and low construal level, respectively. These items were measured on seven-point Likert scales ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ (1) to ‘‘strongly agree’’ (7) (Table 2).

The items that measure PEoU, PU, attitude toward using the elearning platform, and intention to use the platform were adapted from existing literature. These constructs were measured on seven-point Likert scales ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ (1) to ‘‘strongly agree’’ (7). The items are listed in Table 2.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Occasionally, many institutions and organizations may introduce a new e-learning system to replace the old system. During the transition period, users are presented with both the old and the new systems, and have the option to use either system. Under such a context, this study explores how individuals who construe a new e-learning system at different levels of concreteness or abstraction place different levels of emphasis on the benefits and costs associated with using the new system and how such a construal influences the effects of PEoU and PU on users’ attitude toward using the system, which in turn affects usage intention. In a laboratory experiment with university students, we show that the construal level strengthens the effects of PEoU and weakens the effects of PU on the attitude toward using the new e-learning system. These findings contribute to theory and provide important implications to practice.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions and managerial implications

This research offers several major theoretical contributions. First, CLT is introduced to extend the current understanding of technology acceptance by investigating how users’ construal of a system affects their attitude and behavioral intentions. The existing literature on technology acceptance focuses on examining the direct effects of user perceptions on specific technologies, social norms, and facilitating conditions. The possible contingency effects of psychological factors, such as construal levels, have received little attention. CLT has been widely studied in other disciplines, such as marketing and social psychology; however, it has not been widely applied whilst investigating system adoption issues. With reference to CLT, this study introduces a new perspective to the study of system acceptance, that is, users can conceptualize an e-learning system at different levels of concreteness or abstraction. Based on this perspective, we make and empirically test predications on users’ acceptance of a new elearning system; these predications are not readily made by prior studies in this area. Therefore, the present study presents a new avenue to investigate how individuals’ construal levels of the system may affect their attitude and behavioral intentions.

Second, we enrich the technology acceptance research, in general, and user resistance research, in particular, by investigating the development of different psychological factors, attitudes, and behavioral intentions of large-scale system users with different foci on system usage (i.e., why vs. how to use the system). In recent years, there has been a call for research investigating how interventions at the early stage of a large scale system’s life cycle could have a favorable immediate and ongoing impact on user acceptance [47,48]. Previous studies on user resistance have primarily focused on the effects of contextual factors, such as the training environment, whilst ignoring the effects of individuals’ psychological states [46]. The current study, in response to the call of research, shows that by appropriate manipulation of users’ psychological state, namely, the construal level of the target system, facilitates the development of consistent and favorable attitude and behavioral intentions that are conducive to users’ buy-ins to system adoption.

Third, our research extends the technology acceptance theory by considering the situation in which users face alternative options. As shown by our research, construal levels affect the weight of PU and PEoU in influencing users’ evaluation of a new elearning system when they have alternative options. A high construal level of the alternatives leads users to treat alternative systems as similar in terms of desirability feature (PU) and rely on feasibility feature (PEoU) in formulating their attitude toward

The findings of the current research also have managerial implications for institutions and organizations. Specifically, the results indicate that construal levels affect the emphasis placed by stakeholders on different system characteristics (i.e., PEoU and PU). This finding means that stakeholders with varying construal levels will likely use different mechanisms to evaluate a system. For example, the management oversees the entire organization from a bird’s eye view, sets long-range plans, and provides overarching directions for the organization. Therefore, the management makes decisions for the organization, such as those pertaining to the adoption of an e-learning system, at a high construal level. By contrast, students, who are the end users of elearning systems, primarily focus on the detailed, contextualized, and subordinate information regarding the system. Therefore, they tend to adopt a low construal level. Such a misalignment in the management’s and end users’ construal of the same system results in a different evaluation and judgment of the system. Our research findings imply that the organization can get around such problems by manipulating the related parties’ construal level of the target system.

Furthermore, our research findings indicate that when an organization introduces a new system before the old system is phased out, the management with a high construal level of the system tends to focus on the PEoU aspects of the new system. By contrast, users with a low construal level tend to focus on the PU of the new system. Therefore, the management should focus on the issues raised by users and represent the system from the users perspective to overcome the potential conflict between the management and users. This approach enables the organization to align the system assessments made by the management and users. In doing so, the chance of user resistance at later stages of the implementation of a system can be reduced. Consequently, the chance of a successful implementation in which potential benefits are obtained can be increased.

## 5.2. Limitations and directions for future research

This research has several limitations. First, student participants were recruited for the experiment; the external generalizability of the research results to other populations may therefore be limited. Nevertheless, the student participants in this study evaluated a new e-learning system and decided whether to adopt it or to stick with the old system. This scenario is a typical situation of elearning system adoption. Therefore, the results should be highly relevant to other e-learning system adoption contexts. Additionally, employing an experimental method allows us to manipulate and control the construal levels of the participants and enables us to examine the causal relationships between users’ construal level of the system and user reactions to the system. However, university students may behave differently from other groups of students, such as high school students. Future research should therefore replicate these findings in other institutional contexts.

Second, subjective data was collected from one source at one time. Therefore, the study may involve the risk of common method bias. Although the common method bias test in our study shows that common method bias is not a problem, future research should collect objective data, such as the actual system usage of users, and conduct a longitudinal study to enhance the understanding of the casual relationships among related constructs.

Finally, some CLT-related findings may lead to predictions that oppose our contentions. Some previous studies have shown that a high construal level drives people to focus on primary and desirability attributes, whereas a low construal level induces people to emphasize secondary and feasibility attributes [11,24]. Based on this line of logic, a high construal level should drive users to place more weight on PU, whereas a low construal level should direct users to weigh PEoU as more important in making adoption decisions. Although the possibility of such effects is acknowledged, it is believed that an important question should be the conditions under which these rival effects versus the effects proposed in the current study are likely to occur. In fact, a recent study on assortment size preference [12] has demonstrated that depending on the situations, CLT can induce opposing effects on people’s preference on choice set size. Further investigation to identify the conditions under which our proposed effects prevail is a relevant direction for future research. Knowledge in this area will provide significant insights into the reconciliation of inconsistencies in the preference of management and student users when adopting new e-learning systems in institutions.
