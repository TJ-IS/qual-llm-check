# Social Inclusion: The Use of Social Media and the Impact on First-Generation College Students

Ester S. Gonzalez,<sup>1</sup> Xuefei (Nancy) Deng<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>California State University, Fullerton, USA, esgonzalez@fullerton.edu <sup>2</sup>California State University, Dominguez Hills, USA, ndeng@csudh.edu

## Abstract

Social inclusion has become a pressing issue for organizations wishing to close the inequality and disparity gaps associated with underrepresented or disadvantaged groups. The challenge is becoming particularly critical in higher education institutions suffering from low retention rates and low graduation rates among first-generation college students (FGCS). In response to the challenges imposed by the notion of social inclusion, some universities and colleges are exploring the use of social networking technologies such as social media (SM) in ways that can impact social inclusion. In order to improve the understanding of how social networking technology affects the socia inclusion of FGCS, we conducted a case study in a public, Hispanic-serving institution in the United States. We used technology affordance theory to reveal various affordances actualized by FGCS and various outcomes resulting from those affordances. To explain how the actualization of differen strands of affordance produces certain outcomes, our analysis of 102 FGCS’ narratives followed an established five-step framework for identifying generative mechanisms, revealing three SM user types—Community Builders, Scholars, and Information Seekers—and four actualized affordances, namely interconnection, inspiration, insightfulness, and intense comfort, which are conceptualized into three generative mechanisms: identity booster, academic growth, and self-care. Our results provide insights into SM usage for social inclusion outcomes. By revealing how technology use can promote social engagement and mitigate exclusion experienced by FGCS, this study contributes to the broad social inclusion research on technology and disadvantaged communities.

Keywords: Social Inclusion, Social Media, Technology Affordances, Generative Mechanism, First-Generation College Students, Disadvantaged Community

Michelle Carter was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on March 30, 2021 and underwent two revisions. This paper is part of the Special Issue on Technology and Social Inclusion.

## 1 Introduction

Historically, organizations have encountered challenges in socializing and embracing new entrants (Leidner et al., 2018). While organizations have developed strategic plans focused on the onboarding and retention of new hires (Leidner et al., 2018), educational institutions have focused on increasing the retention and graduation rates of underrepresented student populations. Over the years, organizations have been aware of the need for social inclusion. Yet organizations have struggled to effectively socialize and ensure the social inclusion of their community groups. The negative impacts from the lack of social inclusion can be detrimental to organizations, whose backbone is the relationships between their internal and external customers. In recent years and without notice, a global pandemic heightened the issue of social inclusion for organizations. The pandemic increased the need for organizations to provide a sense of security and support to their community groups during turbulent times. Organizations and educational institutions were forced to make changes to their business models, business operations, and educational learning platforms quite abruptly. Organizations faced a sense of urgency in transforming themselves in a manner that was mindful of the need to create and ensure a sense of community where individuals would not become isolated or “lost” in their working environments. Thus, the issue of exclusion threatened organizations in a new way.

Notwithstanding the pandemic and similar crises, any social exclusion experience may cause people to suffer emotional distress or physical ailments (Kawamoto, 2017). For example, when people are left out of a group chat or a meeting, they are more likely to feel disconnected and alone and might wonder why they were not invited or included to begin with. In addition to perceiving a lack of support, such individuals might experience serious health issues or poor self-esteem (Kawamoto, 2017). Generally, individuals are social beings who rely on social networks for support, guidance, and a sense of community (Kawamoto, 2017). Thus, feeling “left out” may lead individuals to withdraw from their community much more quickly than expected and often without notice. As a result, it may be problematic for organizations if individuals withdraw due to a low sense of community because this can potentially cause decreases in trust and loyalty, leading to negative impacts on business operations.

Recent information system (IS) research has noted that social inclusion and the lack of social inclusion are imperative to understanding different populations (Trauth, 2017). Social inclusion refers to the notion of individuals’ ability to extensively participate in their community and social world (Sen, 2000). In addition, social inclusion is concerned with the “haves” and “have-nots” (Trauth, 2017) and the inequalities that are experienced between them. Therefore, being able to leverage technologies that support social structures may close the gap between the “haves” and “havenots” (Hsieh et al., 2008). As such, it has become critical for organizations to accelerate the digitization of the interactions between organizations and their customers. However, there is much to understand about the role of social technologies and their impact on individuals’ social inclusion. In this paper, we provide insights into technology use and social inclusion within the context of first-generation college students (FGCS). Thus, our research question is:

## RQ: How does social media use impact the social inclusion of FGCS?

First-generation college students are a systematically disadvantaged population of students enrolled in colleges or universities whose parents did not receive a postsecondary education (Redford & Hoyer, 2017).

Compared to their continuing-generation peers (e.g., students whose parents have college degrees), FGCS have higher risks of attrition in higher education. Research has found that FGCS are twice as likely to leave college without a degree (Chen & Carroll, 2005). FGCS often fail classes at a higher rate, have more student loan debt, and lower GPAs (Chen & Carroll, 2005; Ho & Wei, 2011; Ramos-Sanchez & Nichols, 2007; Voung, Brown-Welty, & Tracz, 2010). If we are to build a nation where everyone pursuing higher education will have equal opportunities and experience a successful academic life, higher education institutions must not ignore the low retention and graduation rates among FGCS. Therefore, it is critical to understand how FGCS can be empowered to take part and take advantage of the various opportunities offered at their institutions.

Researchers have suggested that social and academic integration, such as engaging FGCS in campus communities and increasing their interactions with peers, faculty, and other institutional agents improves FGCS’ likelihood of continuing in higher education (Schwartz et al., 2018). Among the useful means for enhancing social and academic integration is technology, which has been found to play a critical role in this process, as technology can “provide vehicles for active student involvement” (Terenzini & Pascarella, 1998; p. 159). Given the vast growth of social media (SM) technologies, many institutions are trying to understand how to leverage these technologies to become more inclusive and reduce the disparities that exist between FGCS and their continuing-generation peers. According to Griffin (2015), more than 98% of college-aged students use some form of social media. We believe that SM platforms—i.e., Wikipedia, Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, Instagram, Snapchat, WhatsApp, and others—are likely to impact the social inclusion experienced by the FGCS population.

Research on college students’ use of SM has demonstrated that SM use may lead to the development of resources and networks, improve students’ access to information, and heighten students’ self-esteem (Ellison et al., 2011). SM allows users to be creators and editors of web-based content that can be exchanged (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010) regardless of geographical boundaries. SM tools have been found to afford connections with various key people and sources, resulting in closer ties and a greater sense of belonging, going beyond the embedded functions and features of SM tools (Leidner et al., 2018). Furthermore, research has found that SM tools facilitate organizational processes, from acclimating new employees to enabling knowledge sharing, increasing collaboration efforts, and facilitating open communication (e.g., Boyd & Ellison, 2007; Leidner et al., 2018; Thomas & Silverstone, 2015).

Similarly, campus connections have been found to assist FGCS in effectively coping with the challenges in their college environment (e.g., Baker, 2013; Matson & Clark, 2020). As such, educational institutions have proposed intervention programs focused on developing the skills and attitudes necessary to empower FGCS to cultivate connections and discover other individuals and resources that can support their academic and career goals (Schwartz et al., 2018). Considering the potential of SM use, FGCS may be able to identify support systems and gain a better understanding of their academic culture through SM use. However, prior studies have not investigated the use of social media for developing and facilitating social inclusion processes. In a study of the digital divide and FGCS technology use, researchers called upon equity-minded global leaders to improve the outcomes experienced by FGCS because the problem of technology use (not simply access to technology) for mitigating inequalities among marginalized communities should be considered beyond the focus of digital divide research alone (Fernandez et al., 2018).

To address this and to respond to the JAIS special issue call to study social inclusion and technology, we investigated SM use and its impact on the social inclusion and academic life experiences of FGCS. In particular, this study engages in an exploratory case study on a Hispanic-serving institution in order to gain insights into how SM use impacts FGCS’ college experiences. This paper is organized as follows. First, we review the theoretical foundation of social inclusion, social media usage, and technology affordance theory. Then, we present the research method, case description, and data analysis. We conclude the paper with a discussion of our findings and their implications and offer directions for future research.

## 2 Theoretical Background

In this section, we present the theoretical lenses of social inclusion and technology affordances. We first reflect on social inclusion with a focus on FGCS. We then present the technology affordance perspective, followed by a review of the literature on social inclusion, which informs our understanding of the factors that impact one’s sense of inclusion in an unknown situation. We then apply the technology affordance lens to understand how SM technologies can support and drive social inclusion processes and outcomes.

## 2.1 Social Inclusion and FGCS

Social inclusion is based on the concept of social exclusion. While social exclusion describes the ways in which individuals are hindered from completely participating in society (Wilding, 2009), social inclusion refers to “the process of improving the ability, opportunity, and worthiness of people, disadvantaged on the basis of their identity, to take part in society” (World Bank, 2013). In essence, social inclusion can be described as a process where efforts are made to ensure equal opportunities for all individuals. Social inclusion is a multidimensional, relational, and dynamic process (Selwyn, 2002).

For FGCS, who often face obstacles in pursuing higher education and have low graduation rates, social inclusion is a critical aspect of college success. It has been reported that 43% of low-income FGCS do not continue their studies after six years, and 60% of those who leave do so after their first year (Engle & Tinto, 2008). As the nation’s economic growth relies on the postsecondary education and training of all groups, including underserved minority groups, it has become a top priority for higher education institutions to improve the continuance and degree attainment of firstgeneration college students.

Because FGCS typically have different characteristics from their continuing-education counterparts, it is imperative to understand the factors that prevent this population from achieving success when they are given the same opportunities as their counterparts. According to Nunez and Cuccaro-Alamin (1998), FGCS are more likely to be low-income, single-parent students and often enroll at two-year institutions. Similarly, Engle and Tinto (2008) found that most FGCS attend two-year commuter colleges, struggle financially, and work fulltime while attending college part-time, which leads to higher debt. As such, these factors are negatively associated with FGCS college continuance because many of these students have lower socioeconomic status (SES) and experience less academic and social integration in college.

Like all students, FGCS need social support from their family and peers while in college. However, FGCS may face particular challenges in this regard. For example, some may have greater family responsibilities and may experience guilt when stepping back from them to pursue a college education; others may not have the “know-how” to give them confidence when entering college. Thus, having social support inclusive of external resources, such as peer networks and family support, (Burrus et al., 2013) can help FGCS feel that they are not alone in this new journey and support their academic success. Prior studies have identified specific stressors that prevent students from staying on track. For example, FGCS typically have greater family obligations and personal circumstances (e.g., caregiving responsibilities) that may have a negative effect on their success in college (Burrus et al., 2013). Thus, the more support they receive from higher education institutions and the college network of advisors, counselors, and faculty, the more likely FGCS will be to feel connected and discover resources to help them manage such daily stressors. FGCS often start their college life without knowing what to expect as a new college student. One way that institutions can help FGCS increase their sense of community and achieve higher retention is by facilitating campus connections and providing targeted resources.

The use of technology can help facilitate the dissemination of resources and information and facilitate connections with campus communities to help individuals form ties and increase the number of connections within a network (Deng et al., 2022). Increasingly technologies are facilitating social inclusion (Selwyn, 2002). Specifically, SM has been credited for assisting in the socialization and acclimation of new entrants in an organization, providing resources, enabling open communication, building connections, engaging and empowering individuals, and facilitating the development of self-efficacy (Leidner et al., 2018). In order to delve deeply into the question of how SM can influence social inclusion for FGCS, we draw on the theory of technology affordances.

## 2.2 Technology Affordances

The concept of affordances was first introduced by Gibson (1966) as a way to describe the relationships between humans or animals and objects. In this view, affordances primarily refer to the possibilities of action provided by the environment. According to Gibson (1986), objects are not perceived as what they are but rather as what they can do. Consequently, people do not interact with an object without having a perception of what affordances the object provides. A few years later, Norman (1988) appropriated the term “affordances” to refer to the use of an object or action possibilities as perceived by a user within the context of human-machine interactions rather than the environment. According to this new definition, affordances are tied to “action possibilities,” which might be considered an extension of Gibson’s work (Osborne, 2015). This view considers an object’s intended uses or “real” affordances (Norman, 1999).

In information systems (IS) research (e.g., Kane et al., 2011, Leonardi, 2011; Markus & Silver, 2008), researchers have used the term affordances to describe the possibilities for action offered to an individual by an object (Volkoff & Strong, 2013). A key concept of an affordance refers to the action potential that can be taken given a technology (Gibson, 1986; Majchrzak & Markus, 2013). One can think of an affordance as the opportunity for action (Hutchby, 2001; Stoffregen, 2003), given the properties of the relationship between an actor and the technology (Volkoff & Strong, 2013). The relationship between a user and a technology can lead to not only one but multiple affordances, which can thus result in multiple outcomes (Treem & Leonardi, 2012; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). Although technology use can afford actions for positive outcomes, the interaction between the user and the technology can afford actions that introduce hindrances in other ways (Leidner et al., 2018).

The affordance perspective is difficult to completely understand. IS research continues to struggle with the distinction between a technology affordance and technology use (Leidner et al., 2018). The affordance term has been described by statements that discuss affordances as: “the action potential that can be taken given a technology” (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013), “what is offered, provided, or furnished to someone or something by an object” (Volkoff & Strong, 2013), and “the possibilities of using select features or combinations of features in a way meaningful to the user’s goals, abilities, and line of action” (Faraj & Azad, 2012). However, there is little consensus among IS researchers on how to separate or distinguish between features, use, and action (Leidner et al., 2018). Much IS research describes affordances as attributes or the direct use of the features of the technology but not an actual action (e.g., Majchrzak & Markus, 2013; Treem & Leonardi, 2012). Yet affordance views emphasize that an affordance is an action (or potential for action) that extends beyond technology use or technology feature use (Leidner et al., 2018).

Despite the inconsistent views of affordances in the research, the affordance lens is nevertheless a powerful tool for helping IS researchers understand the choices made about a technology and the outcomes of those choices (Leidner et al., 2018). In order to advance the knowledge about social media technologies, it is important to further investigate affordances. SM technologies can trigger individuals to discover meanings of the system that provides an opportunity for action. SM has been described as a tool for “engaging,” “communicating,” “building relationships,” “collaborating,” “generating ideas,” and “information sharing” (Gonzalez et al., 2015). Since SM affords individuals with actions that go beyond embedded functions or features, we suggest that delving deeper into SM use will help us better understand the affordances and outcomes experienced by SM users. Individuals use technology for different purposes (e.g., cognitive, social, and hedonic), which can either facilitate or hinder a variety of experiences or outcomes (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015). Because of this, we consider use, affordances, and outcomes separately. Applying the affordance lens to our context of SM use by FGCS allows us to further investigate the interactions between the FGCS and SM in a manner that will help explain how the affordances actualized by FGCS impact their college experience.

## 3 Method

Because studies of SM use by FGCS are scarce, our research focuses on one case in a higher education institution in depth. According to a study by Dube and Pare (2003), single-case studies are well-represented in top IS journals because single cases have the potential to provide insights. It has been found that 60% of all studies in IS positive case research are based on a single case, with 40% being multicase studies (Dube &

Pare, 2003). Single-case studies are appropriate in three situations—when the case (1) represents the critical situation in testing a well-formulated theory, (2) represents an extreme or unique instance, or (3) is a revelatory inquiry (Yin, 1989). At the time of our study, the case was unique and revelatory. Higher education institutions are interested in leveraging SM tools and understanding how SM use impacts student academic success. In recent years, with most institutions conducting business virtually, the importance of SM use for academic success has increased and it has been identified as a phenomenon that needs further investigation.

## 3.1 Data Collection and Sample Characteristics

We refer to our case organization as Metropolitan College (MC), a pseudonym. We chose MC because it is a Hispanic-serving institution and the majority of its students identify themselves as FGCS. According to the US Department of Education, Hispanic-serving institutions (HSIs) are accredited, postsecondary, higher educational institutions with at least 25% total full-time enrollment of Hispanic undergraduate students (https://sites.ed.gov/hispanic-initiative/).

We collected data via an online survey on Survey Monkey in April and May of 2018. To elicit individual narratives, the survey contained open-ended questions and questions to collect individual demographics information. The open-ended questions asked participants to describe their SM use experiences in college and reflect on their academic work and sources of support. In particular, participants were asked to reflect upon their SM use and daily activities in college and provide examples to illustrate their social media use and experiences. The intent was to both describe and understand their purpose for using social media and the sources of support they sought to help them with their academic work. The survey also included questions about participants’ SM usage behavior, such as time spent on SM, frequency of using SM, and the SM platforms they have used.

The online survey link was sent to students across five classes in the College of Business at MC. The students were pursuing different business majors and were not just IT or IS students. Participants were offered extra credit as an incentive to complete the survey and were assured that their names would not be recorded to protect their confidentiality. In the survey, we asked the students to identify themselves as “first-generation college students” or not; students that did not meet this criterion were not considered in this study. A total of 102 FGCS from five classes participated in the study. The average completion time of the survey was 16 minutes. Of the 102 FGCS in our sample, 64 were female (62.7%) and 38 were male (37.3%). The average age of the participants in our study was 27 years (median age: 25 years), with the youngest being 19 and the oldest 59. Participants in their senior year of college accounted for 52.9% and those with Hispanic or Latino ethnicity accounted for 69.6% of the total FGCS in the sample. Table 1 lists the demographics of the 102 study participants.

Participants in our study reported using multiple social media platforms. The most frequently used SM platforms were Instagram (47.1%) and Facebook (32.7%), followed by Snapchat (14.4%), LinkedIn (9.8%), Twitter (7.8%), and Pinterest (4.5%). In addition, seven respondents (7% of the 102) also reported using other SM platforms, such as TikTok, WhatsApp, GroupMe, and Discord. On average, participants reported using at least three social media platforms, such as Instagram, Facebook, and Snapchat.

Our FGCS participants were invited to share their social media usage behavior, including the frequency of social media usage and the amount of time spent on social media. Participants showed different patterns in their social media use behavior in terms of the frequency of their SM use. Among the 102 participants, 15 of them did not answer this question; thus, we collected valid data on this question from 87 participants. Overall, the highest percentage of respondents reported using social media every 2-3 hours (32.2%). This usage frequency was observed most frequently among students in their junior year (40.9%) and students without concurrent employment (63.6%). The distribution of usage frequency by gender, school year, and employment status is illustrated in Table 2.

Participants also reported the amount of time spent on social media, which revealed different patterns. Overall, the highest percentage of students reported spending between 1-2 hours (40.5%) per day on SM, followed by 3-4 hours (25.5%), 5+ hours (16.7%), and < 1 hour (15.7%). Using social media for 1-2 hours was observed most by those employed full-time (53.3%), followed by graduate students (52.4%) and juniors (44.4%). 53.8% of students not concurrently employed reported using social media 3-4 hours per day. The distribution of usage time by gender, school year, and employment status is summarized in Table 3.

Finally, the distribution of the total number of SM platforms used varied according to individual daily use time. As shown in Table 4, the majority (61.3%) of the light users (i.e., < 2 hours per day) reported using only one social media platform, compared to 35.3% of the heavy users (4+ hours), who were more likely to use more SM platforms—64.7% of them reported using more than three SM platforms, with 29.4% reporting three, 23.5% reporting four, and 17.6% reporting five. For medium users (i.e., 2-4 hours of daily use), nearly 39.1% of users reported having one SM account, 26.1% had two SM accounts, and 26.1% had three SM accounts.

## 3.2 Data Analysis

Because the purpose of our study was to understand how the affordances were actualized through SM use and what outcomes were experienced by FGCS, we adopted a critical realist data analysis approach. Critical realist methods focus on understanding rather than simply describing social reality, which uses retroduction to approximate causal mechanisms (Vincent & O’Mahoney, 2016). This approach focuses on uncovering the generative mechanisms that explain empirical outcomes. According to Volkoff and Strong (2013), the identified technology affordances help researchers specify mechanisms that explain the outcomes of new technology adoption within organizations. Empirical outcomes can be explained by the causal structures of generative mechanisms (Bygstad et al., 2016). Although we do not expand on critical realism principles (see Mingers et al., 2013; Williams & Karahanna, 2013), we provide the specific data analysis procedures that have been identified to identify generative mechanisms. While some authors describe various four-step processes (e.g., Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Mingers et al., 2013; Williams & Karahanna, 2013), others provide a six-step framework (Bygstad et al., 2016). Most recently, a fivestep approach has been applied (Leidner et al., 2018). We present the five frameworks for identifying generative mechanisms in the Appendix.

Considering the various approaches, we adopted the fivestep process described in Leidner et al. (2018). First, we conducted an open coding of the semi-structured survey data with a view toward identifying the key features and functionalities of SM use. Second, we analyzed the perceived outcomes of SM use. In this step, we performed two rounds of data coding, where we specifically looked for references to the impacts of SM use on the FGCS population. This process was iterative in that the initial list of outcomes was grouped into generalized categories. Third, we coded affordances, which involved digging deeper into the data to identify statements about how SM was used. The process of separating system feature use versus the affordances provided by SM use was critical in this phase. Two researchers worked independently to identify the affordances, discuss them, refine them, and return to the data to confirm them with examples. Without referencing other affordances in the IS literature, we allowed the SM affordances to emerge from the data. Fourth, in the retroduction phase, we linked the affordances into strands of affordances and associated the affordance strands with specific outcomes. It is in this process that we were able to identify the generative mechanisms. Lastly, we established the context of the affordance, outcomes, and mechanisms. We looked for insights into how SM use provided a sense of support and comfort for some students but not for others. This analysis revealed four affordance strands: interconnection, inspiration, insightfulness, and intense comfort.

## 3.3 Metropolitan College (MC) Case Description

Metropolitan College (MC) is part of a large network of higher education institutions that provide undergraduate and graduate degrees. Located in a large metropolitan area in the western US, at the time of the study, this four-year public, urban university offered 45 undergraduate majors, 22 master’s degrees, and a number of certificate and credential programs. By the designation of the US Department of Education, MC qualifies as both a minority-serving and Hispanicserving institution. It is among the most ethnically and economically diverse universities in the United States with a student population that is 64.3% Hispanic/Latino, 13.4% Black/African American, 8.5% White, 10.2% Asian or Pacific Islander, 0.1% American Indian, and 3.1% two or more races.

The campus serves 16,916 undergraduate, graduate, and credential-seeking students, including 15,070 undergraduates; 63% are female, nearly two thirds are low income, and 61% are first-generation college students. The campus serves predominantly underrepresented minority students, with 67.6% of the students being Pell Grant eligible and over 60% requiring remediation in English and/or math. In 2020- 2021, MC awarded 3,745 undergraduate degrees and 760 graduate degrees. According to a recent Diverse report, MC is among the top 50 universities nationwide in granting undergraduate and graduate degrees to minority students. Given the diversity of the student population and the high percentage of FGCS, this university served as an ideal site for us to study how SM use impacts student academic experiences.

The university has encountered challenges in improving the student graduation rate. According to its latest report in Spring 2018, the four-year and six-year graduation rates for the Fall 2010 cohort were 5% and 42% respectively. However, the graduation rates have gradually improved over the past five years (2015- 2019).

The university has launched multiple initiatives to support student transition and improve student retention. One such initiative is the Summer Bridge program, designed to improve the transition into college for incoming freshmen, focusing specifically on the needs of FGCS. The Summer Bridge program is a six-week program that allows students to complete English and math courses before entering college in the fall semester. The purpose of the program is twofold: First, the Summer Bridge program seeks to help students—particularly FGCS—strengthen their English and math skills while providing them with a head start on completing certain required courses with little or no tuition cost. Second, Summer Bridge students attend workshops to learn about the university services, curricula, and graduation requirements. The

Summer Bridge program seeks to endow FGCS with the connections needed to navigate the various academic and student services offered by the college. The program has been well-attended and well-received by students. For example, among 1,294 first-year freshman students (2017-2018 academic year), 61% were FGCS and 86% participated in a summer course and the 6-week Summer Bridge program. According to the university’s 2017-2018 Student Success Report, first-time freshman students achieved the highest retention rate to date, with 72% continuing through the sophomore year. To keep all students on track toward timely degree completion, MC is interested in identifying how to better engage and support their underrepresented minority and FGCS students in order to provide a positive college experience and help them achieve academic success.

## 4.1 Affordances

## 4.1.1 Interconnection

Most SM platforms that our participants reported using afforded users the ability to connect to others in order to become friends, influencers, or followers. By “following” or “connecting” to others via SM (e.g., on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, or Snapchat), our FGCS participants reported being able to create bonds, build bridges, and make connections. Consequently, SM helped them become friends with each other and cultivated a sense of belonging in the new environment. SM enabled FGCS to support each other and navigate college life together in ways going beyond the limited face-to-face contact in the classroom. As one Community Builder stated: “Instagram has made it easy to collaborate with classmates because you are able to use a group chat method without having to have people’s phone numbers.”

Thus, SM appears to allow FGCS to easily connect with their peers. Through their SM use, FGCS reported that they were able to “share with friends” and “communicate in various ways” outside of class without any hindrances and interact on a more personal level. This is reinforced by the following statement made by another Community Builder: “Most people have access to Instagram at all times so they are able to answer any questions. You are also able to put a face to the person especially when most people aren’t interacting in (online) class.”

Our FGCS participants reported being able to develop closer ties with others because SM provides a “comfortable” platform where they were able to have “open and transparent communication.” Sharing things using SM was commonly viewed as “easy” and “fun” for them. Within college communities, the value that comes from building social networks to enhance relationships and access resources can allow FGCS to achieve academic goals that they would not be able to achieve on their own.

When FGCS use SM to connect with peers, family, and friends, they are building a social network that serves them in various ways. Our FGCS participants reported using SM to share information, learn about others, discover new knowledge, and gain a level of trust with those they chose to interact with on a regular basis. A Community Builder described his social media usage as a way to “connect more with friends and family” and “learn about what is going on with them.” He was deeply concerned about knowing more about his peers on a deeper level. Another Community Builder stated that: “It helps shy people break out of their shells and connect more with others.” Creating a sense of community can help those that would not otherwise reach out, were it not for their use of SM.

Our FGCS participants also reported using SM to “find” others with similar interests, engage in online communication, receive updates on missed work, and stay current with others’ lives. One FGCS stated: “For academic study, I feel SM is the most useful because it was a good way for my groupmates and I to message each other and to share files.” Another FGCS stated: “I am able to share pictures with my family and see their pictures of what they are doing. It is a way to communicate with my nieces and nephews who are digital natives to technology.”

For Community Builders, connections tend to not be short-lived; they maintain connections for long periods of time and increase their daily interactions with other peers. Eventually, these connections serve as resources and provide a sense of support. As such, one Community Builder expressed: “It does help my social interactions with friends and you are able to connect with them in the long run.”

Although the frequency of SM usage may vary among the three types of users, Community Builders are highly interested in having close bonds with others. The Scholars and Information Seekers in our study did not tend to actualize the affordance of interconnection because they were generally less concerned about forming relationships via SM. Our Scholars tended to only actualize affordances that helped them gain resources to increase their knowledge, skills, or abilities. Even though they might initially connect to others, they do not tend to actively pursue closer ties. The Information Seekers in our study only actualized affordances that directly applied to gathering information needed for specific course activities. Therefore, the social networks of the Scholars and Information Seekers were less likely to be as vast and meaningful as those of the Community Builders.

## 4.1.2 Inspiration

Our findings indicate that SM affords FGCS the ability to gain inspiration when they view creative and topical posts, search specific keywords, browse media updates, and follow special interest groups. Similar features (e.g., search buttons and news feeds) across SM platforms can inspire FGCS, for example, on “how to write a paper” when they are unsure of how to get started or help them “discover educational material” or “identify ideas from other educators to gain ideas” on relevant course material. One Scholar described her use of SM as follows:

SM is helpful because you can find videos about peoples’ experiences in certain areas that you’re studying, then you’re able to have a new idea to share in class. For example, my disability class focuses a lot on films and social media.

Our FGCS participants reported that their inspiration to complete work tasks was often facilitated through their SM use. Students are often asked in class about current events and new trends. Our participants reported that SM helped them “keep up with industryrelated things” and “learn about the latest news.” One Information Seeker searching for current events for a class stated: “I find a lot of good articles to read that are related to my career as well as gain ideas for my class.” Another Information Seeker stated: “Social media is a place where most students spend time to receive their news.” Similarly, the experience of being inspired was also noted by a Scholar:

If I go to the search field, I can type the topic of interest on various things. When I use social media to aid in my studies, I will query it to find study tips that will help me be a better student.

Through various networks and connections, our findings indicate that SM serves as a medium that FGCS can use to reach out to peers with new ideas, influence others, become inspired, and ask questions. Our participants indicated that SM usage facilitated inspiration on multiple fronts. For example, one FGCS described SM as a creative outlet: “SM helps me connect and share with friends, while also getting to express my creative side.” As another FGCS explained, “I am constantly inspired by what I see on Instagram, whether it be a beautiful yoga pose, inspirational quote, or just some cool art.”

Such inspirational experiences are likely to transfer to specific academic purposes and expose users to new ideas. While we found that Community Builders and Scholars seek to share new ideas with others, Information Seekers are primarily interested in exposing themselves to new ideas. Although all three types of users experience inspiration, Scholars and Information Seekers are typically more focused on actualizing the affordance of inspiration than Community Builders.

## 4.1.3 Insightfulness

Regarding open communication and transparent discussion forums (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, Twitter), our findings indicate that SM afford FGCS opportunities to freely express personal views and opinions. Most SM users post, hashtag, or otherwise share content and images that will relay some form of message that they intend to be shared openly. Therefore, SM facilitates the distribution and access to meaningful content on various topics of interest across multiple SM platforms. By using SM, FGCS can learn about others’ opinions and be enlightened by different perspectives. Our FGCS participants described SM as “the tool” that facilitates “searching” and “seeking for meaningful information.” Specifically, Scholars typically search SM for tutorials and learning modules to help them gain new knowledge or skills or enhance their understanding of a topic. One Scholar stated: “I used LinkedIn learning to get some skills like advanced Excel and Power BI.”

We found that FGCS made use of SM to gain insights in order to settle cognitive dissonance by reading, processing, and thinking about new material in order to gain new insights. Even though SM can help FGCS “look at various topical projects and pages,” for Scholars, it is the idea of learning and gaining a new understanding via SM that motivates them to “dig deeper” and connect to special interest groups on the subject matter. Our participants noted that being exposed to experts in their areas of interest was inspiring. As one Scholar stated: “SM has helped me with trying to find resources since there are many tutorials on many subjects.”

Our findings show that Scholars and Community Builders derive satisfaction from learning something new and building connections with experts. In the words of one Scholar: “There are some smart people on Twitter that actually provide facts and documents that provide meaningful arguments.”

## 4.1.4 Intense Comfort

The last affordance of SM usage we identified is intense comfort. Our FGCS participants reported often using SM to take breaks and participate in virtual games that provided them some form of escape from the real world. Although some participants viewed SM as a “distraction” or “time waster,” others perceived it as a way to “relax” and “get away.” As one participant noted, “SM is used more as an escape to take a break from work.” Also, SM can be a stress reliever for students overwhelmed with course preparation. As another participant noted, “studying for exams can really stress you out, so you can go to games on SM and enjoy them for a while.” Thus, SM use affords

FGCS a respite from academic work and a sense of comfort. Our FGCS participants emphasized that SM allows them “to take a mental break from assignments,” “get away from the stressful student life,” and provided “a way to laugh through an otherwise stressful day.” According to one Community Builder: “It helps me to take a break and not fry my brain while working on homework for hours.”

For Community Builders taking breaks appears to be instrumental to their substantial SM use. In addition, Community Builders identify with a sense of comfort when they are able to tap into members of their community who are experiencing similar stressful situations. As one Community Builder described: “SM allows me to find out that other students are having the same problems as I and we then can discuss it on SM and help each other out.”

Leveraging peer connections on SM also endows Community Builders with a greater sense of support. As one Community Builder stated: “SM use helps because if you don’t have a classmate’s number, but you have their SM you can always message them if you forgot what’s for homework or if you need any help.”

We found that Scholars and Information Seekers tend to spend less time on SM and use SM for specific purposes and found that they experience less comfort from the sense of community provided by SM than Community Builders.

First-order affordances are no easier to actualize or activate than second-order affordances nor are secondorder affordances “harder” nor do they demand a higher level of thinking to actualize (Leidner et al., 2018). In this study, interacting with others, following special interest groups, and engaging in open communications acted as first-order affordances. Activating these firstorder affordances then allowed users to actualize second-order affordances, which collectively resulted in various outcomes. ## 4.2 Generative Mechanisms

## 4.2.1 Identity Booster

Interacting with others is a first-order affordance that makes other affordances possible—for example, creating bonds, building relationships, socializing, and relieving stress. The outcome of togetherness and trust is explained through the generative mechanism, which we refer to as the “identity booster.” The identity booster mechanism refers to FGCS experiencing a newly found identity within their new college environment.

Many students, and particularly FGCS, experience an identity crisis when entering a new college or university because they don’t know what to expect or how well they will identify with other students. SM can help FCGS gain a sense of support and social acceptance in a new and uncertain world. As one participant said: “I met some of my friends on SM.” Another stated: “SM does help because we can find people that you have things in common with and helps someone become more diverse and social than before.”

For FGCS, maintaining their connections to family and friends is often crucial for experiencing a sense of moral support. While family or long-distance friends may not know how to help FGCS with their studies or clearly understand their needs, our FGCS participants reported feeling a sense of comfort knowing that they could reach family and friends with ease via SM. One participant stated: “It [SM] does help me in college because this is how I stay connected with friends and family.”

Furthermore, we found that our FGCS participants tend to use SM to identify others to whom they can relate to. For example, one participant described their own SM use as follows:

I think SM is a booster because when school comes around, you see your friends on Snapchat or Instagram their day at school or their homework; so, I think it’s cool to know that you are not the only one going through the stress of attending school.

FGCS who form closer relationships and ties with others are likely to experience a greater level of closeness in their new community. The closer the tie, the more safe and protected they will likely feel in their new environment. FGCS often take comfort in knowing that they are not alone. Our findings indicate that it is reassuring to FGCS to know that others are going through similar experiences and facing similar challenges, as expressed in the following quote: “In my SM connections, I am able to see that others are experiencing the same thing as I.” A Community Builder stated: “I like using social media to see people post how they feel while they’re in school; like being stressed and feeling like it’s not just you.”

Over time, we found that FGCS using SM regularly are likely to find themselves becoming more engaged in SM college networks than originally expected. In the words of one Community Builder, “communication on SM is good when you need help on a project with friends since everybody’s on it more often now.”

In addition, interacting with others can help FGCS to stay involved in the community. This is described by a Community Builder: “SM can be used to inform students on activities that may be going on, both social and academic campus events.” Also, we found that SM allows FGCS to feel like they are in a “comfort zone” where it is safe to share. Many participants reported experiencing uncertainty as new college students and stated that they often reach out to others in their networks for guidance and support. As one Community Builder explained:

I can use social media to try and stay in touch with classmates. We can set up our own meetings that feel meaningful to us, rather than just the generic group chats. For example, we can send each other photos and videos as well to help aid the work we are doing. It can also help us schedule meetups as well just in case we need to see each other or study together.

Over time, trust is built as ties are strengthened among FGCS and others. The closer the bond, the more likely it is that FGCS will share more about their private lives because constant interaction builds trust. Although not all of our FGCS participants reported experiencing this outcome, some noted an increase in their level of trust when they “connect more with others” and “stay connected.” One participant simply said: “SM has been the most useful to me because of group chats. It allows me to ask questions with others when I am confused. I know that they will have an answer and help me understand.”

In essence, our findings suggest that SM usage enables FGCS to access support, resources, and comfort in their new college community. Because SM use “helps social interactions,” it can assist FGCS in developing connections that allow them to “have online conversations without much thought” or hindrance. Constant connections and communication managed through SM use can provide FGCS with the reassurance that they have a trusted social network that they can use to build a sense of community. As one participant stated: “SM does help because some people are shy to talk face to face.” Without the comfort zone provided by SM use, some individuals would never have a voice in their community and find themselves feeling alone and disconnected. Thus, the notion of an identity or online presence can provide meaningful affordances to FGCS in a world that they know nothing about and hope to navigate successfully.

## 4.2.2 Academic Growth

The first-order affordances of following special interest groups and engaging in open communication make it possible to access the affordances of creating new content, accessing stimulating content, accessing resources, acquiring new knowledge, and gaining insights on various topics. Together, these affordances explain the outcomes of self-efficacy and vision through the generative mechanism that we refer to as “academic growth.” By academic growth, we mean the ability of FGCS to grow in their academic life.

Through SM use, FGCS can post on and search for special topics to gain new resources. Specifically, this type of SM usage is useful when FGCS are unsure about a class assignment. As one participant stated, “SM helps build confidence when I can reach out and ask questions.” SM provides FGCS a platform for interacting with others without feeling intimidated by a traditional classroom setting. FGCS often need to access content and resources to help them navigate things they do not understand. Thus, they are more likely to gain a new sense of understanding through the affordances that facilitate acquiring various types of information without any set restrictions or boundaries. One Information Seeker described SM usage as follows:

Throughout my academic study, I have only used Instagram just to get information from a company for a school project. Same thing goes for Twitter. I used it to see how companies were approaching their customers and how they were marketing themselves.

Because SM affords FGCS various ways to build and foster relationships and interact with others, it is possible for them to gain an online identity where they feel validated. Consequently, SM can enable FGCS to obtain a level of confidence that motivates them to continue to search and identify ways to create new content based on the insights and new knowledge gained. One participant stated that SM use allows them “to keep in touch with multiple people throughout the day.” When things are being shared on SM, it gives FGCS “confidence” among their peers. Another participant said, “I use SM to search articles and news online. It can keep me informed of current events and that benefits me academically.” The idea that SM usage serves as a resource for supporting FGCS by providing vast amounts of information and learning tools is noted in the following quote by an Information Seeker:

I think social media is useful for academic study. If I go to the search field, I can type the topic of interest on various things. It is a large pool of resources and professionals that have varying skill sets and experience. The platform also offers free webinars, podcasts, and articles from published experts. I use social media to aid in my studies. In addition, social media alerts and notifications allows for me to stay current and up-to-date with news and information.

In addition, our FGCS participants agreed that SM usage helps them with their academic work because it provides a place where they can work with team members. For example, one participant stated that SM allows students to “get in touch with each other for help with homework when they do not have each other’s phone numbers.” SM can be used to “communicate and offer help.” It is a place where “information is more accessible to everyone.” A couple of participants, including one Scholar, described SM as follows:

Instagram is helpful for looking up information as an easier alternative on how to solve a homework problem, which on average many incoming freshmen struggle with and the faster way to receive pictures that may include content to review if another student does not come to class one day as well as a place that can be used for online study groups.

With social media it has helped me with trying to find resources. Just like YouTube they often have quick tutorials on how to do things with different tools often used. For example, there is a profile where they show how to do various excel activities, helping in everyday life and in school. Every now and then people will post where they get different resources such as books for less cost that are often expensive to buy.

Because of the various resources available via SM sites, SM can help FGCS understand difficult subject matter. This is illustrated in the following quote from a participant:

Social media directly helps through videos that are especially helpful if you are confused by what a professor said or didn't understand the way they taught a certain subject. The resources in the videos can show a different perspective that makes things clear.

The outcome of academic growth is also described by a Scholar, who said: “I watch TikTok videos to help me better write essays. I watch TikTok videos to help me understand math problems.” SM affords FGCS the ability to find resources and work with others, which can lead to positive academic impacts.

## 4.2.3 Self-Care Awareness

Engaging in open communication and interacting with others are first-order affordances that enable the affordances of relieving stress, clearing the mind, escaping real-world situations, and socializing. Jointly, these affordances explain the outcomes of enhanced well-being and distraction through the generative mechanism we refer to “self-care awareness.” The selfcare awareness mechanism refers to FGCS’ ability to focus on their well-being.

FGCS need to develop ways to destress and escape overwhelming situations when they are trying to balance academic work, a new environment, and isolation or disconnection from family. One participant described SM “a way to get away from the stressful student life” and a “quick break.” Similarly, another stated, “I think SM is more of a distraction; I use it to take a break when I am doing work instead of using it as an academic support.”

Although the term “distraction” can be perceived as negative, “taking a break” has positive connotations, as a means of clearing the mind and stepping away from work to reenergize. One FGCS noted that SM provides “an escape from work and lets me get a good laugh.”

For FGCS entering college without any prior background to effectively prepare them, the transition to college life can be intimidating and “scary.” As one participant stated, “I talk more on SM than in person because I am shy and I get anxiety talking to people I do not know.” Another said: “As a shy person, liking someone’s picture on SM is like the door to finally speaking.” A third, describing SM use, stated: “it can be an easier way to talk to people that attend the school.” A fourth stated that “social media is just a way to express yourself and show people a piece of your life that I might not do in person.” SM provides a way for FGCS to express oneself and a context in which they feel comfortable reaching out to others, which offers them a means of connecting and finding their voice and minimizing the fear of being left out.

SM usage for entertainment and as a tool for relief during a hectic day can improve self-care among FGCS by providing comfort. SM usage not only affords FGCS the ability to interact with others in an open manner but, through this process, they can become aware of their emotional and social needs and of the importance of reaching out to others for help.

## 5 Discussion

The objective of our study was to understand how SM usage impacts the underserved FGCS community. Research on SM usage has examined various issues, including technology affordances, socialization, collaboration, knowledge management, and work-life balance (e.g., Kane et al, 2011; Leidner et al, 2018; Majchrzak & Markus, 2013). Our study extends the work on social inclusion, which has implications for research in the areas of technology affordances and SM practices at higher education institutions.

## 5.1 Social Inclusion

This study has two major implications for the social inclusion literature. First, social media is known to help individuals with socializing and making connections (Leidner et al., 2018). SM users are likely to feel socially included via these connections, which can create a sense of belonging. Our research provides insights into the outcomes experienced by SM users. We found that while individuals may connect to others in SM, they do not solely depend on these connections to feel that they are part of a community. Instead, the use of SM is viewed as a means of building skills and abilities that help users gain a sense of confidence where they feel capable of managing uncertain situations. Also, we identified SM use as a source of vast information from experts that gives users a sense of awareness about the world around them. This new information enables individuals to actively participate in discussions where they can gain a greater sense of belonging as they share new ideas and comment on current events.

Second, we found that the accessibility of SM technologies and tools digitizes and heightens social inclusion in organizations. Our study focused on the significance of the use of SM in the development of social inclusion. Reliance on technology for social inclusion gained has gained importance in recent years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, organizations had to depend on the use of technology even more than before. As individuals were expected to be remote workers that relied on technology to stay connected, organizations were challenged with leveraging SM tools to ensure that individuals’ well-being and sense of community were not jeopardized. With this in mind, our study demonstrates that SM use has become a significant factor for social inclusion. SM use digitizes the interactions between individuals, provides resources, creates social networks, and facilitates community awareness. Thus, the use of SM for social inclusion has become crucial in today’s world of remote work necessitating various technologies.

Our study demonstrates that social inclusion increased due to SM use. We found that SM was perceived as helpful for developing a greater sense of identity and self-confidence among peers. In addition, we found that the technological factor played a major role for FGCS by giving them an online presence within their communities. Being able to make use of technology to increase social inclusion indicates that digital inclusion leads to positive outcomes for users. Our findings suggest that SM provides FGCS with a safe haven where they feel supported and not alone in the process of navigating their academic life. By looking at how FGCS find strength, resources, and comfort from using SM to engage on online platforms, our study offers educational institutions insight that can help guide investments in SM technologies and help them implement equity-minded practices and policies to provide digital spaces for FGCS. This will help FGCS feel included instead of excluded in digitally facilitated higher education environments.

## 5.2 Technology Affordances

Our research offers two implications for technology affordances. First, given that social media can serve as a mechanism for the development of socialization and that socializing or acclimating an individual can be helpful for gaining a sense of community, one may wonder why not all users experience the same outcomes in relation to academic success. The availability of external resources, such as peer networks, is one critical factor contributing to students’ academic success (Burrus et al., 2013). Our research provides some insights into the outcomes of SM use. Our findings reveal that SM technologies afford underserved community members improved higher educational outcomes by providing them with resources that would otherwise be difficult to access. These resources can increase the FGCS success rate in college and facilitate their workplace engagement for professional growth after graduation. Individuals who use SM to not only connect to others but also to search for resources and gain new information about course requirements are more likely to develop a sense of community, where they can reach out for support in a more academically focused way. Underserved individuals may suffer from missed social, environmental, or cultural opportunities that can limit their professional growth and pathways. Thus, SM technology can play a vital role in engaging and acclimating specific populations to organizations (Leidner et al., 2018).

However, we found that not all FGCS use SM in the same way. Although SM usage affords interconnectedness among users, users may take actions that are related to academic work, personal self-care, or both. Because SM use does not necessarily translate directly to achieving academic success, educational institutions may witness users who lose focus on academic life and fail to complete their programs. Future research is needed to understand the direct impact of SM use on academic success so that institutions can improve graduation and retention rates by designing SM initiatives that encourage usage that targets specific academic outcomes.

A second implication of our study regarding technology affordances is that even though FGCS leveraged SM for completing academic tasks, the possibility of the “distraction” element in SM may raise concerns for institutions. Research on FGCS has shown that FGCS are likely to be part-time students who work full-time and live off campus (Engle & Tinto, 2008) and may feel overwhelmed when new responsibilities are added to an already stressful daily life. In addition, the cultural backgrounds of FGCS and the role that SM plays to enable users to find comfort are important. Our study suggests that FGCS required a greater sense of family, support, and resources than their peers. It is important to note that SM usage by

FGCS varied according to their different use purposes. Consequently, we found that FGCS experienced both positive and negative outcomes. While SM usage afforded FGCS the ability to connect to much-needed resources, SM usage also offered FGCS a way to engage with entertainment. When FGCS interacted with virtual games or simply watched videos for fun, they reported experiencing some relief and feeling a sense of comfort without initially realizing that this could make them less productive in their schoolwork.

However, it should be emphasized that when not monitored or controlled, SM usage may hinder users’ achievement of goals. Consistent use of SM can create a dangerous cyclical pattern for users. Uncontrolled SM use may result in users experiencing a high level of dissatisfaction and discontent when they feel unproductive (e.g., have incomplete assignments) or may lead them to feel that they are “wasting time” (e.g., spending too much time scrolling), which may, in turn, cause them to return to SM for a sense of comfort and relief. While some research has found that positive emotions (e.g., joy, happiness, and comfort) can be experienced through SM usage (Leidner et al., 2010), others have found that continuous use of SM can lead to bad habits or technology addiction (e.g., Turel, 2015). However, our research demonstrates that the initial intent of SM usage is purposeful, whether it is to gain academic resources or to destress from an overwhelming day. Future research is needed to investigate and further understand the point at which social media use for personal self-care leads to negative outcomes.

## 5.3 Practical Implications

Our research contributes three implications to the education domain. First, the integration of SM usage for educational purposes can take a unique role in educational high-impact practices (HIPs). Because many institutions place an emphasis on teaching and learning practices that target retention and graduation rates, focusing on the FGCS population may potentially lead to a higher impact that addresses an institution’s strategic goals and objectives. Leveraging a technology (e.g., SM) that is commonly used by college students daily in order to provide them with a gateway to enhancing their learning experiences may lead to significant educational benefits, especially for historically underserved students. Our study informs and provides insights into how SM tools can serve FGCS. Specifically, SM tools can be used for course delivery, collaborative assignments, capstone courses, managing internships, and encouraging faculty/student interactions. Although our study offers useful guidance to higher education institutions on designing and implementing HIPs to better support FGCS, future research is needed to further understand the type of SM usage that results in greater positive retention and graduation rate outcomes.

Second, SM technologies afford users many ways to interact, gain information, and discover various networks of resources, which can result in happier and more acclimated individuals (e.g., Leidner et al., 2010). In particular, FGCS use social media to build two forms of social capital (bridging and bonding with friends) and support their academic work, and they mention such usage more frequently, compared to their continuing-education college students peers (Deng et al., 2022). The use of SM for developing social networks and acquiring resources may provide FGCS with the moral support and new information needed to increase the probability of their academic success. Our study demonstrates how SM usage helps FGCS develop interconnections with others and gain new knowledge. However, future research is needed to further understand how SM usage results in facilitating social capital gains that can help FGCS achieve greater academic success.

Lastly, SM usage not only helps FGCS connect with resources and make connections, it gives them an approach to navigating the rather uncertain and intimidating life situation of entering college. For the FGCS community, SM can mean the difference between graduating or dropping out. Thus, for the FGCS population, SM may represent more than just a technology; it can become the lifeline for surviving a life-changing situation. Without having anyone in their family to prepare them for what they will encounter in college, FGCS may not always feel confident in their ability to reach their academic goals. Our findings demonstrate that the use of social media technologies enabled FGCS to access and mobilize a wide range of educational resources, which could increase the chances of economic well-being for this less privileged population. Future research is needed to provide a deeper understanding of the impact of SM usage on long-term FGCS success factors.

## 5.4 Guidelines for Social Media Academic Success

Interest in SM for learning and higher education continues to grow. SM offers a variety of features and functionalities that afford users various capabilities, which may lead to various outcomes. A new understanding of the implications of SM affordances would encourage higher educational institutions to evaluate their capabilities to leverage SM opportunities that account for user backgrounds and goals in designing and implementing organizational SM strategy and policies. Therefore, we offer the following guidelines for effective SM use. Our guidelines are based on the inferences drawn from our observations.

1. Develop meaningful and relevant content that will serve individuals for community engagement.

2. Identify ways to leverage similar SM features in order to reach SM users on various platforms.

3. Develop SM best practices and guidelines to manage SM use and minimize SM distraction concerns.

4. Customize SM support services to individual needs based on their use type and preferences.

5. Launch an SM initiative that focuses on promoting regular interactions among community members.

6. Develop and customize SM internal portals that can provide users with vast opportunities to build social capital.

Although we focused on the use of SM by FGCS, many of our findings and guidelines can also be applied to the implementation of SM within organizations to support knowledge sharing and collaborations among employees. Community and network building is critical to the success of SM initiatives. However, organizations should provide SM use guidelines and best practices and take precautionary measures during SM implementations to achieve the actualization of SM affordances and associated positive outcomes.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Research

Our study focused on first-generation college students and revealed four major affordances actualized in their SM use. Moreover, three generative mechanisms were identified to explain how the affordances lead to the outcomes of college academic life among the FGCS community. IS researchers have defined affordances as possibilities for goal-oriented action that artifacts offer to specified user groups (Markus & Silver 2008) and have emphasized the importance of distinguishing between features, use, and action (Leidner et al., 2018). Our study extends this line of research by distinguishing between technology use, affordance actualization, and outcomes in the FGCS community. In doing so, our study also responds to a research call (Majchrzak &

Markus, 2013) by exploring social media engagement beyond the context of communication.

Our study is subject to certain limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling of first-generation college students from one public urban university with a large population of Hispanic/Latino students may have impacted the findings and their general applicability. In this regard, further research using a broader population and different contexts would be useful for generating additional insights. Second, the actualization of SM affordances requires users to not only perceive affordances but also to have the expertise needed to reach their goals. Our study did not focus on FGCS expertise in utilizing social media features to enhance their college academic experience. However, a future study of user expertise with technology and actualization of technology affordances would likely generate useful findings. Third, the approach used in this study may have led to findings that may be too common and nonspecific to identify the specific needs of FGCS. Further research is needed to better understand the specific needs that impact FCGS academic success.

These limitations notwithstanding, our study provides an in-depth analysis of the interaction effects of SM affordances at the first and second orders and shows how multiple affordances intertwine to create outcomes that benefit FGCS. The three generative mechanisms of identity booster, academic growth. and self-care are conducive to enhancing the psychological well-being of FGCS, as these mechanisms help them experience positive relationships with others, personal growth, and stress relief (Ryff & Keyes, 1995). SM technology plays an increasingly vital role in engaging and acclimating specific populations into their new environment, whether on a college campus or in a professional workplace. Our study takes an important first step in demonstrating the affordances of SM technologies for the underserved community of FGCS. By doing so, we hope that this initial work will attract further studies investigating technology affordances and outcomes in this research space.
