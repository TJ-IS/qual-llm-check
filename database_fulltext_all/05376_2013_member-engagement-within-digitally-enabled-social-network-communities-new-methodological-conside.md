# Member engagement within digitally enabled social network communities: new methodological considerations

Matt Germonprez\* & Dirk S. Hovorka<sup>†</sup>

\*Department of Information Systems and Quantitative Analysis, College of Information Science & Technology, University of Nebraska at Omaha, Omaha, NE USA, email: germonprez@gmail.com, and <sup>†</sup>Information Systems, Faculty of Business, Bond University, Gold Coast, Qld Australia, email: dhovorka@bond.edu.au

Abstract. Digitally enabled social networks (DESN) are a complex assemblage of engagement, re<sup>fl</sup>ection, action, technology, organization and community. DESN create a unique challenge for researchers who aim to understand what social networks are, what they can become and what enablers and constraints underlie trajectories of member engagement. As DESN continually evolve, knowing them as stable and rei<sup>fi</sup>ed representations or as mere technology artefacts provides a limited understanding of their complexity and emergent properties. While DESN are, in part the technology that supports the necessary actions for engagement, they are also the people and behaviours that constitute its community. Through the presentation of new methodological considerations towards Digg, a large DESN, we observe that socia networks entail practices of engagement, change and evolution within a DESN community. We reveal how engagement is a communal endeavour and that the clash of socio-technical trajectories can result in the emergence of new paths of member participation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the potential of netnography and impressionist tales for contributing to the ongoing pluralistic investigations of DESN and also inform research on engagement and community design and change.

Keywords: digitally enabled social network, netnography, case study, community, engagement

## INTRODUCTION

Open communities, crowd-source collectives and massively multiplayer worlds represent new contexts with new responsibilities for both academics and practitioners alike. It is important that we do not treat these digitally enabled social networks (DESN) as slight variations of systems prior, but as fundamental shifts in how we understand people and technology. In seeking new methodologies in their study or new theory in their explanation, we recognize that DESN represent an intersection of people and technology in forms that necessitate evolution, design and emergence.

The technology of DESN is initially designed with the intention of supporting and encouraging members’ contributions to socio-technical processes. However, the technology of a social network alone is quite empty without the engagement of community members: Wikipedia is blank without the contributions of authors and editors; Flickr is empty without the images that people post and tag and Twitter is nothing without the tweets of millions. In each case, it is a social network, not an information technology artefact, that is replete with content, connections, technology enablers and constraints, and social norms of behaviour.

Digitally enabled social networks are unique styles of information system in that they require the engagement of community members willing to share their time, energy and expertise in the ongoing cocreation of a DESN for the parent organization, its members and the overall communal system (Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Shirky, 2010; Germonprez et al., 2011). Some DESN are built around a shared interest in technology innovation (e.g. Linux and Debian), others around relation ship development (e.g. Facebook and Twitter) and still others around information exchange (e.g. Flickr, Wikipedia, Digg and Reddit). But their content, form and social norms are not de<sup>fi</sup>ned ex ante by the technology – they must be designed and negotiated by the members of the community (Germonprez et al., 2011). Therefore, DESN represent not just a socio-technical artefact but a community (Le Bon, 1895; Van Maanen, 1988; Baym, 2000; Kozinets, 2010) composed of content-contributing and content-consuming members who collectively generate, inhabit and design a system. The challenge in researching DESN lies in understanding the intertwining and cocreation of the supporting system with the member-designed practices, norms and values that foster belonging, friendships, debate and understanding (Wenger, 1999; Baym, 2000).

DESN represent a growing context of study to which theory, method and practice must be extended. Our expectations of sociality, innovation and community evolution change as we con sider users as designers in their own right. Theories of design and methods of study must be reconsidered as researchers do not always prede<sup>fi</sup>ne a system’s use and utility, and as a result, must extend representation from the functional de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a system to a critical understanding of the enablers and inhibitors of engagement with DESN forms, meanings and practices. From the Arab Spring to the Occupy Movement, social network engagements create compelling DESN research questions, open methodological approaches and challenge theoretical assumptions. As researchers, we <sup>fi</sup>nd ourselves trailing in the wake of these communities, trying to understand issues such as evolution, repurposing, design and rei<sup>fi</sup>cation. From the secondary design of Wikipedia pages to the creation of YouTube viral ‘stars’, DESN are a class of systems characterized by evolutionary, member-designed trajectories<sup>1</sup> (Germonprez et al., 2011). Finding appropriate positioning in the study of DESN can prove to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult as existing methods and theories are extended in this new perspective. We challenge the assumption that the objects of research interest are the adoption of attitudes towards or use of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c material or informational artefact. Instead, we focus on the question about the practices of community engagement associated with being a member of a DESN, (Brown & Duguid, 2000; Lee & Cole, 2003). This research proposes a methodological approach that recasts the phenomenon of DESN by capturing the perspective of the community participants. This approach acts as a component in the broader landscape for knowing DESN, leading to our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question of, how can coherent representations of engagement and change in DESN be discovered through new methodological approaches?

We adopt the perspective that DESN emerge through interactions of constituent parts rather than being prede<sup>fi</sup>ned or largely shaped through the characteristics of the technological artefact. Emergence (Bunge 2004, Levina & Vaast 2005) recognizes that high-level behaviours are the result of the interactions of lower level parts. In the case of DESN. the social network results from the technology, the language, the expressions, the social behaviours and the coordinated practices in which members engage as they design an assemblage that cannot be fully explained or predicted by examining its constituent parts. People do not participate to explicitly design a social network artefact; instead, they participate in the design of ‘loose networks based on public information goods such as online forums, blogs and databases without initial reference to speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals’ (Chadwick, 2007, pg. 286). Considering social networks as an emergent process of second ary design, new methodological approaches are necessary to reveal new theoretical insights. Thus, our second research question becomes, what processes are revealed in the emergence and changing trajectories of DESN?

The importance of member engagement in DESN strengthens the view that social networks cannot be reductively studied as entities that are stable, amenable to cross-sectional analysis and singularly de<sup>fi</sup>ned (Chadwick, 2007). Instead, researchers should confront DESN as practices of emergent socio-technical trajectories and invite new methodological considerations in efforts to yield new theoretical insights. DESN present an opportunity for researchers to understand deeply technical, deeply social, highly variable and emergent phenomena. We seek to understand DESN as provoking a response from the world through social engagement, connecting with the people and their behaviours and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting concepts, norms and values designed by its members. In this paper, we illustrate that DESN can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and understood in ways that represent how people accommodate and resist change during DESN engagement (Van Maanen, 1988; Kozinets, 2010). Through this investigation, we theoretically understand engagement of a social network commu nity to serve as a component of a larger approach towards understanding DESN more richly (Mingers, 2001; Davison & Martinsons, 2011).

In the following sections, we present new methodological considerations towards DESN as community members participating in practices of engagement, design and emergence. These new considerations contribute to a larger landscape of approaching and then knowing DESN in new ways. We then introduce and present a representative case of engagement within a DESN, which illustrates our methodological approach. We conclude with a discussion of our methodolog ical approach that enables researchers to engage with increasingly complex and rapidly changing events in an ongoing theoretical inquiry into DESN.

## DIGITA L LY E N A BLE D SO CI A L NE TWO R KS

Digitally enabled social networks are evolving ensemble of technology characteristics that support the social engagements underlying the creation, emergence, maintenance and potentia dissolution of its community (Kozinets, 2010). As social networks continue to proliferate and grow, many become heavily monetized and commercially valued (Economist, 2011). Rupert Murdoch purchased the social networking site MySpace; Google purchased the video site YouTube and Conde Nast purchased the online content site Reddit. While the technology of these social networks played an important part in their sale, so did the associated social community, representing a core of people designing content and dedicating their cognitive surplus and thei free time to creative acts for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the social network (Shirky, 2010). Obtaining a critical mass of members for a sustainable DESN is a task made increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult by a potentially migrant online public, the vastness of online alternatives and the rate of online change. Community members can readily switch between online marketplaces, media outlets and social networking sites if there is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient reason to maintain commitments to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c social network (Tapscott & Williams, 2006).

Studies of social networks have examined site structure to understand constructs ranging from individual loyalty, stickiness and trust (Flavian et al. 2006; Dikolli & Sedatole, 2007) to religious authority and control (Campbell & Golan, 2011). These projects provide insight into how to engage community members, with speci<sup>fi</sup>c focus on how the design of the social network technology affects communal participation. Additional studies include Sowe et al. (2008), who examined dig ital communication and knowledge sharing internal to the Debian open community; Kuk (2006), who explored interactions within the KDE open community and Fitzgerald (2006), who investigated organizational participation in the social networks associated with Linux. These studies focused on the characteristics and behaviour of social networks, organizational interaction and the mechanisms and rationale underlying DESN functions. In these studies, the research focus was within a successful DESN, where the social network was conceptualized as a de<sup>fi</sup>nable, stable and rei<sup>fi</sup>ed community, a treatment we suggest needs to be extended.

The view of DESN as highly communal enterprises shifts the focus of study from the network structure to the social practices and engagement that create the network (Wenger, 1999; Baym, 2000; Chadwick, 2007; Ridings & Wasko, 2010). By highlighting the formation of conventions and the practices of negotiation and rei<sup>fi</sup>cation in the secondary design of Wikipedia pages, Germonprez et al. (2011) demonstrate that the formation of a DESN is best understood as a set of evolved practices rather than an ex ante artefact-based system. Social networks emerge from the engagement of people in practices of design and communal creation, not as a result of the design of the technology artefact underlying the <sup>fi</sup>nal www.Wikipedia.com site itself. The emergent trajectory of this case of social network engagement stemmed from a practice of claims, negotiation and rei<sup>fi</sup>cation that extended beyond the technical design to include the function, content and methods that emerged from the design of the social network in practice (Schön. 1983) Through these practices, members recon<sup>fi</sup>gured their environment, designed form and content, and developed internal community governance, norms, repertoire, styles and discourses (Wenger, 1999; Baym, 2000). The perspective of DESN as expressive, interactive, dynamic and intelligent communities of practice where engagement ‘becomes meaningful only in the ways in which they are invoked by participants in ongoing interaction’ (Baym, 2000, pg. 5) raises questions regarding the applicability of research methods best suited for structural analysis. DESN represent unique opportunities and challenges for research, and we now turn attention to the methodological considerations.

## METHODOLOGICAL CONSIDERATIONS TOWARDS DESN: A NEW APPROACH

Rapidly emerging events in DESN create unique research problems. Political actions, consume and environmental activism, and entertainment, athletic and social news can each become the focus of DESN communities. A majority of the communities are the ‘everyday normal’ types of interactions composed of news items, discussions and expressions of support or disparagement. However, in some instances, the community or a section thereof becomes dramatically mobilized to take action in response to an external event or even to occurrences internal to the community. These rapidly changing and highly contextualized interactions may shift the multiple trajectories within a DESN and suggest a need for new methodological considerations. Our methodologica approach highlights the role of ethnography in online settings (Baym, 2000; Kozinets, 2010). We treat social networks as a constant source of change from which understanding emerges by providing closeness to the subject, loosening of theoretical prede<sup>fi</sup>nition and exposing both the usual and unusual of social networks. We position ourselves as interpreters of digital socia networks where the digital world determines and de<sup>fi</sup>nes the nature of the case (Klein & Myers, 1999). To avoid predetermined theoretical over-speci<sup>fi</sup>cation brought to social networks, we lean heavily on stories from the social networks. This allows for the creation of an informed context of the events of a DESN, seeing how interpretations, timing, and details of a particular event are interwoven. The study of emergent properties of DESN does not always afford the luxury of planning a research agenda, entering a prechosen community or even allowing a return to the <sup>fi</sup>eld as needed. Yet, the goal must remain to ‘reveal new dimensions of the problem at hand and yield insights that explicitly revise understanding’ (Kozinets, 2010, pg. 120).

We suggest a pragmatic approach to allow for knowing social engagement as one of change and emergence, in which practices are ‘always and everywhere evolving, adjusting, becoming (Plummer, 1996, pg. 224). Our approach accommodates the focus on interactionist behaviour while maintaining a clear connection to the practice-based empirical setting. We focus on the social and community behaviours and include the subtext, the lived experience and the graphical, visual and written aspects of the community, all while remaining focused on theory as governed by an understanding of the inductive nature of the study (Van Maanen, 1988; Baym, 2000; Kozinets, 2010). We suggest that to study emergent phenomena, focused research questions may not always be fully structured a priori, that researchers may not always be able to preselect a speci<sup>fi</sup>c community for study and that archived data are not able to represent the emergent processes, structures and actions of a studied community.

In response to these characteristics, we turn to netnography from Kozinets (2010) and impressionist tales from Van Maanen (1988).<sup>2</sup> In using both, we respect the everyday uniqueness of DESN to elicit theorizing in an effort to expand, not restrict, the interpretation of rapidly emergent and unique tales from the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Van Maanen, 1988). We understand unique events through the lens of day-to-day activities, revealing how members’ re<sup>fl</sup>ection and actions are guided by their sense of community norms and repertoires. When understanding highly contextualized and emergent social networks, order comes inductively from converging explanations of seemingly ideographic activity, and methodologically inductive approaches act as a representational means of cracking open the community in a way of knowing (Davison & Martinsons, 2011). Netnographies are an ethnographic approach adjusted to an online world (Kozinets, 2010). They are inherently <sup>fl</sup>exible, naturally exploratory and are intended to ‘draw (or re-draw) the map of a new or rapidly changing terrain. [They] help to tell other researchers who will come later what are the most interesting constructs and relationships’ (Kozinets, 2010, pg. 42). Netnographies provide an important framework through which researchers can craft, review and re<sup>fi</sup>ne research questions, build data bridges to those questions and orient themselves for knowing online social engagement. A netnography is primarily oriented to the study of a community through researcher participation and observation (Kozinets, 2010). However, these attributes are broad, open to critique and provide a sense of control that the researcher may not have, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in emerging online settings.

Consider the aforementioned example of the Arab Spring in 2011 where a segment of the Egyptian community quickly mobilized both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine to protest social and political inequity. In retrospect, it is easy to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a series of informative research questions, but were the questions evident in the early days of social unrest? Did researchers even recognize that an unusual set of events was occurring? The questions are less apparent in the immediacy of the moment, community selection is opportunistic as the researcher’s gaze must be drawn to where the action is, and community participation may be <sup>fl</sup>eeting in the short timeframes within which online events live. Reaction and immediacy become essential, and a researcher may have to participate on the basis of the real-time

reading [of] messages . . . (as opposed to downloading them en masse to be searched and automatically coded), following links, rating, . . . offering short comments, offering long comments, [or] joining in and contributing to community activities (Kozinets, 2010, pg. 96).

Entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld in a digital environment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from engagement with, and through, the digital media – sometimes the only place the community exists. For rapidly changing events, research windows are short but ‘we cannot understand these complex dynamics by just thinking about them. Online social worlds are accessible to researchers in ways that few other worlds are. If we want to understand them, we need to look with rigor and detail’ (Baym, 2000, pg. 198).

In some cases of netnography research, the artifactual representation of the community (e.g. the blogs or forums) is treated as an archive (Kozinets, 2002, Xun & Reynolds, 2010). This treatment can be both incomplete and problematic as the digital record of postings alone does not make a suitable research archive. The actual issues underlying statements that something is changing or people are enacting transformations can be buried in the secondary record of online communities. During emergent events, timestamps on digital postings provide a temporal and historical sequence, but community members also experience time as real-time change or delay in process. Shifting and dynamic experiences of time during emergent events are not captured in the digita records but are apparent to participants ‘who are there,’ including researchers. The social network emerges and evolves through discussion, reaction, rei<sup>fi</sup>cation and action, and while digital capture occurs, representation of an emergent process is <sup>fi</sup>lled with changing perspectives, reactions and language. There is no archive of emergence until an individual following and documenting the interwoven threads of community re<sup>fl</sup>ection and action creates it. The changing trajectory of the DESN is itself interwoven through multiple channels (e.g. blogs, websites, twitter feeds and news media) and unravelling common threads post hoc is likely inaccessible in an account of the emergent and changing actions, attitudes, language and behaviour of community members.

In the ‘principle of interaction,’ Klein & Myers (1999) endorse the idea that ‘it is possible to conduct a purely observational netnography’ (Kozinets, 2010, pg. 46). Representation of social network phenomena can be analyzed where ‘events [are] captured through a cinematic ‘voyeur’s gaze’” (Kozinets, pg. 55) in an approach which says, ‘here is the world, make of it what you will’ (Van Maanen, 1988, pg. 103). Nonetheless, it is a tricky balance between what constitutes an observational case and an engaged ethnography. In our approach, we observed <sup>fi</sup>rsthand members’ online behaviours in a DESN: the posted comments and discussions representing people’s attitudes, activity logs of peoples’ participation and the redesigns by people reacting to changes in the technologies and ability to participate in their social network. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author did not participate directly with community members during the observed event but was an engaged member with the community for 2 years prior. This includes posting, commenting and understanding the language practices of the community in a social mode of engagement. As Kozinets (2010, pg. 75) points out, there ‘is a spectrum of participation’ that researchers negotiate in their work and the 2 years spent reading, posting and commenting at the studied DESN reduces the ‘guesswork’ about communal meanings and provides insight to the attitudes, behaviours and actions of members.

In conjunction with netnographic methods, we utilize impressionist tales (Van Maanen, 1988) to reveal and represent our case. An impressionist tale is not the act of doing <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, participating with people or observing communities. It is the creation of representations, the interpretive process necessary to illustrate social observations. Impressionist tales accommodate the routine, sometimes generalizable activities of the people under investigation. but they also accommodate the non-routine nature of the members and their expression of participation. In all cases, a reader of an impressionist tale is naturally abstracted from the actual events and is reliant on the researcher to provide the representative window into the nature of the social setting. Through impressionis tales, we provided robust interpretations at a distance from the area of concern. In this, we tell the tale from a more social, less anthropological position, providing broad representation of the studied situation. Two potential misconceptions about impressionist tales require clari<sup>fi</sup>cation First, an impressionist tale is not de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the amount of time spent or the volume of data collected regarding a community, and time/volume should not serve as a proxy measure for the quality of the data, engagement with a social network, or communal understanding of a socia network. An impressionist tale is about connecting a reader to an observed community: the richer the picture, the richer the connection. It is inaccurate to de<sup>fi</sup>ne an impressionist tale by any measure to which a numeric time or volume threshold must be overcome; as noted by Kozinets (2010, pg. 129), ‘large bulks of data can be a barrier to thinking’. Second, impressionist tales are not an approach to generate requirements for systems development or design. It is the goal of an impressionist tale to reveal and understand rather than to develop or create. Impressionist tales do not promote the closing of speci<sup>fi</sup>c theoretical or practical gaps and are not intended to be used as a way of extracting speci<sup>fi</sup>c constructs. Rather, they are representative of a community, aimed at engaging with both the context of study as well as the readership of the impressionist tale (Van Maanen, 1988).

Impressionist tales compliment netnography in the context of DESN engagement as they present events roughly in the sequence they occurred, with a natural amount of researcher interpretation in how the events are represented (Klein & Myers, 1999). Impressionist tales provide insights into human action, and they can stand alone without complex, prede<sup>fi</sup>ned framing devices or extensive post hoc commentary. The power lies in a story to spark interest and involvement from a reader and is as much a function of staying close to the sequential, immediate and tightly linked <sup>fl</sup>ow of events as it is a function of the substance of the tale itself. This is an effort to support multiple interpretations of the same sequence of events so as to broaden, not predetermine, its understanding (Klein & Myers, 1999). Behind any impressionist tale is the tacit claim that there is something worth saying (Van Maanen, 1988), and what is said becomes the basis for continued debate among researchers. These characteristics help loosen the prestructure inherent in netnography to accommodate the new and emerging social trajectories that often present themselves at a moment’s notice.

## DIGG : A CASE O F DIGI TA L LY E N A B LE D SO CI AL NE TWO R K E NG AGEME N T

To exemplify our methodology, we selected a context in which people engaged a DESN to express a perceived con<sup>fl</sup>ict in community vs. organizational and technology trajectories. Members’ commitment to a DESN <sup>fl</sup>uctuate because of porous social network boundaries (Wenger, 1999) and low barriers to entry. People can and do move in hopes of <sup>fi</sup>nding bette opportunities, improved conditions or a more comfortable location for engagement (Wenger, 1999; Kozinets, 2010) as rules of participation breakdown or as the technical or social components are altered. In this case, we examine a speci<sup>fi</sup>c event to understand the creation of new communal trajectories as members engage with and disengage from, an established DESN.

We tell the story from the <sup>fi</sup>eld, where the <sup>fi</sup>eld is entered via the computer network. We present a case in its real life context: a tale of members’ reactions to changes at the social network Digg.com. In the case, data are sequentially ordered to provide the richest picture possible (Van Maanen, 1988). As the events unfolded, our data net widened to include information appearing beyond the boundaries of the primary DESN (Digg) to include secondary data sources (Facebook, Twitter, Hitwise and Alexa) as well as the DESN to which community members migrated (Reddit), re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the broadening set of community engagements associated with the presented case. The case is naturally on the basis of our understanding of engagement, but our study remains broad enough to illustrate how our case can support an emerging and exploratory approach towards future research on DESN

Digg is a DESN that has historically focused on a community that exchanges technology-related articles but recently, the types of shared articles has expanded to include world, business and entertainment news. Members are able to ‘digg’ or ‘bury’ an article if they <sup>fi</sup>nd it interesting and by ‘digging’ an article, they can move it to the front page of Digg so it is seen by a larger audience.

Members can also ‘bury’ an article by voting against in, thus lowering its visibility. Clicking on any article takes members to the comments page of the respective article (Figure 1).

In the comments page, members post reactions to articles, and the reactions themselves can be ‘dugg’ or ‘buried.’ By commenting on, submitting, and digging articles and comments, community members achieve reputation within the social network. Created in December 2004, Digg evolved through three versions prior to June of 2006. During the <sup>fi</sup>rst three versions of Digg, changes were made to the commenting system, a mobile version of Digg was released, and article thumbnails were provided, but nothing that created signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problems was introduced in any of these versions. During this time, participation was steadily increasing, surpassing one million members in 2007 (Arrington, 2007).

Digg began alpha testing of Digg(Version4) in July of 2010 and invited members to assist with testing. ## METHODOLOGICAL AND THEORETICAL REFLECTIONS

Digg appears to be a victim of their own success stemming from an initial design that supported the formation, engagement and evolution of a DESN. In the time leading up to Digg(v4), the technology and the organization designed and nurtured a large DESN community; as described in about.digg.com

From the biggest online destinations to the most obscure blog, Digg surfaces the best stuff as voted on by our community. We’re here to provide tools for our community to discover content, discuss the topics that they’re passionate about, connect with like-minded people, and make some new friends in the process. By looking at information through the lens of the collective community on Digg, you’ll always <sup>fi</sup>nd something interesting and unique.

Within such communities, we can expect to <sup>fi</sup>nd active knowledge exchange, development of collaborative work and the exchange of attitudes, judgements, appropriateness, slang and practices (Brown & Duguid, 2000; Tapscott & Williams, 2006). Digg community members were able to engage each other directly, coordination was tight and action spread quickly character istics of a DESN community strives for (Brown & Duguid, 2000). However, Digg administration mistakenly believed they understood feature changes that were best for members, members who had themselves spent personal time and effort in the design of shared repertoires, practices and forms of engagement. Moreover, the community of Digg, no matter how strong or old, is only one DESN in a landscape of similar communities (e.g. Reddit.com) to which switching costs are low (Tapscott & Williams, 2006). Digg administration appeared to underestimate the DESN landscape they resided in, a judgement that quickly became ‘most damaging as [administration] tries to predict the effects of new information technology on [community] organization (Brown & Duguid, 2000, pg. 145). As such, the release of Digg(v4), with its technical instability, administrative feature changes, reduction in community participation, con<sup>fl</sup>icted reception by a collaborative community and alternatives across the DESN landscape, may have been the perfect storm that led to a con<sup>fl</sup>ict of trajectories within the Digg community.

We present DESN as a type of emergent socio-technical phenomena of engaged community members. Our application of netnography, informed by an impressionist tale. revealeo practices of engagement and design by members of the DESN community. However, revelation alone does not necessarily imply research rigour or relevance. To evaluate this methodological approach, we propose that research aimed at uncovering practices within DESN include evaluative criteria primarily drawn from Kozinets (2010) and supported by Van Maanen (1988), Klein & Myers (1999), Baym (2000), Mingers (2001) and Davison & Martinsons (2011). These criteria (Table 1) provide consistency and comparison within and between similar studies.

Through attention to these criteria, we involve the reader in ways that are both familiar and revealing, developing original insights to recognizable situations. This research aimed to ‘provide the reader with a sense that they have travelled to a different place and gotten to know and understand a group of people’ (Kozinets, 2010, pg. 166). We promoted an interpretive coherence that a DESN community cannot be viewed as ‘a variable or a set of variables to a particular society’ (Avison & Myers, 1995, pg. 46). This interpretation does not preclude other interpretations as we cannot expect to produce a singular understanding of what a DESN is, but we can foster a broader understanding of what a DESN can become, and identify the enablers and constraints that shape different technical, organizational and communal trajectories. Innovation and re<sup>fl</sup>exivity are evident in our case in the observations regarding member engagement and design. Engagement in DESN was shaped by reinforcing and con<sup>fl</sup>icting trajectories of technology, organizational behaviour and the repertoires of community members. We found that con<sup>fl</sup>ict in trajectories can splinter a DESN community as members mobilize to action. We came to understand the engagement and emergence as members voiced dissent and enacted change in reaction to technical and organizational conditions that clashed with communal and social trajectories (Drury et al., 2003). The enactments were not meanderings of disenfranchised members hoping to wreak havoc; they were collective engagements shaped from and governed by communal standards and norms (e.g. ASCII Art, Infographics, Twitter tweets and linking to Reddit).

As social egalitarianism (e.g. voting postings up/down, visible social capital gained through participation and community driven content) came into con<sup>fl</sup>ict with changes in orga nizational design (e.g. removal of the ‘bury’ feature and power shifting to corporate content) and technological adjustments (e.g. technical instability and removal of functionality), disenfranchised members took mobilizing action and designed new communal trajectories. Communities stay together when trajectories are aligned; clashes between trajectories drive communities apart as

a community is not <sup>fi</sup>xed in form or function, it is a mixed bag of possibility whose meaning and concreteness are always being negotiated by individuals, in the context of changing external constraints (Komito, 1998, pg. 105).

Member-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed trajectories illustrate that design can be more than a process of a single individual, designing a single artefact (Hevner et al., 2004). Rather, design can be a collective process, aimed at shaping the course of communal action: a process of planned and emergent behaviours affecting the participation of members. Design becomes a ‘series of processes among people and the systems they inhabit as they experience the world mediated by the system’ (Germonprez et al., 2011, pg. 627).

## Limitations

Our insights are neither predictive nor generalizable – they are the application of method and the critique of theory at a local level. Thus, the application of insights of this study to emerging contexts should be considered anew in new research programmes. The case example involved a speci<sup>fi</sup>c change in organizational and technological trajectory that is unlikely to be repeated elsewhere, a common problem in any research regarding emergence and change. This highlights that the methods used cannot establish causal inference or replicable ways of knowing; instead, the application of these methods are an exemplar of the problems inherent in researching DESN as emergent phenomenon and provide both methodological and theoretica paths forward for further studies and additional re<sup>fi</sup>nement.

## CONCLUSIONS

This research provides three contributions. First, our methodological approach was aimed at creating theory as a ‘sensitizing device’ to view the world in a new way (Klein & Myers, 1999 pg. 75). We offer new methodological considerations in the description of the lived experiences of DESN by disavowing ‘grand theorizing and in their particular, eventful, and contextual nature, [as] they protest the super<sup>fi</sup>ciality of much of the published research in social science (Van Maanen, 1988, pg. 119). We re<sup>fl</sup>ect on a DESN community and, in doing so, we propose an important step in building methodological bridges towards knowing DESN. We present a case for understanding different aspects of DESN, and how we can know the phenomena embedded in the changes in trajectory and in everyday existence. We construct theoretica insights about the engagement within a social community that, in turn, conditions our experience of the community. The methodological approach identi<sup>fi</sup>es the oft-missed, rapidly evolving community practices of DESN in an effort to gain richer and more reliable understanding of how social networks are strati<sup>fi</sup>ed and differentiated, consisting of a plurality of structures. We provide knowledge that the study of DESN is often an un<sup>fi</sup>nished process and ‘meaning wil be worked on again and again’ (Van Maanen, 1988, pg. 120). We provide necessary bu not comprehensive methodological considerations in the highly emergent, highly participatory, user-designed communities of DESN, positioning the researcher as an ‘explorer and cartographer’ of this exciting new terrain (Kozinets, 2010, pg. 179).

Second, we identify DESN as unique information systems that require new forms of knowing and understanding. Given the emergent characteristics of DESN and their trajectories, we cannot expect traditional analytic or functional approaches to be applicable in these new contexts. We must seek out new forms of knowing for both practitioners and academics. In an environment where change is the purview of the community and design is a process that continues into the hands of members’ engagement, behaviour and emergent trajectories can run counter to a sponsoring organization. DESN emerge through interactions of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c assemblage of technology, actors, content and values, and the behavioural repertoires vary over time. We suggest that any functional explanation of a DESN is limited to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c point in time and that understanding DESN is a continual process of understanding the trajectories of the constituent parts. Recognition of the changing activities of the community members adds a layer of understanding to the phenomenon. Value can be gained for both academics and practitioners in knowing when administrative control is available within a DESN, when it is negotiable and when it is absent.

Third, insights provided by this one case set the stage for future research of other events and different contexts. DESN support a vast variety of human collaborative and constructive activity in addition to being utilized for social activism, corporate activity and political action. Represen tative cases need not only entail struggles as illustrated in our case of Digg.com. In 2009, Iranian citizens broadcast in the face of disputed national election results. In 2011, University of California Davis students organized in support of the international Occupy Wall Street protests. In these cases, the collective was a cornerstone of change in the timeline of how the events unfolded and became realized both physically and within a DESN. These movements can be expected to be encountered, coordinated and linked through information technology and people of a DESN and to entail cultural mobility as an endeavour that gives meaning to the ethics, secularism and politics of an event (Brewer & Dourish, 2008), DESN should be expected to support the processes where event logics are negotiated, sorted and inscribed (Brewer & Dourish, 2008) and support emergent trajectories in expanding, nonlinear processes of engagement and design, as people are now engaging and designing DESN, not for singula purposes but for future and emergent relationships.
