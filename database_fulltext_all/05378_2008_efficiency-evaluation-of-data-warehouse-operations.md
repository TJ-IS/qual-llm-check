# Efficiency evaluation of data warehouse operations

Michael Mannino <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Sa Neung Hong <sup>b</sup>, In Jun Choi <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> The Business School, University of Colorado Denver, Denver, CO 80217, USA <sup>b</sup> University of Seoul, Seoul, South Korea

<sup>c</sup> Department of Industrial Engineering, Pohang University of Science and Technology, Pohang, South Korea

Received 18 December 2006; received in revised form 17 September 2007; accepted 14 October 2007 Available online 25 October 2007

## Abstract

We evaluate an efficiency model for data warehouse operations using data from USA and non-USA-based (mostly Korean) organizations. The analysis indicates wide dispersions in operational efficiency, industry and region differences, large differences in labor budgets between efficient and inefficient firms, few organizations efficient in both refresh processing and query production, and difficulty of providing some variables. Follow-up interviews provide insights about the value of efficiency comparisons of information technology organizations and suggestions to improve the model. Using this analysis, we propose a framework containing data warehouse characteristics and firm characteristics to explain IT operational efficiency at the subfirm level. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Data warehouse; Data envelopment analysis; Data quality; Operational efficiency

## 1. Introduction

Data warehouse, a term coined by William Inmon in 1990, refers to a central data repository where data from operational databases and other sources are integrated, cleaned, and archived to support decision-making. A data warehouse provides management with convenient access to large volumes of internal and external data. Because of the potential benefits, most medium to large organizations operate data warehouses. Many of these organizations have operated data warehouses for five years or more with continuing development to increase the size and scope of the data warehouses.

As data warehouse technology and deployment matures, efficient operation becomes a priority. Due to complex data requests, large volumes of data, incompatibilities among data sources, and other complicating factors, operating a data warehouse may involve high costs for complex hardware/software architectures and significant labor support. To measure and improve the efficiency of delivering information products, organizations should strive to compare with other peer organizations.

In this paper, we present a model to evaluate organizations operating significant data warehouses. The emphasis in our model is to measure relative efficiency of an information technology (IT) organization providing a significant information service, usually for internal usage. The model contains salient variables to evaluate refresh processing and query production, two major operations for data warehouses. The variables in the model include traditional resource consumption (labor usage and computing budgets), system usage measures (users and queries), data quality measures (timeliness and availability), and a size measure (amount of change data). To assess the model, we analyze a data set using Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), comparing efficiencies for significant subsets of the model. In addition to the forma analysis, we propose a framework to explain the efficiency of information technology operations and provide anecdotal evidence about the difficulties of the evaluation.

The results of this study have important implications for quantitative evaluation of IT service organizations, particularly those providing complex data products. Efficiency models for complex data products support evaluation of tradeoffs between costs and data quality levels. Although there are numerous studies of information technology at the firm level, this paper emphasizes subfirm efficiency. As far as we are aware, this paper proposes the first efficiency model for data warehouse operations along with analysis of a data set and a framework to explain subfirm operations.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes related work about data warehouse operations, evaluations of data warehouse success, and efficiency evaluations of information technology. Section 3 presents the efficiency model and data collection details. Section 4 analyzes the efficiency results and discusses follow-up interviews with selected organizations about the impact of the results. Section 5 concludes the study.

## 2. Related work

Data warehouse operations have been studied in detail since the early 1990s primarily with a focus on the refresh process. Although most of the research involves algorithms, some of the research involves the representation and control of the refresh process. Bouzeghoub et al. [5] developed a flexible workflow model to represent the refresh process. Their conceptual model provides a framework to understand the details of refresh processes including constraints on data sources and data warehouses. In a related work, the architecture developed by Theodoratos and Bouzeghoub [25] is notable for its emphasis on data quality goals, specifically data timeliness. These conceptual representations and architectures provide insights into variables that are important in evaluating operational efficiency.

As an alternative to measure operational efficiency, empirical investigations about current practices and success factors for data warehouses have some overlap with the issues studied in this paper. In surveys of data warehouse managers and professionals, Watson et al. [27], reported about current practices in data warehouse management including costs and benefits. Mirroring some of the results in this study, they found wide variation in reported development costs and return on investments as well as qualitative improvements in data quality and information production. In surveys of data warehouse managers and data suppliers, Wixom and Watson [10,29] identified data quality as measured by accuracy, comprehensiveness, consistency, and completeness as an important component of data warehouse success. In a survey of one large organization, Shin [21] indicated that users had a high level of satisfaction with data currency although in separate interviews, data currency was listed as a problem. Although these studies provided evidence about the importance of data quality in data warehouse success, none of these studies provided evidence about tradeoffs to achieve desired quality levels.

Operational efficiency has been a common topic in the information technology literature with a focus on the firm level using secondary data. DEA has been used to study information technology investment efficiency [2,7,14,19, 20,26] and Internet firm efficiency [18], but not information technology operations. At the system level, Han and Kauffman [11] studied website design efficiency using web logs to understand user behavior. Their study provides insight into website design tradeoffs, but the study does not involve operational issues at the subfirm level as presented in this paper.

The most closely related work involves efficiency analysis of software production efficiency [1,17,30] in an individual firm. The unit in these studies is the software project with most emphasis on development team productivity. In the most comprehensive study, Yang and Paradi [30] used effort and project duration as input variables and certification level (a discrete variable) and source lines of code as output variables. Their analysis compared software team productivity using DEA with another internal measure. Due to the extensive project accounting, data was collected from internal software project reports. In contrast, our study emphasizes interfirm comparisons using survey data provided by each organizational unit.

## 3. Measuring efficiency of data warehouse operations

The emphasis in our model is to measure relative efficiency of an IT organization providing a significant information service, usually for internal usage. Efficiency is defined as the ratio of outputs to inputs. IT organizations are classified as efficient or inefficient relative to the peer IT organizations in the data set. A statement that an IT organization is efficient means that no other IT organizations are more efficient considering the combination of inputs and outputs in the data set.

In any efficiency study, it is important to identify the observational unit. In this study, the observational unit is the IT organization, not the data warehouse. Although the ideal data warehouse architecture involves a single data warehouse, many IT organizations operate a small number of data warehouses rather than one large data warehouse [15]. Thus, an important element of the efficiency model is to account for economies of scale involved in managing one or more data warehouses. In cases in which separate IT organizations manage data warehouses, each IT organization is a separate observation. For example, a firm with two IT organizations managing different collections of data warehouses would be evaluated as two observations.

The efficiency model involves two major processes of data warehouse operation. Refresh processing involves the periodic extraction of change data from data sources (operational databases or external data sources), significant data transformations (cleaning, integrating, and standardizing), loading the transformed data into a data warehouse, and publishing transformed data into summarized formats (data cubes and materialized views) for business analysts. The query production process involves extracting data from a data warehouse, data marts, data cubes, or other storage structures to support user queries. The query production process includes both the monitoring and setup of query executions as well as help desk activities to support interpretation of data and formulation of queries. Since the focus of the efficiency model is operations, new software development to extend data warehouse capabilities are excluded from the model.

The efficiency model involves significant variables about the refresh and query production processes as depicted in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 1. All variables in Fig. 1 are measured in monthly periods except for data age and availability. The latter variables are measured on a daily basis, consistent with common practice.

Some of the variables in the model require elaboration beyond Table 1. Data age is a measure of timeliness, an important measure of data quality for data warehouses. Data age reflects the time lag between the time an event is recorded in an operational database and the time it is available for data warehouse users. For multiple refresh processes, the data age is weighted to reflect the age of each batch of change data. In the DEA model, the inverse of data age is used instead of the raw data age because DEA models maximize outputs. Availability is used rather than the time to complete refresh processing because longer refresh processing does not necessarily imply unavailability. With specialized hardware architectures, refresh processing can occur while the warehouse (or at least part of the warehouse) is available for user queries. The flexibility ratio variable indicates the extent to which ad hoc queries are supported and used. Planned queries are easier to accommodate than ad hoc queries.

The input variables are consistent with the definition of IT by Oz [16] in which IT includes hardware, software, communications, and labor. We used monthly operating budgets as a measure of smoothed expenditures. Actual expenditures vary from month to month but monthly budgets should be more stable with reasonable approximation to expenditures over multiple months. We feel that monthly operating budgets for computing and labor capture IT expenditures in a consistent manner across organizations.

The output variables were determined as a result of a previous field study of data warehouse refreshment policies [15], careful review of the literature, and a pilo study to assess the feasibility of data collection. Data age and change data are measures of timeliness and completeness, two important data quality attributes for refresh processes. Many previous studies have reported about data timeliness for data warehouses. Change data indirectly measures the quantity and breadth of a warehouse, two essential aspects for deriving value from a data warehouse. Availability is a measure of data quality for the accessibility attribute [12]. The number of queries, flexibility ratio, and number of users are measures of use frequency, a traditional variable used in studies of IT productivity and efficiency. The number of queries is the primary measure, consistent with traditional use frequency. The number of users reflects the marketing of a warehouse and help desk operations. Ad hoc queries are more difficult to support because they are not scheduled in advance.

Despite the care taken to select and define variables, some variables remained difficult to estimate. The most difficult variable to estimate was the computing budget because organizations had shared computing centers and isolating data warehouse costs was difficult. In some cases, the number of queries and the flexibility ratio were difficult to estimate as business intelligence tools can generate additional queries beyond the queries submitted directly by users.

In addition to studying the overall efficiency of data warehouse processing, we investigate efficiency of refresh processing and query production separately. As shown in Figs. 2 and 3, the input variables in both models remain the labor and computing budgets, but the output variables are changed to reflect the different orientation of refresh processing and query production. The same inputs are used in each submodel because it is not feasible to isolate the part of labor and computing budgets for refresh and query production. In most organizations, the same individuals and computing infrastructure support both parts of data warehouse operations. Separating the two models supports identification of inefficiencies and improved understanding of data warehouse processing among the organizations in the study.

We used Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) [8], a linear programming technique for measuring the relative efficiency of decision-making units (DMU) with multiple inputs and outputs. DEA is used to establish a best practice group of efficient DMUs among the set of DMUs. The essential insight behind DEA is that the input/output weights are calculated to maximize each DMU's efficiency. A DMU that is not efficient with its most beneficial weights is a strong statement of inefficiency in comparison to peer DMUs. DEA has been widely applied and studied with more than 3000 published papers [9].

The two major aspects of DEA usage are orientation (input or output) and returns to scale (constant or variable). In input (output) orientation, the goal is to minimize input (output) usage while maintaining output (input) levels. We chose input-oriented DEA because the outputs of operating a data warehouse are often fixed by organizational constraints [15]. Returns to scale refers to increasing or decreasing efficiency based on size. We chose variable returns to scale (VRS) because it better suites the heterogeneous organizations in our study with different sizes, industries, and data warehouse characteristics. The input-oriented VRS DEA model [3] is used for the analysis of data warehouse operational efficiency.

## 4. Analysis of operational efficiency

Data was solicited from three groups in the second half of 2005. The Center for Information Technology Innovation (CITI) at the University of Colorado at Denver is group of Chief Information Officers from organizations with significant operations in the Denver, Colorado area. Surveys were sent to this group in June 2005 with a response rate of 10 out of 20 organizations.<sup>1</sup> The BI Network (www.B-EYE-Network.com) posted a survey announcement on its semi-monthly newsletter several times in the fourth quarter of 2005. Twelve responses were received as a result of the newsletter announcement although 15 other responses were missing information and not usable. In Korea, responses were solicited through individual contacts of the authors. Surveys were emailed to 45 organizations, mostly in the financial sector. Twenty one responses were received.

## 4.1. Organization background

The organizations in the study represent a wide section of industries and organization sizes. Most organizations have operated data warehouses for at least two years with many organizations operating data warehouses for more than 5 years. There is considerable variation in the number of tables and physical size. Most organizations operate a single data warehouse but a significant subset operates 3 or more warehouses. The number of tables by some organizations is large with 19 organizations reporting more than 1000 tables. Although the organizations were instructed to report the number of tables in the data warehouses exclusive of end user tables, some organizations may have included end user tables. Table A.1 in Appendix A provides complete details about organization characteristics.

To summarize the characteristics, we divide the organizations by economic groups and industry as shown in Table 2. The Korea-Other group consists mainly of Korean firms in the banking, financial services, and insurance industries. There are four firms from other emerging economies including Brazil, Saudi Arabia, and Malaysia. The USA-Europe group has somewhat larger organizations managing older and more data warehouses per organization as compared to the Korea-Other group.

For the efficiency variables, Table 3 summarizes the variables by region while Table A.2 lists the values for each organization. We combine the Korean and other region organizations due to the small number of organizations in the other regions. The USA-Europe subset has wide dispersion for the input variables as measured by the large coefficient of variation and the large difference between mean and median. When DMU 25 is omitted from the USA-Europe subset, the dispersion decreases significantly as the coefficient of variations decrease to 1.35 and 2.18, respectively for the labor budget (LB) and computing budget (CB) variables. The Korean-Other subset has somewhat more variation for the last four output variables (CD, NQ, FR, and NU). DMUs 10, 11, and 37 contribute much of the variation for the output variables in the Korean-Other subset. It seems a reasonable conjecture that DMU 25 will be inefficient and DMUs 10, 11, and 37 will be efficient based on this informal inspection of the data set.

For a more precise analysis of extreme DMUs, we identified outliers using two different methods. Wilson [28] developed a graphical method to identify outliers for non-parametric frontier models such as DEA. The Local Outlier Factor (LOF) method [6] is a distance-based method that compares the local density of an observation to the local densities among its k neighbors. In both methods, DMUs 11, 25, and 37 were identified as outliers. The informal inspection based on statistics of each variable is consistent with this more precise analysis. These observations appear valid as the survey respondents were asked to validate their responses. Thus, the observations are retained in the data set.

## 4.2. DEA evaluation

With a rather large model (two inputs and six outputs) and limited sample size, data reduction is prudent. We investigated data reduction using Principal Component Analysis [4] and statistical tests for irrelevant variables [24]. Principal Component Analysis, although used in some past DEA studies [18] has multivariate normality assumptions. In addition, Principal Component Analysis has been applied to select among all combinations of variables, not to indicate the most significant individual variables. Statistical tests defined by Simar and Wilson [24] require about five times as much data as available in this study. In addition, the tests do not have a well-defined selection procedure to eliminate variables from a frontier model.

## 4.2.2. Discussion

A basic result is caution in interpreting DMUs that appear efficient in the full model. As indicated in Fig. 4 and Table 5, the reduced model provides the most conservative evaluation with only three efficient DMUs. The relatively small sample size and the larger number of variables in the full model lead to more efficient DMUs. The wide confidence intervals in Table 4 provide additional evidence about caution when interpreting efficient DMUs. The wide intervals make it possible that some efficient DMUs may not really be efficient.

An interesting finding is the bimodal distribution of efficiency scores. In both the USA-Europe and Korea-Other subsets, there are relatively few DMUs between efficient and very inefficient. With the limited data in this study, we cannot provide a fully tested model to explain the gap although we provide some data to suggest explanatory variables in Table 9. The organization size (number of employees) and data warehouse maturity (data warehouse age) are negatively correlated with efficiency at the traditional 0.05 level in the full and reduced models. The negative correlations seem counter to conventional wisdom about economies of scale and organizational learning positively impacting returns on IT investments.

A secondary finding is the gap between the USA-Europe subset and the Korean-Other subset. The Korea-Other subset has more efficient DMUs than the USA-Europe subset across models. This finding may be partially explained by the homogeneity of the Korean-Other subset with most DMUs involved in the financial industry. Lower labor rates may explain some of the difference. Another possible explanatory variable is legacy systems. Although this study did not assess the age of operational systems, it seems reasonable that major systems in Korea are more modern because of the relatively recent rise in the overall Korean economy.

To provide insights into the two key findings, we propose a framework in Fig. 5 to understand operational efficiency at the subfirm level. The framework applies to DW operational efficiency but a similar framework may apply to other areas of IT operational efficiency. The firm characteristics have been identified in other studies of IT investments [13]. Firm type differentiates between information intensive and physical product intensive industries. Region indicates the development status of the economy in which the DMU operates. The data warehouse characteristics have been used in studies of current practices and success factors for data warehouses. Data warehouse architecture [27] indicates the extent of centralization between a centralized data warehouse and independent data marts. The age of systems is an indicator of the legacy status of operational systems that provide data for a data warehouse.

In testing the framework, it is important to separate the direct effects of firm and data warehouse characteristics on operational efficiency from the indirect effects of firm characteristics on data warehouse characteristics. To reduce diagram detail, individual variable influences have been suppressed as each firm characteristic can affect each data warehouse characteristic and operational efficiency.

## 4.3. Post analysis review

To obtain insights about the utility of the study, we provided feedback to each participating organization and invited feedback from study respondents. Our intent was to obtain qualitative feedback that could be used to improve the model design for future data collection efforts. • No respondents measure efficiency for internal data warehouse operations or other IT operations. All of the respondents use some measures that are monitored over time for trends.

• All respondents expressed interest in peer comparisons about operational efficiency. The respondents indicated that efficiency comparisons would be most useful if linked to data already provided to IT consulting firms. Efficiency comparison trends would be more useful than single period scores.

• The respondents indicated that peer selection should be based on more than industry and firm characteristics. Peer selection should include the architecture and tool sets used in data warehouse operations.

• Interest in the efficiency models varied by respondent. Some organizations were more interested in the query production side as it is more aligned with user satisfaction. Others were more focused on refresh processing to ensure reasonable operations in comparison to peers.

• Two organizations reported that they do not track some important kinds of ad hoc queries. The study results reminded the organizations about the need to track ad hoc queries more carefully.

## 5. Conclusion

We presented an efficiency model for evaluating data warehouse operations and evaluated the relative efficiencies of a data set of organizations operating significant data warehouses. The efficiency model supports evaluation of refresh and query production processes for a collection of data warehouses managed by an IT organization. The variables in the models include traditional resource consumption (labor usage and computing budgets), system usage measures (number of queries, number of users, and flexibility ratio), data quality measures (data age and availability), and a size measure (change data amounts). We analyzed a data set of primarily USA and Korean firms operating significant data warehouses using input-oriented DEA. In the analysis, we evaluated efficiencies of the full efficiency model, the refresh process subset, the query production subset, and a small subset consisting of one input and two output variables. Follow-up interviews were conducted with several organizations to evaluate the utility of the analysis and efficiency model. Based on the analysis, we proposed a framework using data warehouse and firm characteristics to explain subfirm operational efficiency.

This work is a continuation of an effort to evaluate the organizational impact of data warehouses. A previous study [15] identified significant factors affecting refresh policies. The current study was influenced by the previous study in the identification of significant variables and the need for efficiency comparisons. In future work, we will extend this study to evaluate data warehouse development and operations over multiple periods and test the proposed framework to explain the efficiency differences observed in this study.
