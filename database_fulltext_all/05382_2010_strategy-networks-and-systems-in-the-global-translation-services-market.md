# Strategy, networks and systems in the global translation services market

Duncan R. Shaw <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Christopher P. Holland <sup>b</sup>

<sup>b</sup> Manchester Business School, The University of Manchester, UK

## a b s t r a c t

The globalisation of markets has led to an increased demand for language translation services that support and enable communication between economic partners. For example, technical documents, software systems, business documents and web sites all need to be translated into multiple languages for individual national markets, and the information that they contain changes periodically. This paper sets out a theoretical framework that describes the architectures of business processes within and between separate firms that are used to support the delivery and management of services. This is done by coordinating the fit between externally generated problem complexity, from customers, and the internally generated complexity of different potential network configuration solutions. The theoretical framework is an architecture of how complexity is generated and managed at the different structural levels and across the different processual stages of an industry. A case study of thebigword, a major international translation services company, illustrates how the framework is applied in practice. The case study analyses the implementation of an electronic market platform which enables the coordination of the different stakeholders involved in the translation services market. These stakeholders include translators, translation services companies and their clients in a global business network.

## 1. Introduction

Several authors have identified the influence of information systems on market structure in different industries (e.g. Crowston and Myers, 2004; Holland, 1995). Here we develop this theme in a global business sector, translation services. The globalisation of markets has presented firms with many challenges and communicating with their networks of custom ers and suppliers is a significant one. International firms need to communicate with customers in any market that they target and any firm with a web site has the potential to do business internationally. Similarly, firms that take advantage of differences in the international supply of resources (e.g. materials, knowledge or offshored process capacity) are presented with complex communication challenges in both the supply side and the demand side of their network.

A specific communication challenge for firms comes from the different languages of customers and business partners. It is extremely difficult for companies to manage all of their language translation requirements using internal experts and most large firms use a combination of external translation services and in-house expertise. The difficulties of dealing with a large number of different translators are summed up by the CEO of thebigword:

The biggest problem that clients have is to be able to interface between themselves and their translators.

IT outsourcing has been well documented in academic and business literature (e.g. Lacity and Wilcocks, 2000). Typical advantages include economic cost reduction, flexibility for growth, resilience and access to specific technical expertise. Companies have continued to explore IT outsourcing opportunities and have extended the scope of agreements to include the delivery of a service. BP, for example, outsourced its financial function to an accountancy firm (Holland et al., 2005). This included outsourcing not only the financial IT systems but also the staff involved in delivering the financial function services, i.e. the management information, internal audit and financial reporting staff, Whilst there are some parallels between IT outsourcing and the delivery of translation services, the focus of this paper is on the strategy and architecture of the overall system, composed of clients, translation management companies and translators, rather than the outsourcing of translation per se. The theory base is therefore taken from the literature on systems and modular architectures rather than from the IT outsourcing literature. Having said that, there may be some important implications to consider as it is likely that the trend in outsourcing is away from simple IT outsourcing towards the concept of services outsourcing, whether the services involve finance, operations management, translation or other functional activities. This means that a more holistic approach is required to capture the complexity of the organisation and delivery of business services and to manage the inter-relationships between multiple organisational entities beyond the simple dyadic relationship that has typically existed between a single client and its outsourcing provider.

As in most industries translation services firms compete by attempting to differentiate themselves via the innovative use of information technology and by their business processes. In this paper we investigate the relationships between networks of firms and individual actors in a world market, the information systems that connect them and the strategies they use to manage the complexities these relationships generate. Specifically we ask to what extent information systems can be exploited to differentiate and to deliver competitive advantages in these firms' activities. We also ask if such use of an information system can be sold as a service in its own right to competitors and customers and, at the network level, what is the effect of such technologies on market structure and competition, Of central importance to these research questions is the impact of information system on organisational complexity. The world translation services industry is an under-studied area and this study is a valuable addition to the literature as well as a novel investigation of the relationships between complex supply chains, information systems and market structure.

To explore these questions we use the literatures on Complexity Theory, Product Design and Organisational Design to assemble a theoretical framework for analysing how complexity is generated and managed at different structural levels and across different processual stages of an industry. These structural and processual perspectives enable us to analyse the information systems that link firms together in the form of market structure, business-to-business (B2B) integration, coordination mechanisms, business activities and industry standards. We focus on the firm level and then the market level, before moving onto the information system and business processes that link firms into supply chains and into markets. We use this architecture to analyse the world translation services industry. We investigate how the structure of the market and the activities of the firms are influenced by the information systems they use, in the way these systems help them manage the inherent complexities of this industry. First we describe the sources of complexity for the firms. We then use our theoretical framework to analyse how the information system of one firm is used to manage such complexity. We use this analysis to discuss the relationship between information systems and market structure, showing how information systems can be used to manage complexity at different structural levels and processual stages in order to better use resources, and how this in turn changes the structure of the market and the activities of the firm.

## 2. Background literature

In order to investigate how an information system can help in managing different types of complexity we first examine the fundamental sources of complexity in projects, firms, supply chains and networks. We then describe several architectural approaches to managing complexity that we take from the literature. We have used this approach because we investigate how strategies for dealing with complex businesses, like the case industry, actually influence the industry’s structure. The focus of our study is on how complexity is generated and managed when there is an upper limit on firms’ ability to deal with it (Simon, 1997).

## 2.1. Using organisational architectures to manage organisational complexity

Simon’s concept of near decomposability (1969) and Weick’s related concept of loose coupling (1976; Orton and Weick, 1990) have long been used to analyse organisational problems. Near decomposability and loose coupling are concepts that describe the non-linearity in the intensity of relationships between different entities. This enables managers and researchers to use composition and decomposition in dealing with complex organisational problems (Ethiraj and Levinthal, 2004). The variation in the intensity of relations between different parts of a problem makes it possible to split the problem into smaller pieces and it also enables similar components of the problem to be clustered. The decomposition of complex problems is required because managers and researchers are boundedly rational and so they have an upper limit on their ability to deal with complexity (Simon, 1997). Problem decomposition enables more complex problems to be tackled. The clustering of similar problem components helps the management of complex problems by allowing standardisation (Kindleberger, 1983; Langlois and Savage, 2001) and by allowing organisational complexity to be clustered into a discrete area. For example, Dell’s product offer includes a bewildering number of ways to configure a PC and then combine it with different peripherals (see Dell.com). From the customer’s perspective this is an advantage because it enables customisation. But from Dell’s perspective its offer is organised into a standard menu of components. It is the potential inter-relations of the menu contents that generate complexity and the customer can handle this complexity themselves.

Complexity is generated by the number of possible inter-relations between system elements rather than the number of system elements (Ahl and Allen, 1996). Complexity can be reduced by splitting problems into sub-problems. In splitting up organisational design problems into smaller pieces managers also need to preserve a holistic view of the overall problem since its components are only nearly decomposed not completely decomposed. The danger is that there are inter-relations between problem areas that, whilst relatively weak, still have the ability to produce unexpected consequences for managers. For example, in Hamilton’s description of the design of the Itanium chip by Intel’s engineers (Ethiraj and Levinthal, 2004) a change to one module of the chip would affect many other modules in a manner that was not possible to forecast. Boundedly rational managers can still co-ordinate complex organisations if they use Simon’s concept of hierarchy (1962). In a hierarchical solution to a complex problem, managers abstract away non-salient information from the problem. This is why higher level managers deal with more abstract issues than do shop floor supervisors and why together with other management levels they are able to manage very complex organisations and environments.

The concepts of hierarchy, near decomposability and loose coupling have been used to develop strategies that use modular architectures as solutions to the problems that are generated by complex products or complex organisational systems (Baldwin and Clark, 2000; Langlois, 2002; Parnas, 1972; Simon, 1962). Strategies based upon modular architectures enable problem components, design issues or system characteristics that have commonalities to be clustered together in modules. The intensity of relationships inside modules is relatively high and the intensity of relationships between modules is relatively low. The architecture of such a modular system is a model of the inter-relations between the modules. This architecture is less complex than a model of the whole system because it is an abstraction of the whole, Intra-module information is hidden (Baldwin and Clark, 2000; Langlois, 2002). Another benefit of a modular strategy is that work on different modules can progress in parallel, which speeds production, design and any other problem resolution processes.

## 2.2. Using architectures to manage complexity in markets, supply chains and inter-firm networks

On a higher level of analysis the concept of modular architecture has also been used to study market structures, firm networks and supply chains (Galunic and Eisenhardt, 2001; Garud et al., 2003; Henderson and Clark, 1990; Richard and Devinney, 2005; Schilling and Steensma, 2001). In these studies the modules are the whole firms and the architectural relations between them are the products and services they exchange. These are incorporated by the customer firm into its own production system as raw materials, component parts, sub-assemblies or enabling services. Each supplier-firm module produces these outputs via its internal business processes. The ability to enact and change these processes is conceptualised as a dynamic capability, the sum of the processes which transform resources into firm assets and deal with external change. In a firm network these dynamic capabilities are constantly changing and new configurations of firms and their contributions constantly emerge (Galunic and Eisenhardt, 2001; Winter, 2003).

## 2.3. Using inter-organisational architectures to manage structural and processual complexity

The products, markets or other environmental drivers will typically determine the appropriate organisation of inter-firm relations. Complex organisational and network systems can be divided into sub-systems. A modular perspective divides systems into two levels, the modular or sub-system level and the architectural or system level. However, modules can contain sub-modules and architectures themselves can be part of modules, i.e. a modular perspective is reflexive and the hierarchy of levels is never limited to a stack of just two.

Fig. 1 shows how this hierarchy of levels exists structurally as well as processually. The service/process/infrastructure structures of the supplier and the customer firms have several levels and each of the levels of the two firms are joined by business processes. The business processes that join the structural levels of the two firms have a processual hierarchy of stages. The structural levels of the information system are generally known as the ‘solution stack’: the computer hardware (e.g. a PC) runs the operating system software (e.g. MS Windows) which in turn runs application software (e.g. MS Word). Another example of a hierarchical solution stack is ‘LAMP’: a Linux operating system running an Apache Web server which runs MySQL, a database management system, which can be used via a web page scripting language such as Perl.

Both the process stages and the structural levels are hierarchical, i.e. they have some form of relational asymmetry. Process stages are asymmetrical in that their causal link is only in one direction and structural levels are asymmetrical in that higher levels emerge from lower levels, e.g. operating systems are a dynamic configuration of computer hardware, and software applications are the emergent configurations of operating systems. Higher structural levels have lower natural frequencies and their phenomena are abstractions of lower level phenomena. Process stages are also emergent phenomena but their emergence is due to multiple option choices. Structural level emergence is due to different levels of natural frequency.

Both structural emergence and processual emergence produce complexity. Structural emergence produces complexity by making it impossible for an observer to understand, or even perceive, phenomena from levels that are higher than the observer’s. For example, low level staff find it impossible to fully understand the decisions of the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) that take into account a ‘bigger picture’ – unless these decisions are explained to them. Processual emergence produces complexity by making it impossible for an observer to forecast accurately more than a few process stages into the future. For example, the number of potential permutations in how products are actually used by consumers makes it impossible to accurately forecast every mode of use – unless the consumers are asked. These two forms of emergence produce complexity barriers for firms and individuals that seek to work smoothly and efficiently with each other in markets and supply chains. These complexity barriers take the form of incompatibilities in the ways and means of doing business.

One way to reduce incompatibilities and promote commonality is by the use of platforms. In descriptions of product architectures the platform concept is used to label the structural level whose behaviour supports some higher level phenomena, e.g. MS Windows runs MS Word (Robertson and Ulrich, 1998). If the product is used for inter-organisational purposes there needs to be some sharing of the platform, i.e. in order for two or more firms to share the use of a product they need to have a sub-system or platform in common. Thus some level or sub-system of their information system has to be the same or else they are unconnected, i.e. there is no commonality.

Markets and inter-organisational networks depend upon the multi-level systems that connect them. One example of the different system levels that join organisations is the mixed mode form of inter-firm organisation which displays characteristics of both a market and a hierarchy (Holland and Lockett, 1997). Other examples of different dimensions and their affect upon market structure are inter-operability standards and the process of agreeing to them (Rodon et al., 2007), and open standards (Christiaanse and Rodon, 2005). These inter-firm platforms have two enabling characteristics: (i) they decouple some aspects of the market structure to allow information hiding (Parnas, 1972) and (ii) they couple the information systems of market entities to allow easier business-to-business working. These are two important characteristics of platforms. Plat forms produce support services for their users who can then focus on their specific network roles, and at the same time they act as a common medium for working together (Robertson and Ulrich, 1998).

## 2.4. Sources and methods of managing complexity in markets and inter-firm networks

We have described how complexity is generated by the number of possible inter-relations between the different aspects of firms and their information systems in a supply chain. These complexities present themselves as different potential coupling solutions between different structural levels and different process stages. This in turn takes the form of a large choice of different organisational and business process designs. The challenge for mangers is to choose the optimal, or at least a workable, design from all the potential designs.

Practically speaking these are the strategic and operational decisions for managers in firms and supply chains. But the innate limits on human information processing, i.e. human bounds on rationality, limit the complexity of the systems that people and organisations of people can manage. However, the internal complexity of these systems can be limited by using standards to constrain choice in separate systems; by using platforms to enhance commonality between separate systems; by using known and agreed business rules to automate business processes; by adding organisational levels to use local decision-making where needed; and by adding process stages to make process outputs more specific and therefore more customised. These tools boil down to decisions of whether to couple or decouple elements of the business at every stage and at every level. The sum of these decisions is the architecture of the system of firms, their people, their business processes and their information systems.

The structural and processual architectures of the firms, supply chains and their information systems are a menu of possible choices at every level and every stage. Which customers should be marketed to? Which suppliers should be partnered with? When should this be done? By focusing on a particular set of choices, which constrain other choices to be constants, it is possible to use management resources more efficiently to deal with complexity. This can be applied to the design of organisational structures, business processes and information systems in inter-firm networks. Fig. 2 represents these inter-relations between the externally generated complexity of demands from customers and suppliers, for products, services and other outputs, and the network’s internal capacity to deal with complexity. The network’s internal capacity to deal with complexity is bounded. But this capacity can be reconfigured using many different level perspectives and stage perspectives. Changing the number of organisational levels in the network, i.e. the addition or deletion of new levels between the human level and the network level, increases or decreases the integration and commonality of the network’s members. Changing the number of process stages, i.e. the addition or deletion of different numbers of options to choose from at each business process stage, decreases or increases the customisation of the network’s products.

In Fig. 2 the block arrows represent stages and the black block arrows represent consecutively chosen future stages. Reconfiguration of a B2B network is possible because the network members’ work with each other is mediated by an information system which itself can be reconfigured along structural level and processual stage dimensions. The goal is to reconfigure the architecture of the internal capacities of the network so as to fit the architecture of the external demands upon the network. An example of adding a structural level would be if a firm moved its entire Human Resources staff, sited in different departments, into a central shared Human Resources department. An example of deleting a structural level would be if a multinational firm sacked the General Manager for a country and then managed the staff in that country directly from the corporate headquarters in another country. An example of adding a process stage would be if a standard product or service were given additional customisation to better fit the needs of some highly valued customer. An example of deleting a process stage would be if a firm’s product range was reduced to a smaller number of standard product variants as the result of a business process reengineering (BPR) analysis.

## 3. Research method

The translation services industry is a rich source of data and concepts regarding market complexity, evolving market structures, innovative use of technology and global business relationships. This study focuses on how information systems affect the structure of a market, by helping the firms in it to manage different types of complexity, and how information systems can be exploited to differentiate and deliver competitive advantages. To explore these issues we assemble a theoretical framework that is concerned with the modular architectures of inter-firm relationships and the technology that mediates these relationships. We use the framework in Fig. 2 to analyse a case study, thebigword, by taking structural perspectives, at different levels of analysis, and by taking processual perspectives at different stages of the business processes that relate the network and supply chain members together. We examine the different entities that exist at different system levels, i.e translator, document, project team, firm, supply chain, market. We investigate how these entities worked on the different stages of a translation project, with respect to each other.

This is a multi-level study so it takes an interpretive stance, because of the subjective nature of human interaction, and iterates around a hermeneutic circle, between network, process and service level perspectives so as to consider an interdependent whole (Klein and Mvers. 1999). The novelty of using a combined structural and processual lens to investigate the relationships between the stakeholders in a network points to a qualitative approach because the investigation is concerned with initial questions of ‘how’ and ‘why’ rather than of ‘how many’. Following Yin’s recommendations (2003) our investigation uses a case study approach because it is concerned with contemporary phenomena, over which we have no control, and business relationships between a large number of different firms from a range of sectors. The use of just one case study has external validity, i.e. generalisation, implications (Lee, 1989), but this is justified at the outset of theory generation (Benbasat et al., 1987) and although sample size may limit statistical generalisation it does not degrade analytic or theoretical generalisation (Lee and Baskerville, 2003; Yin, 2003). This use of an exemplar case is consistent with the theory-building objectives of this study. The criteria for choosing the case firm were based upon the complexity of its market, the processual complexity of its service production and its ability to reconfigure the structure and process architectures of its market to deal with this complexity on a project-by-project basis. The characteristics of the case firm are as follows. It produces services:

\- With a complex and dynamically changing network of organisational partners.

\- For a diverse world market of customers with their own specific and changing requirements.

\- By enacting a complicated set of process stages.

\- Using a technological platform that is a structural foundation for the market.

\- Which then enables structural and processual reconfigurations of the network for each project.

We are concerned with dynamic phenomena so we have used different data collection methods and multiple sources (Eisenhardt, 1989). Overall, we used triangulation to converge evidence, analysis and synthesis upon the same process, service and market structure phenomena. The data collection took place over a period of 4 years in different contexts. Initially the authors were involved in an advisory capacity, which developed into a more traditional researcher/case study organisation relationship. In total, the number of formal visits and interviews with key informants was eight, approximately every 6 months. Interviews were conducted with senior management, including the CEO, the Finance Director and the IT Director Interviews ranged from short informal conversations to semi-structured meetings and interviews, and included a videobased case study of the firm.

Our purpose was not so much to capture the dynamic nature of the strategy process but more to ensure an understanding of the content and details of the strategy in the context of a complex organisation operating in a global market network. To support our data collection and analysis, the researchers had several opportunities to gain feedback from the senior management team on our abstractions and frameworks, which enabled us to refine and enhance the concepts and ideas through an iterative process. A good relationship with the case study organisation over this period helped to reduce validity reactivity and increase trust as well as disclosure. Data sources included meeting notes, telephone conversations, archival data, organisation reports and the web site content of the different organisations involved. The case data and interpretation were reviewed, commented on and validated by the senior management team to ensure their accuracy and completeness.

## 4. Case analysis: the architectures of the translation services market

In order to investigate how an information system can help in managing the different types of complexity in an industry we first examine the fundamental sources of complexity in the projects, firms, supply chains and networks of the case firm’s industry. Then we use the theoretical framework that we have developed to analyse how this complexity is managed in the case study firm.

## 4.1. Research setting

In this paper. translation services refers to the set of activities that includes all of the business processes involved in the delivery of a translation: from the initial quoting for a piece of work; through account management, project management and tracking; the translation of a document (such as a user manual, medical journal, press release or travel web site); to the electronic delivery of either a simple document or a more sophisticated technical formatted document. There are some parallels with the relatively simple concept of IT outsourcing, but in this case there is also a tangible service which is inherently linked to the technology. The information systems are used to co-ordinate the market network that includes the customer, the translation services company and the myriad of translators who undertake the translation from one language to another.

Another specific communication challenge for firms is brand marketing. The cultural complexities that are part of communication convey much more information than a core message such as a product’s functionality and price or a service level agreement. In brand marketing the ‘look and feel’, the context and other cultural associations are extremely important for avoiding misunderstandings with suppliers as well as for influencing customer buying decisions.

The language translation and brand marketing translation needs of international firms constitute a US\$ 30 billion per year world market in translation services (LISA, 2007a). The language translation services market is fragmented and the top 20 firms accounted for revenues of US\$ 2,139m in 2006 (Beninatto and DePalma, 2007, see Table 1). Translation services firms translate the language content of products and services from a source language to one or more target market languages (Holland et al., 2004). This applies to software, web sites and media services, as well as to traditional business communication related to standard commercial business processes. For example, British Airways (BA) operates in 93 countries, uses ten operating languages and aims to standardise brand messages on all of its multi-language web sites; these contain several 100 pages of frequently changing content. BA’s customers come from a wide range of cultures and to make itself more attractive to each customer segment BA endeavours to present itself as a global organisation rather than as a purely British company (ibid.). This requires the ‘localisation’ of content, i.e. the translation of concepts into the local language and cultural references, and also the maintenance of such localisation through any product updates or changes. Firms that develop products for world markets may also use the expertise of translation services companies to ‘internationalise’ aspects of new product design. When content changes on one country’s or market’s web site, it has to change on all other customer-facing web sites. Firms with a presence in world markets apply this ‘localisation’ process on a global level where it is called ‘globalisation’. Here we use the term ‘translation market’, rather than the term ‘globalisation market’, to avoid confusion with the globalisation of world trade.

## 4.2. The translation services market

The translation services market is made up of clients that require translation services, translators, translation technology providers and translation services providers. Clients are organisations of all sizes that require translation services in order to have some form of presence in one or more world markets (Holland et al., 2004). These services include the translation of very diverse sources of recorded and live language such as documents, technical writing translation, software, graphics, proofreading, editing, telephone interpretation, whole web site localisation, conference interpretation and escort interpreta tion. They also include ‘internationalisation’, which involves designing or translating products for localisation. For example, software, graphics and screen shots may contain text that requires translation, and phone numbers or other country-centric information may need changing (LISA, 2007a; Otter, 2008). Translation in the context of world markets is concerned with much more than the translation of text. For example, when internationalising software products East Asian languages can require thousands of characters; Arabic and Hebrew are read and written from right to left, which may require a more flexible user interface design; and there are different conventions for numerical separators and date formats (LISA, 2007a). In addition to the complexity of the types of translation objects, and the diversity of source and target languages, the frequency of change to the source text is highly variable. This is especially true for web site content that often needs frequent updating and for portfolios of short life cycle products. The time-based pressures in translation services are best described by client requirements:

Translation services providers are organisations that integrate and manage translators’ activities according to the specific requirements of individual clients. There are a relatively small number of large global translation firms as most of them specialise geographically, by vertical sector or by both. Beninatto and DePalma (2007) report that the top 20 firms accounted for only 18% of the total market in 2006.

Most translation is done by external contractors who work from home and on behalf of a range of clients. This is managed by the translation services company and it requires a high level of coordination. Translation services companies will also employ specialist in-house translators for quality control purposes and to contribute to project management on behalf of clients. Translators typically operate within a narrow range of languages and there are further specialisations for specific industries. For example, in the pharmaceuticals or engineering sector translators need to have knowledge of specialist technological and commercial terminology.

Translation technology providers produce the various technologies that firms and individual translators use to support translation projects. Translation Memory (TM) reduces the work required for retranslation by storing and comparing source texts with past work. This also helps to increase consistency with previous translations, e.g. in terms of style. It also aids productivity. Translation Management Systems (TMS) are used for project management, resource management and to generate purchase orders and invoices. Terminology Management is a sub-process of a TMS which helps to maintain the consistent use of terminologies. Machine Translation (MT) is an automated translation process that uses linguistic or statistical analysis rules and is of a lower quality than human translation. However, it can be used in conjunction with manual translation (LISA, 2007b; Sargent and DePalma, 2007). MT is used most commonly with manual editing, e.g. corrections, if translations are factually wrong or for changing a machine translation into a style equal to that of a manual translation. The different uses of machine translation depend upon the purpose of the translated document itself, e.g. whether it is for an internal memo, for internal legal communications or for external customers

In a commercial environment, the translation activity takes place within a business context and the translation service needs to take this into account.

Translation management companies do not just translate; they also manage the workflows of their clients, the translators and other translation companies on behalf of their clients. (CEO)

Translation Management Systems are also known as Globalisation Management Systems (GMS) and these technologies contain separate tools for use by translators. These translation tools, e.g. SDL’s Trados, run on the translator’s PC as an editor that can integrate with word processors and spreadsheets. A GMS can integrate with other systems such as a Content Management System (CMS). A CMS is used by large organisations to manage the flow of content creation and publishing. Linking a GMS into that process ensures that the translations stay synchronised and allows the organisations to focus on the purpose of the document. A CMS automatically detects changes to the source documents which causes it to send ‘child’ documents in other languages, and instructions, to a GMS for translation. A GMS manages dynamic workflows that would be too complex to be managed manually such as for global web site content synchronisation and updating. A GMS is used by clients or by translation services companies internally, too, to manage the complexity of the network structure and the process complexity of its activities

## 4.3. The complexity of large-scale translation projects

Translation services companies and some larger organisations commonly use a GMS to project manage complex translation projects on behalf of their equally complex clients. GMSs and computer-aided translation and localisation tools manage the different files that pass between the client, the translators and any translation services company. A GMS typically contains:

\- The XML source file that holds the text or source language to be translated.

\- The translation memory file that holds past translations of this source object, e.g. a web site.

\- The terminology file that holds special terminology, instances of terminology use and other information to support consistency.

\- The configuration file that tells the translation tool or the GMS which parts of the source file are to be translated.

\- The instructions file that tells each individual translator what to do.

\- Any reference files.

\- The style guide that helps maintain style consistency.

GMS files have a range of different file formats, e.g. Translation Memory eXchange (TMX), an open XML standard for translation memory data; Term-Base eXchange (TBX), a terminology data standard; Global information management Metrics eXchange (GMX), a future standard that will convey information about the complexity of localisation projects like word or character count; xml:tm, a standard for embedding text memory such as translation memory within XML documents; and Segmentation Rules eXchange (SRX), a standard for defining how texts are segmented by computer-aided translation and localisation tools or a GMS. These open standards are developed and maintained by the OSCAR (Open Standards for Container/Content Allowing Re-use) standards body of LISA, the Localisation Industry Standards Association (LISA, 2007c).

The segmentation of text is particularly important. For example, segmentation is used to divide up work between translators in projects with large source documents to translate. The elapsed time taken to translate a whole document can be reduced by sharing it out amongst several translators in parallel. This can also be applied to a document that needs to be translated into multiple languages. The important capability required with segmentation is not that you can divide up the text but that you can divide up the text and later reintegrate the translated work.

Standardising the way that the source text is divided up between translators means that it can be decomposed and recomposed by workflow systems like a GMS. Standardising the architectural rules for dividing up and recombining source text enables the source text to be distributed for translation between the many different structural configurations of translators that will work on it. Some network configurations that are possible in this industry are shown in Fig. 3. More importantly these architectural rules cover process as well as structural rules so the translated text can be recombined and partially automated in a GMS. LanguageDirector™ is the proprietary platform that thebigword developed for managing the workflow between clients, thebigword, translators and competitor translation services companies.

Instead of using a translation management technology as a portal to collect a job it’s a platform to do a job . . . the portal helps clients manage their own project because it offers the client a window into our job management system. (Group Technology Manager)

Translator networks are networks of freelance translators that form around specific language pairs and specific sectors, e.g. automotive or mechanical engineering. Language pairs, e.g. English and German, are specific to each translator and therefore to the networks that they form In some translator networks one of the translators, the lead translator takes on an addi: tional management role for an additional fee. The lead translator is initially sent the translation job and can either translate it themselves or assign it to another translator in the network. The lead translator can also see the translated file after it has been reviewed and act as a moderator. The lead translator manages the workflow before and after the content is actually translated, and at a more detailed level than the project manager.

## 4.4. The challenge for the translation services industry

The challenge for the translation services industry is managing the complexity of the stakeholders, business processes, data structures, timescales and content types that are the key components of modern translation projects.

The translation bit is the commodity in the whole thing, the added value is managing the complexity. (IT Director)

From a demand perspective, as clients globalise their products and their associated systems for product and service support, then distribution, maintenance, marketing and sales activities are also globalised. Structural complexity is multiplied by the increased process complexity that is caused by shortened product and service life cycles. Process complexity is amplified by the large increase in the different configurations of stakeholders and their data structures. The different content types that clients use on their web sites, in their internal and external documentation and in conjunction with selling and supporting their products also increase process complexity. Potentially, all these stakeholders can exchange all these data objects in all these formats in many different orders and iterations. But not all process configurations are the most efficient and effective use of the different stakeholders’ resources. This generates enormous complexities for the translation services providers, like thebigword, the translators and the clients.

## 5. Discussion

In this section we discuss how the outcomes and results from the case analysis relate to the literature on modular architectures and the literature on the platform concept. This is what Lee and Baskerville call ET Generalizability (2003) and what Yin calls analytical generalisation or generalising to theory (2003). This allows us to generalise beyond a single case study by relating our analysis to a broader theory base. First we discuss some of the architectural dimensions of design choice that cause complexity in this industry and then we discuss how they are managed via an information systems platform.

## 5.1. Dimensions of the architecture of the translation services market

Standards, standardisation processes and aggregators can be viewed as architectural characteristics of a market in the sense that they define aspects of the market for lower level components, such as firms and business processes, which can be thought of as modular characteristics (Baldwin and Clark, 2000; Ethiraj and Levinthal, 2004; Langlois, 2002; Parnas, 1972; Simon, 1962). Standards, standardisation processes and aggregators are potential couplers or decouplers of different aspects of the market that are compatible. They are the interface that links together the market’s suppliers, products, services, customers and technologies. Standards are agreements of commonality because they couple common aspects. But they also agree diversity because they decouple the relationship between different aspects of the market. Design choices in the standardisation process that choose one option over another act to ‘freeze’ or join some relationships whilst simultaneously ‘thawing’ or cutting others. For example, choosing the elements in an XML schema divides the aspects of the market that are represented by the different categories and simultaneously aggregates all other aspects of the market. An XML schema is a description of an XML document composed of the constraints on its structure and content. The design process of the schema sets semantic constraints on the XML elements because when you define what something is you simultaneously define what it is not. This is the equivalent of drawing a line between categories, and the sum of such lines forms a pattern which we refer to here as an architecture. The objective is to design an architecture of elements that fits the architecture of the user’s needs. This in turn should be strongly influenced by the architecture of all users’ needs, i.e. the segmentation of the market. Such an architecture can be used to manage the complexity experienced by different stakeholders in the network by standardising aspects of the network and its activities, Subiective complexity is successfully reduced when a stakeholder, e.g, a translator, a project manager or even a customer, is given a reduced set of choices to choose from without this greatly reducing their effectiveness.

The dimensions, or degrees of freedom, of the architecture of the translation services market are:

\- Fee model – monthly fee versus user licences.

\- Location – online or behind the customer’s fire wall.

\- Modular or monolithic – Service Orientated Architecture (SOA) or non-SOA.

\- Service level – embedded within the client firm versus set up, install, consult only when necessary.

\- File management – automatic versus manual.

\- Organisational levels – multi-level (translator level, lead translator, special translator networks) versus translator level.

\- Project management information – real time versus asynchronous or delayed.

\- Quality assurance – integral translator checks and sign offs versus checking as a separate stage progressed by project managers.

These dimensions decompose into sub-dimensions when two things happen. First, the service and the technological op tions that are available to actors split into finer grain options. For example, project managers may be able to organise their projects into additional organisational levels by inserting a new ‘lead translator’ level or a whole specialist translator network into the organisational structure of the project. Adding another organisational level reduces the effort of managing that part of the project for the project manager. Another example is when the technology used involves an SOA which increases the technological flexibility of the service. Second, the decision-making burden on actors is relieved by the hiding or removing of some options. For example, the automation of processes such as file management means that translators and project managers are relieved of a complex manual task. If the architecture of the market is to change, the increase in some dimensions of choice must be matched by a decrease in some other choice dimensions because boundedly rational actors have a fixed capacity to make such choices.

The dimensions, or degrees of freedom, that listed above represent a development of the theoretical framework presented in Fig. 2. These dimensions develop our understanding of the architecture of the translation services market by theorising on its modular structure (Galunic and Eisenhardt, 2001; Garud et al., 2003; Schilling and Steensma, 2001). Our empirical case analysis highlights modular characteristics of the market at different structural levels and at different processual stages. These dimensions are consistent with the literature on modular architectures because they describe the characteristics of standardisation, commonality and agreement that make up inter-organisational relationships at different structural and processual scales.

## 5.2. The role of a platform in a market’s architecture

Platforms are a form of standard and they act to couple and decouple aspects of the market structure by providing new options for the various actors. They also hide the complexity of too many options by automation or by bundling options together. One characteristic of platforms is that they make it possible for all the users of a platform to link together, thus providing new options for suppliers and customers. Another characteristic of platforms is that their design includes constraints on what the platform can be used for and this reduces the choices that the user needs to make (Christiaanse and Rodon, 2005; Kindleberger, 1983; Langlois and Savage, 2001; Robertson and Ulrich, 1998). The smartness of thebigword’s network is embodied in the LanguageDirector™ platform which links thebigword’s networks of direct suppliers and customers with the clients who use the LanguageDirector™ platform to manage their own translation projects. Like any platform, or standard, LanguageDirector™ does this by managing the options that individual or corporate users are exposed to at any given time. LanguageDirector™ hides the complexity of managing many different translators, files, formats and processual arrangements by automating their relationships according to standardised business rules. This decoupling of the customer from the complexity of the supplier network is accomplished by an associated increase in the complexity of the coupling between the information system and the supplier network.

Web services provide the ability to reconfigure service combinations because of their SOA. The SOA allows any configuration of file transfer between stakeholders. The file standards that LanguageDirector™ supports allow integration between stakeholders and together the use of SOA and industry standards enables automatic workflow management between users. For example, there is no lost time between a translator completing their translation and it being sent for review because LanguageDirector™ automatically sends the translation to the next stage in its workflow. All files are held centrally by LanguageDirector™ which in turn enables groupware benefits. These benefits include automatic integration since files are not actually split between translators. Instead meta-data is just used to divide up the focus of each translator. Projects managers have real-time visibility of progress since the work is not off-line on a translator’s PC so translator resources can be reconfigured in real time if a project has to deviate from the plan

Also, the real-time automation of workflows enables the insertion of a new hierarchical level in the supplier network, Selected translators are paid an extra fee, based upon a percentage of each job, to lead a small team of other translators. The team will start with a face-to-face meeting and then continue work that is mediated by LanguageDirector™’s online environment. The advantage here is not just that the team’s work is linked in real time but that the team leader, who is a translator first and a manager second, is provided with very strong workflow support.

When you outsource translation, why in-source the management of it? (Group Technology Manager)

Lastly, by working within this online environment all stakeholders automatically generate management information for real-time monitoring by project managers. This can be used for client reporting and billing, and to support operational strategies such as continuous improvement. In this way clients can get a monthly invoice, a real-time total of money spent on their project or even an alarm when a pre-specified cumulative cost has been reached.

They [clients] want to know what they are spending up to the minute. (IT Director)

The meta-data that enables this real-time management information includes ‘sender id’, which can identify individuals, systems, areas of systems, product groups or other data needed for cost analysis. Other meta-data includes key workflow information that is specific to the translation process, e.g. reviewer id, in addition to information specific to the client processes, e.g. non-size-limited extensible fields or XML fields designed by the client or others. This meta-data can support many different client services, such as real-time calculations of translation memory savings, i.e. the extra cost to the project if translation memory were not used. The elements of the architecture of data, process, team structure and firm structure are loosely coupled because they are connected in an SOA. This loose coupling enables the dynamic and low cost reconfiguration of people networks, processes and systems for each project.

Our case analysis highlights how the information system used by translation services companies takes on the characteristics of a ‘market platform’ at different structural levels and at different processual stages. This is consistent with the literature on platforms because a platform decouples some aspects of the market’s architectural structure to allow information hiding (Parnas, 1972). It also couples other aspects of the market’s architectural structure to allow easier business-to-business working (Robertson and Ulrich, 1998; Christiaanse and Rodon, 2005; Rodon et al., 2007). This selective coupling and decoupling is a complexity management service that can be sold to other firms in the market.

Our results confirm the findings in the literature regarding the benefits of reducing complexity to users by selectively hiding dimensional choices from users (Parnas, 1972). The analysis also provides new insights in terms of understanding the specific architectural dimensions of the translation services market and how the design of information systems can be used to change the apparent complexity of the system by managing the range of architectural dimensions that are made visible to users.

## 5.3. Managing the architectural complexity of a market’s structure using an information system

Overall our analysis using the elements of Fig. 2 enables us to theorise on the relationship between an information system and the structure of a market and how the use of the information system can be sold as a service to market participants to help them manage complexity. The structural perspectives of different organisational levels, and the processual perspectives of different business process stages, can be further divided into the dimensions of the architecture of the market that we have discussed. These are dimensions of choice for actors and removing or preserving these dimensions affects the complexity of the choices that the actors have to make as they relate to each other in their work. A platform such as the information system in this case preserves or removes the different dimensions of the market’s architecture. It does so because it preserves or removes what actors can when they relate to each other.

The theoretical implications of this are that the detailed architectural dimensions of a market can be theorised; this can be done by using structural and processual perspectives from systems theory (Fig. 2); these architectural dimensions can be selectively standardised or kept flexible by using an information system platform in the market; such a platform can be used to manage the burden of complexity for actors in a market; and use of the platform can even be sold as a service to other actors. The service is dependent on the design of the platform because the design must recognise the different architectural dimensions of the market and either standardise the dimensions into a platform or keep them flexible. The theoretical impli: cations of these results are that they bring together the concepts of market architecture, and the options that are inherent in the design and operation of market structures and processes (Galunic and Eisenhardt. 2001: Garud et al., 2003: Henderson and Clark, 1990; Richard and Devinney, 2005; Schilling and Steensma, 2001), with the literatures on how the complexity of these options can be used in designing market platforms (Christiaanse and Rodon, 2005; Rodon et al., 2007) and how to manage architectural complexity (Ethiraj and Levinthal, 2004). The contribution to these literatures is the theorisation of how a market architecture can be used to design a platform in the form of an information system whose use can itself be sold as a service to firms in the market. That is, we use the complexity concepts of a multi-level structural view of multi-stage processes to extend the theorisation of a market architecture (Fig. 2), which then generates novel implications for information system and platform design.

## 6. Conclusions

The use of an electronic market platform has the potential to change the market structure of the translation services industry because it enables new forms of communication and new network relationships between the different economic partners. This technology helps translators, clients and translation service providers by hiding problem complexity, which increases the complexity of the structural and processual configurations that the users can deal with. The innovative aspect of the technology is its ability to be a platform for managing complex networks that may last only as long as a single project. Knowledge is preserved for re-use and error reduction purposes in the form of translation memory, terminology and style guides. But it is also preserved in the form of the translators' performance, competencies. contact information and payment details. Client preferences and any other data needed to model, improve and even rebuild any particular project network are also recorded. This enables automation within an individual project as well as learning between projects.

The technology enables stakeholders to do more with fewer resources, and in less time, so as to meet the challenge of clients with increasingly global requirements. The complex translation problems that are generated by clients that span international markets with increasingly complex products and under ever greater time pressures can only be answered by increasing the effectiveness of the networks of translators that work on them. This changes the market structure of the industry from an off-line, flat supplier structure, with a slower cycle-time and a distributed data structure, to an online, multi-level hierarchy with a faster cycle-time and a centralised data structure. The faster cycle-time that is created by removing data-transfer delays between the translator and the project manager enables the extra level of the translator team leader. The addition of a team leader reduces the managerial load on the project manager. The project manager can then choose to focus on managing more complex projects or on managing individual projects faster. The coordination cost of adding a new hierarchical level is minimised because of the automated workflow functions of the technology. This enables extra process stages, for a more refined output, or reductions in the total project cycle-time. The technology also makes it easier to integrate in-house and outsourced translator networks as well as speciality and ad hoc translators. This implies that the structural flexibility of the whole market will also increase.

Overall, we use an analytical lens that is based upon a modular architecture of organisational levels and business process stages to analyse a project-based industry in order to find the sources of its complexity. Then we show how this complexity is managed, under the constraints of the managers’ ability to deal with it, by using an information system. The implications for researchers are that information systems can be designed to selectively couple and decouple the structural and processual relationships between the stakeholders in a network, and also the process stages that they enact, which increases the organisational capability of the network. This clever reconfiguration of a network can then produce increased benefits for the customers of the network by supporting them in dealing with more complex markets. These benefits can be sold to customers, and even competitors, as a service in its own right because it helps them manage the complexity of their projects.

The contribution of our model is to the literature on modular architectures and the architectures of markets (Galunic and Eisenhardt, 2001; Garud et al., 2003; Henderson and Clark, 1990; Richard and Devinney, 2005; Schilling and Steensma, 2001). The contribution itself is that this literature does not currently model market architectures in terms of the complexity of choices that they generate. The literature also does not use market architectures to theorise how to design information systems to manage complexity by selectively coupling and decoupling the structural and processual relationships between the stakeholders and also the process stages that they enact, i.e. by selecting which architectural dimensions to hide from users and which to provide as options to users.

The theoretical concepts and case study analysis presented in this paper could be developed further by additional case studies in other high-technology markets that explore how information system platforms can be used to manage complexity in terms of structural and business process design choices. For example, this approach could be used to analyse the new markets for smart-phone and tablet applications and to compare them with the established structure of the personal computer industry.

The implications for managers of complex project networks, and any firm that markets internationally, are that technological platforms can be used to hide complexity, allowing their users to focus on other areas. Specifically, platforms can help to manage the internally generated complexities that form the inter-relations between stakeholders so that users can focus upon externally generated complexity in the form of their clients’ problems.
