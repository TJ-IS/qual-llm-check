# Should We Go Our Own Way? Backsourcing Flexibility in IT Services Contracts

Miche l Ben aro ch, Qizhi Dai, and Robe rt J. Kauffman

His current research interests focus on using real option techniques to evaluate IT investments and manage their risk in an IT portfolio context, as well as on designing declarative ontology-centered modeling formalisms. His research has been published in a variety of outlets, including Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, International Journal of Human–Computer Studies, Information Retrieval, Decision Sciences, International Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control, and Decision Support Systems.

Her publications have appeared in a number of different conferences and journals, including Information Technology and Management, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, and the International Journal of Electronic Markets. She won a Best Paper award at the 1999 Workshop on Information Technology and Systems for her research on real option analysis of investments in object-oriented middleware.

Carey School of Business, Arizona State University, where he holds appointments in IS, Finance, Supply Chain, and the School of Computing and Informatics. He has served on the faculty at New York University, University of Minnesota, and University of Rochester. Ab stract: The emergence of new service science approaches to business problems in information technology (IT) services offers new, unusually relevant insights for the senior management of vendors in this business area. This research examines how service-level agreement contract flexibility should be designed when the technological and business market environments result in volatility of demand, based on an understanding of related changes in the cost drivers that underlie IT services contracts. Our approach draws on a blend of well-known methods from financial economics—the real option pricing method and the contingent claims analysis method. In particular, our research examines a setting in which a vendor provides IT services to a client according to a prenegotiated IT services contract in the presence of demand volatility. We analyze the motivation of and value consequences for a vendor that offers the client the flexibility to opt out of the contract. For example, the client might switch to another vendor, or backsource and provide its own services internally. Our core results offer important foundational thinking for how to specify various forms of IT service-related flexibility in terms of put and call options from the point of view of an IT services vendor, so that their value and exercise timing can be estimated. We show that the client firm’s demand trigger value for deciding when to backsource its IT services varies, and it depends on the degree of demand volatility as well as the usage-based fees charged by the vendor. Working from our modeling approach, we also are able to characterize the extent to which a vendor can benefit from bearing the costs of making a backsourcing flexibility option available to its client.

Key word s and phrases: contingent claims analysis, demand trigger value, demand uncertainty, financial economics, IT services, outsourcing, real options, service science, valuation, vendors, volatility.

A recent Diam ond Cluster International stud y pointed to continuing growth in the information technology (IT) outsourcing market, with 74 percent of the client firms sampled anticipating an increase in their use of IT outsourcing [105], a trend that continues today. According to this study, practitioners identify cost reduction and better management of variable capacity needs as the key reasons for IT outsourcing. This is consistent with theoretical and empirical research that has shown that IT vendors experience volatility of demand for the services they offer, and that such volatility of demand motivates services outsourcing [3, 18, 88]. In times of good economic conditions and technological innovation, demand for IT services tends to grow, and when economic slowdown and technological stability occur, demand drops. IT services vendors enable firms to outsource to avoid the high fixed costs associated with owning sufficient IT capacity (infrastructure, leases, personnel, etc.) to meet their needs [46, 71, 104]. Vendors also offer outsourcing with usage-based pricing to help clients to right-size their overall IT cost and to lower their marginal cost per IT service unit when demand is low [94]. Two well-known examples that follow this logic are GE’s Appliances Division in application maintenance [37] and Deutschebank in business process outsourcing (BPO) [40].

When demand picks up, however, IT services vendors find that firms which are already outsourcing often want to change their contracts to benefit from the drop in their internal marginal cost per IT service unit [57]. They wonder what the duration of their IT outsourcing contracts should be, and the extent to which cross-organizational investments will influence the outcomes [23], as has been noted in other contexts (e.g., [54]). One of the possible changes involves backsourcing, a decision on the part of the firm to reverse a contract and bring outsourced services back in house [7, 78, 103, 106]. Another change is the move to fixed subscription fee pricing [21], which indicates a shift in beliefs regarding how the vendor will reflect its changing costs of service delivery in its variable pricing over time. Such reversal changes are possible when an IT services contract offers the client firm the flexibility to switch its IT services sourcing modes before contract expiry. Still another is that expired contracts will not be renewed, as the firm shifts to insourcing of IT services [90]. There is also the shift to the build-operate-transfer model [39] (also referred to as the transform-operate-transfer model by Deloitte Consulting [67]). In this model, a firm outsources a particular IT responsibility to a service provider only for a set period of time. Soon after though—for example, when a firm is trying to get a complicated new business process going—the IT-related activities will be brought back in house. The possibility of offshoring to different locations further complicates the calculus of managerial decisions [16].

The marketplace and industry practices with respect to outsourcing and the related decision-making processes within the organization are evolving [24, 26, 62, 70]. When an IT services vendor permits a client firm to exercise its flexibility to switch sourcing modes, it essentially is offering an opportunity to the client to achieve a marginal cost advantage per IT service unit at varying demand levels. There may be a loss of business for the vendor and a value gain for the client. In other words, there is a mix of “pain” and “pleasure” [80, 81, 82]. For the client, there will be the irreversible switching costs of searching for a new vendor, and whatever recontracting costs arise in the process [90]. Thus, switching between sourcing modes presents the client with a trade-off that is influenced by the parameters characterizing demand. To balance cost savings against switching costs, the client must be sensitive to the lower and upper demand triggers at which it is optimal to outsource and backsource [28]. The higher the variance of demand, the more frequently will clients consider the attractiveness of switching between different sourcing modes.

We discuss how vendors and clients should respond when the conditions under which IT services contract provisions change due to exogenous forces for demand volatility. Research on effects of the drivers of volatility on IT services constitutes an important theme in the emerging areas of service science [20, 73] and IT governance science [107]. For example, valuation of and managerial actions associated with IT service-level agreements (SLA s) area is one that has been written about recently [57, 58]. Another related issue under research involves managing outsourcing contracts in terms of outsourcing opportunity identification, client tracking, and outsourcing impact analysis, or outsourcing life cycle management [79].

Our work in this paper involves a financial economics model that permits an assessment of the effects of demand uncertainty on client’s outsourcing decisions and vendor’s offering of flexible contracts. The models we develop are based on real options analysis [34, 96, 101]. We consider stochastic demand for a specific type of vendor-offered homogeneous IT service. The kinds of services that we have in mind are similar to desktop, network, data center, and call center operations, as well as help desk transaction support that an IT services provider can supply as pooled labor and charge out by the hour [57, 95]. We examine how volatility of demand affects the demand triggers that drive clients to switch sourcing modes and on which vendors ought to base their service offerings.

A related objective of this research is to demonstrate the effectiveness of a simplified real option analysis approach in the outsourcing services context. Alvarez and Stenbacka [1] and Lammers and Lucke [66], among others, have indicated some preference for using analytical real option approaches such as contingent claims analysis [34, 86]. Analytical approaches have some limitations though. They often assume options with indefinite time horizons. With IT services, however, options to switch sourcing modes usually are short-lived; no vendor will offer clients an opportunity to outsource with a specific set of cost parameters and contract provisions for an indefinite time. Also, analytical approaches can fail under the weight of their own mathematical complexity. For example, to assess the optimal timing to switch from one sourcing mode to another, contingent claims analysis produces a model with four nonlinear equations and four unknowns that must be solved numerically [66]. Modeling how the contractual flexibility to backsource before contract expiry will impact the optimal timing of the client’s decision to outsource is even more challenging because the flexibility to backsource needs to be modeled as a nested option. The simplified real options analysis approach that we suggest avoids this difficulty. It involves a representation of the decision setting and an adaptation of standard option pricing models, such as the binomial and Black–Scholes models, in a spreadsheet.

## Background Literature and Theory

## The Evolution Toward IT Services Outsourcing Flexibility

Som e of the earlier research that d iscusses the rationale for IT outsourcing offered relatively full explanations of the managerial and technological motivations for doing this to achieve a variety of goals. The research, on the whole, reflects the idea that outsourcing is a means that the firm has at hand to achieve operational flexibility, access to stronger technology management and innovation resources, and reductions in the costs of running different kinds of business processes. In the early days of the development of outsourcing, there was considerable novelty associated with moving to these new operational approaches, resulting in a “buzz” of interest in the market and follow-on adoption actions that Loh and Venkatraman [72] called the Kodak effect. Continental Bank of Illinois went so far as to outsource IT, which it referred to as its “crown jewels” [51]. Although many of the elements of lock-in and flexibility were not fully understood at the time—what Kern et al. [61] aptly termed relational trauma—there were hints of a key trade-off that would only later be much better understood. These involved contracting with multiple vendors [23, 27], relying on single vendors via unified procurement [59], and deciding to insource rather than relying on any vendors at all [64]. Interfirm relationship issues have always been critical in this area of management practice [41, 44], with some firms, such as British Petroleum, even choosing to manage multiple-firm IT outsourcing relationships as though they were one [25].

The new forms of information and communication technologies (ICT s) that emerged in the 1990s may have led, as Cairncross [14] argued, to the “death of distance,” and the “transformation of markets,” as Gurbaxani and Whang [45] stated, but clearly issues remain. These issues relate to ownership-induced risk [47, 48], intellectual property rights [22], changes in the costs of IT services in the market, cost-driven shifts to alternative locations for IT services provision (e.g., India, Ireland, Canada, Eastern Europe) [74], and backsourcing and insourcing disruptions [39]. The latter include lost jobs, forgotten business process mechanics, ineffectively monitored IT projects, and inappropriately stalled investments. Rouse and Corbitt [89] have referred to some of these issues in terms of a hysteresis effect—in other words, the permanent effects of a temporary change or shift—to indicate that outsourced operations are easier to get into than they are to get out of, and have significant long-term effects.

When business changes, as Chakrabarty [16] points out, a firm wants the flexibility to backsource, but nevertheless will be faced with coping with some inevitable problems that it brought upon itself when it adopted the business policy of outsourcing. Whitten and Leidner [106] have further noted that although more and more firms are choosing to bring IT services back in house, backsourcing has attracted little attention in research. Another study, by Cha et al. [15], analyzes backsourcing decisions by considering the interactions among production costs, coordination costs, and knowledge transfer between clients and outsourcing service providers. These studies consider outsourcing of software development services from the client’s perspective. To the best of our knowledge, though, no research to date has looked at backsourcing from the vendor’s perspective, another area that calls for further research.

In outsourcing deals, vendors face a range of risks from the business environment, client and vendor management processes and organizational structures, and the nature of the outsourcing services [17, 89]. These uncertainties affect vendor decisions on pursuing outsourcing contacts. For example, Jiang et al. [52] have shown that vendors are more willing to make an outsourcing deal when there is limited uncertainty associated with the demand for client services and the profit stream that can be appropriated. Mao et al. [75] further point out that a vendor’s sense of control in an outsourcing relationship is an important factor, because it may also affect the client firm’s perception of trust and relationship performance.

Hence, in this work, we study backsourcing flexibility in IT services management and evaluate its effects on service provider profits. We expect to contribute to the literature on IT outsourcing and service science by expanding our understanding of the motivations for IT outsourcing clients and vendors to incorporate reversal flexibility in their outsourcing contracts.

Numerous studies have used a variety of management science and finance methods to examine IT services and related problems. These include optimal timing of sourcing decisions [1, 66], managing application service provider (ASP) contracts [99], technology adoption decision-timing choices in the presence of technology standards and financial issues [55], sequencing of interdependent software projects [4], pricing of software rental agreements [93], and hedging against unpredictable cost changes in IT projects [57, 58].

In this study, we tackle issues related to demand volatility, switching of sourcing modes, and reversal flexibility embedded in IT services contracts. Although these issues are rather general, our approach is in the vein of the latter research stream: modeling focused, intended to distill new managerial policies under different operational and environmental scenarios, and focused on some key underlying financial relationships that will determine the capacity of a firm to maximize its value relative to outsourcing.

Another related issue deals with the risk profile of managerial decision makers. Lankton and Luft [68] and Tallon et al. [98] have written that attitude toward risk matters, and more specifically, that most managers are not repeated, risk-neutral decision makers. Instead, they are apt to handle one-shot single-decision risks based on their need to “get things right” on current projects and outsourcing relationships, lest they lose their jobs. They may do this even though their longer-term success in management will be determined in the context of repeated decisions. Many of the decision-making models that have been published to date in the information systems (IS) literature have taken a somewhat different tack, though, in part because risk-neutral decision making is often more in harmony with the vagaries of the business environment. This reflects the progress that managers are making to understand IT investment decisions in terms of their risk management characteristics [10, 11, 57].

A characteristic work is by Benaroch and Kauffman [9], who assume a risk-neutral world in which decision makers’ risk preferences are irrelevant in the decision process. Although this assumption is often considered problematic for IT investment decision making, very often the goals of the research are focused on qualitative insights that are not likely to be affected by risk preferences, and quantitative outputs that are just one of many different decision-making inputs [8]. This was the case in these authors’ study of the timing and value of point-of-sale debit systems rollout [9]. Our examination of demand-driven adjustments in outsourcing arrangements will be cast in a similar light, emphasizing qualitative policy outcomes, albeit with quantitative decision-making inputs that can be leveraged through sensitivity analysis.

## Real Options Analysis in Outsourcing

Alvarez and Stenbacka [1] develop a model for finding when it is optimal to outsource and backsource, as well as what portion of the client firm’s demand for production services needs to be outsourced. They assume demand uncertainty, usage-based pricing, and a perpetual, nonexpiring flexibility to outsource. They make no assumptions about resource ownership, though, and in effect ignore considerations surrounding the firm’s fixed cost for insourcing. Using a dynamic programming derivation approach, they solve for demand trigger values for when to outsource and when to backsource. Importantly, their model only considers the flexibility to switch from outsourcing to insourcing when the firm is already outsourcing. Yet, when evaluating an opportunity to outsource, up-front inclusion of contract reversal flexibility adds value to the opportunity. Options of this sort that are embedded in investment projects typically are value bearing [36]. Including them can lead to a different optimal demand trigger value for when to outsource.

Lammers and Lucke [66] take an IT cost-savings perspective on BPO, without considering the possibility of partial outsourcing. Their assumptions are comparable to those of Alvarez and Stenbacka [1], although they do consider the client firm’s fixed IT costs. Using a contingent claims analysis derivation approach for a risk-neutral decision maker, they produce a model with four unknowns and four nonlinear equations that must be solved numerically, using a tool such as Mathematica or Gauss. They do not consider how the up-front inclusion of backsourcing flexibility can impact the conditions under which it is optimal to outsource in the first place, though.

Techopitayakul and Johnson [99] develop an ASP model involving several uncertainties, including software value, usage level, and the number of users. They add to the model three real options that help the client and the vendor to manage these uncertainties. The options are switching from usage-based pricing to a flat subscription fee, bringing the software in house, and ending the contract prior to expiration. Two features of their model make it less applicable to the problem we are studying, though. They use a specialized mean-reverting stochastic process [13, 76] to model the ASP-specific characteristics of the uncertainties involved. These include software cycles, learning effects, and so on. In addition, due to the complexity of their resulting model, the authors were only able to approximate the option values and the exercise triggers using a Monte Carlo simulation approach. In contrast, analysis using standard real option models appears to skirt some of these difficulties.

To summarize, the specific derivation approach and assumptions that are used with real option analysis produce models that will have important differences. The key differences pertain to whether the flexibility to outsource and backsource is perpetual or expires after a finite time, to the underlying mathematics and challenges it may pose when applied to nontrivial IT sourcing problems, and to the ease with which rich managerial insights can be derived using comparative statics or numeric simulations. In this light, we use two derivation approaches—namely, contingent claims analysis and what we call the simplified real option analysis approach—to develop multiple models. Not only do these models allow us to show their equivalence in terms of the novel qualitative insights they offer, but they also permit us to examine the practical implications of the differences resulting from their different derivation approaches.

## Modeling IT Services Outsourcing Decisions

Next, we construct an IT outsourcing d ecision-m ak ing m od el that emphasizes the role of demand uncertainty and contributes to the foundations of service science for effective management of outsourcing contracts and client–vendor relationships. We conceptualize the IT outsourcing decision as a real option and examine the client’s objective function in making IT outsourcing decisions. We do the same for a vendor and characterize the expected profit for the vendor in offering the service. In this baseline case, we do not include any embedded contract reversal option, but rather just focus on insourcing and outsourcing.

## Model Setup

We examine the case where a firm, as a user of IT services, considers the option it has to outsource its IT operations to an IT services provider. In this context, we refer to the IT user firm as the client and to the IT services provider as the vendor. To the client, its decision on the sourcing mode for a particular IT service depends on which mode offers the same IT services at a lower cost, with no compromises for service quality, reliability, and continuity of the interorganizational relationship. The client’s total cost of obtaining the IT service varies with its business demand for this service, which may fluctuate over time. The client produces the IT service for itself at the outset, thus insourcing IT services production. In the meantime, it holds an option to outsource. Later, as the business demand for the IT service changes, and if it finds that outsourcing costs drop by more than a certain threshold amount, it will exercise the outsourcing option. We assume that outsourcing is offered initially as a usage-based subscription service, where the vendor charges a subscription fee per unit of service. This outsourcing arrangement is also assumed to be a long-term contract. If the vendor allows it, the client will have an option to switch to a fixed-fee schedule or to backsource within the terms of the contract. This option may be exercised later on, when the total costs for fixed-fee-based outsourcing or for insourcing are substantially less than the usage-based outsourcing costs, given the client’s demand for the IT service.

Our goal is to find out how a client’s decisions on the IT service sourcing modes will be affected by changes in service demand and how the vendor’s profit is affected. Our modeling notation is in Table 1.

We consider demand level as a stochastic state variable representing the volume of IT service needed by the client. Our model is aimed at revealing the critical level of IT service demand at which the client switches to outsourcing. We next describe the client’s cost functions and the vendor’s profit function.

If the client outsources the IT service, it also has to bear some extra expenses. First, the client incurs the cost for setting up the outsourcing arrangement, including the investments in vendor selection, contracting, and organizational redesign. We represent these expenses as a lump sum cost $S _ {  o }$ . Second, the client has to pay the vendor a usage-based subscription fee $p ,$ which represents the vendor’s price for each IT service unit used by the client. Third, the client has to expend management cost M per unit time to oversee the execution of the outsourcing contract. The expected cost of outsourcing is

$$
\Omega_ {o} (V) = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} e ^ {- r t} (p V e ^ {\mu t} + M) d t + S _ {\rightarrow o} = \frac {p}{r - \mu} V + \frac {M}{r} + S _ {\rightarrow o}.\tag{2}
$$

Equations (1) and (2) represent the client’s sourcing costs when the demand level grows with no volatility at the rate $\mu { }$ . Using these equations, we can derive the indifference demand level $\tilde { V }$ at which the client will be indifferent between insourcing and outsourcing:

$$
\tilde {V} = \left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right)\left(\frac {r - \mu}{p - c}\right).
$$

If current demand is less than the indifference demand level, outsourcing will reduce the firm’s marginal cost per IT service unit. Otherwise, the firm’s internal marginal cost per IT service unit will justify continuing to insource.

Suppose the client is facing an opportunity to outsource with a specific set of cost parameter values and contract provisions that the vendor offers, where the opportunity expires at time $\tau > 0$ . If the client considers switching to outsourcing on or before τ , its goal is to find if starting to outsource from some time $\tau ^ { * } \leq \tau$ can minimize the sourcing cost incurred thereafter. Another way to look at this is as the minimization of the total expected sourcing cost for the client who has the flexibility to outsource. This cost function is defined as the difference $\Lambda ( V )$ between the expected cost of continuing to insource and the expected cost savings from starting to outsource from some optimal time $\tau ^ { * } \leq \tau$ onward:

$$
\begin{array}{l}\Lambda (V) = \Omega_ {i} (V) - \max _ {\tau} E _ {V} \left[ \int_ {\tau} ^ {\infty} e ^ {- r t} \Delta (V e ^ {\mu t}) d t - e ^ {- r \tau} S _ {\rightarrow o} \right]\\= \Omega_ {i} (V) - \max _ {\tau} E _ {V} \left[ e ^ {- r \tau} \left(\frac {C - M}{r} - \frac {p - c}{r - \mu} V _ {\tau} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right)\right].\end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Because a client will only incur switching cost $S _ {  o }$ for moving from insourcing to outsourcing if there is a time when $E _ { \scriptscriptstyle V } [ \cdot ]$ is positive, we can recast Equation (3) as

$$

$$

$$

$F ( V _ { \tau } )$ has the functional form of a put option granting the client the right to lower its sourcing costs by “selling” underlying asset $[ ( p - c ) / ( r - \mu ) ] V \tau$ for exercise price $\lbrack ( C - M ) / r ] - S _ {  o } .$ This option represents the client’s optional cost savings from switching to outsourcing.

Under demand uncertainty, V evolves randomly instead of just growing at a fixed rate. Now, because V evolves randomly, there is no unique optimal time for switching sourcing modes. However, we can convert the optimal timing problem into an optimal stopping problem [1]. We do this by solving for a demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$ at which it is optimal to stop insourcing and switch to outsourcing. We think of $F ( V _ { \tau } )$ as an American option. This kind of option permits the decision maker to flexibly exercise it any time leading up to its expiry. Solving the optimal stopping problem entails finding when it is optimal to exercise the option. The solution for the stopping problem will be the same whether we solve it for $F ( V _ { \tau } )$ or $F ( V )$ . Why? This is due to the strong Markov property of Brownian motion [38], through which the value of V begins to move stochastically again once a stopping time is reached. So no matter what happens up until switching time τ , after that the time will be reset to zero. Thus, we need a way to find the demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$ at which it is optimal to exercise the related option.

## Vendor’s Profit

To the vendor, offering the IT service generates income or a subscription fee $p$ per unit of service. To offer the service, the vendor will need to bear a cost d to deliver a unit of service. We can reasonably assume that the vendor charges a subscription fee that is greater than its variable cost for delivering the IT service—that is, $p > d . { \mathrm { T h i s } } .$ in essence, is an incentive compatibility condition for the solution of the problem. It permits the vendor to earn a profit, rather than a loss, for each unit of service it provides. This logic is consistent with the common practice that service vendors use in setting unit price greater than variable cost. Assuming that the demand changes at a drift rate µ, and if the client decides to outsource all its demand, then the vendor’s expected profit $\pi ( p , V )$ is

$$
\pi (p, V) = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} e ^ {- r t} [ (p - d) V e ^ {\mu t} ] d t = \frac {p - d}{r - \mu} V.\tag{5}
$$

We later show how this expression can be adapted to calculate the vendor’s profit from the moment the client decides to switch to outsourcing when demand drops below the demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$

## Real Option Analysis for IT Services Outsourcing

How can we characterize the appropriate tim e to exercise an option to outsource? We develop two models for valuing option F(V ) in Equation (4) and finding a demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$ for its optimal exercise. Like Lammers and Lucke [66], our first model is derived using Dixit and Pindyck’s [34] contingent claims approach. This is commonly used in academic research, but it has limitations. It only can handle perpetual options with infinite lives, and it becomes mathematically unwieldy to apply when complex options are involved. This makes its application in practice rather difficult for managers, even with a highly competent analytics staff. Our second model is derived using a simplified real option approach, which involves adaptation and use of standard option pricing models. This model avoids some drawbacks of the contingent claims approach. We next present these models in order to derive insights on the optimal timing of outsourcing decisions and their effects on the realized cost savings.

## Contingent Claims Analysis Derivation

The idea of contingent claims analysis is explained by Dixit and Pindyck:

To value a new asset, we try to replicate its return and risk characteristics through a portfolio of existing traded assets. The price of the new asset must equal the market value of this portfolio. Any discrepancy would be exploited by arbitrageurs who look for sure profits by buying whichever is cheaper . . . repackaging . . . and selling it in the more valuable form. [34, p. 114]

Contingent claims analysis supports the derivation of an optimal option exercise rule that maximizes the firm’s value, without requiring us to make any assumptions about managerial risk preferences or the discount rate. It uses the risk-free rate of interest $r _ { f r e e } ,$ which is exogenously determined by the capital markets, in lieu of an arbitrarily chosen discount rate associated with the risk of the outsourcing project. Balancing this advantage, however, is the countervailing limiting assumption of spanning, also discussed by Dixit and Pindyck:

Stochastic changes in [the option-bearing asset] must be spanned by existing assets in the economy. Specifically, capital markets must be sufficiently complete so that, at least in principle, one could find an asset or construct a dynamic portfolio of assets whose [contents are adjusted continuously as asset prices change], the price of which is perfectly correlated with the [price of the option-bearing asset]. [34, p. 147]

Some view the spanning assumption as having the potential to be restrictive. In our context, this is not really a problem, though, because we will use the resulting model only to draw qualitative insights related to the decision-making problem. Others argue that spanning is reasonable in general terms. Why? In our context, for example, there are many publicly traded outsourcing vendor firms, and they typically specialize in different types of IT services [2]. This would permit one to define a risk-replicating asset portfolio [19, 31, 102] that is tuned to the risk-payoff characteristics of the various sourcing modes.

## The Client’s Sourcing Costs

Let x be the price of a spanning asset that is perfectly correlated with V. If asset x pays no dividends so that its return comes only from capital gains, it will evolve according to a standard geometric Brownian motion process in the following way:

$$
d x = r x d t + \sigma x d z.\tag{6}
$$

In Equation (6), r is the expected rate of return from holding this asset, as well as the drift rate of x indicating its growth or change over time. The parameter σ is the volatility of $V ,$ and dz is a standard Wiener process [35]. According to the capital asset pricing model, the rate of return r should reflect the asset’s systematic, nondiversifiable risk, so that $r = r _ { _ { f r e e } } + \Phi \rho _ { _ { x m } } \sigma$ . In this expression, $\rho _ { x m }$ is the correlation between the price movement of asset x and the price movement of market portfolio m. The market price of risk is given by $\Phi = ( r _ { _ m } - r _ { _ { f r e e } } ) / \sigma _ { _ m } ,$ , where $r _ { m }$ is the expected return on the market portfolio and $\sigma _ { m }$ is the standard deviation of that return. So r can also be thought of as the risk-adjusted expected rate of return that investors would require if they were to own the spanning asset x, the IT services sourcing project. The riskadjusted expected rate of return is the equilibrium rate set by the capital market, and it includes an appropriate risk premium to compensate the holder of the asset for its payoff characteristics.

We let $\delta = r - \mu$ be the expected percentage rate of change of V. If V were the share price of stock, then δ would be its dividend rate. The expected return on the stock would be $r = \delta + \mu ;$ in other words, the dividend rate plus the expected rate of capital gain. If $\delta > 0$ , the opportunity cost of keeping the option alive without exercising it will equal the foregone dividend stream by holding the option rather than the stock. So $\delta$ is the opportunity cost of delaying a switch to outsourcing, keeping an option to switch alive.

Now, consider managerial actions involving an outsourcing portfolio. First, assume that a manager holds an option to outsource, and that this option is worth $F ( V )$ . Second, imagine that the manager can short sell $F ^ { \prime } ( V )$ units of the sourcing project, or an appropriate number of units of the spanning asset x. The portfolio’s value will be given by $F ( V ) - F ^ { \prime } ( V ) V .$ To fund the short position, the manager will need to commit $\delta { F ^ { \prime } ( V ) V }$ dollars per time period. The value is adjusted by the opportunity cost δ of delaying the sourcing mode switch to outsourcing. Taking this payment into account, the manager’s total return from holding the portfolio over a short time interval dt will be given by $d F ( V ) - F ^ { \prime } ( V ) d V - \delta V F ^ { \prime } ( V )$ )dt. Applying Ito’s lemma for $d F ( V )$ , the total return on the portfolio will be $( 1 / 2 ) F ^ { \prime \prime } ( V ) ( d V ) ^ { 2 } - 8 V F ^ { \prime } ( V ) d t$

Based on the above conceptualization of outsourcing as an option with uncertain demand, we can derive a demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$ and an option value $F ( V ^ { * } )$ given by the following expression (see the Appendix for the technical details):

$$
V ^ {*} = \left(\frac {r - \mu}{p - c}\right)\left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) \frac {\varphi}{\varphi - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right)}\tag{7}
$$

$$
F (V) = \left(\left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}\right)\left(\frac {V}{V ^ {*}}\right) ^ {\varphi},\tag{8}
$$

where

$$
\varphi = \frac {1}{2} - \frac {\left(r _ {f r e e} - \delta\right)}{\sigma^ {2}} - \sqrt {\left(\frac {1}{2} - \frac {\left(r _ {f r e e} - \delta\right)}{\sigma^ {2}}\right) ^ {2} + \frac {2 r _ {f r e e}}{\sigma^ {2}}}.
$$

If the observed value of $V$ is lower than the optimal demand value trigger $V ^ { * }$ , then the firm should switch to outsourcing immediately. Otherwise, it should postpone the switch until this trigger value $V ^ { * }$ is reached.

## Vendor’s Profit

We wish to find how a change in the subscription fee the vendor charges per IT service unit outsourced, $p ,$ will affect the optimal demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$ and the vendor’s profit. Can comparative statics with the analytical solution for $V ^ { * }$ be helpful? Comparative statics can offer only part of the answer. To see why, it suffices to look at the function we presented in Equation (5) for the vendor’s profit. Based on this function, the vendor’s profit for a demand volume $V ^ { * }$ is

$$
\pi (p, V) = \frac {p - d}{r - \mu} V ^ {*}.
$$

The vendor’s strategy is to set the subscription fee $p$ to maximize its profit. Applying Ito’s lemma again, we can derive the partial derivative for the vendor’s profit relative to a dollar change in the subscription fee:

$$
\frac {\partial \pi}{\partial p} = \frac {1}{r - \mu} V ^ {*} + \frac {p - d}{r - \mu} \cdot \frac {\partial V ^ {*}}{\partial p}.\tag{9}
$$

From Equation $( 7 )$ , we can derive a similar partial derivative for the client’s demand trigger also relative to a dollar change in the subscription fee:

$$
\frac {\partial V ^ {*}}{\partial p} = \frac {\varphi (2 a - \varphi)}{a ^ {2} (\varphi - a) ^ {2}} \cdot \left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) \cdot \frac {1}{r - \mu}, \text { where } a = \frac {p - d}{r - \mu}.\tag{10}
$$

For the client to consider outsourcing, it must have positive demand for the IT service in general and also at the specified subscription fee $p .$ . In other words, the optimal demand trigger value should be greater than zero—that is, $V ^ { * } > 0$

Under the above conditions, there is only one scenario that may occur which is managerially interesting. This scenario must satisfy three conditions consistent with Equation (3):

Condition 1 (The Growth Rate of Demand Condition): $r > \mu ,$ , the drift rate of the demand for the IT service, µ, must be lower than the risk-adjusted discount rate r.

Condition 2 (The Fixed Cost of Service Delivery Condition): $C > M + r S _ { { \scriptscriptstyle {  o } } }$ , the fixed cost for the client to produce the IT service internally must be larger than the total of the cost of managing the outsourcing contract M and the discounted switching cost to outsourcing $S _ {  o }$

Condition 3 (The IT Outsourcing Service Subscription Fee Condition): $p > c ,$ , the subscription fee p per outsourced IT service unit must be larger than the cost c for the client to produce that unit on its own.

The reasons are as follows. If the Growth Rate of Demand Condition (C1) is violated, the client can borrow money at rate r and purchase the IT service to make a riskless profit. Also, if the Fixed Cost of Service Delivery Condition (C2) and the IT Outsourcing Service Subscription Fee Condition (C3) do not hold, then either insourcing will always be preferred to outsourcing or outsourcing will always be preferred to insourcing. So a scenario will only be managerially interesting if it meets all three of these conditions.

## Implications

In this scenario, we can derive the following implications. First, under the condition of positive demand with $V ^ { * } > 0$ in Equation (7), the vendor can choose a subscription fee $p$ greater than the risk-adjusted discount rate r—that is, $p > r .$ . Second, based on Equation (10), we find that $( \partial V ^ { \ast } / \partial p ) < 0$ . This indicates that the optimal demand level $V ^ { * }$ for the client to exercise the outsourcing option decreases with an increase in the subscription fee. Third, using Equation (7), we can transform Equation (8) and find that $( \partial F ( V ) / \partial V ) < 0$ and $( \partial F ( V ) / \partial V ^ { * } ) > 0$ . In other words, a client is more likely to insource if it has a high demand for a given IT service because the outsourcing option’s value $F ( V )$ decreases as the demand level V increases. In contrast, with a given level of demand for an IT service, the client will lean toward outsourcing when the optimal demand level trigger $V ^ { * }$ rises. Considering that $V ^ { * }$ increases as the subscription fee drops, we can tell that a client is more likely to outsource its IT service when the vendor lowers the subscription fee. But we cannot determine how the vendor’s profit changes with a subscription fee. To determine this, we must develop another model using a simplified real option analysis approach.

## The Vendor’s Perspective

For the vendor, we need to find out whether the results obtained for the client necessarily imply that the vendor will be able to generate lower revenues and possibly over a shorter time period. From Equation (14), we approximate the vendor’s profit for the demand level range and contract duration over which the vendor has the client’s outsourcing business. The lower boundary of this range is $V ^ { * }$ and the upper boundary is infinity, as no contract reversal flexibility is offered and the client has to continue outsourcing for the contract duration T no matter how demand changes once it reaches $V ^ { * }$ . The simulations we ran are based on parameter values in Table 2. Contract duration $T$ is set to 10 years, as is typical for many outsourcing deals [40]. And, based on the only viable managerial scenario we identified earlier with the contingent claims analysis, the client’s cost structure is set to satisfy the relationship $C > M + r S _ {  o }$ , the subscription fee $p$ is above the client’s cost c of offering one service unit internally, and the rate of demand change $\mu$ is less than the risk-adjusted discount rate r.

The simulation results in Figure 2 show the sensitivity of the vendor’s profit as a function of the subscription fee for varying degrees of demand volatility. The overall trend is that the profit increases as the subscription fee goes higher, but at a slower rate for larger demand volatilities. This is so despite the fact that the demand trigger value $V ^ { * }$ drops as the subscription fee $p$ increases, as we saw from the sensitivity analysis from the client’s perspective. This decrease in $V ^ { * }$ has a negative effect on the vendor’s profit with a usage-based subscription fee schedule. However, the increase in the fee raises the vendor’s profit, and this positive effect offsets the negative effect, leading to increased profit as a whole. According to this analysis, this trend would stop only when the fee reaches a level that causes $V ^ { * }$ to decline almost to zero.

The simulation results in Figure 3 show the sensitivity of the vendor’s profit to demand volatility for varying subscription fees. We cannot see a consistent overall trend. This provides useful managerial information. For lower subscription fees, the vendor’s profit appears to increase even as demand volatility grows and lowers $V ^ { * }$ as we saw from the sensitivity analysis from the client’s perspective. However, for higher subscription fees, the vendor’s profit declines and exhibits a U‑ shape as demand volatility increases. This says that the vendor’s profit goes down slightly when there is substantial uncertainty about the level of IT service demand. The only explanation for this trend is the sharp decreasing trend of the optimal threshold demand level $V ^ { * }$ when both the demand volatility and the subscription fee increase. Specifically, with higher demand volatility and subscription fee levels, the client will choose to engage in outsourcing when its expected demand level is vastly lower. This, in turn, will lead to lower profit for the vendor.

## Contract Reversal Flexibility in IT Services Outsourcing

Our previous analysis consid ered the client’s outsourcing d ecision as being irreversible before contract expiry, which affects the value of outsourcing for both the client and the vendor. In some cases, the demand level V may grow large during outsourcing. When this occurs, outsourcing may no longer offer a relative cost advantage to the client. Instead, contract reversibility will allow the client to achieve a lower internal marginal cost per IT service unit. The potential cost savings that can be achieved from the reversibility of an IT services outsourcing decision is represented by the shaded area marked “cost outsourcing could add” in Figure 1. Prior research argues for building reversal options into outsourcing contracts; for example, the flexibility to switch to a fixed subscription fee contract or to backsource on or before time $\tau _ { 1 } , \tau < \tau _ { 1 } < T ,$ earlier than the contract duration T [5, 99]. As the first example suggests, there is no implication that the client will revert to whatever sourcing arrangement was used before, even though the word “reversal” might imply that. While such contract flexibility is expected to benefit the client, it is unclear how the vendor’s profit will be affected. Nevertheless, it is clear that the vendor will not wish to lose an income stream.<sup>2</sup> We next use the simplified real options derivation approach discussed earlier to develop a model that incorporates reversal flexibility to switch to a fixed subscription fee for outsourced IT services. We also consider contract reversibility involving backsourcing and show that the two forms of reversal flexibility are similar, and as a result, we can draw similar conclusions from the analysis. There are other reversal options worth considering, and examining these will provide the research and practitioner audiences with important intuition and an understanding of the interplay among demand volatility, cost savings potential, and contract flexibility.

## Modeling Contract Reversal Flexibility

## Switching to a Fixed-Fee Schedule

One potentially attractive aspect of outsourced IT services contracts for the client is the flexibility to switch to a fixed-fee schedule instead of subscription fee that is based on usage. We depict the cost structure for outsourcing with the flexibility to make such a switch in Figure 4. Upon making such a switch, the client will pay a fixed subscription fee W for the outsourced IT service, independent of the level of demand for the IT service that is outsourced. The switching cost for this is $S _ {  f ^ { \dagger } }$ and the cost to manage the contract M remains. The total sourcing cost with a fixed-fee schedule becomes

$$
\Omega_ {f} (V) = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} e ^ {- r t} (W + M) d t + S _ {\rightarrow f} = \frac {W + M}{r} + S _ {\rightarrow f}.\tag{15}
$$

After the client starts outsourcing its IT service with the usage-based pricing scheme we saw earlier, the client may wish to switch to a fixed-fee schedule when its total sourcing cost increases due to increased demand level. Based on the solution approach in Equations (4) and (12), the client’s decision will be determined by the function

$$
\begin{array}{c}\min \left| \Omega_ {o} (V), \Omega_ {f} (V) \right| = \Omega_ {o} (V) - \max \left(0, \frac {p}{r - \mu} V - \left(\frac {W}{r} + S _ {\rightarrow f}\right)\right)\\= \Omega_ {o} (V) - G _ {f} (V),\end{array}\tag{16}
$$

$$
\text { where } G _ {f} (V) = B I N O M I A L ^ {C A L L} \frac {p}{r - \mu} V, \frac {W}{r} + S _ {\rightarrow f}, \sigma , \tau_ {1}, r _ {f}, \delta .
$$

Here, from the real options perspective, the client holds a call option, denoted by $G _ { \rho } ( V )$ which gives the client the right to buy asset $( p / ( r - \mu ) ) V$ for exercise price $( W / r ) \dot { + } S _ {  f }$ on or before maturity time $\tau _ { \mathrm { 1 } }$ . This American call option can be evaluated using the binomial model.

Figure 4. Cost Structure for Outsourcing with Flexibility to Switch to a Fixed Subscription Fee

Assuming the client will not have yet started outsourcing (i.e., it is still insourcing), if one of the terms of an IT services outsourcing contract the client is considering permits flexibility for switching sourcing modes, then it is appropriate to think of the call option in Equation (16) as being nested within the more general option to outsource. As a result, the client’s outsourcing decision will be driven by the expression min $( \Omega _ { i } ( V )$ , min $( \Omega _ { o } ( V ) , \Omega _ { r } ( V ) ) )$ . Again, we can look at the solution approach in Equations (4) and (12) used for a similar problem without the nested option. So we represent the client as holding a put option in which a call option of switching flexibility is embedded:

$$
G _ {o + f} (V) = B I N O M I A L ^ {P U T} \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu} V, \left(\frac {C - M}{r} + G _ {f} (V) - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right), \sigma , \tau , r _ {f}, \delta\right).\tag{17}
$$

The above equation has the functional form of a compound put option to sell asset $( ( p - c ) / ( r - \mu ) ) V$ for exercise price $( ( C - M ) / r ) + G _ { \scriptscriptstyle f } ( V ) - S _ { \scriptscriptstyle  f }$ on or before the maturity time τ of the outsourcing option. It can be valued using a standard binomial model.

To complete this cycle of analysis, we can find an upper demand trigger level $V _ { f } ^ { * * }$ for the optimal exercise of the reversal option on or before $\tau _ { 1 }$ and a lower demand trigger level $V _ { o + f } ^ { * }$ for the optimal exercise of the compound outsourcing option on or before τ . The method is similar to what we did earlier involving the smooth pasting condition and Excel’s goal-seeking function with Equations (16) and (17).

## Backsourcing Flexibility

The derivation for the case of backsourcing flexibility is similar, so we will only summarize it briefly. With backsourcing, the client brings the delivery of the IT service back in house (or transfers it to another vendor) and incurs a lump-sum switching cost $S _ {  b } .$ This switching cost includes a possible penalty for premature contract termination and the cost of reinstalling the internal capabilities to support and execute the IT service. Assuming the client is already outsourcing, the client’s total sourcing cost for backsourcing is

$$
\Omega_ {b} (V) = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} e ^ {- r t} (c V e ^ {\mu t} + c) d t + S _ {\rightarrow b} = \frac {c}{r - \mu} V + \frac {C}{r} + S _ {\rightarrow b}.\tag{18}
$$

To the client, backsourcing flexibility can be readily valued as a call option $G _ { \scriptscriptstyle b } ( V )$ , given that it has already been outsourcing this IT service. Based on the logic applied in the case of switching to a fixed subscription fee, we can adapt the call option representing the reversal flexibility in Equation (16) by substituting the underlying asset $( p / ( r - \mu ) ) V$ with $( ( p - c ) / ( r - \mu ) ) V$ and the exercise price $( W / r ) + S _ {  f }$ with $( ( C - M ) / r ) + S _ {  b } ,$ and likewise adapt the put option representing the compound option to outsource in Equation (17) by substituting $G _ { \mathrm { \it f } } ( V )$ with $G _ { b } ( V )$ . Finally, the upper and lower demand trigger values for these options can be found based on the smooth pasting conditions we have been using.

## The Vendor’s Perspective

We now turn back to how reversal flexibility affects the vendor’s profit. Whatever approach is used to yield the vendor’s profit function, it will be similar for both of the contract reversal options we examined. We approximate this profit function based on the same approach used in Equation (5), except that the function will depend on both the lower and upper demand trigger values obtained for the client, $V _ { o + f } ^ { * }$ and $V _ { f } ^ { * * }$ . This essentially involves changing the upper boundary value for the integral in Equation (5) from infinity to $V _ { f } ^ { * * }$ . ## Analysis of Contract Reversal Flexibility for IT Services

We next present simulation results for both the reversal option to shift to a fixed-fee schedule for outsourcing and the reversal option to backsource, but we will postpone the detailed discussion of managerial insights and their implications to the next subsection. Our simulation is based on the same assumptions we used in the Simulation of Sourcing Model Decision Making subsection. In addition, for the switch to fixedfee pricing, we assume that the fixed fee is \$900 per time unit independent of the demand volume of service provided to the client, the client’s switching cost is \$20, and the reversal option can be exercised within a four-year period $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \tau _ { 1 } = 4 )$ . For backsourcing, we assume a larger cost of switching to backsourcing, \$400, because of the effort associated with bringing an IT service back in house. The last assumption regarding $\tau _ { 1 }$ is the same for backsourcing. It is suggestive of whether the same set of circumstances that would permit a firm to reverse a contract today will still permit reversal after a period of time goes by. The actual length of time would need to be fixed by a manager or financial analyst at a client firm. In any event, the results we report in this section are robust to changes in these parameters. We discuss next the client and vendor perspectives in a way that illustrates and emphasizes the intuition of the main findings.

## The Client’s Perspective

The numerical simulation results we obtained show that both the optimal trigger demand levels $V _ { f } ^ { * * }$ and $V _ { b } ^ { * * }$ for switching to fixed-fee pricing and backsourcing are generally smaller than the values we found for $V ^ { * * }$ when no reversal flexibility exists. (We do not graph or include the detailed numerical results for this reason.) In fact, both $V _ { f } ^ { * * }$ and $V _ { b } ^ { * * }$ get smaller and smaller than $V ^ { * * }$ as demand volatility $\sigma$ increases. Moreover, the same is observed as the usage-based subscription fee $p$ increases, although $\boldsymbol { V } _ { \boldsymbol { f } } ^ { * * }$ shows a lower sensitivity $\mathrm { t o } p$ than $V _ { b } ^ { * * }$ <sup>\*</sup>. These results indicate that a client firm that is already outsourcing would tend to delay its decision to switch to fixed-fee pricing or to backsourcing for a shorter time, as we expected.

As to the optimal demand trigger values for outsourcing with the two forms of reversal flexibility, $V _ { o + f } ^ { * }$ and $V _ { o + b } ^ { * } ,$ we found them to be less than the demand trigger value without flexibility, $V ^ { * }$ , and the higher is the demand volatility $\mathbf { o } ,$ the smaller will $\boldsymbol { V } _ { o + f } ^ { * }$ and $V _ { o + b } ^ { * }$ be in comparison to $V ^ { * }$ . To see why, it suffices to look at the case of switching to fixed-fee IT service pricing. The exercise price of the put option $G _ { _ { o + f } } ( V )$ in Equation (17) is larger than that of $G _ { _ { o } } ( V )$ in Equation (12), indicating that the client may prefer waiting until the IT service demand level V drops below a more stringent demand trigger value, $V _ { o + f } ^ { * } < V ^ { * }$ . This will ensure that the cost of switching to a fixedfee outsourcing payment arrangement will be avoided before the cost savings from outsourcing is able to recover the switching cost to outsourcing that was previously expended. This observation implies that, with increased demand uncertainty, building reversal flexibility into a contract results in delaying the decision to outsource longer than when there is no reversal flexibility. This result goes against the natural intuition that the firm should be less hesitant to outsource earlier when reversal flexibility is built into the contract.

With this said, we must further note an important difference between the two forms of reversal flexibility. For the flexibility to switch to fixed-fee pricing, $\boldsymbol { V } _ { o + f } ^ { * }$ declines as the usage-based subscription fee $p$ increases, but it has a lower sensitivity to increased demand volatility σ . By contrast, for backsourcing flexibility, $V _ { o + b } ^ { * }$ declines with increased subscription prices $p$ as well as increased values of $\mathbf { \sigma } _ { \mathbf { 0 } _ { : } }$ and it starts dropping fast toward zero when both $p$ and $\sigma$ get larger. Overall, this tells us that building reversal flexibility into a contract will reduce the likelihood that the client will choose to outsource in the first place, especially with backsourcing flexibility.

## The Vendor’s Perspective for Switching Flexibility to Fixed Subscription Fees

The vendor’s profit levels show somewhat different numerical results than we saw earlier, and we include two figures that illustrate the relationships. Figure 5 shows that the vendor’s profit is sensitive to the usage-based subscription fee p it charges per IT service unit outsourced. In the case of switching to fixed-fee pricing, the pattern varies with the degree of demand volatility σ . For higher levels of σ , the vendor’s profit grows as the usage-based subscription p charged during the outsourcing period increases up to a point where it starts declining slightly. For lower levels of σ , the profit function exhibits a characteristic shape. It declines slightly as p starts increasing above the client’s cost c for producing an IT service unit internally. It then climbs sharply when p gets larger than c, and subsequently flattens out and even starts declining as p becomes too large compared to c. In the case of backsourcing flexibility, a somewhat different pattern emerges. For higher degrees of demand volatility σ , the vendor’s profit consistently drops as p increases. For lower levels of σ , profit climbs sharply as p starts increasing above the client’s cost c for producing an IT service unit internally, and then drops sharply as p gets larger and larger than c.

The overall conclusion from both of these patterns is straightforward: when the vendor offers the client either form of contract reversal flexibility, the subscription fee $p$ that the vendor chooses to charge per outsourced IT service unit will critically impact its profit, either positively or negatively. This is so especially in the case of backsourcing flexibility. All of these results can be explained by the effects that we observed earlier of demand uncertainty and the subscription fee p on the lower and upper demand trigger levels at which the client will find it appealing to start outsourcing and to reverse the outsourcing arrangement.

Turning to Figure 6, the vendor’s profit is clearly sensitive to the level of demand volatility σ that the client experiences. For the flexibility to switch to fixed-fee pricing, the vendor’s profit tends to increase as σ increases. This pattern revealed by our results yields a useful managerial insight. When demand uncertainty $\sigma$ is low or medium, charging a higher usage-based subscription fee $p$ increases the vendor’s profits, but when demand uncertainty is sufficiently high, a higher subscription fee p will result in the erosion of profit. For backsourcing flexibility, a different pattern emerges. Charging a higher subscription fee p will generally yield lower profit, independent of the degree of demand uncertainty. In fact, except when demand uncertainty is very low, charging a lower subscription fee p yields a higher profit for the vendor.

Here, too, the overall conclusion is clear: when the vendor offers the client either form of contract reversal flexibility, the usage-based subscription fee p that the vendor chooses to charge would critically impact its profit, again either positively or negatively. This is so especially in the case of backsourcing flexibility. And, again, all these results can be explained by the effect of demand uncertainty and the subscription fee p on the lower and upper trigger demand levels that govern the client’s behavior.

## Summary of Managerial Observations

To summarize our results, three main managerial conclusions emerge regarding the vendor’s perspective in the base case with no reversal flexibility and the last two cases involving reversal flexibility.

Overall, the ability for the client to switch to another sourcing mode after it starts outsourcing is something that the vendor should recognize as an opportunity for profitability. As is typical in most option pricing settings, we know that the effect of a higher level of volatility is beneficial for the option holder. In this case, the holder of the option is the client, not the vendor. As a result, the reader can think of the curved lines in Figure 6 being compressed for higher levels of volatility, because the client is the beneficiary for being able to exercise the fixed-fee reversal option. We further observed that the vendor’s profit generally tends to increase with higher levels of demand volatility, in the range of parameter values that we used in our simulation.

A more important and related managerial insight follows from our results. It is critical in this IT services context for managers to understand the logic of the service science and financial economics that underlie this analysis. The client benefits from having the real option to shift from usage-based pricing to fixed-fee-based pricing, or to backsource, but the vendor also has the capacity to maximize profit through smart business policies in a competitive market that requires offering such flexibility. In particular, at times, the vendor may find it more profitable to charge a lower subscription fee than a higher one. In fact, we can see from the graphs in Figure 5 that for every level of demand volatility there appears to be a unique maximum of the fixed subscription fee. A key insight related to vendor profitability is that, if the other related costs are not too high and permit it, the vendor can obtain the highest profit by setting a subscription fee that is slightly above the client’s variable insourcing cost. This is interesting. It probably will be viewed in a favorable light from the client’s perspective. And the client would probably be less resistant to paying for the flexibility that an optional fee change-bearing contract permits beyond the service-specific fee. In effect, our results point to the need for the vendor to also price out its ability to sell the reversal flexibility to the client, as opposed to just making it available.

Going a step further, among the three cases we discussed, the vendor may find it most profitable to offer outsourcing contracts that give the client the flexibility to switch to fixed-fee pricing from usage-based pricing. The vendor then can effectively retain the client’s business at a reasonable fixed fee, while offering the client a means to handle demand uncertainty. To put this another way, vendor profit will be much lower when the vendor offers backsourcing flexibility than the flexibility to switch to a fixed-fee schedule. The main reason for this is that the lower and upper demand trigger values for the client to start outsourcing and switch back to insourcing are notably lower when demand uncertainty is large. Hence, not only will the likelihood that the client will switch to outsourcing be smaller, but even if the client decides to outsource, it will outsource for a shorter duration of time. This is a managerially interesting finding because it suggests that by offering the client contractual flexibility to switch to fixed-fee pricing in midstream, the vendor is able to expand the range of the IT service demand level over which it will be able to retain the client, which ought to result in increased profit. This observation is also striking in more general terms, since it is in line with the effects of contract flexibility that are well known in bond issuance, mortgage banking, and credit card banking.

## Contributions

Our primary contribution is to provide novel insights into the way demand volatility will affect the business policies of the IT services vendor, especially the offering of contract flexibility for clients who are considering outsourcing. A key insight of this research is that increased demand uncertainty actually increases clients’ tendency to insource, counter to the prevailing wisdom of most vendors that it stimulates outsourcing. This is in line with the more general intuition of real option analysis that suggests optionbearing assets are more valuable to their holders because they provide flexibility value to either exercise or not exercise the related option when future information makes the appropriate decision plain. Increased demand uncertainty requires the client to use a more constrained demand trigger value below which it is optimal to outsource. A decision to outsource before the observed demand level drops below the optimal demand trigger for outsourcing can erode the cost savings a client expects from outsourcing, because even small increases in demand could turn outsourcing into a strategy that adds rather than cuts cost. Based on this insight, the pattern of client behavior that will follow will clearly lower the vendor’s prospect of getting the client’s business and the profit it can generate even if the client decides to outsource.

In this light, another key insight of this research is that the vendor can modulate the client’s tendency to outsource using the usage-based subscription fee it charges per IT service unit outsourced. We specifically found that, for varying degrees of demand uncertainty, it is more profitable for the vendor to charge a subscription fee that is neither too close to nor too much higher than the client’s cost of producing an IT service unit internally.

Another related insight from this work is that building reversal flexibility into an IT services contract tends to delay a client’s decision to outsource, despite the fact that it also increases the client’s cost savings potential from outsourcing. With increasing demand uncertainty, the demand trigger value below which it is value maximizing for the client to outsource drops more rapidly than when contract opt-out flexibility is not available, to the extent that the client may never make a decision to outsource. Heretofore, though, researchers and practitioners for the most part have accepted the conventional wisdom that contract flexibility ought to play an important role in motivating IT services outsourcing decisions in volatile market and technological environments, and that it lays the foundation for outsourcing success [65, 77]. In contrast to this common view, a related key result that emerged from our analysis is that, when the vendor offers the client contract reversal flexibility, the client’s tendency to postpone its decision to outsource even more will negatively affect the vendor’s prospects for profit. With contract flexibility, the vendor may end up obtaining more small customers that have lower demand levels, while the large customers with the higher demand levels would likely postpone outsourcing.

Here, too, another main insight is related to the twofold response that the vendor can have. First, the vendor can modulate the client’s tendency to outsource, and at the same time increase its likelihood of greater profitability, by varying the usage-based subscription fee it charges per IT service unit outsourced. More specifically, the vendor can tailor the subscription fee it charges, depending on the degree of demand uncertainty the client faces. This approach will enable the vendor to induce some clients to outsource earlier by charging a lower subscription fee, and, at the same time, increase the vendor’s profit. Second, the vendor ought to consider when it is beneficial to offer one form of reversal flexibility versus another. For instance, when demand uncertainty is high, offering the client the flexibility to switch to fixed-fee pricing is generally more profitable than offering the flexibility to backsource.

Compared with the analysis of backsourcing implemented by Cha et al. [15], this study offers a contrasting perspective on backsourcing in the context of IT services outsourcing. First, Cha et al. focus on the effects of the knowledge transfer on backsourcing decisions in the context of system development outsourcing, while our study emphasizes the benefits of the flexibility that backsourcing option provides. Second, Cha et al. also treat backsourcing as a decision that occurs after outsourcing. Our approach is to model backsourcing as an embedded option when outsourcing is considered right from the start. Third, the former models the effects of backsourcing through the changes in the client’s unit costs for system development, while our study considers the changes in the client’s costs due to demand volatility. Fourth, the former takes the client’s standpoint, while our research also considers the vendor’s profit. In short, this study adopts a unique angle to look at backsourcing, and the findings provide valuable implications for research and practice.

Overall, the perspective that our analysis and key insights offer have interesting connections to other services business settings where options are embedded in contracts. With rental cars, clients have the option to bring a vehicle that has been rented for a two-week period back to the rental agency in ten days with prorated costs, if the need arises (e.g., family emergency, work requirements, etc.). With employee stock option compensation, firms often provide multiyear deferred compensation with qualified and unqualified options that either permit the full purchase of a stock or only the capture of the difference between the current stock price and the option strike price. Employees often are given the flexibility to choose different kinds of employee stock option plans that permit them to make choices about the kinds of options they can hold as investments from the same provider. We also see airlines, hotels, and public transportation firms (e.g., railroads, buses) making revenue yield management decisions in which they “bucket” or segment customers so they can specify a few fare classes as their main products. We expect additional thinking to emerge that leverages our simple characterization of demand volatility sensitivity and makes it operational for a portfolio of IT services contracts that exhibit characteristically different demand volatility levels and embed different reversal flexibilities. So even though our managerial findings in this and the prior sections are early and exploratory, they nevertheless are suggestive of a new and rich agenda for creating a foundational science for IT services.

A secondary contribution is that we demonstrated the efficacy of a simplified real option analysis approach, which allows us to address real-world IT services problems and produce useful insights for business policy and sourcing strategy. The spreadsheetbased sensitivity analyses we conducted with the simplified real option models we developed have proven to be relatively easy to carry out and yet they lead to powerful insights and managerial conclusions. More than that, though, our modeling approach can also be used to analyze the impact of endogenous factors that are subject to uncertainty from the client’s perspective, such as the vendor’s service quality or the vendor’s capacity to scale its IT services offerings—even though we only focused on demand for IT services. These kinds of insights will help managers to determine how different features will affect the vendor’s service-level contract design decisions as service demand changes. This is a problem to address in the context of service-oriented technology management and the emerging area of service science, since it establishes conceptual, theoretical, and analytical foundations for evaluating decision making in a key area of managerial concerns—contracting for IT services.

## Additional Issues and Further Applications

Other interesting problems in the management of IT services involve vendor contract designs for partial or shared IT services, demand volume–driven pricing, and outsourcing with lower bounds on the IT vendor’s service delivery levels. Because all of these different and more complex considerations will have real-world effects on the capacity of a vendor to successfully price its contracts for IT services, it will be helpful to evaluate how far we can push our simplified modeling approach for the creation of new service science techniques. This research dovetails well with the defined purposes of this special issue.

The applications of the ideas are not limited to the IT services context, though. There are many other contexts where it is possible to conceptualize managerial investment decision-making activities under uncertainty in terms of demand for some key factor in the operational or strategic environment of a firm. One such context, for example, involves the provision of IT and computer security services. In this case, a firm’s choice of how much to protect its intellectual property, its customers’ data, its business partners’ trusted relationship information, and so on are all implicitly subject to variations in demand that are introduced by changes in the real world. Examples include cases in which intellectual property is lost in the context of the normal conduct of business, with the result that the firm’s demand for information protection applications will increase. This demand is often expressed in terms of the need to purchase packages from outside vendors, to build new capabilities in house, and to engage consultants that can benchmark the current readiness of the firm to defend it against attacks. Recent research has probed these kinds of issues, especially hacking [87], peer-to-peer data loss [53], and customer data privacy [60]. However, the work has yet to be fully formulated in terms of its connections with the emerging area of service science, management, and engineering.

Our ideas in this research fit two other related domains. In the area of operational risk management for IT, the connections are plain and strong. Most organizations desktop computing, data management, and telecommunications operations are all subject to a variety of internal and external risks that are likely to affect demand for the services associated with these areas. As a result, we can think of the related operational processes as needing to staff up (as in the context of expanding the call center when a firm’s strategy changes), reduce head count (when market conditions do not permit profitability under the usual operational resources), and enhance capacity (related to the need to process more transactions when unexpected market, product, and service demand conditions require it). All of these can be understood from the point of view of our real option formulation of the stochastic demand and services sourcing problem. In some cases—such as with enhancing short-term computing capacity—the options may involve different internal sourcing choices, and in other settings—such as the short-term build-operate-transfer approach that we discussed at the outset of this paper—it may be more appropriate to go to the market in search of technical and managerial solutions.

In this study, we considered the uncertainty in client’s demand for the IT service capacity as the factor that triggers the outsourcing and, later on, the backsourcing decisions. However, in IT outsourcing service-level contracts, many factors need to be considered, and different terms for changing the outsourcing contracts, such as terminating the contracts prematurely, may be included [6]. One such factor is the quality of service that the vendor provides. Our proposed modeling approach can be extended to examine how the uncertainty in the demanded service quality level affects backsourcing decisions and the related SLA clauses, especially considering that most contracts for interorganizational transactions are incomplete [48]. For example, protection of non-copyrightable data has become a big concern to firms with a large collection of product and customer data [108], and concerns about data security and privacy may trigger backsourcing decisions [29]. Thus, a possible issue for further research is the relationship between the level of data security that the IT services vendor provides and backsourcing decisions. Our study can serve as a starting point for further pursuing this line of research.

## Limitations

In closing, it is worthwhile to share some thoughts with the reader about the limitations of the analysis work that we have done up to this point. Because our research is intended to provide some new ideas and modeling approaches based on real options theory, as well as to build some fundamental methods for IT services management and service science, it is appropriate to point out that the theory and the methods that we presented are based on a particular view of the world for insourcing, outsourcing, and backsourcing. The put and call option analysis we presented has a specific set of premises, and is founded on a well-known set of assumptions about the properties of assets in market situations. First, using real option theory directly to provide the mathematical basis for business policy valuation—as opposed to embedding real op tion analysis concepts in a separately conceived model—did not permit us to represent the problem in game-theoretic terms. Clearly, the gains of one party, the client, come only at the cost of a loss for the other party, the vendor.

Second, an alternative view may be to think of the sourcing mode change decision in different terms, as an incentive-based negotiation between the buyer and the vendor to split the value. The vendor will need to lose a bit of profit for the client to effectively manage its costs. This might forestall the client from shifting its sourcing mode. Because the client will not have any incentive ever to make a sourcing mode switch to an arrangement whose all-in costs are higher, it is clear that the negotiations never will take the form of a Nash bargaining game, where the incremental gains in profit are shared equally. There will be no incremental profit that can be shared by the client and the vendor. Thus, rational vendors will always wish to prespecify a price for contract flexibility—in addition to the price for the remaining SLA terms for delivery—because it will enable them to be compensated on a probabilistic basis for changes that a client may wish to make. One expects the rational vendor to levy a price for contract flexibility that reflects what the market will bear in terms of the expectation of the conditions under which a sourcing change will occur. This is similar to what we see with warranty policies on consumer electronics products at companies such as BestBuy.

Third, another issue is contract completeness relative to the reasons for the client to adjust the sourcing mode. In the real world, we can observe IT SLA contracts that do not specify all the details for sourcing mode–change decisions, with the result that a key part of the business relationship will be noncontractible. In this vein, our modeling approach does not consider other reasons for backsourcing and subscription fee changes beyond shifts in IT services demand. Still, many other reasons may lead clients to their decisions. Among them, service sufficiency and service quality often are identified and made contractible in SLA contracts. So when the vendor does not supply IT services at the level of performance and quality that are expected, the client often will have the capacity to levy a prespecified penalty on the vendor. Based on our field study experience in the IT services industry, there are many other reasons for noncontractibility that may make the client want to change its IT services sourcing arrangements. Common among them are changes in the business strength of the outsourcing services provider, differences in the perceived capabilities of its senior management, the presence in the market of new or other vendors who have emerging competencies that have not been fully understood, and so on. Clearly, there are some additional noncontractible costs that may be worthwhile for the vendor to explore in advance of specifying its SLA contracts. Even if not all of the dimensions that will create risk for the vendor to effectively contract and deliver IT services can be understood in advance, it is possible nevertheless that both parties to the transaction—vendor and client—will come to appreciate the all-in costs for the flexibility to backsource, and the importance of maintaining strong business relationships.

There are three boundary conditions for our case of the put option. The first simply states the value of option $F ( V )$ when V goes to infinity. The other two are free boundary conditions determining the transition from the continuation region to the stopping region at the critical demand switching value $V ^ { * }$

Boundary Condition 1 (The High Underlying Asset Value Condition): The high underlying asset value condition states that when the value of the underlying asset is $^ { \ast } h i g h ^ { \prime \prime } ( i . e . , V  \infty )$ , the option is worthless: $F ( \infty ) = O .$

Boundary Condition 2 (The Value-Matching Condition): The value-matching condition at the demand trigger value of $V = V ^ { * }$ requires that, when the option is exercised, the benefits must equal the cost of switching at the point of transition:

$$
F \left(V ^ {*}\right) = \left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}.
$$

Boundary Condition $3 \ : ( T h e$ Smooth Pasting Condition): The smooth pasting condition requires that

$$
F _ {V} \left(V ^ {*}\right) = \left(\left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V\right) _ {V} \Bigg | _ {V - V ^ {*}} = - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right),
$$

which represents the identity of marginal benefits and costs at $V ^ { * }$

For the High Underlying Asset Value Condition (BC1) to hold, we must set $A _ { \mathfrak { r } _ { 1 } } = 0$ because $\Psi > 1$ . The trigger value $V ^ { * }$ may then be derived from the Value-Matching Condition (BC2) and the Smooth Pasting Condition (BC3). We use BC2 and Equation (A3) so that

$$
A _ {2} \left(\left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}\right) ^ {\varphi} = \left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}.
$$

Hence,

$$
A _ {2} = \frac {\left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}}{\left(\left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}\right)}.\tag{A5}
$$

Substituting for $A _ { 2 }$ in Equation (A3) gives the following expression for the put option’s value:

$$
F (V) = \left(\left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S _ {\rightarrow o}\right) - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right) V ^ {*}\right)\left(\frac {V}{V ^ {*}}\right) ^ {\varphi}.\tag{A6}
$$

Taking the derivative of Equation (A3) by V and using the Smooth Pasting Condition (C3), we have

$$
\varphi A _ {2} \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu} V ^ {*}\right) ^ {\varphi - 1} = - \frac {p - c}{r - \mu}.\tag{A7}
$$

Then, substituting $A _ { \scriptscriptstyle 2 }$ from Equation (A5) into Equation (A6), we get

$$
V ^ {*} = \left(\frac {r - \mu}{p - c}\right) \left(\frac {C - M}{r} - S\right) \frac {\varphi}{\varphi - \left(\frac {p - c}{r - \mu}\right)}.\tag{A8}
$$
