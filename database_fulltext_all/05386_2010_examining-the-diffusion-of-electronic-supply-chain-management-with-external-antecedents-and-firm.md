# Examining the diffusion of electronic supply chain management with external antecedents and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance: A multi-stage analysis

Ing-Long Wu ⁎, Cheng-Hung Chuang

Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, 168 University Road, Ming-Hsiung, Chia-Yi, Taiwan

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 14 August 2009 Received in revised form 8 February 2010 Accepted 18 July 2010 Available online 23 July 2010

Keywords: e-SCM diffusion Innovation diffusion theory External antecedents Organizational performance

## a b s t r a c t

In modern business, competition is no longer between organizations, but among supply chains. Supply chain management (SCM) is the major weapon for <sup>fi</sup>rm's competition. e-SCM implementation is the key to the success in SCM practice. Innovation diffusion theory (IDT) with multi-stage analysis provides insight into the complicated process. Several key external antecedents affect the rate of e-SCM diffusion, in particular, technological and collaborative structures. Little research on IOS has discussed the performance impacts from the diffusion perspective among trading partners. Studies on IT performance have been traditionally focused on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance and inconclusive in their results. This study thus considers both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances in a complementary manner. Grounding on IDT and relevant theories, this study proposes a novel research model for the relationships among external antecedents, stage-based e-SCM diffusion, and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings have concluded the relative effects of external antecedents on diffusion stages and in turn, <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

## 1. Introduction

Business organizations face a more complex and competitive environment than ever before in the Internet era. Many organizations are gradually considering that they must compete, as part of a supply chain against other supply chains, to quickly re<sup>fl</sup>ect customers' changing demands. Supply chain management (SCM) is an important concept and discipline which enables business partners to integrate products and services effectively and to build long-term relationships. SCM can be extensively de<sup>fi</sup>ned as effective coordination on material, product, delivery, payment, and information <sup>fl</sup>ows between enterprises and trading partners [60,63]. Effective <sup>fl</sup>ow management across inter-organizational boundaries is the major focus of SCM concept. Information technology (IT), such as Internet and communication technology, is therefore an important tool to make the deployment feasible. For example, supply chain partners need to share production information to rapidly and accurately respond to inventory demand.

For these reasons, SCM has been widely recognized as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant area for IT investments to support its processes. Electronic SCM (e-SCM) is therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the physical implementation of SCM process with the support of IT while also making a distinction from the general management concept of SCM. However, e-SCM, although still considered to be in its early stages and with a high reported failure rate, is nonetheless believed to be the key to the <sup>fi</sup>nal success of

SCM process [48]. Due to this trend, IT innovation issue is at the core of modern SCM research. Nevertheless, most studies have been focused on a single decision view of adoption/acceptance based on relevant theories such as the technology acceptance model (TAM), theory of planned behavior (TPB) and their extensions [2,19]. In contrast, innovation diffusion theory (IDT) is a theory to understand the diffusion of innovations across time [59]. Based on IDT, researchers have presented models speci<sup>fi</sup>cally concerning information system (IS) innovation. These models were often addressed as stage-based processes, such as the three stages of initiation, adoption, and implementation [27,79]. Furthermore, some studies have empirically investigated the diffusion of Inter-Organizational Systems (IOS), such as Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) and e-business, using stagebased analyses. [30,53,56,57,78].

Moreover, because this diffusion process is dynamic and complex in nature, analyzing this process requires an understanding of the fundamental of evolutionary change across time. According to innovation researchers, a variety of contextual factors, originally de<sup>fi</sup>ned in IDT, may affect an organization's decision to diffuse new technologies. Five major contextual factors have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned: innovation (new technology), individual, task, organizational, and environmental factors [35]. In general, these <sup>fi</sup>ve factors can be further classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as technological and non-technological structures. The nontechnological structures consist of four factors: individual, task, organizational, and environmental factors. The technological structure is de<sup>fi</sup>ned to include one factor, innovation. e-SCM diffusion basically involves both internal diffusion among functional units within an organization and external diffusion across a large number of inter-organizational trading partners. This study was mainly focused on examining the aspect of the external diffusion of e-SCM across trading partners. Technological and environmental factors were therefore the major concerns in this study [55,57]. Individual, task, and organizational factors, however, were mainly de<sup>fi</sup>ned for intraorganizational diffusion and would not be considered in this study. Much research has also differentiated between intra-organizational and inter-organizational factors in assessing their different impact on IOS diffusion [47,51,56,57].

Furthermore, e-SCM diffusion has been considered to be an open collaborative system among trading partners, and the literature has greatly emphasized particular collaborative relationships rather than the general IS innovation environment [34,56,57,60,61]. Collaborative relationships between participants concern the degree of communication, trust, and interdependence which they enjoy, and which results in more stable transactions and in a reduced level of uncertainty and risk in the market [34,65]. Accordingly, collaborative relationships are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as consisting of a composition of relevant relationship attributes. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne it as the collaborative structure. Next, in IOS research, attributes of supply chain technologies are often de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of innovation characteristics, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Rogers [59]. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne it as the technological structure. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, technological structure includes three attributes: relative advantage, complexity, and ability to provide security; and collaborative structure comprises four attributes: peer pressure, transaction climate, environmental uncertainty, and supplier interdependence. In addition, a comprehensive literature review was initially carried out to theoretically de<sup>fi</sup>ne the stage-based structure to include three stages, adoption, implementation, and assimilation. This diffusion structure will be further con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by an empirical data for the latter analysis. These three components will be described in detail in the next section.

Finally, the ultimate goal of IT innovation diffusion is to increase <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Previous studies have investigated the impact of IT investments on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in general and have been inconclusive: namely the IT productivity paradox [11,21,22]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, little research on IOS has discussed the performance impacts from the diffusion perspective among trading partners [56,57]. Grounding on these ideas, this research also examines the important linkage between e-SCM diffusion and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Historically, studies have focused on <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, and one major reason for the IT productivity paradox may be that inappropriate measuring methods have been used. This study therefore considers both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures in a complementary manner [7,8,19]. In addition, many studies on the issue of organizational innovation have suggested a control effect of some organizational characteristics, such as industry type and <sup>fi</sup>rm size, on the realization of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [5,59,67,71]. We thus speci<sup>fi</sup>ed industry type and <sup>fi</sup>rm size as two control variables. In summary, this study proposes a novel research model to explore the relationships among external antecedents, stage-based e-SCM diffusion, and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it discusses two questions: (1) the impact of the two external structures on various diffusion stages diffusion and (2) the effect of various diffusion stages on <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances.

## 2.1. External structures and e-SCM diffusion

## 2.1.1. IDT and external structures

IS innovation may be broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as innovation in the organizational application of IT [68]. IDT is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system” [59]. While the theory has been originally applied to a wide range of organizational innovations, much research has been undertaken to explore IS innovation diffusion in particular. Furthermore, the diffusion of IS innovation has indicated a critical role in determining the <sup>fi</sup>nal success of its usage [26,28]. Basically, two types of research approaches were used to investigate the diffusion of IS innovation: factor research and stage research [54,59]. Factor research generally employs crosssectional research designs and is used for identifying variables that are related to particular implementation outcomes. Stage research generally uses longitudinal or repeated measures to explore how the diffusion process with multiple stages is guided and affected by changes in related variables over time.

While much innovation research with IDT was mainly based on intra-organizational context, little research has been focused on interorganizational context, for example, e-SCM diffusion. A brief review of relevant literature for the major antecedents of inter-organizational diffusion is presented as below. First, several studies for examining the adoption of various supply chain tools have reported many factors that affect their adoption and use. These factors are divided into those related to IT attributes and those related to maintaining the relationships that are important in managing supply chain linkage [56,60,61]. Next, other studies have examined the diffusion of web technologies or EDI with the impacts of internal and external drivers and further, the realization of SCM performance [55,57]. Their results revealed that IT attributes and supplier interdependences are the major factors in affecting this diffusion. In summary, the above studies have clearly indicated a similar argument that the success of diffusing interorganizational systems is mainly dependent on two key external factors: IT attributes and collaborative relationships. In this study, we named IT attributes and collaborative relationships as the technological and collaborative structures, respectively, for the purpose of consistence and easy contrast between using the two terms. The following subsections further elaborate these two structures.

## 2.1.2. Technological structur

As discussed previously, technological structure is mainly de<sup>fi</sup>ned according to the innovation factor discussed in Rogers [59]. The innovation factor basically includes <sup>fi</sup>ve attributes: relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, trialability, and observability. According to a comprehensive literature review of IS innovation adoption, we considered two attributes, relative advantage and complexity, which are commonly recognized as the most important factors in e-SCM diffusion. Relative advantage re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which an innovation is perceived as providing better organizational advantages than either the status quo or its precursor. Complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand and use. The reasons behind this consideration are twofold.

First, since the <sup>fi</sup>ve innovation attributes were originally de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Rogers [59], most of the later studies, including both IS innovation in general and IOS in particular, have particularly emphasized the impact of three attributes: relative advantage, complexity and compatibility [3,52,61,72,78]. One of the studies investigated the relationship between innovation attributes and innovation adoption with a review and meta-analysis of seventy <sup>fi</sup>ve articles, and concluded that three attributes, relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility, enhance the likelihood of innovation adoption [72]. Second, the TAM is an important theory in explaining the acceptance of IS innovation from the perspective of technology [18,19,37,74]. This theory considers two key drivers in determining the use of IS innovation: perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU). PU and PEOU in TAM are equivalent to the attributes of relative advantage and complexity, respectively [44].

More importantly, recent research in the Internet-based innovation, such as B2B e-marketplace and e-commerce, has well focused on the consideration of two innovation attributes, relative advantage and complexity [37,77,78]. Their arguments for this consideration are as below. For the Internet-based technologies with an open access and common experience to all users, such as supply chain technologies and e-commerce rather than EDI, compatibility has been becoming congruent with existing experiences and practices among trading partners and was suggested to be neglected in the relevant research or merged with the attributes of PEOU [77,78]. Additionally, while the deployment of e-SCM is primarily through the support of Internet and communication technologies, the guarantee of the security of information <sup>fl</sup>ow is the important concern in the adoption decision among trading partners. Thus, the ability to provide security is critically relevant to the further adoption of e-SCM diffusion [60,65]. In summary, technological structure was considered to include three major attributes for e-SCM diffusion: relative advantage, complexity, and the ability to provide security.

## 2.1.3. Collaborative structure

Some studies argued that the central principle in creating <sup>fl</sup>exible supply chains is the existence of collaborative relationships, which can facilitate a mutual decision-making process directed toward achieving common goals across their partners [34,60,65]. The term “collaborative relationships” implies the fact that a certain level of communication, trust, and interdependence of <sup>fi</sup>rms in their transactions of various supplies has resulted in a reduced level of uncertainty and risk in the market [34]. A good collaborative relationship allows trading partners to jointly have a clearer understanding of future demand, develops feasible plans to <sup>fi</sup>t the demand, and coordinates relevant activities to achieve the plans in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective manner [65]. Much research has attempted to identify prerequisites for collaborative relationships in terms of the need of information sharing and integration in supply chains [6,41,42]. Accordingly, collaborative relationships, in essence, are a combination structure with comprising relevant relationship attributes. We termed it as “collaborative structure” in this study. Consequently, this structure was de<sup>fi</sup>ned to explain externally relevant attributes in facilitating a high level of communication among supply chain partners. The following discusses the important collaborative attributes for measuring this structure, including peer pressure, transaction climate, environmental uncertainty, and supplier interdependence.

According to Porter's <sup>fi</sup>ve-force competitive model [49], competitive pressure (one of the <sup>fi</sup>ve forces) was found to be an important external driver for initiating the deployment of IOS among trading partners [3,30,58]. Researchers have highlighted the external role of competitive pressure in the diffusion of EDI [50–52]. Another research also argued institutional pressure is positively related to e-SCM adoption [33]. Accordingly, peer pressure was considered to be an important attribute of collaborative structure for the diffusion of e-SCM. Next, another important attribute of collaborative relationships is a favorable transaction climate between partners since a favorable climate indicates a high level of trust and commitment partnership [33,46,47,51]. A high level of trust and cooperation is required, since there are no boundaries for future transactions among inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm participants; while most of the manual control mechanisms and paper trails, which currently exist to ensure the accuracy and integrity of various transactions, will eventually be removed. As a result, a good transaction climate with mutual trust and commitment between partners may play a critical role in facilitating the diffusion of e-SCM diffusion.

SCM aims at building a mutual understanding and trust of partnership for purchase and sale of various materials and components, the partnership is de<sup>fi</sup>nitely in a position to reduce uncertainty for the unexpected change of material and component supply market [47,73]. Moreover, researchers have argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms facing environmental uncertainty have a greater incentive to adopt IOS for improving information exchange and reducing uncertainty between trading partners [1,47]. Finally SCM emphasizes the building of power relationships and interdependence between organizations and other entities in the environment that affect their managerial processes and activities [50,57]. Some studies have indicated that supplier interdependence may play an important role in the formation of supply chain partnerships. While inter-organizational systems, such as e-SCM and B2B commerce, are primarily in building electronic partnerships across trading partners, supplier interdependence is more likely to be important in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the potential participants to adopt IOS [14,29,57].

## 2.1.4. IDT and e-SCM diffusion

IS innovation diffusion is dynamic and complex in nature. It may vary with distinct sets of characteristics across time and involves different loci of organizational impact [54]. To better understand IS innovation diffusion problems and how they can be solved; a multistage analysis provides more insight for this [26]. The purpose of IDT is primarily to explore how a diffusion process with multiple stages is guided and affected by changes in related variables over time. A twostage model for innovation diffusion was initially proposed, including adoption and implementation [59]. The adoption stage further includes three substages: knowledge acquisition, persuasion and learning, and decision, leading to the actual adoption decision. The implementation stage includes a further three substages: preparation for organizational change, process, and technology necessary for innovation deployment. Furthermore, Meyer and Goes [43] also de<sup>fi</sup>ned a <sup>fi</sup>ve-stage model for organizational innovation, that is, knowledge awareness, evaluation, adoption, implementation, and expansion.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this theory has been widely applied to traditional IS settings while IS resource has increasingly become an important source of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's competitive advantage. Kwon and Zmud [35] <sup>fi</sup>rst developed a six-stage model for IS innovation diffusion based on Lewin's three-stage change model [38], comprising initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routinization, and infusion. Later, Rajagopal [55] applied the six-stage model to understand various contextual factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rms to implement enterprise resource planning systems. Moreover, Swanson and Ramiller [68] described a four-stage model: comprehension, adoption, implementation, and assimilation, to comprehend a <sup>fi</sup>rm's involvement in IT innovation diffusion. In particular, many researchers have unanimously presented a similar three-stage model for traditional IS innovations. For example, Zmud [79] de<sup>fi</sup>ned the three stages as initiation, adoption, and implementation for investigating the diffusion of general software practices; Saga and Zmud [62] described the three stages of acceptance, routinization, and infusion for IT acceptance as a whole; and Zmud and Apple [80] described the three stages as earliness of adoption, routinization, and infusion to understand the incorporation of electronic scanners in supermarket chains. One study has examined a two-stage model, adoption and infusion, to <sup>fi</sup>nd out the interaction of task and technology factors on MRP implementation [17].

While the stage-based model has been widely applied to traditional IS settings, few studies have reported on the particular e-SCM or IOS diffusion. Some studies have proposed a number of threestage models for IOS diffusion. For example, Premkumar et al. [52] discussed the diffusion of EDI in terms of three stages: adaptation, internal diffusion, and external diffusion. Zhu et al. [78] developed an integrative model to examine three stages of e-business diffusion: initiation, adoption, and routinization. In addition, one study has claimed three sequential types of implementation outcomes for the decision of adopting EDI: extent of adaptation, internal diffusion, and external connectivity [51]. Other studies have explored the diffusion of web technologies in SCM with the major stages of internal diffusion and external diffusion [56,57]. However, prior research on IOS diffusion has restricted itself to a dichotomous measure of use versus non-use [46,75], which provides insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient insight into this diffusion. These evidences indicate the importance of considering a stage-based diffusion structure in e-SCM diffusion.

Lewin [38] proposed a three-stage change model, unfreezing, moving, and refreezing, to describe the phenomenon of a system implementing organizational innovation. Hence, while consulting to this change model and other researches [17,35,53], we found out that the various stage-based frameworks with distinct number of stages inherently follow a similar diffusion pattern. This pattern can be described as below. An initiation/adoption stage, where a decision is made to invest in IS innovation and a preparation is initiated for business processes redesign, is followed by other stages starting with an adaptation/acceptance stage, where the organization begins to learn and adapt to the innovation by trying it out and then their feedbacks are collected for the further improvement of the innovation. After that, subsequent diffusion stages initiating with a routinization/infusion stage capture the experiences of expanded use and <sup>fi</sup>nally lead to widespread transfer for regular use in an organization.

Accordingly, a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme proceeds for de<sup>fi</sup>ning the diffusion stages as follows: (1) adoption classi<sup>fi</sup>ed for initiation, comprehension, earliness of adoption, and adoption; (2) implementation classi<sup>fi</sup>ed for adaptation, acceptance, and implementation; and (3) assimilation classi<sup>fi</sup>ed for routinization, infusion, and assimilation. Moreover, an extended study of IDT with attitude theory, examining the impact of user belief and attitude on IS innovation adoption, has found differences between pre-adoption and post-adoption stages [32]. This provides further evidence for rationality of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation process. Based on the above discussion, we initially proposed a threestage model to examine e-SCM diffusion, that is, adoption, implementation, and assimilation. Accordingly, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned adoption stage as the decision for investing e-SCM and the preparation for redesigning SCM processes, implementation stage as the process for trying to use e-SCM and further accepting to use the innovation, and assimilation stage as the process for continuously gaining experiences of using e-SCM and <sup>fi</sup>nally leading to regular use of the innovation.

## 2.1.5. Development of hypotheses

Karahanna et al. [32] tested the differences between two stages of Windows software diffusion, pre-adoption and post-adoption, for a subset of innovation characteristics. Cooper and Zmud [17] investigated the differences in task and innovation factors across two stages of MRP diffusion, adoption and infusion. Particularly focused on the IOS setting, some studies explored the effect of technology factors on two stages, internal diffusion and external diffusion, for e-SCM/EDI diffusion among trading partners [56,57]. Another study examined the impact of innovation factors on three stages of EDI diffusion: adaption, internal diffusion, and external diffusion [53]. In addition, one study discussed the impact of innovation factors on the adoption process of different e-commerce communication and application technologies [3]. These studies have reported that technology factors have differential impacts on various stages of diffusion. Accordingly, we can argue that technological structure has an important linkage with the three different stages in e-SCM diffusion. Hypothesis 1– Hypothesis 3 are thus proposed for these.

Hypothesis 1. Technological structure is positively related to e-SCM's adoption.

Hypothesis 2. Technological structure is positively related to e-SCM's implementation.

Hypothesis 3. Technological structure is positively related to e-SCM's assimilation.

While collaborative structure was previously de<sup>fi</sup>ned to include four relationship attributes, some studies have examined IOS diffusion with a partial set of this structure. One study investigated the differences in collaborative structure between two adoption stages in EDI diffusion, with the attributes of peer pressure, transaction climate, and supplier interdependence [51]. Another study assessed the differences in collaborative structure over the stages of internal and external diffusion, with the attributes of peer pressure and supplier interdependence, for the diffusion of web technologies [57]. In addition, a similar study was carried out with two attributes to examine the impact of collaborative structure on two stages, internal integration and external integration, in EDI diffusion [56]. Furthermore, many researchers tested the impact of collaborative relationships on adoption and post-adoption stages in diffusing supply chain technologies/IOS [34,60]. Similarly, we can argue that there are potential relationships between collaborative structure and the three different stages in e-SCM diffusion. Hypothesis 4–Hypothesis 6 are thus proposed for these.

Hypothesis 4. Collaborative structure is positively related to e-SCM's adoption.

Hypothesis 5. Collaborative structure is positively related to e-SCM's implementation.

Hypothesis 6. Collaborative structure is positively related to e-SCM's assimilation.

## 2.2. e-SCM diffusion and firm performance

## 2.2.1. e-SCM value

For over a decade, studies of the value of IT have attempted to measure the performance realized from IT investment. However, the results have been inconclusive for <sup>fi</sup>nding little or no improvement in productivity despite massive IT investment, namely the IT productivity paradox [9,10,22]. Perhaps the most serious issue in the problem has been the use of inappropriate methodology to assess <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The focus has been on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial aspect to the exclusion of other organizational practices [21]. Researchers have thus suggested the need to use non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures such as customer satisfaction and quality improvement, although there is little agreement on precisely which measures to use [13]. This study uses both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures for e-SCM value in a complementary manner.

Productivity indices, such as return on investment (ROI) and return on assets (ROA), are widely accepted for <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures and have been often used to measure e-SCM value [20,60,66,70]. In addition, revenue and cost structures, including sale revenue and inventory cost, are also the major indicators of <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance in SCM domain [20,25,39,66]. Next, Beamon [7] proposed a performance measure system for supply chain management with three major indicators: resource, output, and <sup>fl</sup>exibility. Resource is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the utilization of the resources in a system to meet system objectives, such as manufacturing costs, inventory costs, and ROI. Output is used to measure customer responsiveness, on-time delivery, design speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, and product performance. Flexibility is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a system's ability to accommodate supply, demand, and schedule <sup>fl</sup>uctuations from suppliers, manufactures, and customers. Output and <sup>fl</sup>exibility are non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures, while resource is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial measure.

Other researchers argued that dependability, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, quality, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency are the key indicators for measuring SCM performance [4,76]. Dependability is the ability to meet delivery dates at promised prices [39,64]. Flexibility is the ability to react to market changes, new product developments, and customer requirements [62,63]. Quality determines how well products/services meet customer needs [39,66]. Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, the improvement of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, can be achieved through supply chain mechanisms such as lowering inventory levels, reducing manufacturing costs, and increasing production volumes [47,64]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst three indicators are more likely classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measure while ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial measure. Comparing the two performance schemes, <sup>fl</sup>exibility is commonly recognized as an independent non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measure. Next, output, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned above, seems to cover both the attributes of dependability and quality. However, quality is generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned to include all product attributes across all stages of the product life cycle. The term “quality” is also better known to practitioners, and so we have replaced output with quality as another non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measure. Finally, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, as defined above, is similar to resource measure and is considered to be a <sup>fi</sup>nancial measure.

## 2.2.2. Development of hypothese

The performance impact of adopting IS innovation has generated considerable interest. Many researchers have investigated the payoff of IT investment at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level from both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial perspectives [13,26,30,70]. This is because IT performance has been traditionally measured by <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, which may be a cause of the IT productivity paradox. Recent research has suggested that use of IT, in general, is an important driver to explain IT performance [21–23]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, one study discussed the differences of a set of possible outcomes regarding the diffusion of client/server architecture across the two stages of adoption and assimilation for the user department and the IT department [26]. These possible outcomes can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes, the <sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes including software and development costs, hardware costs, maintenance costs and so on, and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes including ease of use, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, response time, and so on. Consistent with these concepts, this study examines the linkage between various stages of e-SCM diffusion and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance with <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial components.

In general, there is no proven argument that various stages of IOS diffusion can lead to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances. However, many studies have examined the impact of various diffusion stages on similar components of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in terms of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial components de<sup>fi</sup>ned in this study [56,57]. One study investigated the relationship between the diffusion process of EDI and its impact, both direct and indirect, on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, with adoption and integration stages [30]. Direct bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were mostly related to operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and transaction cost reductions, while indirect bene<sup>fi</sup>ts mainly referred to customer service and inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships. These performance indicators can be properly divided into <sup>fi</sup>nance and non-<sup>fi</sup>nance-based indicators. In summary, these studies have articulated the adoption stage and its impact on similar <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial components of <sup>fi</sup>rm performances. Accordingly, we can argue that there are some linkages between the adoption stage and the two types of <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances. Hypothesis 7–Hypothesis 8 are thus proposed concerning this.

Hypothesis 7. e-SCM's adoption has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance.

Hypothesis 8. e-SCM's adoption has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance

Next, we have simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed the adaptation, acceptance, and implementation stages into a single implementation stage in the above classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme. Many studies have investigated the impact of implementation stage on different forms of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. For instance, researchers have discussed the possible impact of four diffusion stages of IT innovation, including comprehension, adoption, implementation, and assimilation, on organization's engagement, commitment and achievement, which can entail a learning process for reaching various types of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, including operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, competitive advantage, quality, and both internal and inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships [68]. These performance indicators can be properly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial indicators. Another study has examined the effect of EDI diffusion on perceived implementation success, including both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances, across the stages of adaptation, internal diffusion, and external diffusion [53]. Drawing on these studies, we can argue some connections between the implementation stage and both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances. Hypothesis 9–Hypothesis 10 thus are proposed concerning this.

Hypothesis 9. e-SCM's implementation has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance.

Hypothesis 10. e-SCM's implementation has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance.

Finally, the previous studies used to develop Hypothesis 7 and Hypothesis 8 have also indicated the same relationships between the assimilation/integration stage and <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances [26,30]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, one study has examined the effect of two stages of EDI diffusion, internal and external integration, on two types of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance: operational and market performances [56]. Operational performance places emphasis on cost-related and productivity attributes and market performance on customer-related attributes. The former and the latter can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances respectively. Another study has investigated the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the diffusion of web technologies, with a number of measuring indicators, across the two stages of internal and external assimilation [57]. These indicators consisted of cost reduction, inventory management, cycle-time reduction, customer service, supplier-relationship management, and competitive advantage, and can be properly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as <sup>fi</sup>nance and non-<sup>fi</sup>nance-based. In summary, these studies have in common to indicate the impact of assimilation stage from a constitution of internal and external integration. Drawing on these studies, we can argue that there is some relationships between the assimilation stage and <sup>fi</sup>nance and non-<sup>fi</sup>nance-based performances. Hypothesis 11–Hypothesis 12 are thus proposed for this.

Hypothesis 11. e-SCM's assimilation has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance.

Hypothesis 12. e-SCM's assimilation has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance.

## 2.3. Control variables

Many studies on organizational innovation have argued a number of additional variables for inclusion because of their potential impact on the innovation adoption or diffusion [59,67,71]. The <sup>fi</sup>rms in high dynamic industries, such as electronics and high-tech manufacturing, show higher revenue volatility and customer turnover while compared to those in low dynamic industries [40]. Some researchers thus suggested that industry type is an additional variable for controlling the achievement of SCM process [5,45]. In addition, larger <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to adopt e-SCM than smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms because they possess the resources and skills necessary to assimilate the innovation effectively. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rm size has been found to have a positive impact on adoption behavior toward the innovation [59] and should be incorporated in the relationship model for controlling some extraneous effects [45,67,71].

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Instrumentation

This research used a survey to collect data. The instrument was designed with a four-part questionnaire. The <sup>fi</sup>rst part used a nominal scale and the others used 7-point Likert scales, as attached in the Appendix.

## 3.1.1. Basic information

Here, organizational characteristics were collected including industry type, annual revenue, number of employees, and number of suppliers, as well as respondent characteristics including experience, education level, age, and position.

## 3.1.2. External structure

This part, as discussed previously, comprised two major external structures: technological and collaborative structures. Technological structure further consisted of three factors: relative advantage, complexity, and ability to provide security. The measuring items for relative advantage and complexity were adapted from the instrument developed by Moore and Benbasat [44], each containing three items. Ability to provide security was de<sup>fi</sup>ned from the discussion of Ruppel [60], including two items. Collaborative structure further consisted of four sub-constructs: peer pressure, transaction climate, environmental uncertainty, and supplier interdependence. The measuring items for the <sup>fi</sup>rst two were adapted from the instrument developed by Premkumar and Ramamurthy [51], each containing three items. Environmental uncertainty was adapted from the instrument developed by Patterson et al. [47], including two items. Supplier interdependence was adapted from the instrument developed by Ranganathan et al. [57], including two items.

## 3.1.3. e-SCM diffusion

This part measured the extent of e-SCM diffusion across trading partners. The nature of IT innovation has been evolving rapidly with a large number of possibly interrelated innovations, which suggested a wider emphasis on aggregated measures for its performance. Two dimensions were used for the aggregated measures: the degree of change of behavior across major IS innovations and the degree of change of behavior across major diffusion stages [23]. These two dimensions were used to identify the diffusion stages of e-SCM, that is, adoption, implementation, and assimilation. Accordingly, the measured items for these two dimensions were adaptively de<sup>fi</sup>ned from the relevant discussions of IOS diffusion [56,57], each including three items. The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension contained three IOS innovations: electronic data interchange, information sharing in supply chains, and epurchase with ordering and ful<sup>fi</sup>llment functions. The second dimension considered three transactional diffusion stages: proportion of total suppliers, proportion of total transactions with suppliers, and average proportion of <sup>fi</sup>rm suppliers' transactions with their trading partners. As a result, a total of six items were de<sup>fi</sup>ned for this part.

## 3.1.4. Firm performance

This part only de<sup>fi</sup>ned non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures while <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures can be obtained from the database of Taiwan Stock Exchange Corporation. Financial measures, as discussed previously, mainly included four measuring items: return on investment (ROI), return on assets (ROA), return on sales (ROS), and inventory turnover ratio [7,39,76]. For the real value of each measuring item, it was discretely rede<sup>fi</sup>ned in a 7-interval form in order to match a 7-point Likert scale in the instrument design for the purpose of the whole data analysis. Non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures consisted of two attributes: <sup>fl</sup>exibility and quality. These measures were further de<sup>fi</sup>ned to include seven measuring items, such as reactions to market change and improvements to product performance [7,63,64,66]. As a result, a total of 11 items were de<sup>fi</sup>ned for this part.

## 3.1.5. Control variables

Industry type comprises three categories, that is, high-tech manufacturing, traditional manufacturing, and service. Firm size was measured using the total number of employees in the <sup>fi</sup>rm. It consists of three <sup>fi</sup>rm sizes, that is, large size, medium size, and small size.

## 3.2. Sample design

This study primarily examines the contributions of e-SCM diffusion in organizations. To qualify for this study, <sup>fi</sup>rms require massive investments in supply chain technologies and experience in supply chain management. Thus, it was assumed that larger <sup>fi</sup>rms would be more likely to have this kind of experience. A study sample of top 1500 <sup>fi</sup>rms was thus selected from the 2005 listing of manufacturing and service sectors published by the Taiwan Stock Exchange Corporation. Furthermore, the objectives of this study include the understanding of relevant managerial issues relating to e-SCM diffusion, including <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Top managers including vice presidents and manufacturing executives are the persons most familiar with the issues. Previous research has also suggested that senior executives are an appropriate source of information concerning IT investment and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [69]. Both executives were therefore selected as the respondents, as this avoids the response bias from a single informant.

Most of the sample <sup>fi</sup>rms are publicly listed in the stock market and the <sup>fi</sup>rms' information, including basic information, <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports, and e-mail addresses for top managers, is made publicly available on their web sites. Thus, it was convenient to conduct a web-based survey to collect the empirical data. The information available about the positions of top managers listed on web sites differed between sample <sup>fi</sup>rms. Most of the web-sites only had information available for vice presidents or operational executives.

Accordingly, only one respondent was selected for each sample <sup>fi</sup>rm in the survey. First, an invitation letter asking about their experience on e-SCM diffusion was prepared for the potential respondents. If they agreed to participate, the same invitation letter with a web site hyperlink, which instantly directed the potential respondents to an online questionnaire, was sent out by e-mail. In addition, in order to improve survey return, a follow-up procedure was carried out with another invitation letter for the non-respondents after 2–3 weeks. One month later, the same invitation letter and questionnaire were sent to the non-respondents by regular mail.

## 3.3. Scale validation

Initially, pretest was conducted for the scale. The scale was carefully examined by selected practitioners and academicians in this area, including translation, wording, structure, and content. The initial reliability and content validity of the scale should be acceptable. After the questionnaire was <sup>fi</sup>nalized, 1500 invitation letters for the study sample were sent out by e-mail to the potential respondents. 1326 letters were successfully sent out and 184 valid questionnaires were received. The response rate was 13.9%. The seemingly low response rate raised possible concerns about non-response bias. To check non-response bias, the responding sample was divided into two subsamples, that is, early and late ones, including 113 and 71 respondents respectively. The two groups were compared, using various demographic characteristics, for their correlation with t-test, including annual revenue, number of employees, and number of suppliers. All of them (t value =0.86, 0.65, and 1.84) showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences at the 0.05 level. Their results indicate that there is no systematic non-response bias for the responding sample. Table 1 summarizes the demographics of the respondents. Furthermore, to ensure that the online questionnaire did not cause a selection bias among the respondents, statistics of the sample frame were compared with statistics of the respondents in terms of various demographics using $\chi ^ { 2 }$ tests. The results show that their demographics did not have any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences at the 0.05 level. This increases the external validity of the survey data in the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 3.3.1. Measurement model

PLS is a structural equation modeling (SEM) technique that employs a nonparametric and component-based approach for estimation purposes. PLS allows latent variables to be modeled as either formative or re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs and places minimal demands on sample size and residual distributions [15,16]. Theoretically, the sample size for executing PLS requires 10 times of the number of indicators associated with the most complex formative construct or the largest number of antecedent constructs linking to an endogenous construct. This study has been categorically de<sup>fi</sup>ned as having three different stages in e-SCM diffusion and the total sample needs to be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three sub-samples for the analysis of each of the three stages. PLS is the best analytical tool available to <sup>fi</sup>t the requirement of small sample size. This study thus uses PLS to analyze the research model.

In PLS model, re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators are used to examine an underlying construct which is unobservable such as attitude. Formative indicators are used to form a superordinate construct as a categorization and measurement device for complex phenomena, where the individual indicators are weighted according to their relative importance in forming the construct [15,36]. Moreover, formative indicators need not to be correlated nor need to have high internal consistency. In our research model, for example, technological structure is mainly viewed as an explanatory combination of its indicators, namely relative advantage, complexity, and ability to provide security. Therefore, the two exogenous constructs, technological and collaborative structure, were formulated as the second-order formative constructs, which were further determined from a combination of their <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators/subconstructs. Accordingly, a second-order measurement model was built to assess reliability and construct validity of the scale.

Reliability for a construct is assessed by Cronbach' α. Construct validity includes convergent and discriminant validities. Convergent validity for a construct is assessed by three criteria: (1) all item loadings (λ) larger than 0.70, (2) composite construct reliability larger than 0.80, and (3) average variance extracted (AVE) larger than 0.50 [24]. Discriminant validity between constructs is assessed using the criterion that the square root of AVE for a construct should exceed its correlations with all of the other constructs [15,24]. ## 5. Findings and discussions

As indicated in Fig. 2 and Table 4, our discussion will focus on two aspects: <sup>fi</sup>rstly, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the path relationships among the main constructs, and secondly, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the sub-constructs in re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the main constructs.

## 5.1. Relationships among the main constructs

In general, both the technological and collaborative structures were important antecedents on each of the three stages in e-SCM diffusion. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the two structures both demonstrated a greater effect on the adoption stage while compared to the other two stages (β=0.53, 0.21, and 0.30 for the technological structure and β=0.50, 0.24, and 0.38 for the collaborative structure). The reasons for the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be explained as below. The adoption stage was de<sup>fi</sup>ned previously as the stage when a decision is made to invest in IS innovation and a preparation is made for beginning business process redesign. The other two stages were described as beginning to learn and adapt to the innovation as well as eventually bringing it into widespread and regular use. Since the investment of e-SCM requires massive intangible and tangible resources, top management needs to

consider very carefully the initial decision to deploy the innovation. There must be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient necessity and motivation to support the decision. This therefore brings about the relative importance of the technological and collaborative structures to the initial decision process. These two contextual structures together explain 52% of the variance in the adoption stage while only 32% in the implementation stage and 36% in the assimilation stage.

Furthermore, the technological structure had a greater effect on the adoption stage while the collaborative structure was more critical in affecting the implementation and assimilation stages, as indicated in their coef<sup>fi</sup>cients' comparison with $\beta = 0 . 5 3 > 0 . 5 0 , 0 . 2 1 < 0 . 2 4 ,$ , and 0.30b0.38 for adoption, implementation, and assimilation. This may be explained as follows. While in the adoption stage, managers are working on the initial decision process regarding e-SCM investment and business processes redesign, and the major task is the evaluation of various feasibilities of supply chain technologies. On the other hand, during the implementation and assimilation stages of diffusion, the primary tasks are to build mutual trust and commitment among trading partners in order to quickly establish supply chain networks.

Next, the three diffusion stages play an important role in impacting both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances, except for the relationship between the adoption stage and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the initial and middle stages, adoption and implementation, have a greater impact on non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance than on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance $( \beta = 0 . 5 0 > 0 . 1 8$ and 0.26N0.22 for the two stages respectively). In contrast, the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage, assimilation, has almost same impact on both performances (β=0.54 and 0.52). This may be explained as below. While non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance includes the components of quality and <sup>fl</sup>exibility, they may play an intermediate role in the <sup>fi</sup>nal realization of <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, such as return on investment and return on sales. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings are similar to the concept of the balance scorecard (BSC) in which non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial perspectives, that is, learning and growth, internal processes, and customer, lay basic foundations for the achievement of <sup>fi</sup>nancial goals [31]. Moreover, the explained variances $( R ^ { 2 } )$ for <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances among the three diffusion stages both indicate a soundly increasing pattern. This also explains that <sup>fi</sup>nancial or non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance may be realized over an extended period of time, that is, associated with the impact of time lag. Prior research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed similar arguments for performance realization [11–13].

## 5.2. Subconstructs in reflecting the main constructs

First, relative advantage is an important element in forming technological structure across all the three diffusion stages. Complexity was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in determining technological structure at the adoption stage, but not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the implementation and assimilation stages. The reasons may be discussed as below. When beginning users try to use a new information system like e-SCM, perceived ease of use (complexity) plays an important role in affecting their intention to use it. As IS innovation diffuses in progress and users become more deeply involved, perceived ease of use increasingly becomes less in<sup>fl</sup>uential. Perceived usefulness (relative advantage) is always important for users in providing useful information to support their decisions and regular work. In addition, ability to provide security is also a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant component in forming technological structure across all the three diffusion stages. This is because e-SCM builds on an internet-based and open network for the purpose of sharing information among trading partners. Greater levels of collaboration signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly expose sensitive and important information to potential risk and thus suggest that a greater emphasis should be placed on information security.

Next, peer pressure is a critical element in forming collaborative structure across all the three diffusion stages. If key competitors or suppliers use e-SCM to successfully achieve their goals, the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the arena will certainly respond to the pressure by deploying new technologies; in particular, their supply chain operations which are closely tied to those of trading partners. Researchers have well cited its importance for IOS diffusion [51,56,57]. Transaction climate is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in determining collaborative structure at the adoption and implementation stages and not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the assimilation stage. This may be explained by the importance of the transaction climate at the earlier diffusion stages for initially building a high level of mutual trust and faith among trading partners for coordinating relevant business activities without impediment. When the relationships among participants have evolutionarily become deeper and stable, the importance of the transaction climate gradually decreases and partners' focus is shifted to other important antecedents, such as usefulness and information security.

Environmental uncertainty demonstrates a similar role as the transaction climate in determining collaborative structure. Environmental uncertainty is initially the major concern for pushing supply chain partners to quickly build their cooperating network. After a certain level of alleviation of environmental uncertainty due to the deployment of e-SCM, its importance declines progressively for switching to other important antecedents. Finally, supplier interdependence was found to be the major indicator in forming collaborative structure at the assimilation stage while the other two diffusion stages are less relevant to the determining role of this indicator. This may be due to the important role of time lag in well establishing long-term relationships of supplier interdependence. Long-term relationships among trading partners are not built at the earlier diffusion stages until they have worked together for an extended period of time and have committed themselves to a common goal.

## 6. Conclusions and suggestions

More organizations are adopting e-SCM in their supply chain operations. Building on IDT, SCM research, and IT value research, this research empirically investigates the impact of two external antecedents, technological and collaborative structures, on e-SCM diffusion, and in turn, <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances. Moreover, according to the evolutionary view of IDT, a multi-stage analysis provides better insight for understanding the dynamic nature of e-SCM diffusion among trading partners. Important <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been reported in this study. First, the two external antecedents both present a greater impact on the adoption stage than on the implementation and assimilation stages in e-SCM diffusion. In particular, the technological structure is a more important indicator for the adoption stage while the collaborative structure is more important for the implementation and assimilation stages. Next, the three diffusion stages play an important role in impacting both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performances, except for the linkage between the adoption stage and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. In particular, earlier diffusion stages have a greater impact on non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance and later diffusion stages have a greater impact on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. This stage-based approach associated with different diffusion stages has provided deep insight into the e-SCM diffusion.

The implications for practitioners are noted below. Firstly, for the relationships between external antecedents and diffusion stages, when <sup>fi</sup>rms and their partners are planning to initiate e-SCM deployment, their focus should be on the design of supply chain technologies. Important considerations for the technology design include perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and ability to provide security. Once <sup>fi</sup>rms and their partners have deployed their e-SCM and are ready for moving to further diffusion stages, building good collaborative relationships should be the <sup>fi</sup>rst priority such that supply chain networks can be used effectively and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently.

Important attributes for building the collaborative relationships include peer pressure, transaction climate, environmental uncertainty, and supplier interdependence.

Secondly, concerning the relationships between diffusion stages and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, because e-SCM diffusion is an evolutionary process and may need an extended time to fully realize <sup>fi</sup>nal <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, alternatively, non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance should be considered during the early and middle stages of the diffusion process. These non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial performance indicators are usually realized at the earlier stages and lay foundations for the further achievement of <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. Eventually, both non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performances are fully realized at the assimilation stage. Practitioners should recognize these facts and therefore plan their management of e-SCM diffusion effectively.

The implications for researchers are discussed below. Previous research has often discussed the technology implementation issue from a single decision view of adoption or acceptance and has traditionally used only a <sup>fi</sup>nancial perspective, which has often caused inconsistent performance <sup>fi</sup>ndings. This study has used both a multi-stage diffusion approach and a complete set of performance measures. Working on this approach, this research has proposed a novel framework for e-SCM and has empirically demonstrated its powerful capability to explain the problems of implementation and performance assessment. While the problem of the IT productivity paradox is mainly attributed to the time-lag effect, mismanagement of performance measures, and mismeasurement [9,13], this research has provided additional evidence in explaining these issues. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings for different performance focuses from different diffusion stages explain both the time-lag effect and mismanagement in performance measures. The use of both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial measures and the particular <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study explain the mismeasurement which results from using a single measure or separating it from other organizational practices. Next, this research adopts the analysis of a second-order structural model to provide more insight into the main constructs. Thus, we are further able to discuss the determining power of these subconstracts on the main constructs.

Furthermore, subsequent research could be based on this foundation. First, this research proposes a theoretical framework concerning the relationships of external structure, e-SCM diffusion, and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, and further empirically validates it by a largesample survey. Future research could conduct a case study longitudinally to understand the impact of this framework in depth. Second, since the study sample was selected from a combination of various industries, the conclusions are more general and comprehensive. Follow-up research could be targeted toward particular industries, for instance, the high-tech electronics industry, to understand their differences and similarities. This would provide more insight into using e-SCM in that particular industry. Besides, of the objectives in this research is to understand the impact of e-SCM diffusion on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The BSC is also considered to be an important performance evaluation model, and future research could try to examine <sup>fi</sup>rm performance by using it.

Finally, although this research has produced some useful results, a number of limitations may be inherent in it. First, the response rate was lower than desirable, despite the various efforts to improve it. This might be due to lack of relevant experience in considering stage-based e-SCM diffusion. However, the responding sample indicates no systematic non-response bias. Next, while the respondents were originally designed for vice presidents or manufacturing executives in a <sup>fi</sup>rm, there were approximately 25% of the respondents with senior staff. Although this would more or less produce different views in experiencing e-SCM, it could also increase the diversity of data sources from multiple informants since they experience e-SCM diffusion differently, and therefore increase the variance in the variables of interest. In addition, while senior managers of larger <sup>fi</sup>rms are always busy, some of the questionnaires might have been completed by subordinates and therefore, the data may have some biases.
