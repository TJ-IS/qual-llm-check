# Managing metadata: Networks of practice, technological frames, and metadata work in a digital library

Michael Khoo ⁎, Catherine Hall

The iSchool at Drexel University, 3141 Chestnut Street, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 28 June 2011 Received in revised form 8 January 2013 Accepted 12 January 2013 Available online 29 March 2013

## a b s t r a c t

The organizations that develop information systems are usually composed of members and groups with different technological backgrounds and experiences. While these different backgrounds are necessary to support the many dimensions of information system development, at the same time they can result in barriers to sharing organizational knowledge, and can thus impede this work. Understanding how the technological backgrounds of organizational groups are constituted and mediated thus provides useful insight into how information system development occurs in organizational contexts. This article contributes to this discussion with a qualitative and interpretive case study of a small team engaged in creating metadata for a digital library. A number of unexpected and recalcitrant problems were encountered that delayed this metadata work. Drawing on theories of networks of practice, technological frames, and perspective making and perspective taking, the article uses ethnographic- and action research-based interviews, to probe project members' understanding of metadata. The analysis identi<sup>fi</sup>ed different networks of practice and technological frames in the project, including the IT workers, who had a systems administration perspective, and the faculty members, who had a theoretical research perspective. The tensions caused by these differences are described, as are the ways in which the project resolved them. Two <sup>fi</sup>ndings are that the intercommunal negotiation was established not just between individual networks of practice in the project, but with reference to an emerging community of practice that served as a boundary object, and that intercommunal negotiation also had to be carried out diachronically across time, with this latter form of negotiation being dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems are increasingly complex phenomena, and the development of information systems is a complex activity. In any organization, multiple groups of developers, designers, users, and others, may be involved in the development of diverse aspects of information systems. As the number of groups in this technological work grows, so does the number of relationships between them, and the amount of time that has to be devoted to organizational communication between these groups. Communication is especially important if there are differences in technical backgrounds and expertise between different groups, and understanding how these differences affect organizational communication and work is a useful contribution towards understanding technical organizational work in general. These issues are examined in this article through a study of technical work, analyzed from the point of view of Brown and Duguid's (2001) theory of networks of practice, Orlikowski and Gash's (1994) theory of technological frames, and Boland and Tenkasi's (1995) model of perspective making and perspective taking.

The article describes a small voluntary team engaged in the development and operation of an educational digital library, the Internet Public Library (ipl2: http://ipl.org/) (the authors were members of the project team). Digital libraries like ipl2 are “online collection[s] of digital objects, of assured quality, that are created or collected and managed according to internationally accepted principles for collection development and made accessible in a coherent and sustainable manner, supported by services necessary to allow users to retrieve and exploit the resources” (IFLA/UNESCO, 2011). In the case of ipl2, the library's resources consist mainly of links to Web pages on a variety of topics, either created by ipl2, or hosted elsewhere on the Web. The library is aimed especially at K-12 students and teachers (primary and secondary school audiences in the U.K. context). It is no good building a digital library full of resources, if the library's users cannot <sup>fi</sup>nd those resources. Digital library resources are therefore described with metadata (‘data about data’; Gilliland, 2008), in the form of catalog records. Examples of the types of metadata used in ipl2 include the title of a resource, its description, its URL. the subiects of the resource, and so on, and this metadata helps users to search ipl2 for resources. Part of the work in ipl2 involved the creation of metadata, and while digital librarians strive to create good quality metadata, this is often not an easy task (Greenberg, 2010). In the case of ipl2, a number of problems were encountered that delayed the metadata work. These problems were often recalcitrant and unexpected, which led to them being labeled as ‘surprises’ by the project team.

Both for theoretical reasons (to understand better why they were occurring on the <sup>fi</sup>rst place), and for practical reasons (to make the ipl2 metadata work more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient), it was decided to investigate these surprises further. Two sets of theoretical and practical research questions guided this inquiry:

• To what extent were networks of practice and technological frames present in ipl2? What were the characteristics of these networks of practice and technological frames? How did these phenomena affect the metadata work in the project?

• How could ipl2 organizational processes be improved, to support intercommunal negotiation, and to support the metadata work?

These research questions were operationalized in an investigation that was informed by the authors' ongoing experience working with the project, and which used a series of semi-structured interviews to probe project members' understandings of metadata and ipl2's organizational processes. The analysis identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several different networks of practice and technological frames in the project, which were affecting the metadata work. Changes were subsequently made to ipl2's organizational processes that partly succeeded in making the metadata work more productive.

Section 2 of this paper introduces the theoretical approaches that will be used in the analysis; Section 3 provides some background on digital libraries and metadata: Section 4 describes the data collection: Section 5 describes the data analysis; Section 6 discusses the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in terms of the theoretical framework, and outlines some of the ways in which the <sup>fi</sup>ndings were folded back into the project; and Section 7 outlines some limitations and possible future directions.

## 2. Theoretical framework

One of the aims of this paper is to gain a better general understanding of the issues involved in sharing and making sense of complex technical knowledge and practices between groups in a small organization.

This section introduces several useful theoretical approaches to such issues, including networks of practice, technological frames, and perspective making and taking.

## 2.1. Networks of practice

Networks of practice are loose and extended epistemic groups that share similarities in knowledge and practices. The concept is situated within the wider context of a rich <sup>fi</sup>eld of inquiry into how knowledge is constituted and shared amongst individuals and groups in organizations (the concept is loosely similar to, but also distinct from, concepts such as communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998), communities of knowing (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995), occupational communities (Bechky, 2003; Orr, 1996; Van Maanen & Barley, 1984), and others.) Networks of practice are introduced by Brown and Duguid (2001) in their theoretical article Knowledge and Organization: A Social Practice Perspective, where it is used to explain two apparently contradictory characteristics of organizational knowledge. The <sup>fi</sup>rst characteristic is that knowledge has a tendency to be mobile, for instance by moving beyond organizational settings in which it is required (such as knowledge of the graphical user interface from Xerox to Apple to Microsoft); such knowledge is characterized as ‘leaky.’ The second characteristic is that knowledge has a tendency to be not mobile enough (for example, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to share between the different sectors of an organization); such knowledge is characterized as ‘sticky.’ To explain this dualism, Brown and Duguid draw on theories of social worlds (Strauss, 1978), epistemic cultures (Knorr-Cetina, 1999), public knowledge (Ziman, 1967), embeddedness (Giddens, 1990), and communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998), to develop a model of “extended epistemic networks,” or networks of practice. Organizational knowledge is not a substance or thing, but a practice that is mutually constituted, perpetuated, and sustained, within social interaction (c.f. Duguid, 2005). It is thus at least as relevant to speak of networks of practice as supporting the sharing of practices, as it is to speak of them supporting the sharing of knowledge.

Practice creates a common substrate. With the term network, we also want to suggest that relations among network members are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly looser than those within a community of practice. As Strauss indicates, and unlike in communities of practice, most of the people within such a network will never know, know of, or come across one another. And yet they are capable of sharing a great deal of knowledge. (Brown & Duguid, 2001, p. 205)

Networks of practice can be used to explain leaky and sticky knowledge. First, the members of networks of practice share common world-views, which allow them to share knowledge (and practices) across organizational boundaries. Second, at the same time, members of networks of practice have to share world views with members of other networks of practice, but the practice-based nature of this knowledge means that networks of practice can also inhibit knowledge sharing in an organization. Brown and Duguid then discuss how organizations can manage and coordinate networks of practice to overcome these issues. Hierarchical control can be used, although there is a corresponding cost here in terms of capacity for innovation. Brown and Duguid recommend instead a process they call intercommunal negotiation, which involves the ongoing translation of knowledge, and re-alignment of perspectives, between networks of practice. Intercommunal negotiation is supported through the use of boundary spanners (organizational members with background in multiple domains who can translate between those domains), and also through the use of boundary objects, informational objects which inhabit multiple social worlds and which also satisfy the informational reguirements of each of these worlds (Star & Griesemer, 1989). For Brown and Duguid, boundary objects include such things as “shared documents, tools, business processes, objectives, schedules, and the like” that can be used to articulate practices, to signal occasions of conceptual difference, and to offer grounds for resolving these differences. They conclude that while the use of intercommunal negotiation in integrating networks of practice may appear to be a “vague or anodyne” strategy, it is also “profoundly different from conventional organizational coordination” (p. 209).

Studies of networks of practice have looked at how these loosely articulated networks learn, adapt, and function within and across organizations. Examples include Yamauchi and Swanson (2010), who analyze the diffusion of information systems from core to periphery in a distributed organization, and advance the idea of ‘familiarity pockets’ that users use to orient their use of enterprise-wide systems to local needs and practices.

Vaast and Walsham (2009) describe ‘trans-situated learning’ and network formation within a network of practice, in the context of the implementation of a Web-based information system in the health industry, and look at the co-emergence of the network and its supporting information infrastructure. Wasko and Faraj (2005) report the motivations and incentives for participants to share information in online discussion boards. The nature of intercommunal negotiation between networks of practice is less studied, although Sapsed and Salter (2004) discuss the use of project management tools, including timeline and deadline tools and visualizations, as forms of boundary objects in a large distributed project. They argue that the peripheral nature of these boundary objects between different networks of practice in the project contributed to their being used less, and that boundary object use can essentially be a zero sum game, with greater relevance for one network leading to decreased relevance for other networks

## 2.2. Technological frames

Intercommunal negotiation in technical work includes the negotiation of technological knowledge. A useful approach for understanding how technical knowledge is constituted and shared in and between groups is Orlikowski and Gash's (1994) theory of technological frames. This approach synthesizes a number of prior approaches, including Goffman's (1974) theory of frames, Giddens (1984) theory of structuration, Gioia's (1986) theory of sensemaking, and Bijker's (1987) model of technological frames, to build a model of technological frames as shared tacit cognitive frames of reference, which underlie and inform individual and collective perceptions of technology:

To interact with technology, people have to make sense of it; and in this sense-making process, they develop particular assumptions, expectations, and knowledge of the technology, which then serve to shape subsequent actions toward it. While these interpretations become taken-for-granted and are rarely brought to the surface and re<sup>fl</sup>ected on, they nevertheless remain signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing how actors in organizations think about and act toward technology. (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994, p. 175)

Technological frames are constituted partly in relation to work practices. In the case of information systems in organizations, for example, the associated frames of managers, IT workers, and users may be different. While the technological frames of the members of a group will not be identical, they bear ‘family resemblances’ to each other, based on core assumptions, knowledge, and expectations that are shared with other group members (c.f. Wittgenstein, 1974). Different groups with different work practices can therefore frame the same technology in different ways. Orlikowski and Gash describe these differences in terms of ‘congruence.’ Congruent frames, while not identical, provide similar expectations of a technology, while incongruent frames imply “important differences in expectations, assumptions, or knowledge about some key aspects of technology,” and where incongruent technological frames exist, therefore, “organizations are likely to experience dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and con<sup>fl</sup>icts” (p. 180). Orlikowski and Gash's case study is of the implementation of Lotus Notes in a large professional services <sup>fi</sup>rm. Using interviews and observations, they found that the technologists and IT workers emphasized the abstract technological power of Notes to increase organizational creativity, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and productivity, and assumed that users would easily incorporate Notes into their existing work practices. Users' frames, on the other hand, were less technologically oriented, and more skeptical of the power and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Notes. They were less con<sup>fi</sup>dent in their ability to just start using Notes, and concerned about the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and intellectual property issues involved in using an enterprise knowledge base, especially for knowledge work that was considered at times to be intensely individual. These differences in frames hindered the implementation, and Orlikowski and Gash recommend that “early articulation, re<sup>fl</sup>ection, discussion, [and] negotiation” of frames can “reduce the likelihood of unintended misunderstandings and delusions around the implementation and use of a new information technology.”

Subsequent studies of technological frames have often been <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained examinations of how congruent and incongruent frames support or interfere with information system use in organizational settings (Davidson, 2006). Examples include those of hospital staff adapting to a new patient record system (Karsten & Laine, 2007), software implementation in a public sector organization (Yeow & Sia, 2008), and farmers, outreach of<sup>fi</sup>cers, and scientists implementing a new agricultural decision support system (Jakku & Thorburn, 2010). Common outputs of these studies are descriptions of the different groups in the setting concerned, the different ways in which they can frame the same information system, and the outcomes of interactions between these frames. Two studies of technological frames report <sup>fi</sup>ndings relevant for the present article. McGovern and Hicks (2004) studied IT implementation in a UK cable manufacturer by an academic research team, in which the academics both studied and supported the implementation. In this case, the academics saw the company partly as a research site for data gathering, while the managing director saw the researchers as consultants, and became frustrated with the latter's lengthy requirements gathering processes; and a similar tension between ‘research work’ and ‘operational work’ was found in ipl2. Khoo (2005) describes technological frames found in a study of educators and digital librarians developing a digital library. Here, the educators thought of the digital library as an online version of a school library, while the digital librarians thought of it as an information system with some library-like characteristics, and these differences led to ongoing misunderstandings of the project's metadata work<sup>fl</sup>ow.

## 2.3. Perspective making and perspective taking

While negotiation is required between networks practice, and also between technological frames, neither Brown and Duguid, nor Orlikowski and Gash, describe in detail what such negotiation might entail, or the organizational conditions, tools, processes, etc., which can support it, or the barriers that may be encountered. Some requirements can be inferred however from the fact that meaning, as it is described in both approaches, is a complex phenomenon constituted in ongoing social practice. One useful approach to understanding the character of intra-organizational negotiation and communication is therefore Boland and Tenkasi's (1995) theory of perspective making and perspective taking. Drawing on (amongst others) Lave and Wenger (1991), and Brown and Duguid (1991), Boland and Tenkasi model organizations in terms of overlapping communities of knowing. A community of knowing is a group of experts that has specialized knowledge that supports the overall goals of the organization. Communities of knowing represent their knowledge in terms of perspectives. Perspectives are maintained by individual community's practices, while new perspectives and knowledge are created by taking into account and responding to the perspectives of other communities.

Following Wittgenstein (1974), perspectives are generally based on a community's ‘form of life’ and ‘language-games,’ in which the meaning in language lies not in abstract ostensive de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, but in everyday use. Language is intimately connected with practice, and to become familiar with the meanings in a language-game, one has to become familiar with the form of life associated with that language-game. A community of knowing therefore communicates its knowledge with other communities not just by de<sup>fi</sup>ning concepts, but through active re<sup>fl</sup>ection, and ‘perspective making’ (creating, maintaining, and expressing their ideas to other communities) and ‘perspective taking’ (recognizing and interpreting the perspectives of other communities). In cases where perspectives are incommensurable (c.f. Kuhn, 1996), communication between groups has to be particularly rich, longitudinal, supported (for instance) by forums that allow for the re<sup>fl</sup>exive construction of community narratives (Bruner, 1990), and the use of boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989). In general, however, organizational communication of ‘deep knowledge’ between different groups with different social worlds and thought worlds, is not easy (Majchrzak, More, & Faraj, 2012).

The case study in this article examines these relationships and processes in the case of metadata work in a digital library, the Internet Public Library (ipl2). This work encountered a number of dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and delays. It will be argued that these delays were caused partly by the fact that project staff were members of different networks of practice, each of which had its own world-view and technological frame(s), as well as by the largely undocumented history of the project. ## 3. Digital libraries and metadata

A digital library is a “managed collection of information, with associated services, where the information is stored in digital formats and accessible over a network” (Arms, 2000). With its emphasis on networked access to indexed electronic documents, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a digital library has been technically feasible only since the beginning of the 1990s, and the development of the Internet, and HTML and Web browsers (Arms, 2000; Candela et al., 2007; Tedd & Large, 2005). Digital libraries now provide Internet access to a wide range of educational and scholarly resources (web pages, written documents, images, videos, etc.) for students, instructors, researchers, scholars, librarians and others. A simple conceptual model for a digital library is shown in Fig. 2. It shows a database that stores the library metadata, an index of that database, a search engine, and a Web site. Patrons interact with the library through its Web site, and either enter search terms, or browse Web pages, in order to locate the resources that they want. Rapidly changing computer and Internet technologies, including Web 2.0 and social networks, have led to increasingly sophisticated visions for digital libraries. The European DELOS network, for instance, now envisages a digital library “as a tool at the centre of intellectual activity having no logical, conceptual, physical, temporal or personal borders or barriers on information. It has moved from a content-centric system that simply organises and provides access to particular collections of data and information to a person-centric system that aims to provide interesting, novel, personalised experiences to users” (Candela et al., 2007).

Digital libraries can also be considered as examples of information infrastructure, “large-scale ensembles of hardware, software, people, and practices, which support the sharing of information, and which display adaptive and dynamic growth” (Borgman, 2007). Examples of information infrastructures include cyberinfrastructure, which connects scientists, researchers, educators, and others (Edwards, Jackson, Bowker, & Knobel, 2007; National Science Foundation, 2003, 2007), and cyberlearning, which connects educators, pedagogists, and students (National Science Foundation, 2008). Like other forms of infrastructure (Hughes, 1983), information infrastructure is a sociotechnical system, which use standards to shape technological components and social practices, which in turn interact to continually (re)constitute the infrastructure (Bowker & Star, 1999; Star & Ruhleder. 1996). Many information structures are relatively new and emergent, with the dynamics between standards, technologies and practices being <sup>fl</sup>uid and dynamic, and open to ongoing contestation (Bowker & Star, 1999; Edwards, Mayernik, Batcheller, Bowker, & Borgman, 2011; Edwards et al., 2007; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Tilson, Lyytinen, & Sørenson, 2010).

![](/api/attachments/HJCHXMMB/fulltext/images/8f6271a600b3c5570a188de4efbdc8291ea63f6e2dc0c84dad185252f26f19a1.jpg)  
Fig. 2. A model of a simple digital library.

The Internet Public Library (“ipl2”) (http://www.ipl.org/) is an example of a digital library. It was founded in 1995 as an online reference service. Soon after, it began to develop collections of Web resources to support this reference service, and it has since developed into a version of the digital library portrayed in Fig. 2, with a database with catalog records, a search engine, and a website (there are a number of other functions of the ipl2 that will not be discussed here.) Visitors to ipl2 can either enter a term into the search box on the front page and get taken to a page of search results, or browse through the collection pages to locate speci<sup>fi</sup>c resource. ipl2 has a catalog of approximately 40,000 records and a web site of approximately 12,500 pages, and at the time of writing it received approximately 800,000 visits and 2.2 million page views per month. As the users are remote, it is not known for certain who they are, but we can infer from annual patterns of use, including lower volumes of use during school vacations, that many of them are from a K-12 audience (students, teachers, parents, etc.).

In 2008. the servers were transferred to a new university, and work began on the next version of the library. The aim was to develop a new look for the site and to provide Web 2.0 services such as user accounts and personal collections. Part of this work included improving the existing metadata.

## 3.1. Metadata and metadata work

Metadata is commonly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “data about data.” More precisely it is “the sum total of what one can say about any information object at any level of aggregation. In this context, an information object is anything that can be addressed and manipulated as a discrete entity by a human being or an information system” (Gilliland, 2008; emphasis in original). There are different types and formats of metadata, which are used to help manage information, and information systems, in a variety of contexts. NISO (2004) recognizes three general types of metadata:

Descriptive metadata describes a resource for purposes such as discovery and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. It can include elements such as title, abstract, author, and keywords

Structural metadata indicates how compound objects are put together, for example, how pages are ordered to form chapters.

Administrative metadata provides information to help manage a resource, such as when and how it was created, <sup>fi</sup>le type and other technical information, and who can access it. (NISO, 2004, p. 1)

Administrative data can include ‘rights management metadata,’ which deals with intellectual property rights, and ‘preservation metadata,’ which contains information needed to archive and preserve a resource (NISO, 2004). The types of metadata that are the focus of this study is primarily descriptive metadata, ‘metadata that supports resource discovery’ (Greenberg, 2010), in this case the discovery of Web sites cataloged in a digital library. Descriptive metadata is used in many different contexts, including: searching an online library catalog for a book (the metadata here includes names of authors, and titles of books); searching an email account for messages from a particular person (metadata includes the names of correspondents, date of email, and size of attachments); and booking a <sup>fl</sup>ight online (the metadata here includes dates and places of travel). A hypothetical example of a metadata record for a book is shown in Fig. 3a. This shows the book, a catalog card that describes the book, and an idealized electronic database version of the same record In the database record, categories such as author, title, and date of publication, have been added as columns, and the associated information (author name, and so on) has been added to each row. Good quality metadata helps users to <sup>fi</sup>nd what they want in a catalog or database, while poor quality metadata can lead to poor search results and dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed users (Barton, Currier, & Hey, 2003; Beall, 2004; Bruce & Hillmann, 2004; Moen, Miksa, Eklund, Polyakov, & Snyder, 2006; Ward, 2003; Wilson, 2007).

Digital library metadata supports the categorization, organization and sharing of digital resources and collections, and it is available in different standards and formats (Greenberg, 2010). Different metadata standards and formats can support the information needs of different communities of users, by allowing the description of digital objects and resources in terms that are relevant to those communities. While individual standards and formats are useful for specific communities. however, mixing different metadata standards in a single digital library or system of digital libraries will reduce the overall usefulness of the combined metadata (Arms & Arms, 2004; Gazan, 2003; Shreeves et al., 2005). On the other hand, if digital libraries share a common metadata format, they can provide access to each other's resources. In Fig. 2, for instance, Alice and Bob are using Digital Library 1, while Carol is using Digital Library 2; and if Digital Libraries 1 and 2 share a metadata format, then Alice, Bob, and Carol can search for and retrieve resources from both digital libraries.

Standardized metadata allows a digital library to 'plug into' wider information infrastructures (Fox. 2003; Ribes & Bowker, 2009). If a digital library does not already follow an existing metadata standard, then a library has to ‘map’ and ‘crosswalk’ its existing metadata to that standard. Mapping involves “the intellectual activity of comparing and analyzing two or more metadata schemas,” in which the <sup>fi</sup>elds in two sets of metadata are compared and the best matches are sought between each <sup>fi</sup>eld, while a crosswalk is a “visual and textual product of the mapping process” (Woodley, 2008). An example of a mapping and crosswalk is shown in Fig. 3b; the bold headings at the top of the refer to different metadata formats, while each horizontal line shows how similar metadata is expressed in the same <sup>fi</sup>eld in each format. In an ideal world, old and new metadata formats would exhibit a one-to-one relationship. For instance, the old format would have a ‘Title' field with the title of the resource, and the new format would also have a ‘Title' <sup>fi</sup>eld, and all that would be required would be to take the metadata from the old ‘Title’ <sup>fi</sup>eld and place it in the new Title' field. In practice. there may be considerable mismatches between metadata formats and <sup>fi</sup>elds. A mundane analogy is to think of moving to a new house. Ideally, one could take all one's possessions in the old house and move them to the same locations in the new house (kitchen objects from the old kitchen to the new kitchen, plates from the old plate shelf to the new plate shelf, etc.). In practice, every house is different, and some objects will end up in different places in the new house.

Designing and implementing mappings and crosswalks is a non-trivial tasks. Technically, the complex structural differences between metadata formats can result in a variety of mismatches between formats, which can be hard to resolve (Woodley, 2008). In addition, metadata work itself is not necessarily easy. Importantly, as Greenberg (2010) points out, this work is, because of the rapidly increasing numbers of resources that require description with metadata, increasingly carried out by those without formal professional training in metadata theory and practice. ## 3.2. Metadata work in ipl2

This study focuses on the creation and use of a new metadata format for ipl2. The first version of the library (dating from 1995) had developed its own metadata format from scratch. As part of ongoing work to develop a new version of the library, it was decided to adopt a more common metadata standard, Dublin Core. Dublin Core was chosen as a metadata format as it was perceived to offer simplicity and <sup>fl</sup>exibility in terms of elements and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, and it would also facilitate sharing ipl2 metadata with other digital libraries. The metadata work in ipl2 therefore involved, <sup>fi</sup>rst, crosswalking ipl2 metadata from the original custom format to Dublin Core, and second, moving that metadata from a MySQL database to a new Fedora database (Extensible Digital Object Repository Architecture: http://www.fedora-commons. org/). As this initial project progressed, ipl2 began a collaboration with a second digital library, the Librarians' Internet Index (LII), and when LII lost its funding, it was decided to add LII's resources to the ipl2 collections, and to crosswalk the LII metadata to Dublin Core and also add it to the Fedora database.

This metadata work was complicated. Some tasks, such as creating an inventory of existing ipl2 metadata <sup>fi</sup>elds, identifying little-used <sup>fi</sup>elds, and deciding which DC metadata elements to crosswalk metadata <sup>fi</sup>elds to, were semi-predictable and could be carried out in a reasonably systematic fashion. However, crosscutting these tasks was a series of unexpected problems that the project team began to refer to as ‘surprises.’ A surprise often took the following form. The project team would be working on a metadata task that would appear to be relatively simple, but which would require a growing length of time to complete. After a while it would be realized that the team was working with one or more misconceptions of the metadata issue in question. These problems were often interdependent and regressive, with the resolution of problem A turning out to depend on understanding problem B, which in turn required addressing problem C, and so on (Khoo & Hall, 2010). Five examples of surprises are as follows.

First, it was assumed that all the resources in ipl2 would have a catalog record. However, while all ipl2 collections had collection-level records, individual items in some collections lacked item-level metadata. These latter resources were therefore not discoverable through the ipl2 search engine, and web metrics indicated that users were in fact <sup>fi</sup>nding these items through Google's index of the site. Second, the original metadata records contained a number of specialized elements that were rarely used (c.f. Moen et al., 2006); for example, the ‘Resource Title’ element included Main Title (a reasonable match for the dc:title <sup>fi</sup>eld in Dublin Core) but also elements such as Former Title, Sort Title, Acronym, Alternate Title, and Alternate Spelling. There was considerable discussion regarding what to do with the metadata in these rarely used elements. Third, there were several highly specialized and idiosyncratic <sup>fi</sup>elds. For instance, the metadata for one ipl2 collection had a ‘body’ <sup>fi</sup>eld, which contained the HTML code necessary to display the web page of the resource itself. Fourth, there were problems with the browsing metadata. Both ipl2 and the LII could be browsed by clicking on a series of subject headings to drill down into the collections to reach speci<sup>fi</sup>c subjects and resources, and the team wanted a common browsing structure that could be represented in the new ipl2 metadata. Both libraries appeared to have a similar subject hierarchy, and so it was assumed that it would be relatively easy to map LII subjects onto the ipl2 subject hierarchy. However, it turned out that neither library had applied controlled vocabularies and subject term relationships in a consistent fashion, and both the browsing structures in both libraries had a number of network-like elements in which (for example) topics and sub-topics were linked in circular ways. In a <sup>fi</sup>nal example, it was assumed that the metadata would be stored in the same database and in the same format; however, some ipl2 collections were stored in a separate FileMaker Pro database, in a different metadata format, and it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to design a separate crosswalk for this latter metadata.

## 3.3. Understanding surprises

The surprises in the ipl2 metadata work caused considerable delays in the project, and consistently confounded the expectations of the project team. To further understand these issues, and to gain a wider general understanding of technological work in small organizations, as well as to contribute to organizational analyses of metadata work, it was decided to carry out a qualitative and interpretative analysis of the organizational dimensions of metadata work in ipl2. As noted above, there have been several studies of metadata work that have analyzed various aspects of this work from organizational perspectives. However, there are no studies framed within the speci<sup>fi</sup>c theoretical approach introduced in the previous section, which combines networks of practice, technological frames, and perspective making and perspective taking (although Khoo, 2005, provides a study that employs a technological frames approach to metadata work). As introduced above, therefore, the two general research questions (theoretical and practical) that guided the inquiry were:

• To what extent were networks of practice and technological frames present in ipl2? What were the characteristics of these networks of practice and technological frames? How did these phenomena affect the metadata work in the project?

• How could ipl2 organizational processes be improved, to support intercommunal negotiation, and to support the metadata work?

## 4. Data collection and analysis

A starting assumption of the study was that the ipl2 project members were members of different networks of practice, and had different technological frames of the project. To investigate this assumption, and to elicit the networks and frames, a qualitative and interpretive approach combining elements of ethnography and action research was adopted.

Ethnographers observe the cultures, social worlds and in situ practices of groups and collectives over time (Calhoun, 2002; Monaghan & Just, 2000). Examples of ethnographic approaches to the study of information systems include D'Mello and Sahay's (2007) study of an Indian software company, Jackson's (2011) study of a higher education institution attempting to implement a virtual learning environment, and Vaast and Walsham's (2005) structurational study of intranet implementation in an occupational network in an insurance company. The study described in this article is shaped by the interpretive anthropological approach (Geertz, 1977), which pays attention to the webs of meaning that constitute both ‘models of’ and ‘models for the everyday practices of the members of a culture, and emphasizes the building up of layered ‘thick descriptions’ of the <sup>fi</sup>eld site (Geertz, 1977).

Action researchers study and shape change in organizations through theory building, intervention and action-taking (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999). Their investigations and outcomes are dialectically constituted in longitudinal interactions between researchers and research subjects (Avison, Lau, Myers, & Nielsen, 1999; cited by Simonsen, 2009). These processes are complex, and so action research can take different forms, and produce different outputs. Mathiassen, Chiasson, and Germonprez (2012) carried out a content analysis of 83 action research studies published between 1982 and 2009, and derived a typology of three action research ‘composition styles’ present in the majority of the reports, namely premise style, inference style, and contribution style. The premise style of an action research study describes the overall types of arguments advanced by the article, including the balance between theoretical and practical arguments. The inference style describes how evidence is collected and analyzed, usually either inductively or deductively. The premise and inference styles combine to produce a third style, the contribution style, which includes experience reports (practical <sup>fi</sup>ndings for IS design and practice), field studies (contributions to speci<sup>fi</sup>c frameworks), theoretical development (contributions to wider frameworks and meta-theory), problem-solving methods (contributions to organizational problem solving within IS as a discipline), and research methods (contributions to action research as a method).

There are tensions between these ethnography and action research. Ethnography is concerned not to intervene during data gathering in ways that affect the analysis (although in applied ethnography intervention does occur after the analysis) (Monaghan & Just, 2000). Action research, in contrast, emphasizes the ongoing engagement of the researcher in the <sup>fi</sup>eld site (Susman & Evered, 1978; Wastell, Kawalek, Langmead-Jones, & Ormerod, 2004). Ethnographic practice also has complex cultural, interpretative and other dimensions, which can extend the analytical process and add further demands to the already ‘time-consuming and risky’ enterprise of action research (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996; Simonsen, 2009). At the same time, the two approaches can complement each other. Ethnography provides sociocultural insights into organizational practices, while action research provides a useful framework for applying ethnography in organizations. Hartmann, Fischer, and Haymaker (2009) describe an iterative ethnographic-action research cycle in which ethnography is used to gain an “in-depth understanding about local project routines from the [viewpoints of] project team members,” and support ongoing information system development and implementation, in turn generating a <sup>fi</sup>eld site for further rounds of ethnographic inquiry (see also Tacchi, Slater, & Hearn, 2003). Such an iterative cycle ensures that any theoretical research questions are not subordinated to the needs of organizations (such as ipl2); theoretical development and practical intervention may each be emphasized at different points, but ultimately, over the whole cycle, both are necessary contributions.

The study described in the rest of this paper is therefore an ethnographic exploration of ipl2 members' understandings of metadata, aimed at generating action research recommendations for the project. In terms of premise style (Mathiassen et al., 2012) it seeks to integrate challenges in IS practice (in this case with the metadata work), with challenges in IS theory (in this case the integration of networks of practice, technological frames, and perspective making and taking). In terms of inference style, it follows an inductive approach, seeking solutions through a grounded analysis of qualitative data, and folding those <sup>fi</sup>ndings back into a problem-solving cycle with the project. Finally, in terms of contributions, the study provides speci<sup>fi</sup>c outcomes in terms of experience reports, <sup>fi</sup>eld studies, and theoretical developments.

The main data for the study were collected in ten semi-structured interviews, designed to probe project members' perceptions (Myers & Newman, 2007; Schultze & Avital, 2011), in this case of metadata work with ipl2 (see Appendix A for the full interview instrument). Excluding the authors, all ipl2 project members directly involved with the metadata work were interviewed (see Fig. 4: the hatched area represents the ipl2 metadata group, the interviewees are represented by boxes with double-line borders and bold type, and the authors are represented by boxes with single-line borders and normal type). The interviews lasted an average of 33 minutes each; seven were held at the of<sup>fi</sup>ce or workplace of the interviewee, two students were interviewed in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce of the <sup>fi</sup>rst author, and one interview was held over the telephone. After each interview, the authors discussed any interesting themes, and the interview sound <sup>fi</sup>les were sent to a transcription service, generating transcripts with approximately 54,000 words, including 37,000 words spoken by interviewees (see Table 1). Printouts of the transcripts were then reviewed and inductively coded to identify themes that addressed the research questions outlined above, by being marked up with colored pens, sticky notes, etc. (the extent to which this was a grounded approach is debatable, as the design of the interviews, and thus the coding strategy, was shaped by a number of prior assumptions regarding metadata — see Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999).

## 5.1. Organizational structures, actors, roles, and processes

ipl2 has a relatively <sup>fl</sup>at and informal organizational structure. Fig. 4 shows the organizational chart iteratively constructed and re<sup>fi</sup>ned with the interviewees (there was otherwise no formal chart). There is an upper management layer of partner universities that provides the <sup>fi</sup>nancial support for ipl2 operations. Below that, there is a pedagogical service layer that includes instructors at partner universities who use ipl2 as a teaching tool in LIS classes (for instance, to train students in cataloging or in digital reference). These two layers are distributed across multiple organizations in the United States. Below that is the research and production layer of the ipl2, headed by the ipl2 director. Most of the people in this layer are based at the same university in the eastern United States, where they work on the ipl2 collections and metadata. They include a <sup>fl</sup>uctuating and voluntary team of 2–3 faculty, 5–6 doctoral students, and 2–3 technical staff, and also the collections manager, who was appointed at the previous university, and moved to the College when the IPL servers moved.

ipl2 work is coordinated through regular meetings, held face-to-face in conference rooms equipped with communication equipment such as a conference telephone, a computer, a projector, a screen, and an Internet connection. Meetings included a review of progress since the last meeting, the distribution of documents, a discussion of problems, and the tasks to be completed by the next meeting. Initial meetings in 2008 were small, but as the project grew, more students, faculty and IT staff began attending. Despite the fact that most members of the team worked in the same building, the only face-to-face contact that project members regularly had as a group was at these meetings. Between meetings, project members scheduled ipl2 tasks into their existing commitments. Project communication was carried out by email, and supported by documents shared through an online document server.

Interviewees were <sup>fi</sup>rst asked to describe their roles in and history with ipl2. Their replies described three overall groups of project members: faculty, IT staff and students. Faculty members were interested in research into librarianship. digital libraries, and metadata, and looked for opportunities to use ipl2 as a test-bed for grants and publications. They were busy with teaching, administration, meetings, and other activities. The IT staff who supported ipl2 were members of the College's regular IT staff, who scheduled ipl2 meetings and tasks into their overall work. The IT staff maintained the ipl2 servers; developed new tools and services, such as the metadata tool; and provided feedback on ideas proposed by the faculty (such as compatibility with existing systems, and security issues). The Ph.D. students were usually assigned to work on ipl2 as research assistants. Their skills included Web development, human–computer interaction, metadata and cataloging, and database programming. The students were busy with classes, their dissertations, and other activities.

These different groups de<sup>fi</sup>ned the purpose of ipl2 in different ways. On one hand, faculty (and to an extent students) viewed ipl2 in terms of research, an open-ended project of inquiry that was evolving as the project itself grew. On the other hand, the IT staff saw ipl2 as something to be maintained in production status, according to <sup>fi</sup>xed requirements and deadlines. These de<sup>fi</sup>nitions could con<sup>fl</sup>ict. The longitudinal and evolving nature of ‘research’ was taken-for-granted by the faculty, was being learned by students, but was at best opaque to the IT staff, who preferred de<sup>fi</sup>nite requirements and form deadlines.

Yeah, my experience with research is relatively new. I'm coming from a corporation where my work was all production all the time; there was no time to do anything else. So it was get this done, get this out the door, and everything was a <sup>fi</sup>re drill, constantly … I'm still trying to wrap my head around how the research world works … it's nice because you're in kind of a safe environment, I guess, until the time comes where you actually have to–I don't know–publish or whatever the end result [of research is]; I'm still not too familiar with the entire process. (IT staff)

## According to the Director:

I think one of the problems was, again, that we didn't differentiate between development and research. So you've got a bunch of faculty members involved, they need to be conducting research, they need to publish … but we [also] had an actual [development] deadline that had to be met … regardless of research questions, or anything else. (ipl2 Director)

While interviewees could quite easily identify the roles they brought to ipl2, they also commented on the ambiguous nature of roles within ipl2, describing them as “vaguely delimited” and “very virtual with certain people,” especially for the students, interns and volunteers, who were described as ‘part-time or less than part-time.’ Meetings, particularly early on, were therefore shaped by the existing backgrounds of the attendees, who focused on the parts of the project that they were interested in:

At <sup>fi</sup>rst it was — what is this? What can we do with this? Everybody is like, kind of reaching into the grab bag and pulling out a piece and saying, “All right, I'll take this piece, I'll take that piece.” But then when it came to the part of putting those new pieces back together, “Oh, I didn't know you were doing that with your piece, 'cause I would have done,” you know? (IT staff member)

There was confusion over reporting lines. As Ph.D. student described, “there's no one person who's in charge of each other, so each of the teams are also distributed, and it's all like a <sup>fl</sup>at hierarchy underneath a <sup>fl</sup>at hierarchy underneath a <sup>fl</sup>at hierarchy.”

Awareness of these various differences and of the need to bridge them was emerging amongst project members. The IT manager started to brief IT staff on what research involved, and to refrain from expecting production-oriented goals from faculty: “[The] tech guys [were] used to doing things in the production environment, in the shop environment where we had to get things kind of, you know, knocked out. It was very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to not take control of that.” He continued:

[For instance] the email server can't be down. There's no time to look at the email server and say, ‘How can we make the email server better? How can we break this down?’ You don't have time for that. The email server has to run so that people can get their email. ipl2 is not an email server. It's something different … there was de<sup>fi</sup>nitely an adjustment period, to say the least, for [the IT staff]. (IT manager)

As the project developed, a Ph.D. student noted that the tasks became less ‘nebulous’ and more cohesive over time. This perspective was echoed by an IT staff member who, after remarking on the uncoordinated nature of the <sup>fi</sup>rst few months, said “what really has developed in a positive fashion along the way is the group's ability to work together, to make decisions and to follow through on the decisions.”

Another IT member commented positively on the emergence of a more focused organizational structure and task-oriented meetings:

Accomplish[ing] the common goal is sometimes tough … I'm here going, “servers, let's get it on servers,” and X is going, “Hey, let's talk about cataloging” … And that made collaboration a little more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, too, I think. Whereas now we have a [subcommittee], I think that's a little more productive as we hit some technical things. We can kinda all put our heads together, talk about how we're gonna accomplish the goal without muddying the waters of the bigger group, I guess. (IT staff member)

## 5.2. Definitions of metadata

Metadata was de<sup>fi</sup>ned in different ways by different members of the project. The faculty and students provided various de<sup>fi</sup>nitions drawn from library and information science. A faculty member with a library and information science background de<sup>fi</sup>ned metadata in terms of card catalogs and bibliographic records, and another faculty member researching digital libraries referred to metadata as “a way to improve the description of the digital objects in digital collections.” The collections manager explained: “Probably the best example is the old card catalog. It's a card that would represent that we have this book or newspaper or magazine in this library. Here's the author, here's the title, here's where it was published, subject lines, et cetera, and all that. That's what metadata is.” The IT staff provided more IT/IS-oriented de<sup>fi</sup>nitions: “My view of metadata is just descriptors to a larger data set and individual records within that data set,” said one. Another discussed metadata in terms of site architecture: “I think it's really the backbone of the site … [The site] is all meaningless without clean, solid, well-structured data.” Another drew an analogy with technical project management: “it's the same thing as tracking code, you know, when you have multiple developers working on things. You have to have some sort of standardizing. Metadata is the standard that you set for your projects.”

## 5.3. Organizational communication and knowledge

For a project involving many volunteers working within an informal structure, effective communication of organizational knowledge was important. Sometimes, this went well, as in the case of the initial physical transfer of hardware to the College in 2008, when an ipl2 programmer went to the previous site to talk with the system administrator there, and this contact was continued after the servers had been shipped

[O]verall it [the transfer] went pretty well … And, of course, it was helpful, too, to still have the person on the [other] side that was essentially acting as a systems administrator to work with on this stuff. So as we ran into problems, she was still kinda around … [and] so we kinda hit her up as we went through this. [IT staff member]

However, not all transitions proceeded as smoothly. For instance, the ipl2 programmer associated with this handover subsequently left the project, and his replacement also departed abruptly. According to his replacement (the third and current programmer): “It was a nightmare … he [the second programmer] found out, I think, two days ahead of time that he was leaving. So I think there was a total of maybe eight hours that I spent with him looking over the code, and taking as many notes as possible … and we made the best out of a bad situation.” “When those things get passed from person-to-person it's insanity,” said another IT staff member, “because it's really hard to understand what the last person was thinking.” As a student who worked with the programmers noted, “the shifting or the moving of people in and out of the project, and also people with different background and different programming language preferences, [made] it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to actually to build on top of each other's work.” This situation varied with from student to student; some were with the project for just one term and found it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to get to grips with the project, while others were with the project for several years and acquired a reasonable background knowledge of ipl2.

A second knowledge issue was that momentum and continuity between meetings were affected by busy schedules; as one student noted, “you have a meeting and you go off and do what you were assigned to do, the next day you might discover a problem, but you wouldn't be able to report that problem until the next meeting. So in that meantime, you couldn't do a thing about it, so you'd lose a week or two in progress.” Busy schedules postponed the discussion of project issues to later meetings, where it once again contributed to the complexity of the agenda. It was hard to share knowledge in meetings, because the meetings were busy, and it was hard to share knowledge between meetings, because the people were busy.

A third concern was the amount of time and commitment that each person could devote to the project; an IT staff member commented, “there are people like you [the interviewer] or I [the interviewee] who might be on this project for the long-term, and then there's other people who might be here for three months.” Students in particular entered and left the project frequently, and for almost everyone, the work was described as ‘extremely part time,’ what one faculty member described as an ‘add-on’: “it wasn't something that would come to the very top of someone's priority list.” Another faculty member pointed out, “Are [project members] doing what they need to do? Well, probably not. But that's because everybody's overloaded and nobody really has the assignment.”

A fourth concern involved various legacy issues. For instance, the original MySQL database that had supported the previous version of the library, had now grown into a highly customized structure that was hard to decode. “It's a very ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, it's an excellent design,” said a faculty member, “but that also makes it less practical other than [for] a speci<sup>fi</sup>c purpose. It's very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to update it, very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to make any changes to.” “I stay pretty far away from [the MySQL database] just because it hasn't been one of my major projects,” said an IT staff member, “but I did brie<sup>fl</sup>y look at the data with [the collections manager], and just in general that was kind of scary.” Much of the other information architecture was also idiosyncratic, involving what an IT staff member called “custom programming — the in-house, home-brewed programming … We had no idea.” The collections manager, who constituted the ipl2's longest-term organizational memory, had spent considerable time becoming familiar with the project; “It's taken me years to <sup>fi</sup>gure [it] out,” he said. “It's been a hodge-podge project.”

I know I've had to <sup>fi</sup>gure out many systems. Again, there wasn't documentation. We've developed all of this documentation. There was some technical documentation that helped with the set-up. We had like, a PowerPoint … So it's — yeah, so I've written a lot of the documentation. (Collections manager)

A persistent theme across much of the project was that a lack of project continuity made it hard to understand how or why the original IPL had been designed the way it had been. “That's what you ran into with the metadata,” said a Ph.D. student, “you wanted to know how it was created, and there was a big black hole.” At the time of the interviews, some project members were using a shared document server, but this was not universally adopted. A student observed:

I keep some documentation about how I work on the Fedora and those things. I share it using the Google Docs. Yeah, it's a solution, but I don't think it's a good solution to share documentation with the group, because I cannot see other people's feedback from that … and I have no idea how many people actually read that… (Ph.D. student)

Another student also expressed a preference for face-to-face communication: “When you're online, it's impersonal; and if I don't have a strong reason to check a Google Doc spreadsheet, I'm probably not gonna do it. Not because I don't want to do it or it's not important to me, but because it just doesn't come up.”

## 6. Discussion

The aim of this study has been to identify the underlying reasons for the unexpected problems encountered in the ipl2 metadata work. The investigation was shaped by theories of networks of practice, technological frames, and perspective making and taking. This section discusses how the factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the analysis – organizational structures, actors and roles, de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of digital libraries and metadata, and organizational knowledge and communication issues – can be explained in terms of this wider theoretical framework. This is followed by a description of how the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the study were re-applied in the project, and the some of the subsequent changes in the project work which have emerged since the original analysis was carried out.

## 6.1. Networks of practice and technological frames

In the analysis, three distinct networks of practice were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed amongst ipl2 staff. The IT staff were members of a ‘systems administration’ network of practice, the faculty were members of an ‘academic’ network of practice, and the research assistants were members of a ‘student’ network of practice. Each group had a distinctive view of the project. The IT staff generally viewed the library as an operational service requiring routine maintenance, while the faculty often viewed it as a test-bed for research. The students were busy with their student careers, and viewed ipl2 at least partly as the focus of their required research assistant activities, which meant in effect supporting the faculty's project management and research activities.

As the discussions in the ipl2 meetings were often driven by the faculty, these discussions frequently revolved somewhat loosely about possibilities for new tools and services for ipl2, rather than addressing more precise articulations of more immediate maintenance needs. These emerging ideas that the faculty had for research work with ipl2 con<sup>fl</sup>icted with the desires of the IT staff to keep ipl2 running for users; a research perspective was not something that the IT staff were used to dealing with, while a maintenance perspective was not something the faculty were used to dealing with (c.f. McGovern & Hicks, 2004). In an example that arose during writing, the team discussed the need for gathering usability requirements from a prototype of the IPL metadata tool, in order to schedule any re-design work with the IT staff. The faculty, and also the students, many of whom had a background in HCI, interpreted these requirements in terms of human– computer interaction (HCI), and proceeded to carry out appropriate testing related to the interface design. However, the IT staff were primarily interested in functional testing related to the underlying architecture how the tool actually worked, and the HCI requirements were not useful to them in this regard (functional testing has since been carried out).

These networks of practice were in turn aligned with different technological frames of databases and metadata. The faculty had a frame derived more from library and information science, while the IT staff had a frame derived more from database administration. Each group tended to take their frames for granted, and especially at the beginning of the project, these frames were not articulated explicitly. The same technical term could be understood in different ways by different project members, leading to different meanings being used, without participants recognizing that these differences existed (c.f. Khoo, 2005). An interesting example of such ambiguity that arose after the interviews were conducted concerned different de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the term ‘identi<sup>fi</sup>er.’ The faculty assumed a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘identi<sup>fi</sup>er’ associated with Dublin Core metadata, in which an identi<sup>fi</sup>er is “An unambiguous reference to the resource within a given context. Recommended best practice is to identify the resource by means of a string conforming to a formal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation system” (Dublin Core Metadata Initiative, 2012). In the case of Dublin Core records of Web sites, it is a common practice to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the identi<sup>fi</sup>er in terms of the Web site's URL, and the faculty assumed that the dc:identi<sup>fi</sup>er <sup>fi</sup>eld in an ipl2 record would contain the URL of the resource: and they further assumed that the identifier would be editable. in the event that the URL, of a resource changed. The IT staff, however, assumed a database management interpretation of 'identifier' in terms of a database record key, an unambiguous and non-editable value assigned to each record in order to keep track of it across multiple tables in a database. ipl2 records also have this form of identi<sup>fi</sup>er, although it is referred to in terms of ‘ID’ rather than ‘identi<sup>fi</sup>er.’ However, as the IT staff had assumed that the metadata <sup>fi</sup>eld for ‘identi<sup>fi</sup>er,’ which contained the resource URL, was (because of its name) functioning as the record ID, and had made the this <sup>fi</sup>eld non-editable. This in turn caused dif<sup>fi</sup>culties when it was required to edit the URL in a record, for instance because the Web resource had moved to a new URL. This may appear to be a small technical difference, but it was an important one that required additional work to remedy.

The characteristics between the networks of practice, and technological frames of the faculty and the IT staff, are summarized up in Table 2.

The <sup>fi</sup>nding that there were different networks of practice and technological frames in ipl2 project members, which were producing communication dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, was expected. Less expected was the <sup>fi</sup>nding that interviewees frequently elaborated on these dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in the context of various forms of time, ranging from the short-term (days or weeks), to the long-term (years) (note that the asynchronous notion of ‘time difference’ in this context is different from the sense of synchronous ‘time zone’ distance sometimes mentioned by researchers looking at globally distributed work; e.g. O'Leary & Cummings, 2007). Examples of short-term dif<sup>fi</sup>culties included the time between meetings, which, combined with project members' busy schedules, led to the fragmentation of project conversations. Long-term factors included legacy issues with the databases, metadata formats, site code, and other factors, which stretched over years, and which led to the fragmentation of organizational knowledge about site architectures and metadata. Generally, much of the knowledge of how to run ipl2 on a daily basis was not recorded, and there were many undocumented design decisions whose rationale had faded into the background Especially with regard to long-term issues, previous developers might just as well have been located in a different part of the world, speaking an obscure dialect.

Overall, the issues in the ipl2 metadata work arose not just because of the technical complexity of the work, but also partly because project members were also members of different networks of practice, and viewed the project through distinct technological frames. As the project had a long history, there were also differences between networks of practice that were spread out over time. Intercommunal negotiation, and perspective making and taking, were therefore needed, both synchronously between the networks of practice, and asynchronously between the different stages of the ipl2 project.

## 6.2. Organizational interventions

ipl2 responses to these issues can be considered as the current phase of a project history that, to date, consists of three overlapping phases (see also Fig. 5, below). The <sup>fi</sup>rst two phases cover the history of the project prior to and parallel with the analysis described in this article, while the third phase covers the introduction of some of the organizational changes informed by the analysis in this article.

Phase 1 of the project involved the transfer of the project to its new location in 2008, the formation of the local project team, the initial discussions, decisions, and activities regarding the direction and scope of the metadata work, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to carry out this work, and the encountering of the <sup>fi</sup>rst problems. Phase 2 involved ongoing efforts to implement this work. It was at this point, as the metadata problems continued to emerge, that discussion began to turn towards the possibility that there may be some underlying problems in the project. As part of this process, the project members began to acknowledge different points of view in the project; the IT manager, for instance, began to distinguish between production and research activities, and to explain these differences to the rest of the IT staff. This prompted the setting up of sub-committees that met between the regular meetings to discuss speci<sup>fi</sup>c technical questions (such as the issue of the browsing metadata). These meetings proved only partially successful, and the technical issues often re-emerged; it was still be hard to keep track of issues between meetings, and it was still dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to come to agreement on speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues. It was at this point that the analysis described so far in this article was planned and carried out.

Phase 3 of the project, which is ongoing, has been informed by the analysis reported in this article, and has included interventions designed to increase organizational communication and knowledge sharing in the project. The implementation of these interventions has been facilitated by the lead author, who has also assumed a more central managerial role in the project, and acted as a boundary spanner and translator, especially between the faculty and the IT staff.

In line with Brown and Duguid's recommendations involving the use of translators, boundary spanners, and boundary objects, many of these interventions included attempts to formalize project management and work<sup>fl</sup>ow, and to introduce re<sup>fl</sup>exive knowledge sharing moments, in which project members ‘checked in’ to see that they shared common ground with respect to the work at hand. For example, meeting management was formalized with itemized agendas, pre-circulated minutes, a chair and minute taker, and the recording of action items and staff responsibilities. Meeting moderation included scheduling periodic points at which all members of the project ceased conversation, in order to agree upon the issue under discussion, and the best way to address that issue. In cases where issues needed more time to be resolved than was available at the meeting, further ad hoc meetings were scheduled with relevant project members, which identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in advance speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues to be addressed, and the project members to be involved. These meetings were in turn supported by documents appropriate to that meeting; in many cases, these documents supported interaction in the ad hoc meeting, but were not circulated more widely at the full project meetings.

With the caveat that these organizational changes were only one set of variables in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the evolution of the project, the impact of these changes on project progress is observable in a number of improvements related to the metadata work. There has been an overall reduction in misunderstandings and ambiguities with regard to the metadata work, although these do still occur (see Section 6.1, above), and the discussions of metadata in the project are more focused, and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and misunderstandings are resolved in a more timely fashion. As a result:

\- the crosswalking of existing metadata to Dublin Core has been accomplished;

\- the new Fedora database to house the Dublin Core metadata has been completed and implemented;

\- a new cataloging tool, ‘Erasmus’ (Khoo, MacDonald, & Park, 2012a), which allows the Dublin Core metadata in the database to be created and edited, has been developed, pilot tested, and evaluated;

\- new cataloging assignments for digital library classes using the Erasmus tool have been developed and pilot tested; and

\- the new Dublin Core ipl2 metadata is now being used in collaborative research involving ipl2, another U.S., digital library, and two institutions in the United Kingdom (Khoo, Tudhope, et al., 2012b).

Note that these are not separate metadata projects within ipl2; rather, there are complex dependencies between many of these tasks, and each ongoing problems in one area would have led to dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in all the other areas.

These improvements in the metadata work can be framed in terms of the different networks of practice in the project, and the intercommunal negotiations, and perspective making and perspective taking, which were undertaken. While technical knowledge concerning metadata was sticky within each network, elements of this knowledge were gradually shared, particularly amongst the longer-term members of the project. Intercommunal negotiation was less relevant to the legacy issues in the project, not least because a conversation with the past is usually neither dialogic nor dialectical. Historical documents (in this case in the form of previous databases, metadata formats, web site code, etc.) have little ‘agency’ of their own to contribute to negotiation in the present. In many cases the past, as represented by artifacts, simply is, and has to be dealt with on those terms.

As the project progressed, the longer-term members of the project began to distinguish themselves from newer members (such as the students). The latter found it hard to get up to speed with the project, and the longer-term members began to describe ipl2 to newer members in terms of a technical complexity that would require some effort to understand, suggesting in turn that these longer-term members saw themselves members of a community of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998). This emerging (if fragile) ipl2 community of practice provided a common arena for sharing knowledge between different networks of practice, and in this sense it functioned as a boundary object (Star & Griesemer, 1989) that re-oriented the attention of project members towards common project goals. When (or rather, if) project members learned to participate in ipl2, they could then begin to situate their world-views in relation to those of other project members, and begin to consider how to translate between knowledge differences, in the process making their own knowledge less sticky, and allowing it to circulate within and contribute to the development of ipl2.

Some of the general characteristics of this situation are summarized in Fig. 5, which shows the longitudinal evolution of relationships between networks of practice in ipl2. At Time A, members are drawn from three external networks of practice (c.f. Fig. 1); at Time B, these networks of practice are linked by emergent intercommunal negotiation; while at Time C, intercommunal negotiation is now established, and there is also an emergent community of practice situated between these networks of practice, which also functions as a central boundary object for the project.

This con<sup>fi</sup>guration at Time C provides an added dimension to Brown and Duguid's discussion of the organizational challenges arising from sticky knowledge located in different networks of practice. According to Brown and Duguid, “For such organizations, the most important relationship between quasi-autonomous communities within a dynamically structured <sup>fi</sup>rm must be one of negotiation-negotiation that allows change, on the one hand, to occur locally and, on the other, to affect global strategic change if necessary.” The model advanced in this current article suggests that these communities can also negotiate with each other through the medium of a central practice space located between, rather than within, any individual network of practice. It is thus, in contrast to Brown and Duguid's implicitly dyadic model of negotiation, a complex multi-stranded model of intercommunal negotiation.

## 7. Limitations and opportunities

There are a number of limitations to, and also opportunities offered by, the present study. First, in terms of methods, there are tensions between the ethnographic and action approaches that need monitoring, especially with regard to the role of the researcher in the research process, which oscillates between external and neutral, and internal and engaged. The discussion in Section 4 addressed this through the introduction of iterative ethnographic action research cycles, in which each individual approach is emphasized at different points in the cycle, and hopefully it has been demonstrated that this hybrid approach can yield useful results. The second limitation is that of relatively small sample size and interview length; however, all of the project members signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly involved with the metadata work were interviewed. However, it was clari<sup>fi</sup>ed several times with the interviewees that they had discussed everything they wanted to about metadata and organizational work; further, the interviews were focused, and framed within the authors' prior observations and understanding of metadata work in ipl2, which helped to interpret and enrich the data gathered in the interviews. Third, the approach integrates several theoretical approaches, and many of the connections between these approaches remain to be explored, such as the links between how knowledge is actually modeled in networks of practice, technological frames, perspective making and taking, etc. Fourth, the use of boundary objects in the project has not been explored deeply. Boundary object theory offers a rich and subtle approach to understanding how diverse organizational stakeholders manage to ‘go on’ with each other in organizational work, although (as researchers have pointed) if the notion of a boundary object is extended to every interactional artifact in an organization, nothing is gained, and theoretical traction is lost (Nicolini, Mengis, & Swan, 2012). Thus, the lack of use of documentary boundary objects in ipl2, and the use of ipl2 as a social boundary object (c.f. Doolin & McLeod, 2012) is an interesting phenomenon that requires further investigation. Fifth, there is scope to expand the analysis from digital libraries and metadata to address research questions in information infrastructures. Sixth, there is a general opportunity to develop further theoretical and methodological approaches to the investigation of the organizational dimensions of metadata work in information infrastructures.

## 8. Conclusion

This study has drawn on theories of networks of practice, technological frames, and perspective making and perspective taking, to analyze metadata work in a digital library project. Based on an ethnographic action research approach, an analysis of interviews with project members identi<sup>fi</sup>ed different networks of practice and technological frames, associated with the faculty, students, and IT staff, and showed how, as a result, the knowledge required to work with the ipl2 metadata was sticky both between networks of practice, and also across time. The intercommunal negotiation required to overcome this stickiness was not simple. The negotiation required re<sup>fl</sup>exive communication by project members, in diverse areas such as database management, metadata development, and cataloging, and addressed the different forms of domain- and time-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge embedded in webs of software code, metadata formats, and organizational practices. Legacy issues proved signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and intercommunal negotiation had to be carried out across time, although this was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve. Overall, the study shows that intercommunal negotiation was carried out not just between individual networks of practice, but also with reference to an emerging community of practice within the project that served as a boundary object. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also have implications for metadata work in wider information infrastructure, a vastly more complex phenomena consisting of numerous overlapping networks and multiple contexts of use. Metadata work, while sometimes seen as a relatively narrow area of technical expertise, is foundational for the wider success of such infrastructure. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant opportunity going forward is therefore to investigate the transferability of the model presented in this paper at larger scales, to see what technological and organizational recommendations it can generate for information infrastructure design and use.
