RESEARCH ARTICLE

# How user risk and requirements risk moderate the effects of formal and informal control on the process performance of IT projects

Mark Keil<sup>1</sup>, Arun Rai<sup>1</sup> and Shan Liu<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems and Center for Process Innovation, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, U.S.A; <sup>2</sup>School of Management, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China

Correspondence: Mark Keil, Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, 30302-4015, USA. Tel: 404-413-7365; Fax: 404-413-7394; E-mail: mkeil@gsu.edu

## Abstract

Improving the management of information technology (IT) projects is of prime concern to both IS researchers and practitioners, as IT projects are notorious for poor process performance, frequently running over budget and behind schedule. Over the years, at least two separate streams of research have emerged with the aim of contributing to our understanding of IT project management. One of these focuses on the exercise of formal and informal controls, while another focuses on identifying and managing key risks such as those associated with requirements and users. Proponents of the control stream would argue that the exercise of formal and informal controls can improve process performance and there is some evidence that this is so. An obvious question that emerges, however, is how effective these controls are in the presence of particular risks. In this study, we seek to answer this question by developing and testing a research model that integrates these two streams of research. On the basis of data collected from 63 completed IT projects in China, we examine the moderating effects of requirements risk and user risk on the relationship between control (both formal and informal) and the process performance of IT projects. We contribute to the current state of knowledge by clearly demonstrating that both types of risk moderate the effects of formal and informal controls on performance. Specifically, both requirements risk and user risk were found to reduce the positive influence of controls on process performance, implying that implementing solid controls is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition to ensure good process performance. ## Introduction

Information technology (IT) projects are notorious for poor process performance, frequently running over budget and behind schedule. Over the past several decades, at least two separate streams of research have emerged with the aim of improving IT project management. One of these focuses on the exercise of formal and informal controls (see, e.g., Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996; Kirsch, 1997; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Tiwana, 2008), while another focuses on identifying and managing key risks such as those associated with requirements and users (see, e.g., Boehm, 1991; Barki et al, 1993; Jiang & Klein, 1999; Schmidt et al, 2001; Wallace et al, 2004b). Surprisingly, there has been little attempt to integrate these two streams.

Proponents of the control stream would argue that the exercise of formal and informal controls can improve process performance and there is some evidence supporting this (e.g., Henderson & Lee, 1992; Bello & Gilliland, 1997; Tiwana & Keil, 2010). At the same time, we know from prior research that a multitude of risks can negatively impact IT projects, stemming in large part from user resistance and the difficulties in obtaining accurate and stable requirements (Nidumolu, 1996; Wallace et al, 2004a, 2004b; Han & Huang, 2007). What we do not know is the extent to which the control to performance relationship is affected by such risks. In this study, we focus on requirements risk and user risk (Schmidt et al, 2001; Wallace et al, 2004a) and their interactions with formal and informal control. Requirements risk is defined as uncertainty surrounding system requirements or frequently changing requirements, and user risk is defined as the lack of user involvement during system development.

To date, there has been little attempt to understand how risks moderate the relationship between control and performance. With very few exceptions (e.g., Nidumolu, 1996; Barki et al, 2001; Jiang & Klein, 2004), prior research has focused on controls or risks in isolation, and there has been limited systematic attempt to understand their joint effects. The importance of this void was highlighted in a recent study by Tiwana & Keil (2010), which reports that requirements volatility (defined as the extent to which project requirements unpredictably change over the course of the systems development life-cycle) moderates the effect of specific control modes on performance. In this study, we build on this work to examine how two key risks – requirements risk and user risk (Schmidt et al, 2001; Wallace et al, 2004a) – moderate the relationships between formal control and process performance and between informal control and process performance, from the user perspective.

We focus on the user perspective (as represented in this research by the user liaison) as users are often the stakeholder best equipped to provide oversight to ensure that IT projects deliver business value and adhere to proposed schedules and budgets (Kirsch et al, 2002). Consistent with Kirsch and other control researchers, we conceptualize the user as the controller for all modes of control with the exception of self-control.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. First, we introduce relevant theory, our research model, and present our hypotheses. Next, we describe our research methodology for hypothesis testing, which relied on survey data collected from 63 IT projects in China. Then, we present the results of our study, in which we tested our hypotheses using a hierarchical procedure wherein separate models are used to first test the effects of the controls, then the main effects, and finally the interaction effects. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings for both research and practice.

## Theoretical framework

We conceptualize control in a behavioral sense (Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al, 2002) as attempts to ensure that individuals working on organizational projects act according to an agreed-upon strategy to achieve desired objectives. In the broadest sense, there are two types of controls: formal and informal. Formal control can include specifying particular behaviors, such as standard practices that must be adhered to, as well as particular outcomes that must be attained. Formal control relies on mechanisms designed to influence the controllee’s behavior through performance evaluation (Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003) and is comprised of two control modes: behavior and outcome-based controls. In behavior control, controllers define appropriate steps and procedures for task performance and evaluate controllees’ performance according to their adherence to prescribed procedures (Ouchi, 1979; Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch, 1997). In outcome control, controllers define appropriate output targets and allow controllees to decide how to meet them. Performance evaluation then focuses on the extent to which targets were achieved, and not on the processes used to meet the targets (Ouchi, 1979; Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al, 2002).

In contrast to formal control, informal control focuses on the interpersonal or self-regulating dynamics that govern behavior (Ashford & Tsui, 1991; Kirsch, 1997). Informal control relies on informal social strategies to reduce goal differences between controller and controlee, and is comprised of two control modes: clan and selfcontrol (Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch, 1997; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). In clan control, all members of the work group embrace the same values, adopt similar problem-solving approaches, and commit to achieving group goals (Ouchi, 1980; Kirsch & Cummings, 1996; Kirsch, 1997; Kirsch et al, 2002). In self-control, an individual sets his/her own goals, and monitors his/ her own goal achievement and rewards or sanctions him/herself accordingly (Jaworski, 1988; Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al, 2002). (Note the concept of self-control has been applied to a development group or a vendor firm; e.g., Rai et al (2009) refer to CMM-5 level offshore developers as exercising selfcontrol through their internal processes.)

Prior research on control of IS projects has focused primarily on three areas: (1) understanding the specific modes of formal and informal control (e.g., behavior, outcome, clan, self-control) used during a project (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003); (2) exploring the factors (e.g., project size, outcome measurability, behavior observability) that affect the choice of control modes (Beath, 1987; Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al, 2002); and (3) probing the dynamics of control (e.g.. how do control portfolios change during the course of an IT project) (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 2004).

Surprisingly, only a few studies have probed the relationship between control and performance (Henderson &

Lee, 1992; Na et al, 2004; Klein et al, 2006; Tiwana & Keil, 2010). Henderson & Lee (1992) suggest that an increase in the total level of control behavior is positively correlated with performance. Klein et al (2006) find that behavioral controls directly influence project performance. Na et al (2004) find that controls standardization is associated with increases in process performance. Tiwana & Keil (2010) find that controls appear to enhance performance for internal projects, but not for outsourced projects.

On the basis of the limited work in this area, it appears that there is a positive relationship between control and an IT project’s performance in general. However, the extent to which key risks moderate the relationship between control and process performance is not known, leaving it unclear as to whether formal and informal controls are effective in the presence of salient risks that must be contended with in IT projects.

## Formal control and informal control as aggregate multidimensional constructs

Since our objective is to develop and test theory that contrasts formal vs informal controls in terms of their influence on process performance, we model the user liaison’s assessments of formal and informal controls as aggregate multidimensional constructs. This enables us to achieve correspondence between the specification of our research objectives and the level of abstraction at which formal vs informal controls are differentiated in their effects on process performance. While controls in the IS literature have been contrasted at more granular levels (e.g., behavior, outcome, clan, self-control), Wong et al (2008) note that it is important to achieve correspondence between the research objectives and the level of abstraction of the constructs. Also, they note that when the objective is to develop and test theory in terms of higher-level constructs with underlying dimensions as in our case with formal vs informal controls, the form of the multidimensional constructs need to be clearly defined in terms of their relationships with the underlying dimensions.

Specifically, researchers need to decide whether the multidimensional construct is a profile, latent, or aggregate model in terms of its relationships with its dimensions (Law & Wong, 1999; Wong et al, 2008). Although these choices do not change the meaning of the dimensions, they do change the meaning of the multidimensional construct. We conceptualize formal and informal controls as aggregate constructs for two reasons: (1) we are interested in the total level of the user liaison’s assessments of formal and informal controls, making both the unique and shared variance of the lower-level dimensions relevant; and (2) we do not expect the dimensions of formal and informal control to have opposing influences on performance (Kirsch, 1997). On the second point, it should be noted that formal and informal control represent two different mechanisms through which control is applied, both of which are directed toward improving performance. Relatedly, even though developers and not the user liaison exercises self-control in development, user liaisons form perceptions of developers’ self-control, making their perceptions about self-control an important constitutive element of their overall assessment of informal control. Hereafter, when we refer to formal and informal controls, we mean the aggregate multidimensional constructs comprising the constituent control modes (or dimensions).

## Risk and control

While risk and control are clearly related, prior research on control of IT projects is not well integrated with the literature on IT project risk. On the basis of our review of the literature, with very few exceptions (e.g., Nidumolu, 1996; Barki et al, 2001; Jiang & Klein, 2004), prior research has focused on controls or risks in isolation, and there has been no systematic attempt to understand their joint effects. This is very likely because control and risk have evolved as separate research streams within the IS literature. Table 1 lists a sampling of research in the areas of control and risk. It is not intended to serve as an exhaustive set of references on controls or risk, but merely to highlight some of the key articles in these areas and to suggest that there have been comparatively few articles that have attempted to integrate risk and control in any meaningful way.

As the pattern in the table illustrates, most studies focus on controls or risks in isolation (e.g., numbers 1–18). Indeed, there are only a handful of studies (i.e., numbers 19–23) in which researchers have made any attempt to investigate both control- and risk-related constructs. These few studies vary significantly in the definition and type of control- and risk-related constructs: They consider constructs that are similar to, but distinct from, controls (e.g., horizontal coordination (Nidumolu, 1995)); evaluate how the impact of behavioral risks on performance is mediated by behavioral risk-reduction strategies (Jiang et al, 2001); evaluate the fit of a gestalt of project management practices with the project’s overall risk (Barki et al, 2001); and examine the moderation of the impact of specific modes of formal or informal controls by constructs that are related to, but distinct from, risk. For example, Tiwana & Keil (2010) examine how specific modes of control are moderated by requirements volatility defined as the extent to which requirements change during the system development life-cycle and consistent with Nidumolu’s (1995) requirements instability scale. Requirements risk, in contrast, is a much broader construct. As Wallace et al (2004a, p. 293) note: ‘Frequently changing requirements are not the only possible requirements-related problem associated with system development projects. Incorrect, unclear, inadequate, ambiguous or unusable requirements may also increase the problems, or risks, associated with a software development project’.

We conclude that the few studies investigating both control- and risk-related constructs provide only very limited insight concerning the joint effects of risk and control, and that there remains a considerable gap in our understanding of the overall effectiveness of formal and informal controls in the presence of key risks (e.g., requirements risk and user risk).

## Risk and the relationship between control and performance

Consistent with prior literature (March & Shapira, 1987; Wallace et al, 2004b), we define risks as conditions that can pose a serious threat to the successful completion of an IT project. Many prior studies focus on identifying, assessing, and mitigating IT project risks (e.g., Alter & Ginzberg, 1978; McFarlan, 1981; Boehm, 1991; Barki et al, 1993; Moynihan, 1997; Schmidt et al, 2001), and a few studies examine the relationship between risk and performance (Nidumolu, 1996; Jiang & Klein, 1999, 2000; Wallace et al, 2004b). From the latter, we know that high risk is often associated with poor process performance of projects in general as well as IT projects specifically (i.e., going over budget or behind schedule). Although there is a striking absence of studies focusing explicitly on the relationship between risk and control, the literature suggests that process performance can be improved when risk-mitigation strategies are fitted to the risk profile of a project (Barki et al, 2001; Jiang & Klein, 2004). This suggests that there may be some interplay between risk and control, thereby requiring careful theoretical specification of the nature of fit between controls and risks that influences process performance. Before developing our logic for the fit between formal and informal controls and specific types of risk, we note that our conceptualization of fit is informed by Venkatraman’s (1989) notion of fit as moderation. This conceptualization of fit is appropriate when (1) the variables involved in the fit relationship have high specificity; and (2) their interaction is posited as the primary determinant of the variance in a specific criterion variable. These conditions match both our conceptualization of the predictor variables (i.e., formal and informal controls and the risks) and their fit for the process performance of IT projects.

Conceptually, we regard risks as conditions that may materialize unexpectedly during a project and therefore it is logical to assume that when certain risks materialize they may dampen the effect of existing controls. Therefore, we specify risks, rather than controls, as moderators because the portfolio of controls used on a project is something that managers can select and later change if needed, whereas the risks that materialize during a project are often unforeseen events over which management has little or no direct influence. While there is some prior research examining the direct effects of risks and demonstrating that they can adversely affect performance (e.g., Nidumolu, 1996; Jiang & Klein, 1999; Wallace et al, 2004b), we argue that the causal mechanisms through which controls and risks influence performance requires an examination of their interaction.

specifically how risks change the effectiveness of formal and informal controls.

We posit that risks act as pure moderators (i.e., risks do not have a direct effect on performance in the presence of controls). (See Sharma et al (1981) for a detailed discussion of the distinctions between pure moderators and quasi-moderators). More specifically, we assert that the relationship between control and process performance will be weakened in the presence of certain risks. This has some intuitive appeal because risks may materialize somewhat unpredictably during the course of a project, thereby interfering with the efficacy of previously chosen controls. Indeed, while a few studies report a positive relationship between formal control and performance (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Na et al, 2004), some studies do not find the relationship to be significant (Klein et $^ { a l , }$ 2006; Tiwana & Keil, 2007), suggesting that there may be other factors (e.g., risks) that influence the effectiveness of control. Our conceptualization of risk as a moderator is also consistent with Tiwana & Keil (2010) who theorize that requirements volatility moderates the relationship between specific control modes and performance.

In this research, we focus on two key risk factors that may serve as moderators of the relationship between control and performance: requirements risk and user risk. Requirements risk is defined as uncertainty surrounding system requirements or frequently changing requirements. This encompasses incorrect, unclear, inadequate, ambiguous, or unusable requirements (Nidumolu, 1996; Schmidt et $^ { a l , }$ 2001; Wallace et $^ { a l , }$ 2004a). User risk is defined as the lack of user involvement during system development. This encompasses negative attitudes of users toward a new system and lack of cooperation during development (Barki et al, 1993; Jiang & Klein, 1999; Wallace et al, 2004a).

We decided to focus on these two risk factors for four reasons. First, they are broadly acknowledged to be critical. In a cross-cultural study of IT project risks, Keil et al (1998) reported that requirement and user risks dominated the top five risks. According to Nidumolu (1996), the requirements analysis phase is the most important phase of the software development life-cycle. This is because requirements risk can greatly impact subsequent phases and can lead to other risks as well (Zmud, 1979; Wallace et al, 2004a). The rich literature on user involvement suggests that managing user risk is also critical to the success of IT projects. Second, while there are other risks that can affect performance, requirements risk and user risk are likely to moderate the relationship between control and performance. Prior evidence suggests that some risks, such as broader environmental uncertainty, are unlikely to interact with control of IS projects in a manner that would significantly impact performance (Rustagi, 2004). Instead, these broader risks may manifest as proximate risks to the context of work, such as environmental risk manifesting as requirements risk for IS projects. Third, previous research suggests that the effect of requirements risk and user risk on performance may be indirect (Nidumolu, 1996; Wallace et al, 2004b). For example, Wallace et al (2004b) posit that requirements risk and user risk impact performance indirectly through project management risk. Fourth, while a few studies find a positive relationship between formal control and performance (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Na et al, 2004), some studies do not find the relationship to be significant (Klein et al, 2006; Tiwana & Keil, 2007), suggesting that there may be other factors that influence the effectiveness of control. In addition, Hunton & Beeler (1997) show that user involvement and control interact to affect performance. Consequently, we suggest that requirements risk and user risk will negatively moderate the relationship between control and performance.

## Process performance as an outcome variable of IT projects

IT project success can be measured in different ways. Two common approaches are ‘budget and schedule performance’ or ‘perceived system quality’ (Markus & Mao, 2004, p. 525). The former approach of assessing budget and schedule performance is a simple way of assessing process performance. We use process performance as the outcome variable of interest because it conveniently captures two of the critical elements normally associated with the so-called triple constraint facing the IT project manager. Process performance is defined here as the extent to which the IT project is delivered on schedule and within budget (Wallace et al, 2004a), from the perspective of the user liaison. While other outcome measures could have been investigated, such as whether the desired functionality is actually delivered, or whether the system quality is perceived to be adequate for its intended use, we restrict our focus to process performance as this is widely acknowledged to be one critical aspect in determining whether or not a project is judged to be successful. Moreover, empirical evidence indicates that IT projects that exhibit poor process performance are less likely to be deemed successful along other more product-related criteria (Wallace et $^ { a l , }$ 2004b).

## Research model and hypotheses

In this section, we present our research model (see Figure 2) in which the influence of formal and informal controls on performance is contingent on the risks (requirements risk and user risk) that characterize the IT project.

We posit that user liaisons will naturally favor informal controls and view these as more effective. For supporting these conjectures, we turn to Ouchi (1977, 1979) who suggested a framework for determining which types of control are more effective. If the ability to measure outputs is high and knowledge of the transformation process is perfect, then formal control is more effective and should be chosen. If the ability to measure outputs is low and knowledge of the transformation process is imperfect, then informal control is more effective and appropriate. Support for Ouchi’s framework can be found in Kirsch et al’s (2002) research where it was shown that low levels of outcome measurability and knowledge of the transformation process were associated with increased exercise of informal control.

User liaisons will generally have less domain-specific knowledge of IT and will lack an in-depth understanding of the IT development (i.e., transformation) process. They will also tend to be further removed from day-to-day project activities and will therefore have less ability to observe the process and to measure outputs from the IT project. For example, they are less likely to have access to information on both the status of resource consumption relative to budgeted amounts for various activities and on the status of schedules relative to projected timelines and milestones. Also, they are unlikely to have a sophisticated understanding of the transformation activities in different types of IT projects (e.g., development, maintenance, conversions, upgrades etc.). On the basis of Ouchi’s (1979) framework, we can expect that users would tend to be more comfortable with and therefore favor the use of informal controls. Accordingly, we posit that they will perceive informal controls to have a greater impact on performance than formal controls. Thus, we state the following hypothesis:

H1: From the perspective of the user liaison, the relationship between informal control and process performance will be seen as being stronger than the relationship between formal control and process performance.

## Moderating role of requirement and user risk

As mentioned above, we posit that requirement and user risk act as pure moderators (i.e., that these risks do not have a direct effect on performance in the presence of formal and informal controls). (In testing the model, we examine the possibility that the two risks act as quasi-moderators rather than pure moderators (i.e., that there may be direct effects between risk and performance)). Requirements risk can be thought of as uncertainty surrounding system requirements. While the literature does not explicitly discuss requirements risk as a moderator of the relationship between control and performance, there is some evidence that both uncertainty and requirements volatility, which are closely related constructs, may serve as moderators. For example, in the marketing literature, Jaworski (1988) suggest that environmental uncertainty will have a moderating effect on the relationship between control and individual performance. Sengu¨n & Wasti (2007) suggest that informal control mechanisms between supply chain partners may be rendered ineffective due to uncertainty (brought about by such factors as changing demand).

In the IS literature, the findings in this area have been mixed. Rustagi (2004) hypothesize, but fail to find support, for formal control and environmental uncertainty interacting to affect IS outsourcing success. Tiwana & Keil (2010), however, find significant interactions between requirements volatility, a construct related to requirements risk, and formal controls.

We posit that if there is uncertainty regarding users requirements (i.e., requirements risk), this will suppress the relationship between formal control and performance. Under formal control, there would be significant emphasis on completing project goals (e.g., delivering the project on schedule and within budget) and the project team would be expected to follow a documented and repeatable process toward the accomplishment of these goals. When requirements risk is present, however, this leaves the project team with unpredictable and dynamic sets of tasks to perform, making it difficult or impossible to follow any pre-established process or to meet project goals. Moreover, the project will be delayed because of changing requirements and rework associated with incorrect or unclear requirements. Therefore, the effectiveness of formal control on performance will likely be suppressed by requirements risk, as suggested in the following hypothesis:

H2a: From the perspective of the user liaison, the positive effect of formal control on perceived process performance of IT projects is moderated by requirements risk such that the positive effect is greater when requirements risk is lower.

Requirements risk is likely to result in conflicts among team members and between users and developers about project direction and objectives. Such conflict can be expected to create an environment in which it would be difficult to exercise informal control. Clan control, for example, would be hard to establish and maintain in such an environment. Similarly, team members will likely find that self-control is minimally effective in an environment in which they face tremendous task uncertainty. Thus, the benefits of informal control are unlikely to be exploited to their full potential, and we therefore state the following hypothesis:

H2b: From the perspective of the user liaison, the positive effect of informal control on perceived process performance of IT projects is moderated by requirements risk such that the positive effect is greater when requirements risk is lower.

We now turn our attention to user risk, which is one of the most cited risk factors in the IS literature. Lack of user involvement and participation during IT project development is regarded as user risk and it is usually related to less successful project outcomes. User involvement is regarded as a means of mitigating user risk and is seen by many as a necessary condition for successful development of information systems (Hwang & Thorn, 1999). In an empirical study, Hunton & Beeler (1997) find that user participation with instrumental voice, which provides users with a sense of control, yielded higher performance than either user participation with noninstrumental voice or no participation. The results of their study suggest that user participation interacts with control to affect performance.

User risk, which is essentially the lack of user involvement, means that users will have fewer opportunities to express their opinions, preferences, and concerns. In this case, users will not be in a position to exercise instrumental voice (or any voice for that matter), which means that users will have less control over the development process and we can expect that performance will be lower (Hunton, 1996; Hunton & Beeler, 1997). On the basis of this, we would expect that user risk may suppress the effect of control on performance.

When user risk is present, the user will be more distant from the day-to-day development process and will not be in a position to closely observe team members behaviors. The presence of user risk will also create a situation in which the users will have less understanding of the IS development process and may find it more difficult to effectively measure outcomes (due to lack of involvement). In either case, this will reduce the effectiveness of formal control mechanisms. Thus we state the following hypothesis:

H3a: From the perspective of the user liaison, the positive effect of formal control on perceived process performance of IT projects is moderated by user risk such that the positive effect is greater when user risk is lower.

User risk can also be expected to suppress the effectiveness of informal control on performance. One aspect of user risk involves resistance to change. When users exhibit resistance, they do not participate fully in the development process and they are unlikely to share the same values, goals, and norms as the developers. Even if the project team attempts to autonomously regulate their goals, tasks, and activities, this would seem to require some efforts and resources expended to interact with users. In either case, we can expect that the effectiveness of informal control would be reduced. Thus, when user risk is present, the benefits of informal control are unlikely to be exploited to their full potential, and we therefore state the following hypothesis:

H3b: From the perspective of the user liaison, the positive effect of informal control on perceived process performance of IT projects is moderated by user risk such that the positive effect is greater when user risk is lower.

## Control variables

We introduce four control variables into our model that are expected to affect process performance: project cost, project duration, outsourcing arrangement, and strategic orientation of the project. While there are a host of variables that could conceivably have an impact on process performance, based on prior research we honed in on these four variables as being the ones that were most likely to have an influence. Some studies have suggested that project size (or scope) influences the outcome of software project development efforts (e.g., Zmud, 1980; Jiang & Klein, 2000; Wallace et al, 2004a; Rai et al, 2009) and that larger projects are more likely to have higher cost and longer project duration. Thus, it was necessary to control for project size. Since project cost and duration are two indicators of project size that can be expected to affect performance (McFarlan, 1981; Wallace et al, 2004a), we included these variables as controls in our model.

We also deemed it desirable to control for project sourcing. Insourced vs outsourced projects differ in a number of respects and it has been suggested that outsourcing not only carries its own set of risks, but that choosing to outsource a project may affect the project’s schedule (Gowan & Mathieu, 2005). Thus, while we know of no empirical results that provide evidence that sourcing arrangement significantly relates to process performance, we deemed it prudent to control for this possibility.

From prior work, we know that different projects carry different risks and may therefore require different management approaches (McFarlan, 1981; Barki et al, 2001). It is therefore important to control for project type as certain kinds of projects may be inherently more risky. Developing a strategic application, for example, is fundamentally different from developing an application to automate transactions or aid in decision making (Clemons, 1991) and it may therefore present a different risk profile. In particular, strategic applications may exhibit a higher level of complexity, which may in turn influence process performance. Therefore, by controlling for strategic orientation, we control for the possibility that different types of projects may exhibit different performance profiles.

## Research methodology

## Data collection

A survey instrument was developed to collect the quantitative data needed to test our hypotheses. To effectively manage the survey process and get reliable data from industry, we cooperated with the Information Industry Office in the Hubei People’s Government of the People’s Republic of China (P.R. China) to co-administer the data collection. A list of 300 representative companies across different industries in China (e.g., information and electronic technology, business and finance, insurance, public utilities, and manufacturing) was provided by the Information Industry Office in the Hubei People’s Government of P.R. China. These companies were identified as having considerable experience with IT projects in the past few years. With the assistance of the Information Industry Office, senior IS executives in these companies were contacted and asked to participate in the survey. The survey was distributed to senior IS executives who were asked to identify appropriate projects and survey respondents in their companies. By enlisting senior managers and letting them distribute the surveys, this helped us identify appropriate respondents. This method is also consistent with Kirsch et al (2002) who asked executives to identify appropriate projects and respondents.

To qualify for inclusion in the study, the project had to meet two criteria: (1) it had to be the latest completed project undertaken by the organization so that there would be no recall issues in responding to the survey; and (2) its cost had to be over f100,000 RMB so as to exclude projects that were so small that they would not require elaborate control systems. The f100,000 RMB threshold was set based on our pre-testing of the survey with project managers and user liaisons. In the process of pre-testing the questionnaires with potential respondents, they expressed concern that for small projects it was more difficult to judge both risks and controls. Specifically, the pre-test respondents suggested that the cost of IT projects should be over f100,000 RMB so that they could better answer the questions related to risk and control.

The senior managers distributed the survey to a user liaison who was very familiar with the selected IT project.

The user liaison was defined as the individual responsible for overseeing the project from a user perspective. User liaisons are in a good position to reliably report on process performance. They act as communication conduits between the technical staff and business unit (Kirsch et al, 2002). In this capacity, they often discuss and communicate with project managers and senior executives about project schedule and budget problems. In addition, they provide oversight to ensure that IS projects make progress in conformance with the business value propositions and proposed schedules and budgets (Kirsch et al, 2002). Some researchers also suggest that perceptual assessments of performance provided by stakeholders (who can influence design activities or are affected by the resulting IS) have a high level of convergence with objective measures of performance based on records (Venkatraman & Ramanujam, 1987; Guinan et al, 1998).

Both paper-based and email questionnaires were mailed to the 300 senior IS executives giving them the option of using whichever medium was most convenient. The executives were then contacted by phone to ensure that the questionnaires had been received and distributed to appropriate respondents, and to obtain the contact information of the respondents. The respondents were subsequently contacted to ensure that they were comfortable responding to the questions in the survey. Survey participants were asked to retrospectively assess controls, process performance, and materialized risks at the conclusion of the project. A total of 63 usable questionnaires were received (50 paper-based questionnaires and 13 email questionnaires), yielding an overall response rate of 21%. The user liaisons had 0–31 years of IT experience (Mean ¼ 7.3, SD ¼ 6.0). The project characteristics are shown in Table 2. The 63 projects varied in terms of project cost, duration, sourcing arrangement, and strategic orientation.

Both Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to examine whether there were any differences between respondents who returned the paper-based questionnaires and those who completed the email version of the questionnaire. As shown in Table 3, the results revealed no differences, suggesting that there was no method bias and that the data could be pooled for subsequent analysis.

The external validity of the findings is likely to be threatened if the responses are generally from extraordinary successful projects. Table 4 shows the descriptive statistics for process performance of the IT projects in our sample. The responses of process performance are well distributed since the mean and median are similar. In addition, both skewness and kurtosis are less than 2 (Ghiselli et al, 1981), suggesting that the distribution does not deviate from normality.

Non-response bias is also a potential threat to the external validity. We assessed non-response bias by comparing early responses to late responses of the questionnaire, as late responses are assumed to be more similar to those from non-respondents. T-tests on the means of requirements risk (P ¼ 0.36), user risk $( P = 0 . 8 6 )$ , behavior control $( P = 0 . 8 4 )$ , outcome control $( P { = } 0 . 5 7 )$ , clan control $( P = 0 . 8 8 )$ , self-control $( P = 0 . 6 1 )$ , and process performance $( P { = } 0 . 1 3 )$ showed no significant difference between the early and late responses. Therefore, it appears that there is no significant threat of non-response bias in our sample.

## Construct measurement

As noted earlier, we conceptualized the user as the controller for all modes of control with the exception of self-control (where we view the development team as the controller). Thus, all of our measurement items (with the exception of the self-control items) focus on the user as controller.

Measures for all constructs (except control variables) were adapted from existing instruments. Five-point Likerttype scales ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ were used for the measurement items. For all constructs except performance, three or more measurement items were used. To assess process performance, we used a two-item measure that was adapted from Nidumolu (1996) and Rai & Al-Hindi (2000) and used previously by Wallace et al (2004b). We chose this particular measure because it had exhibited good measurement properties (composite reliability greater than 80) (Wallace et al, 2004b). To ensure that the measures were understandable and reliable, the questionnaire was pre-tested with 11 user liaisons. On the basis of the feedback received, modifications were made to enhance the understandability of the instrument. Table 5 summarizes the measures for the constructs and their informing sources.

## Hypotheses testing

We followed a hierarchical analysis procedure using PLS in order to test our moderation hypotheses. Our analysis approach follows the recommended hierarchical analysis procedure for testing moderation effects (Sharma et al, 1981), is also recommended for PLS (Chin et al, 2003), and has been applied in prior IS studies that use PLS to evaluate interaction effects (e.g., Im & Rai, 2008; Klein & Rai, 2009). The hierarchical analysis approach entails running a series of models beginning with just the control variables, then main effects, and finally interactions. The purpose in doing so is to tease out the incremental variance explained as we move from one model to the next (Carte & Russell, 2003). The main effects on the dependent variable (DV) allowed for comparing the relative effects of the independent variables (IV). Each two-way interaction was evaluated separately to safeguard against suppression of an interaction effect due to shared terms, which can increase multicollinearity. In our case, we specified and evaluated the following three models in order to test our hypotheses pertaining to the relative effects of controls on performance (H1) and the moderation of the effects of formal and informal controls by requirements risk and user risk (H2 and H3).

In Models 3a, 3b, 3c, and 3d, we mean centered the LV and then created a cross-product term of the moderator and the IV. The interaction of moderator (M) and IV is calculated as [LVS (M)-MEAN (M)]\* [LVS (IV)-MEAN (IV)]. LVS (M) is the unstandardized LV score of moderator (M) and LVS (IV) is the unstandardized LV score of IV in bootstrap sample i, with $i = 1 , 2 . . . . n ;$ MEAN (M) is the mean of LVS (M) and MEAN (IV) is the mean of LVS (IV) across the n bootstrap samples. This procedure is recommended when one or both constructs in a two-way interaction term are measured formatively as is the case here with both formal and informal controls modeled as formative second-order constructs (Chin et al, 2003). ## Implications

Our study has important implications for IT project management and for our understanding of the relationship between controls, risks, and process performance.

## Implications for theory

We contribute to theory by showing that risks moderate the effectiveness of both formal and informal controls on process performance. While prior research has tended to examine controls and risk in isolation of one another, this is the first study that has integrated control and risk and examined their joint effects on process performance. Thus, our finding in this area is of theoretical significance because it provides insight into how specific risks can moderate the relationship between control and process performance. ## Implications for practice

The results of our study affirm that both formal and informal control can help ensure that IT projects are delivered on schedule and within budget. Managers should note the significant role that risks play in influencing the relationship between control and performance. In particular, requirement and user risk suppress the effectiveness of control on performance. Therefore, managers would do well to consider risk management strategies for the IT projects that they are involved in. While professional organizations such as the Project Management Institute have long recognized risk management as being central to the project management body of knowledge, there is still reluctance in many organizations to engage in risk management practices. In part, we believe that this is because risk management principles involve asking ‘what if we cannot do this?’ or ‘what if something should occur that prevents us from being successful?’ and this runs counter to the kind of ‘can do’ positive attitudes that managers often try to promote within their organizations.

In order to reduce the threat posed by requirement and user risks, managers can employ both technical and social strategies to reduce the probability that these risks will materialize or to reduce their impact if they do materialize. On the technical side, for example, managers can use an evolutionary system development approach and build a flexible system architecture that enables changes to be made more readily in response to new requirements that may emerge. On the social side, managers can seek to involve the users throughout the development process and put them in the driver’s seat when it comes to determining requirements. At the same time, managers can educate users on the costs associated with change requests and seek to distinguish between ‘must have’ functionality and ‘nice to have’ functionality.

In order to improve performance, practitioners should consider the fit between risk and control mechanisms, adapting control strategies to the degree of risk exposure (Barki et al, 2001). At the same time, managers must recognize that no amount of control is going to eliminate the negative impacts on process performance posed by user and requirements risks. Indeed, one important message of our work is that because requirements risk can dampen the effectiveness of controls, managers should do their best to insure that core requirements are carefully identified at the outset of a project. Likewise, since user risk can also weaken the effectiveness of controls, managers should be certain from the outset that users are truly committed to the project and should be proactive in maintaining user commitment over time.

## Limitations and directions for future research

As with all research, this study has some limitations. First, as is the case with other studies on control (e.g., Kirsch et al, 2002), our results are based on a limited sample size. Further, the threshold for inclusion that we set for project size meant very small projects (of100,000 or approximately U.S.\$15,000) were excluded from our study. Still, our sample did include a range of differentsized projects.

Second, respondents from different countries may have different perceptions of IT project risk as well as the relative importance of formal and informal controls. For example, people from collectivistic culture may perceive informal control as being more important than people from an individualistic culture. Thus, while the findings from this study may hold in similar cultures, the generalizability of this study, which was conducted in China, may be limited to a certain degree. Additional research is needed to determine if our results can be replicated across different cultures.

Third, there are other outcome measures that could have been used to assess performance and different stakeholders may target different forms of success. We chose to focus on process performance (i.e., the extent to which a project is delivered on schedule and within budget). It is possible that our results would be different if we had focused on another outcome measure such as whether the desired functionality was actually delivered, or whether the system quality was perceived to be adequate for its intended use. In other words, process performance may not correlate with delivering a successful product. Project success is clearly a multidimensional construct and one direction for future research would be to explore whether our results hold for other outcome measures that tap into different aspects of performance.

Fourth, it is likely that some of our results, particularly with respect to the perceived importance of informal vs formal control, were influenced by the fact that we chose to focus on user liaisons as our respondents. Future research should explore whether the perceptions of the joint effects of risk and control vary across different types of stakeholders (e.g., project managers, senior managers etc.).

Finally, since our objective was to develop and test theory that contrasted formal vs informal controls in terms of their influence on process performance, we modeled the user liaison’s assessments of formal and informal controls as aggregate multidimensional constructs. Clearly, such a choice limits us from generating insights at the more granular level of individual control modes. Future research that examines controls at the level of specific control modes (e.g., behavior control, outcome control, clan control, and self-control) could shed additional light on the joint effects of risk and control.

There are several additional directions for future research. First, one could examine the moderating effect of other risks (e.g., team risk, technology risk, planning, and control risk) on the relationship between control and performance. Second, one could examine the effectiveness of formal and informal control on other aspects of performance (e.g., product performance and team performance) and how risk factors moderate the relationship between controls and other aspects of performance. Finally, it should be noted that different stakeholders may target different forms of success and that this constitutes another area for future research.
