# The influence of website dimensionality on customer experiences, perceptions and behavioral intentions: An exploration of 2D vs. 3D web design

## A B S T R A C T

As online retailers seek to attract customers through innovative electronic storefront designs, some are experimenting with three-dimensional (3D) websites. This study examines the influence of website dimensionality on cognitive absorption, perceived ease of use, and, indirectly, perceived website usefulness and intentions to buy online using the website. Findings indicate that shopping websites using 3D environments are associated with lower perceived ease of use and lower cognitive absorption, compared to traditional 2D websites. The effect of website dimensionality on cognitive absorption is moderated by user experience. The implications of the study for research and practice are discussed. - 2014 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

The rise of the Internet in the 1990s and the evolution of Internet technologies have dramatically changed social and business practices, from shopping and banking to making friends and dating. Online shopping has become a viable, and often preferred, alternative to visiting traditional brick and mortar stores. As the technology through which we access the Internet continues to evolve, businesses are pressed to create and refine web-access applications in response to user demand [60]. Research indicates that the design of online storefronts has a significant influence on sales [55]. Thus, companies continue to innovate their website designs to gain a competitive advantage [51,91] and to enhance customer experience by creating an enjoyable website atmosphere<sup>1</sup> [24].

The illusion of the three-dimensionality of visual representations has proven to attract customers in the entertainment industry. PricewaterhouseCoopers found that 3D movies accounted for 50–70% of box office revenue [79]. Improvements in the bandwidth and increases in the processing power of personal computers made it possible to create websites that include immersive three-dimensional environments [75]. The use of such 3D computer generated environments has become one of the signature characteristics of virtual worlds [90]. The evolution of computer generated 3D environments is likely to spur website developers and further increase the importance of 3D websites. The number of virtual worlds has increased to almost 900 in 2012 from 450 in 2011, and virtual world revenue has reached 9 billion in 2013 compared with 6 billion in 2012, according to one virtual space consulting firm (http://www.kzero.co.uk/blog growth-forecasts-for-the-virtual-worlds-sector/).

Lured by the increasing number of users seeking the immersive experience offered by virtual worlds and computer games, online retailers are experimenting with utilizing 3D designs for their electronic storefronts to replicate the shopping atmosphere of traditional shopping venues. These 3D shopping websites allow customers to move through a three-dimensional store in a manner similar to playing a computer game and to select and view products in a manner that imitates shopping in a brick-and-mortar store (i.e., www.enjoy3d.com, www.kinset.com, www.virtwayworld.com). Although the concept of 3D design is intuitively attractive, the effect of dimensionality on online shopping behaviors has largely not been empirically explored. Among the notable exceptions, a study of the effects of website dimensionality on brand equity and user intention grounded in the theory of flow found 3D virtual world environments to have both a positive and negative effect on brand equity [59]. The positive effect has been attributed to feelings such as telepresence and enjoyment, whereas the negative effects were explained through distraction-conflict theory [59]. In another study, Debbabi et al. [19] investigated the mediating role of telepresence on the effect of online 3D advertising on consumer responses. They list as a limitation of their study the failure to consider the familiarity of users with the website environment. Our study provides insights into this matter by considering both first time users and experienced users. A study by Jeong and Choi [39] investigating the effects of picture presentations on customers’ behavioral intentions suggests that customers’ hedonic and utilitarian attitudes should be included in future studies.

Interestingly, several attributes of a website design that are related to dimensionality were identified as important predictors of user experiences. The results of a study examining the relationship between image interactivity, intention to buy and other psychological and perceptional factors suggest that image interactivity influences the global attitude, willingness to patronize, and willingness to purchase of online buyers [28]. A study investigating the impacts of web page visual complexity and order on psychological outcomes and behavioral intention found that visual attributes of the website influence users’ affective reactions but that the relationship is moderated by the meta-motivational state of the user [21].

The purpose of this paper is to extend existing research [2,59,89] and to examine the effect of 3D environments (compared to traditional 2D website designs) on psychological and perceptual factors that were demonstrated to influence online shopping behaviors. Specifically, we draw on Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences [42], the cognitive absorption nomological net [2], and the technology acceptance model [17] and examine the effects of 3D environments on dimensions of cognitive absorption associated with the use of the website and perceived ease of use of the website. As a part of this study, we also seek to deepen our understanding of the relationships among cognitive absorption, ease of use, usefulness and intentions to buy online outlined in the cognitive absorption nomological net. Motivated by the evidence suggesting that combining all four dimensions of cognitive absorption into a single construct is problematic [94], we examine the role played by individual dimensions of cognitive absorption.

The contribution of this manuscript is threefold. First and foremost, this study examines the effects of 3D computer generated environments on user outcomes in the context of utilitarian information systems. Although the use of 3D environments was widely examined in the context of hedonic systems, such as games and virtual worlds, this is among the first studies to examine user outcomes of introducing a 3D environment in the online shopping context. Our results suggest a significant negative effect of the 3D environment on both perceived ease of use and cognitive absorption dimensions and an indirect effect on perceived usefulness and intention to buy. This study illuminates challenges and opportunities associated with incorporating novel interfaces into utilitarian systems. Interestingly, the negative effect of the 3D environment on the cognitive absorption dimensions is only significant for first time users of the website, which once again highlights the importance of user experience in evaluating novel types of user interface designs.

The second contribution of this study is that it deepens our understanding of cognitive absorption because it decomposes the cognitive absorption construct into its dimensions and tests the relationship between individual dimensions and antecedents and consequences of cognitive absorption. As a result, we are able to demonstrate that the relationships outlined in the cognitive nomological net hold for some, but not all, dimensions. This may also help practitioners to develop websites that trigger a high level of cognitive absorption in users. Previous studies have investigated the relationship between cognitive absorption and perceived ease of use or perceived usefulness as a whole, but none of them, to the best of our knowledge, have investigated whether the models hold for dimensionally different websites. Finally, the results of our study confirm the moderating effect of user experience with the website on the relationships between cognitive absorption, ease of use and usefulness.

## 2. Theoretical underpinnings

In this section, we discuss the concept of dimensionality in the design of the user interface and provide an overview of three theoretical frameworks that inform this research study, including Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences, the technology acceptance model (TAM) and the cognitive absorption nomological net and related research.

2.1. Illusion of three-dimensionality and its use in human–computer interface

The term ‘‘three-dimensional’’ (3D) is broadly defined as relating to three dimensions or aspects. In the context of visual media and design, the term involves creating an illusion of depth and varying distances in flat images and other visual representations [57]. The illusion of depth is achieved when we look at a flat surface but perceive that objects on that surface are located at different distances from us. However skillfully executed, traditional flat representations of 3D images fail to completely fool the observer as to their shape. When viewing paintings or photographs, humans are able to perceive the 3D nature of the objects represented on the painting/photograph but are also aware of the fact that the painting is flat.

Advances in computer graphics and stereoscopic technology help to create a more realistic illusion of three-dimensionality. Some tools work with the motion scale and display different visual representations depending on the movements of the observer [13]. Other tools help to create a binocular disparity illusion [36]. The former is usually employed in computer games, where the movement of the observer is critical to the creation of the illusion of the 3D reality. The latter is more frequently utilized in cinematography, where the viewer is presumed to be static. In IS research, the term 3D environment is usually utilized to refer to computer systems that create the 3D illusion by varying the perspective and motion scales, i.e., that allow users to ‘‘walk’’ through the computer generated 3D space by altering the images presented to the user in real time depending on the ‘‘movements’’ of the user. This is the sense in which the term 3D is employed in this work. Although 3D effects have long been utilized in computer games where depth and distance to objects are critical to the achievement of game objectives, advances in 3D technologies and increased bandwidth have allowed the creation of the 3D illusion in web-based systems where users can interact with each other in real time.

Although a three-dimensional interactive computer generated environment is a signature feature of virtual worlds [90], a distinction exists between a 3D environment and a virtual world. In the context of this research, we consider a system to provide a 3D environment if its user interface creates the illusion of depth in two-dimensional representations and allows the user to navigate in the three-dimensional space. In contrast, virtual worlds have been defined as 3D interactive environments wherein the user assumes an identity within the virtual world through an avatar and constitute a special case of 3D environments [75,90].

The interest in virtual world research has grown continuously, and research has examined the impact of technological and spatial environments on online intention to purchase [1,59]. Other researchers argue for the value of the virtual worlds for business [7,32] and the collaboration of users in such environments [45]. In addition, researchers consider legal principles governing the virtual worlds [64] and the design principles for the development of virtual world environments [10,75].

For the purpose of this study, we focus on the use of 3D environments in utilitarian systems, such as shopping websites. As such, the systems are expected to help users to achieve their goals, rather than enjoy the interaction with the system per se.

## 2.2. Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences and website dimensionality

Kaplan’s research on environmental preferences offers unique insights into human perceptions of 2D and 3D environments. Kaplan and his colleagues [42,43] proposed a model that identified preferences in visual representations for understanding and exploring the environment. The preferences are based on four characteristics of visual representations, including coherence, complexity, legibility and mystery. Coherence refers to the organization of elements in a visual representation. A highly coherent visual representation is orderly, and its elements are organized in meaningful categories and regions. Complexity is related to the richness of the elements in a setting. Complexity is high if a visual representation has many diverse elements and is low if it has few elements. Legibility refers to how easy it is to find your way through the visual representation and is higher if a representation contains memorable elements and landmarks. Mystery is defined as the ability of the visual representation to arouse the feeling of curiosity in viewers. Curvy paths and partially obscured views are among the ways to enhance the mystery of the visual representation. Mystery is proposed to be particularly important in promoting exploration on the part of the viewer [43].

As Kaplan and his colleagues posit, the distinction between 2D and 3D resides in the manner in which people perceive and interpret images [43]. The picture plane or two-dimensional pattern represents the surface of the picture. At a primary level, perception involves a rapid assessment of the patterns of light and dark. Elements and textures in the scene, including their grouping and location, are extracted from this more primary information. The three-dimensional aspects involve the inference of what is deeper in the scene, despite the fact that the actual image is on a flat surface. This inference about the third dimension occurs rapidly and unconsciously, although it may take a few milliseconds longer than the relatively more rapid processing of the twodimensional aspects [43].

The four characteristics of the visual representations – complexity, coherence, legibility and mystery – play a differential role in influencing the perception of 2D vs. 3D representations. In a 2D representation, all elements are located on the same plane; thus, processing 2D visual representations involves rapid scanning of the elements on the surface of the representation to understand the relationships among the elements. It is the interplay of complexity and coherence that influence the level of understanding and exploration in 2D representations. A higher degree of coherence promotes better understanding, and a higher degree of complexity encourages exploration. In processing 3D representations, even those displayed on a flat surface, such as a photograph or a computer screen, additional information about the relationships among elements is embedded in the location of these elements in the third dimension (closer vs. farther away).

Therefore, processing of such representations is influenced not only by coherence and complexity, but also by such factors as legibility and mystery.

In sum, Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences suggests that 2D and 3D representations differ in how they are perceived by viewers in terms of complexity, cohesiveness, legibility and mystery. These perceptual differences result in a differential effect on viewers’ ability to understand the representation and their desire to explore it (see Fig. 1).

Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences has been applied in the field of IS, specifically in evaluating user preferences for different website designs. In the context of 2D website design, coherence (referred to by the authors as order) was shown to have a negative effect on arousal, and complexity was demonstrated to influence feelings of pleasantness, although such a relationship was moderated by the meta-motivational state of the users [21]. Furthermore, arousal and pleasantness were related to the approach tendency toward the website. The effects of coherence, complexity and legibility were also found to be positively related to the overall impression of the website and the intention to revisit the website [65]. Here, we apply Kaplan’s visual representation characteristics for understanding differences in user perceptions and experiences of 2D vs. 3D websites by relating them to IS acceptance and use models, including the technology acceptance model and the cognitive absorption theory.

## 2.3. Technology acceptance model

In the information systems (IS) literature, technology acceptance models are among the most accepted theoretical frameworks for studying individual IT usage intentions [4,17,18,85]. The TAM and related models have been shown to explain IT usage decisions in a variety of contexts and in relation to different types of information systems, from shopping websites to healthcare information systems [11,12,23,25]. In its original form, the technology acceptance model (TAM) posits that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness influence individuals’ attitudes toward using a system, which in turn influence intentions to use the system [17,74]. TAM also proposes that perceived ease of use influences perceived usefulness. TAM was successfully applied to the online shopping environment, and perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness have both been found to be significant predictors of intentions to buy online [44,47,49,88]. Later incarnations of TAM identified additional significant predictors of usage intentions and behavior. TAM2 incorporates such factors as performance and effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, voluntariness of use, experience, age and gender [84]. TAM3 incorporates factors such as computer self-efficacy, perceptions of external control, computer anxiety, computer playfulness, perceived enjoyment, and objective usability [83]. The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) considers such additional factors as subjective norms, image, job relevance, output quality, and result demonstrability [85]. UTAUT II considers such additional factors as performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, hedonic motivation, price value, habit, age, gender and experience [86].

In spite of the numerous revisions of the original Technology Acceptance Model, its core constructs, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and intention to use, continue to be employed in a variety of contexts, including online learning [66], user acceptance of e-shopping on the web [71], and user acceptance of virtual worlds [27]. For the purpose of this study, we focus on the core TAM constructs of usefulness and ease of use because they have been widely used in the online shopping context and incorporated into the cognitive absorption nomological net (which is also used to inform this study) [2,66,69].

Perceived usefulness and ease of use are most closely related to the characteristics of the system and are therefore the focus of this study. A variety of organizational, individual and system factors have been shown to influence perceived ease of use and usefulness [37,38,48]. Among individual, organizational and environmental factors, such factors as ‘‘big five’’ personality traits, personal innovativeness, web skills, management and external support, as well as task type, were demonstrated to influence perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use [23,25,47,50].

Surprisingly, there has not been an extensive number of studies that have examined the effects of system characteristics, particularly website design, on user technology perceptions [9,62,76]. Existing research suggests that support for the provision of customer reviews and product recommendations, as well as value-added search mechanisms, results in higher perceived usefulness [95,96]. Videos and virtual product experiences were found to positively influence perceived website diagnosticity and, consequently, the perceived usefulness of the website [40]. A more holistic construct, perceived visual attractiveness, was also found to be positively related to the usefulness, enjoyment and ease of use [82]. However, similar to other perceptual factors, such as perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, perceived visual attractiveness does not allow for distinguishing between objective technology characteristics and individual perceptual differences. In the context of DSS, interface design (attribute-based versus alternative-based) was demonstrated to influence perceived usefulness [41]. We extend the current research by examining the effects of characteristics of the IT artifact such as website dimensionality on perceptual characteristics such as ease of use and usefulness.

## 2.4. Cognitive absorption and its nomological net

The construct of cognitive absorption was introduced to IS research with the purpose of capturing holistic experiences associated with the use of IT and was defined as ‘‘a state of deep involvement with software’’ [2,77]. Cognitive absorption as a state incorporates elements of such constructs as cognitive absorption as a trait [78], the state of flow in human computer interactions [81], and cognitive engagement [93]. The state of cognitive absorption is posed to be ‘‘exhibited through five dimensions: (1) temporal dissociation or the inability to register the passage of time while engaged in interaction; (2) focused immersion, or the experience of total engagement where other attention demands are, in essence, ignored; (3) heightened enjoyment, capturing the pleasurable aspects of interactions; (4) control, representing the user’s perception of being in charge of the interactions, and (5) curiosity, tapping into the extent the experience arouses an individual’s sensory ad cognitive curiosity’’ [2].

Individual characteristics of playfulness and personal innovativeness were identified as important antecedents of cognitive absorption. In turn, cognitive absorption has been shown to influence perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, the key antecedents of IT usage intentions. It was also shown to have a direct influence on the IT usage intentions [1]. In the context of online shopping, cognitive absorption was found to be, at least in some cases, a stronger predictor of consumer behavior than perceived usefulness [70].

Although the cognitive absorption construct was proposed to capture holistic user experience with IT, the nomological net of cognitive absorption includes only individual user characteristics, perceptual constructs and behavioral constructs. Other studies also focus on perceptual antecedents of cognitive absorption. For example, a study of user intentions to return to virtual worlds suggests that social awareness, location awareness and task awareness are positively related to cognitive absorption [31]. Whereas the cognitive absorption nomological net does not identify which characteristics of the IT artifact influence the state of cognitive absorption, research suggests that technology characteristics, such as feedback, quality, and richness, influence the theoretical precursors of the cognitive absorption construct, cognitive engagement and flow [35,92,93]. We seek to understand whether and how the dimensionality of the website design influences the cognitive absorption experienced by the user.

In this study, we formulate hypotheses for each dimension of cognitive absorption (focused immersion, curiosity, temporal dissociation, and heightened enjoyment). Previous studies suggest that the first three dimensions of cognitive absorption represent cognitive dimensions, whereas the last dimension represents an affective dimension; therefore, the combination of the four dimensions into one construct was found to be challenging [8,58,94].

## 2.5. Summary

We examine the effect of website dimensionality on several psychological and perceptual factors that have been identified in the literature as predictors of technology-related behavioral intentions. These include the four dimensions of cognitive absorption (heightened enjoyment, temporal disassociation, focused immersion and curiosity), playfulness, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness (see Table 1 for summary and definitions).

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The complete research model is presented in Fig. 1. Relationships between 3D environments and perceived ease of use and cognitive absorption dimensions are hypothesized based on Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences [43]. Relationships between cognitive absorption and perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are hypothesized based on the cognitive absorption nomological net [2], and the relationships among perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and intention to buy online are hypothesized based on TAM (see Appendix A for a summary of the theoretical grounding for each hypothesis). The proposed model extends these existing models in two ways. It adds website dimensionality as an independent variable influencing cognitive absorption and ease of use, and it decomposes cognitive absorption into individual dimensions and posits relationships between individual dimensions of cognitive absorption and ease of use, usefulness and intention to buy online.

## 3.1. Website dimensionality and perceived ease of use

According to Kaplan’s theory, in a 2D representation, the information is contained in a single plane and is thus perceived immediately. In a 3D representation, some information is contained in the relative position of objects in a three-dimensional space, and thus, its comprehension requires additional effort [43]. Moreover, it is easier to achieve coherence in 2D representations because all the information is represented on a single plane.

This, in turn, has a positive effect on understanding. Kaplan’s theory suggests that 2D representations are likely to be associated with a higher level of understanding [43]. Perceived ease of use is related to the individual’s perception of the physical and mental effort required to use the system [17]. ## 3.2. Website dimensionality and cognitive absorption

Similar to other visual representations, website design influences user preferences through the use of space, text, pictures, layout and sounds by affecting the mental maps, motivation and the amount of informational inflow [27]. As proposed by Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences, 3D representations (including 3D computer generated environments) are expected to be characterized by higher levels of complexity and mystery than 2D representations. The complexity and mystery of the representation influence exploration on the part of viewers and are therefore instrumental in explaining the level of absorption during website use, exhibited through heightened enjoyment, curiosity, temporal disassociation and focused immersion.

Mystery is essential to the processing of three-dimensional representations, and 3D representations are associated with a higher level of mystery than their 2D counterparts because of the wider range of cues for increasing mystery that are available in 3D. Such mystery cues, including curved paths and partially obscured views, suggest to the user that there is something unknown, and worth knowing, at the end of the path. Thus, they entice the users to follow the path (even if just in their imagination) and make them curious about the obscured part of the view. Research suggests that mystery is ‘‘a particularly effective factor in making the scene highly favored’’ [43]. As the heightened enjoyment dimension of cognitive absorption captures the ‘‘pleasurable aspects of the interaction’’ [2], the level of enjoyment is likely to be higher for 3D environments, which are characterized by higher levels of mystery.

Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2a: Websites with 3D design are associated with a higher level of heightened enjoyment than 2D websites.

Curiosity can be defined as a gap between what a person knows and what he/she wants to know [56]. Traditionally, ‘‘advertisers harness the power of curiosity in ‘‘mystery’’ ads that reveal the identity of the product only at the end of the advertisement’’ [54], with complexity being a trigger for curiosity [38]. Curiosity can also be increased through the manipulation of visual representation characteristics such as mystery. Mystery cues help increase the perceived knowledge gap by either lowering users’ perceptions of what they know by implying that there are other unexplored areas or by increasing their perceptions of what they want to know by inflating the subjective expected value of the unknown information. Three-dimensional environments characterized by higher levels of complexity and mystery are therefore more likely to arouse feelings of curiosity. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2b: Websites with 3D design are associated with a higher level of curiosity than 2D websites.

Three-dimensional websites are likely to have higher complexity than 2D websites because multiple visual layers allow for incorporating more visual components and increasing the richness of the design. Such increased complexity serves as a source of stimulation and leads to increased arousal while using the system [21]. Stimulation is likely to result in users devoting a higher portion of their cognitive resources to using the system and to having less cognitive resources to devote to other purposes, such as engaging with distractions. Focused immersion is defined as an ‘‘experience of total engagement where other attention demands are, in essence, ignored’’ [2], and it is likely to be higher for representations characterized by a higher level of complexity, such as 3D environments. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2c: Websites with 3D design are associated with a higher level of temporal disassociation than 2D websites.

Temporal disassociation is defined as ‘‘the inability to register the passage of time while engaged in interaction’ [2]. Presented with visual representations characterized by a higher level of complexity and mystery, such as 3D environments, users are likely to have less cognitive resources devoted to keeping track of time and, therefore, are likely to experience a higher level of temporal disassociation. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2d: Websites with 3D design are associated with a higher level of focused immersion than 2D websites.

3.3. Cognitive absorption, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use

Cognitive absorption has been related to antecedents of technology acceptance and use, particularly to perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use [2,66–68,80]. Perceived usefulness is defined as ‘‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance’’ [17]. Cognitive absorption is related to higher perceived usefulness of the system due to the cognitive dissonance and selfjustification mechanisms [26,73]. As users realize that they have spent more time than expected using the system, they alter (inflate) their perceptions of the benefits they received from using the system. This leads to higher perceived usefulness. We seek to understand the role of individual dimensions of cognitive absorption. Building on the demonstrated relationship between cognitive absorption and usefulness, we hypothesize the following:

H3a: A higher degree of time disassociation is associated with higher perceived usefulness.

H3b: A higher degree of focused immersion is associated with higher perceived usefulness.

H3c: A higher degree of curiosity is associated with higher perceived usefulness.

H3d: A higher degree of heightened enjoyment is associated with higher perceived usefulness.

Cognitive absorption is also related to ease of use. ‘‘The intrinsically motivating state of cognitive absorption creates perception of a lower cognitive burden’’ [2]. In addition, as users spend more time with the system, they are likely to become more experienced using the system and, consequently, perceive the system as easy touse. We focus on individual dimensions of cognitive absorption. Therefore, building on the demonstrated relationship between cognitive absorption and ease of use [68], we hypothesize the following:

H4a: A higher degree of time disassociation is associated with higher perceived ease of use.

H4b: A higher degree of focused immersion is associated with higher perceived ease of use.

H4c: A higher degree of curiosity is associated with higher perceived ease of use.

H4d: A higher degree of heightened enjoyment is associated with higher perceived ease of use.

## 3.4. Cognitive absorption and behavioral intentions

Cognitive absorption has been empirically demonstrated to directly influence intention to use IT. In the context of online shopping, research suggests that the longer the amount of time spent online, the greater are the chances of making a purchase online [14]. Because absorption is characterized by temporal disassociation, consumers experiencing cognitive absorption are likely to spend more time on a website, and thus, they are more likely to examine more products and find products that meet their needs. Therefore, consistent with the extant research and focusing on individual dimensions of cognitive absorption, we hypothesize the following:

H5a: A higher degree of time disassociation is associated with stronger intention to buy online.

H5b: A higher degree of focused immersion is associated with stronger intention to buy online.

H5c: A higher degree of curiosity is associated with stronger intention to buy online.

H5d: A higher degree of heightened enjoyment is associated with stronger intention to buy online.

## 3.5. Perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness and behavioral intentions

TAM suggests that perceived ease of use is positively related to perceived usefulness and that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness together influence behavioral intentions [17]. The costbenefit paradigm is just one of the theories underlying TAM reasoning. Applying the cost-benefit paradigm to IT use, the user is likely to perceive the cognitive effort of using the system as a cost and gains in performance as a benefit. Usefulness represents the gain in performance, and ease of use corresponds to the reduction in cognitive effort, i.e., the cost associated with the use of the system. A rational user, therefore, tries to maximize benefits and minimize costs by using a system that is useful and easy to use. The reasoning behind the relationship between ease of use and usefulness is that the reduction in the cognitive effort will result in performance gains and, consequently, higher perceived usefulness. TAM has been successfully tested in the e-commerce context, where usefulness and ease of use were positively related to the intention to buy online using a website. The TAM relationships are also included in the cognitive absorption nomological net. Therefore, consistent with prior research, we hypothesize the following:

H6: Higher perceived ease of use will be associated with higher perceived usefulness.

H7: Perceived usefulness positively influences intention to buy online.

H8: Perceived ease of use positively influences intention to buy online.

## 4. Research methodology

To test our research model, we conducted an empirical study in which subjects were asked to use one of two existing websites and subsequently respond to survey questions, a research approach widely used in previous studies [3,6,20,63]. Details of the data collection and analysis are presented below.

## 4.1. Data collection

Study participants were recruited from among university students enrolled in undergraduate business courses at a large public university. Students were found to be suitable subjects for

Table 2

these types of studies [30]. The participants were directed to a web page that introduced them to the purpose of the study and asked them to use an online bookstore to complete two tasks. First, the subjects were asked to find the price for one of three specified books (the search task) and to then browse the store to identify a book of interest to them that they had not heard about before participation in the study. Both websites offered a search tool that the subjects could use for the search task. The subjects were instructed that they do not need to complete the purchase of any book as a part of the study. The subjects were then randomly assigned to one of the two experimental groups and, depending on their group assignment, were directed to one of the two existing websites. The first website was the main Amazon.com website, the books department. This website was used to represent the traditional 2D website. The 3D design was represented by the http://enjoy3D.com/bookstore website, also powered by Amazon, where the visitors can move between shelves in a manner similar to walking through a traditional brick-and-mortar store. The websites had similar book availability, and the books specified in the experimental procedure were available at both websites<sup>2</sup>. We tested the time delay for loading the websites, and we noticed that the initial loading time for the http://enjoy3D.com/bookstore website was four seconds longer than the initial loading time for the https://www.amazon.com/books. After the initial loading, no significant time difference was noticed using the two websites. Because prior research suggests that familiarity with the brand and with the website influences user perceptions and behaviors [50], we captured information about user familiarity with the website, and we selected the two websites powered by the same brand company (Amazon.com).

To ensure that the subjects completed the experimental tasks, they were asked to provide answers to a number of control questions. For the search task, the subjects were asked to provide price information for the selected book, and for the browsing tasks, they were asked to provide the name of the book that they identified as interesting and a short justification of why the book was of interest to them. The subjects were also asked to record the time they spent on the tasks. Next, the subjects were presented with a survey designed to assess their perceptions and experiences related to using the website, their behavioral intentions and demographic information about the subjects. The students were offered a small amount of extra credit in their respective courses for completing the experimental tasks and responding to the survey.

## 4.2. Measurement instruments

The website design dimensionality was experimentally manipulated as discussed earlier. For the rest of the constructs, including dimensions of cognitive absorption, usefulness, ease of use, and intention to buy online, we used self-reported perceptual measures. All the constructs were measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Questions related to cognitive absorption (ABSORP), perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) were adapted from existing research [2,17]. Scales for measuring intention to buy online (IBO) were developed by the researchers following instrument development guidelines [29]. Consistent with Forsythe and his colleagues [29], we reviewed the literature and contextualized items to measure our concept for this study. Contextualization of items is a well-known developmental approach used in IT research [22,87]. We subsequently reviewed the items with several domain experts and then validated the measure using a pilot study with graduate students at the researchers’ university. The results support the reliability and validity of the developed intention to buy online scale.

## 5.2. Assessment of measurement model

The adequacy of the measurement model is determined by examining reliability and convergent and discriminant validities. As shown in Table 3, the alpha coefficients and the composite reliabilities of all constructs are above the generally agreed-upon lower limit of .7, thus showing a satisfactory degree of reliability.

In Table 3, the diagonal values represent the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE), providing a measure of the variance shared between a construct and its indicators, or convergent validity. Therefore, convergent validity is established because each construct has an AVE of at least 0.5.

There are two methods for assessing discriminant validity. First, AVE values must be examined to determine if they are consistently greater than the off-diagonal correlations. Second, each within-construct item must load highly on the construct it is intended to measure, and cross-loadings must be lower than the within-construct item loadings. The outer loadings and crossloadings are shown in Table 4.

One of the requirements in performing multi-group analysis using the PLS approach, measurement invariance, has to be present either at the item level (the most strict approach) or at the construct level (the most lenient approach). ## 6. Discussion

As an increasing number of customers shop online, the design of the electronic storefront is becoming a critical success factor for increasingly more retailers. In this study, we examined how website design, particularly the use of 3D environments in online storefronts, contributes to the customer’s online shopping experience. Drawing on the Kaplan’s research of visual representation, the cognitive absorption nomological net [2] and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [17], we proposed and tested a theoretical model that (1) relates the use of a 3D environment in utilitarian systems (shopping websites) to the cognitive absorption nomological net [2] and (2) decomposes the cognitive absorption construct into individual dimensions and then tests relationships for each of the individual dimensions.

The results of our study suggest that the use of a 3D environment in a utilitarian system (shopping website) does, indeed, have a significant effect on a variety of user experiences and perceptual outcomes, albeit not always in the expected direction. Consistent with our model, the 3D website was found to be more difficult to use. This result points to understanding as an important link between the visual characteristics of a website (such as cohesion and legibility) and its perceived ease of use [43]. To further validate these conclusions, additional research is needed that would directly measure the perceived cohesion and legibility of the website as well as website understanding.

From the practitioner point of view, this suggests that caution should be exercised in introducing new technology to online storefronts. When new technology is introduced, designers and developers must re-examine their usability heuristics. For example, when designing a 2D website, it may be sufficient to ensure that the website has sufficient levels of coherence and complexity. However, when moving to 3D representations, designers must ensure that the website has adequate legibility and maximizes the potential of the 3D representation to introduce mystery. Taking care of such new design dimensions may require changes in the interface design guidelines and evaluation procedures.

Perhaps the most interesting finding of this study is that our hypotheses regarding the relationship between the 3D environment and cognitive absorption dimensions were contradicted for first-time users and not supported for experienced users of the website. There was no significant difference between 2D and 3D websites in reported cognitive absorption for the users who had used the website before. This is surprising given existing research suggesting that 3D representations are more engaging because they offer additional tools for creating the atmosphere of mystery and thus encourage user exploration [43]. There are several potential explanations for these surprising findings. One possible explanation is based on the mere exposure effect [98] and is related to the level of familiarity of the users with the interfaces and visual representations associated with 2D shopping websites, as opposed to websites using a 3D environment. The mere exposure effect suggests that people develop a positive attitude toward stimuli to which they have been exposed in the past, particularly if they are not consciously aware of their familiarity [34,98,99]. We pose that the positive attitude toward the more familiar interface overpowers the specific benefits of the interface components, resulting in the negative relationship between novel interfaces and user attitude toward the website, especially for first-time users. Because the majority of the users have been exposed primarily to traditional 2D interface of shopping websites, they may have developed a positive attitude toward visual components of such interfaces, even though they may not have used a particular website and are not consciously aware of their familiarity and positive attitude. Presented with 3D environment shopping websites, users perceive it as unfamiliar and dislike it. Such negative attitudes inhibit their desire for exploration and, consequently, the level of cognitive absorption with the system. Consistent with this explanation, we found no significant relationship in the level of cognitive absorption between users with prior exposure to the corresponding websites. The mere exposure explanation highlights an important practical issue in human computer interactions. Particularly, it suggests that even if users have a negative reaction to new interface components during the first exposure, they are likely to change their attitude to a more positive one through repeated interactions with the system.

Another alternative explanation is that exploration and understanding are not independent dimensions of user experiences but are, in fact, related, and that user exploration is inhibited by inadequate understanding. As the higher complexity of 3D websites negatively influences understanding, a lower level of understanding may result in a lower desire to explore the website and, consequently, a lower level of absorption. Another explana tion consistent with the existing literature [43,49] is that not all 3D representations are characterized by mystery, and the 3D website employed in this study failed to create an atmosphere of mystery. According to Kaplan, the mystery effect is better achieved through curved (as opposed to straight) paths, and blocked views impair mystery [43]. The experimental website was, indeed, full of blocked views and straight paths. The two alternative explanations, however, fail to account for the differences between firsttime and repeat users in terms of the relationship between the 3D environment and cognitive absorption. However, the possibility of alternative explanations for this unexpected finding suggests that additional research is needed to better understand the issue. Directly measuring cohesiveness, complexity, legibility and mystery and relating them to understanding and exploration, as well as to cognitive absorption, perceived ease of use and usefulness, is one possible direction. There is also the need for future research on how 3D websites can be designed to maximize their mystery and legitimacy.

This study sheds additional light on the relationship between cognitive absorption, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and intention to buy online. First, our results suggest that not all dimensions of cognitive absorption are positively related to perceived ease of use and usefulness. Second, the results point to the moderating effect of user experience on the relationship between cognitive absorption, perceived ease of use and usefulness. For the experienced users, only time disassociation and perceived ease of use were found to be significant predictors of perceived usefulness. For first-time users, perceived ease of use, heightened enjoyment, curiosity and focus immersion predict perceived usefulness. However, all the cognitive absorption dimensions and the group treatment were significant for explaining perceived ease of use for first time users. The effects of time disassociation and focus immersion were not significant for experienced users.

The positive relationship between heightened enjoyment and perceived usefulness for first-time users is consistent with the cost benefit paradigm. Although the users were asked to assess usefulness in relation to performing specific tasks, they rank more enjoyable systems higher than less enjoyable systems. The positive relationship between heightened enjoyment and curiosity and the ease of use is consistent with the reasoning that curiosity and heightened enjoyment serve as intrinsic motivation for the user and thus result in lower perceptions of cognitive effort [2].

Interestingly, time disassociation was found only to be a significant predictor of usefulness for experienced users, and focused immersion was found to be a negative predictor of perceived usefulness, but only for first-time users. The apparent moderating effect of user experience on the relationship is of particular interest, especially considering the relationship between time disassociation and perceived ease of use for first-time users and experienced users. Together, these findings suggest that during early use, cognitive resources are devoted to learning the system, and the heightened enjoyment, time disassociation and focused emersion are indicators of higher cognitive effort. Such higher required cognitive effort associated with learning the system leads to lower perceptions of system usefulness and ease of use. During subsequent uses of the system, the attention of the user is devoted to the task, rather than to learning the system. Therefore, if the system helps the user to focus on the task, it is perceived as more useful. Interestingly, time disassociation was not found to be significantly related to the ease of use.

Our results also suggest a differential role of individua cognitive absorption dimensions in predicting intention to buy. All the dimensions of cognitive absorption appeared to be good predictors for intention to buy online. This suggests that keeping the website visitor engaged with the website can make them willing future buyers. While focused immersion has a positive influence on the intention to buy online for experienced users, the same construct negatively influences the intention to buy online for first-time users. This and other results discussed earlier point to the need to look at the individual dimensions of cognitive absorption, rather than treating it as a holistic construct. Our results constantly demonstrate that the independent variables explain more variance of the dependent variables for first-time users than for experienced users.

Finally, our study provides additional support for the core TAM, including the positive effect of ease of use on usefulness and the positive effect of usefulness on the intention to buy online. This is particularly encouraging because the relationships were significant for experienced and first-time users. Interestingly, ease of use was not shown to have a significant direct effect on the intention to buy online. This finding may be related to the specific experimental procedure (the subjects were not asked to complete a transaction) and needs to be interpreted with caution.

## 7. Limitations and future research

In this paper, we develop and test a comprehensive conceptual model that explains how the dimensionality of websites influences user psychological experience perceptions and shopping outcomes. Although the results of this study are insightful, and sometimes surprising, several limitations of our study need to be noted. First and foremost, because of the exploratory nature of our study as it relates to the website dimensionality, we used existing websites for our 2D and 3D operationalization. Although we tried to select websites that were equivalent in relation to the information necessary for the completion of the experimental task (both had book titles, pictures of the title page and price information), we could not fully control for all characteristics of the websites. Therefore, future research on 2D vs. 3D shopping websites needs to replicate the results of our study through laboratory experiments where all website characteristics, other than dimensionality, are controlled for.

The second limitation is related to our sample and experimental procedure. We employed students as subjects, and our study participants did not need to actually buy a book. Although college students are indeed a major consumer group in regard to online shopping, their behavior may still differ from those of older consumers. It is, however, interesting to note that college students are likely to be familiar with 3D environments because of their potentially higher exposure to computer games, yet they found 2D websites to be more enticing than the 3D alternatives. Therefore, we believe that the results would be even stronger for older populations. Still, future researchers are encouraged to replicate the study with older audiences and to control for exposure to 3D computer games. Additionally, a field study where the participants actually buy products from the websites would be an excellent way to validate the findings of this study. Finally, as discussed earlier, a study that focuses more directly on the relationships between website dimensionality, the perceptual dimensions related to understanding and exploration, and their relationship to TAM and CAT constructs would be a fruitful direction for future research.

## 8. Conclusions

In this study, we examined the effect of the 3D environment in a utilitarian systems application, such as shopping websites, on user outcomes that include cognitive absorption, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and intention to buy online. Consistent with Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences, 3D websites were perceived as less easy to use compared to traditional 2D websites. Contrary to our conjecture based on Kaplan’s theory of environmental preferences that 3D websites would be associated with higher level of cognitive absorption, the first-time users of such websites experienced a lower level of cognitive absorption than the first-time users of 2D websites, whereas no significant differences were found among experienced users. Such results are explained with the help of the mere exposure effect [98] and suggest the need for more research on what impacts the user when introducing novel interfaces. We further confirmed the results of previous studies that cognitive absorption results in higher perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use associated with the use of the website. Also consistent with prior research, we found that absorption, usefulness and ease of use have a positive effect on user intentions to buy online. Our results extend the prior research in several ways. First, this is one of the first studies to examine the effects of the 3D environment on perceived ease of use and cognitive absorption in the context of utilitarian systems, such as shopping websites. Second, this study enhances our understanding of the cognitive absorption construct and its nomological net by demonstrating that individual dimensions of cognitive absorption have a differential effect on user perceptions and behavioral intentions. Third, we identify user experience as an important moderator in the cognitive absorption nomological net. The results of this study have specific implications for both researchers and practitioners that are related to website design and online consumer behavior. The broader significance of this study and of the proposed model is that they bridge three established theoretical frameworks. By showing the interrelationships among the constructs in these three research streams, this study contributes to building a stronger cumulative research tradition in IS.
