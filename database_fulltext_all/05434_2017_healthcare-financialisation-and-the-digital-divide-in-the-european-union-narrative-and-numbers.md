# Healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation and the digital divide in the European Union: Narrative and numbers

Jonathan J.J.M. Seddon, Wendy L. Currie

Audencia Business School, 8 route de la Joneliere, BP 31222, 44312, Nantes Cedex 3, Franc

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 22 March 2016 Received in revised form 2 February 2017 Accepted 1 March 2017 Available online xxx

Keywords: Healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialization Cross-border health eHealth eGovernment ICT infrastructure

## A B S T R A C T

Financialization in health care considers the exchange of goods and services as <sup>fi</sup>nancial instruments. This paper uses multivariate statistical methods to provide comparative cross-country health analysis in two dimensions: ICT infrastructure (availability and access) and Health data (usage and sharing). Based on the quantitative indicators/metrics used in our study, our results reveal three distinct country grouping emerge: Frontrunners, Followers and Laggards. These groupings highlight vastly different socio-political and economic conditions facing national health systems, where health inequalities will only partially be alleviated by building capacity in ICT infrastructure and eHealth.

## 1. Introduction

Healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation relates to the broader financialisation thesis de<sup>fi</sup>ned by, ‘the increasing role of financial motives, financial markets, financial actors and financial institutions in the operation of the domestic and international economies’ [1]; 3. In the European Union (EU), healthcare is undergoing vast changes as <sup>fi</sup>nancial criteria embedded in policy-making agendas aim to improve healthcare for citizens in the 28 member states by reducing the digital divide [2]. Financialisation is a complex and elastic term with many de<sup>fi</sup>nitions [3]. At an abstract level, it is viewed as part of a neoliberal form of government that promotes freedom through the markets [4]. Narratives of <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation are not always supported by solid empirical evidence as powerful ideologies and economic pressures shape policy and practice. Financialisation is thus the outcome of the rhetoric and values that inform and shape actions and behaviour [5].

Thematic concepts of eGovernment, eHealth, eProcurement, public–private partnerships and market testing relate to a loosely coupled theoretical framework of propositions and assumptions discussed in the wider <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation literature [1]. These narratives are empirically demonstrated through the use of formal measurement techniques including benchmarking, performance indicators and balanced scorecards.

In healthcare, as in <sup>fi</sup>nance, a conspicuous effect of <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation is the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of indicators and metrics to measure the cost, access and quality of health services for citizens [63,6,7]. Europe’s policy-makers face the growing challenge of providing citizens with high-quality healthcare in an economic climate of ‘austerity’ with cuts to all public services [8]. Health systems codi<sup>fi</sup>ed as calculable entities are increasingly under the scrutiny of policy-makers and health professionals as the demand for health services continuously outstrips the supply. To address this problem, the European Commission encourages ‘Digital Health through the use of information and communications technology (ICT) to improve citizens access to local, national and cross-border health services as part of the European single market [9].

In this paper, we link healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation and ICT in the empirical context of the EU with over 500 million citizens. We problematise financialisation [10] as both a narrative that shapes policy-making on healthcare and a numerical activity underpinned by <sup>fi</sup>nancial instruments using quantitative indicators and metrics to benchmark the performance of health systems. Highly differentiated by funding levels, access to health services and quality of care [11], EU Member States aim to level the playing field in health systems by improving ICT access and infrastructure in less developed countries [12].

Building on prior work on EU country comparisons [13,14,2], results are presented from a multivariate statistical study using quantitative indicators to measure citizens’ access to ICT infrastructure (mainly general purpose technologies (GPTs)) with usage of eGovernment and eHealth services. Distinguishing eGovernment and eHealth systems is challenging as both offer citizens’ data and information on national, regional and local health services with considerable duplication. We thus combine these terms to gain a deeper understanding of EU citizens’ use of ICT to engage with national and cross-border public health services.

This study uses 12 indicators/metrics on health data (usage and sharing) and ICT (access and availability) to generate EU country pro<sup>fi</sup>les. The relatively small sample of indicators is methodologically viable to achieve a statistically robust set of quantitative data for comparative country analysis. Despite many single-country studies from multiple disciplines on health and technology, concerns remain about the lack of cultural awareness in highly differentiated health systems in EU countries [12]. Comparative country studies illustrate the diversity and complexity of health systems to enable various stakeholders (policy-makers, IT <sup>fi</sup>rms, health providers, NGOs and citizens) to benchmark performance.

Using this data set, we develop two research questions: (1) How do indicators/metrics on health data (usage and sharing) and ICT infrastructure (access and availability) differentiate EU country profiles? (2) What policy/research implications can be drawn from the EU Member States in identifying the opportunities and barriers in providing cross-border healthcare?

This paper is structured as follows. First, we discuss the relevance of the <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation literature for understanding the socio-economic and political pressures facing Europe’s health systems. From the literature, we identify <sup>fi</sup>ve key drivers for healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation as the EU seeks to build a digital single market for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of all citizens. Second, we discuss some methodological issues about measuring ICT infrastructure, eGovernment and eHealth at the pan-European, national, regional and organisational levels. Prior work on cross-country comparisons supports the need to compare and contrast indicators/metrics at the country level [63] as ‘one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all’ policy-making does not take account of the vast diversity in EU health systems [11]. Third, we present our data using principal component and cluster analysis. Three country groupings emerge from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings: Frontrunners, Followers and Laggards. Next, we discuss our results and observations in the context of <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation in healthcare. We conclude the paper by discussing our contributions and identifying potential areas for further research.

## 2. Five key drivers in healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation

Financialisation has become increasingly used by economists, sociologists and political scientists to describe the ‘transformation in which financial activities (rather than services more generally) have become increasingly dominant’ in world economies over the past few decades [15]; 2. Financialisation describes an economic system or a process that reduces all value that is exchanged into a <sup>fi</sup>nancial instrument, such as currency, where goods and services become exchangeable [16]. It becomes easier for people to trade such <sup>fi</sup>nancial instruments as the <sup>fi</sup>nancialised worldview suggests that goods and services are quanti<sup>fi</sup>able and measurable. However, in the domain of ‘healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation’, trade becomes a challenge as the <sup>fi</sup>nancial methods or techniques to quantify and compare such cost and quality of goods offered are underdeveloped or absent.

The critical literature on <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation that juxtaposes capital market reality with socio-economic reality [17] is relevant for our understanding of how <sup>fi</sup>nancial criteria affect health systems. Prior work from the management and information systems <sup>fi</sup>eld examines themes, debates and issues covered in the wider <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation literature [18–20]. Research that extends the technological narrative to include wider socio-political and economic factors is critical for understanding the intersection between <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation and healthcare. Thematic areas include health inequalities through comparative analysis of national health systems [11], the digital divide in GPTs [14], integration of health systems across urban and rural cities and towns in developing countries [21–24], the assimilation and use of information technology on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of hospitals [25], and how medical professionals and other stakeholders engage with the introduction of health IT [26,27].

As a complex and multilevel (macro and micro) phenomenon, variations in health systems in the EU are rooted in the historical, political, economic and cultural conditions of each of the member states [28]. An important objective of the European Commission is to extend the access and availability of health services to all citizens across the region. EU policy to reduce health inequality through cross-border health [38,39], the creation of a digital single market [29] and patient empowerment [30] are expressed in <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation narratives that shape the social and economic fabric of healthcare, with the dominance of <sup>fi</sup>nancial criteria taking on wider sociological signi<sup>fi</sup>cance [31]; 615. Building on a literature review, <sup>fi</sup>ve key drivers are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed which underpin healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation.

## 2.1. Government health spending

Health spending by governments across all the EU Member States <sup>fl</sup>uctuates year on year, depending on economic conditions in the national and international community [32]. European countries have witnessed a reduction in health spending with shrinking budgets in Greece, Italy and Portugal and many other countries reporting real per capita health spending below the levels of spending from 2009. Health spending growth has largely remained parallel with economic growth, with health expenditure as a share of gross domestic product (GDP) remaining stable relative to that in 2012. This is in contrast with the years preceding the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis when health spending strongly outpaced the rest of the economy. In 2013, public and private health spending (excluding investment) as a share of GDP was 8.9% across 34 countries, with 5.1% in Turkey to 11.1% in the Netherlands [32]. As EU health budgets continue to be stretched, the provisioning of healthcare and how it is <sup>fi</sup>nanced, organised and delivered present more acute challenges to policy-makers who remain keen to promote cross-border healthcare but recognise the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties facing national governments from burgeoning demands on health systems, including health tourism.

## 2.2. The digital single market

The creation of a digital single market in Europe shapes health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation. The EU has agreed new rules on public sector procurement to open up the £450 billion a year market to smaller contractors [29]. Governments are encouraged to outsource services, including healthcare, social care and education. Public contracts worth more than s750,000 will need to be advertised in the EU contracts journal so that private sector competitors from across the region can compete for business [33].

## 2.3. ICT business models

New ICT-enabled business models are emerging in healthcare [34]. An important aim is to reduce health costs and increase the quality of services for EU citizens [35]. Disruptive business model innovation resonates with health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation as ‘complicated, expensive products and services (can be) converted into simpler, affordable ones’ [36]; 1329. The challenge, however, is for EU policymakers to devise incentive schemes to encourage health professionals and citizens to adopt new business models in healthcare [11].

## 2.4. Patient empowerment

Patient empowerment is now a powerful narrative in healthcare as patients are encouraged to ‘gain control over their own lives’ by acquiring self-ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, self-awareness, con<sup>fi</sup>dence, coping skills and health literacy [30]. The ‘Patient Empowerment Campaign’ claims that patients suffering from chronic conditions are among ‘the most under-used resource in the health system’. New ‘patient-centred care models’ encourage patients to be ‘active partners,’ and this is expected to result in high-quality care, a better patient experience and lower costs in the long term [30]. However, the shift from passive patients to active consumers of health services involves signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cultural change, which is underplayed by policy-makers and business leaders alike [37].

## 2.5. Complementary services

The EU’s drive to develop complementary eGovernment and eHealth services for citizens has been discussed in numerous policy statements and initiatives in the past two decades [38,39]. More recently, EU policy-makers recognised the inter-connectedness of ICT insocial care andhealthcareinpolicyagendas[28]. eGovernment enables citizens to acquire data and information about a range of health services (i.e., the internal market, procurement of health services, health and social services, preventive medicine and wellbeing, ageing and care for the elderly), which are crucial for developing eHealth (i.e., technologies and services).

## 3. Challenges measuring EU health systems

To gain a wider understanding of the drivers in healthcare, policy-makers need robust, relevant and timely <sup>fi</sup>nancial indicators and metrics to evaluate the quality, value and sustainability of health systems. Methodological shortcomings due to lack of reliable quantitative data and the diversity of the EU health systems make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate the impact of legislative action. This problem becomes more acute as a shortage of detailed and reliable time series data on individual country and crossborder collaboration of health providers and <sup>fi</sup>nancing institutions prevents a meaningful evaluation of the drivers of health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation. For example, data and information about the numbers of patients (citizens) and professionals (health workers) and the volume of health services (clinical and nonclinical) moving across borders remains fragmented, incomplete, unreliable or, in many instances, unknown [28]; p.8.

Measuring different EU health systems presents a number of methodological and analytical challenges for researchers. The lack of comparable quantitative data for country analysis is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant issue. Financialisation is a diverse concept with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions on important societal and economic issues including income and wealth inequality [5]. Healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation resonates with the broader <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation thesis, with an example of the digital divide between member states [40,41]. To understand the digital divide in healthcare, policy-makers call for more comparative studies on healthcare on topics such as bilateral arrangements between health providers and <sup>fi</sup>nancing organisations, reimbursement of health costs, health data sharing between professionals and patients, and common standards of quality and delivery of care. Currently, EU Member States produce eHealth roadmaps outlining policy objectives. However, more robust indicators and metrics are needed for comparing socio-economic and technical diversity across countries.

Comparative studies must clarify the unit of analysis as caution is needed to avoid a ‘one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all’ approach to EU health policy development and implementation. Units of analysis of national, regional, local and organisational levels need to be de<sup>fi</sup>ned [63]. At the national level, the use of aggregated country data, using a single ICT indicator (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>xed broadband subscriptions per 100 people) provides only a snapshot of a country’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le. This indicator can then be compared across all the countries, using a ranking from the highest to lowest. To obtain a deeper appreciation of a country’s ICT pro<sup>fi</sup>le, several indicators can be measured and compared (i.e., broadband and Internet availability and use). Attention to detail is needed as countries have differing rates of broadband penetration and Internet adoption. Thus, any underlying reasons for variation need to be explained.

While it is assumed that the two indicators are comparable, the levels may be different even within the same country, suggesting potentially misleading results [14]. At the organisational level, a different set of problems emerge as the unit of analysis is obscured by complex organisational structures. For example, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of hospital may vary across countries and regions [42]. A hospital may be spread across many buildings or sites. A large teaching hospital on many sites differs from a small community hospital. A hospital may offer widely differing services such as accident and emergency and specialised cardiology care compared with another hospital that may specialise in maternity care. Many academic studies use the organisation as the unit of analysis, but appreciating country variation in symbolic and material de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of hospital is crucial for meaningful comparative analysis.

The drivers of healthcare <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation include a wide variety of factors in an ever-changing socio-political and economic landscape. The 2008 global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected health spending, reducing the progress in cross-border health policy [8]. Selection of a limited range of indicators/metrics (i.e., under 20) gives only a general picture of EU country pro<sup>fi</sup>les, rather than more detailed and nuanced country examples. In addition, our selection of quantitative indicators and variables is restricted to ensure reliable statistical analysis. While some EU Member States appear to be making more progress than others across the range of indicators selected for comparative analysis, our conclusions arise from a narrow dataset and are not representative of performance in other areas. The scope of this study provides greater depth of analysis using fewer indicators, rather than a much larger number of indicators across only a few countries (the latter more common in case study research).

The research for this paper contributes to health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation debates by exploring the intersection between health systems and ICT in the European context. The research is positioned in current policy debates on <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation in the context of crossborder healthcare, which is a key policy objective of EU leaders [11]. The complex relationship between eGovernment and eHealth is further explored, which we argue is critical to tackling health inequality. The next section presents the research method, with some further discussion of methodological challenges in comparative country studies.

## 4. Research method

Key EU policy priorities encourage healthy ageing and lifestyle, preventive medicine, improving health security, reducing health inequalities, and the promotion and dissemination of health information [11]. In a <sup>fi</sup>nancialised health environment, disparities in access to health services and technology greatly reduce meeting these goals. A fundamental requirement of providing citizens with more and better information about health systems (whether in their own country or across the EU) is to ensure that everyone has access to GPTs such as broadband and the Internet [2]. To evaluate any relationship, comparable measures across all the EU countries are collected. Two data sources were used: Eurostat and the World Bank.

Central to the EU is the need to measure and compare data on health systems and ICT. To ensure meaningful comparisons are made, the European Statistical System has been developed over many years, enforced by EU regulation [43,44] and managed by Eurostat. The other source of data is the World Bank, which measures various country health performance indicators. The data are largely supplied by the statistical of<sup>fi</sup>ces of member countries. Although it is outside the scope of this study to assess the validity of the data collection process used by these two organisations, the integrity of their datasets is unmatched by any other public or commercial datasets available to researchers.

To answer the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, data were collected, which allowed comparative country analysis across two areas: (1) health data (usage and sharing) and (2) ICT infrastructure (access and availability). Table 1 identi<sup>fi</sup>es the 12 quantitative indicators that were used.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a technique that is used to analyse this data. The objective is to try and reduce the complexity of what was being measured using a process that is widely used in Marketing and Social Sciences [45]. The goal is to look for broad relationships between the variables and to use this to cluster all the countries into groups that share similar characteristics. To achieve this, several key steps need to be followed. The data are analysed for latent factors that account for any collinearity between the variables, assuming that the relationship between them is linear. If found, a subjective decision is made on the number of factors to be extracted. PCA will then be run and the results rotated to help clarify the relationships that have been discovered. To position the countries into similar groups, they are clustered using mathematical techniques that match countries showing the greatest similarity [46]. The bottomup approach of agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) was initially used to develop a dendrogram of homogenous groups in ‘a special type of tree structure that provides a convenient picture of a hierarchical cluster’ [47]; p. 59. ## 5. Analysis and results

## 5.1. Data validity checks

To con<sup>fi</sup>rm the validity of this data, four tests were initially run. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was the Bartlett sphericity test (see Table A1), which generated a p-value (<0.0001). At the significance level of a = 0.05, the null hypothesis (h ) (that the data came from a population where the intercorrelation matrix was an identity matrix) was rejected. The second was the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (see Table A2), which would return a value of 1.0 if the variables were measuring a common factor. The value calculated was 0.867, suggesting that the sample size was large enough to extract a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of variance. The third test calculated Cronbach’s alpha at 0.944, again suggesting that these variables shared a high coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of consistency (or reliability) if viewed as a group [49]. This value needs to be at least 0.9 if important inference can be made from the data [50]. This last test had a normally distributed score, which meant that any estimate would be more reliable when measuring an underlying (or latent) construct [51]. The <sup>fi</sup>nal test that was run on all the variables was the Kruskal–Wallis test. If a collection of data samples are independent, then they will come from unrelated populations that will not affect each other. This test calculated a p-factor <0.0001, which meant that $\mathrm { h } _ { \mathrm { o } }$ (the samples come from the same population) could be rejected.

## 5.3. Determination of the number of factors to extract

The next stage of the analysis was to determine how many factors should be extracted. The correlation matrix strongly suggested that there were at least two factors because of the pairings that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed; however, support for this subjective decision was made by assessing the eigenvalues that were calculated, and this is shown in Table 4.

The Guttman–Kaiser rule rejects any factors whose eigenvalue (l) is <1 because its contribution to the variance is trivial (unless l > 1, it does not explain any more variance than a single variable). Table 3 shows that only two factors (accounting for over three quarters of the total variance) met this condition. The first l = 7.48 accounted for over 62% of the variance, while the second l = 1.55 accounted for 12.9%. While the third factor accounted for approximately 6%, l = 0.7 was considerably below the minimum value. Another technique that is used to support the number of factors chosen comes from the image created when the eigenvalues are plotted on a graph – the scree plot. This again supported the selection of just two factors.

Because the factors were independent of each other, orthogonal rotation is used to help simplify the reading of the variables. The results of using varimax rotation and calculated communalities’ (h<sup>2</sup>) are shown in Table 5, where the highest loadings are shown in bold. Once again, the Cronbach Alpha values that were calculated for each dimension were ‘Excellent’, (D1 = 0.937 and D2 = 0.924), con<sup>fi</sup>rming the high reliability of the factors that were extracted. Together, these two latent dimensions accounted for 75.2% of the total variance.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst seven variables load highly on just D1 and have all been interpreted as ‘excellent’, while all but one of the remaining <sup>fi</sup>ve variables loads highly on D2, again with an ‘excellent’ interpretation [53]. The remaining variable apptWeb, although complex (loading on D1–Poor; D2–Very Good), contributes much more of its variance to D2. The cut-off value was chosen as 0.4 as this would only contribute 16% to the variance [54]; p.441.

This <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension contributes 42.6% to the variance and is made up by those variables associated with ICT infrastructure (access and availability). The second dimension contributes 32.5% to the variance and includes those variables that are associated with health data (both usage and its sharing). These two dimensions are shown in Fig A1, and the variables that are associated with each have been labelled. Fig. 1 illustrates how the EU Member States have been plotted against the rotated factor scores.

## 5.4. Cluster analysis using factored scores

The next step was to cluster (group) together those countries that had similar scores, and this was performed by <sup>fi</sup>rst using AHC and then seeding a non-hierarchical algorithm (k-means) with the number of clusters that needed to be created. The AHC classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method calculates the dissimilarities between the objects using the Euclidean distance and <sup>fi</sup>ve different agglomeration linkage methods: complete, <sup>fl</sup>exible, single, unweighted pairgroup average and ward. The program used to calculate the optimal number of clusters returned that just three were necessary. Table A3 presents the within-class variance that was calculated by generating one to six clusters, while Fig. A2 plots this information and illustrates where three class cluster lie. Fig. 2 presents the dendrogram that was generated using Ward’s method on the factored scores. The horizontal axis represents the dissimilarity value between each node (country), and the vertical axis is the country (which generated the proximity matrix used to calculate the binary tree). This method is distinct from others, using ANOVA to determine the distances between the clusters. It is an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient process, but this criterion can only be run when using Euclidean distance [55].

Having determined that there were three clusters across all the EU countries, the second step was to seed the k-means algorithm with this value. This calculated the three clusters that are shown in Fig. 3, which have been labelled as ‘Frontrunners’, ‘Followers’ and ‘Laggards’.

Table 6 details both general statistics and the Kurskal–Wallis values that were calculated for each cluster. The Kurskal–Wallis test is used to con<sup>fi</sup>rm whether the members of a sample have come from populations that have similar parameters. For all three clusters, at a signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of 1%, $\mathrm { h } _ { \mathrm { o } }$ (the samples came from the same population) was rejected. Using Dunn’s procedure, it was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that both Factor D1 and Factor D2 were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from each other within each cluster.

While cluster analysis has shown that there are differences between those countries that make up each cluster, further analysis is necessary to try and explain why those differences exist.

5.5. Comparison of the k-means cluster using factored and original variables

As mentioned above, the data that had been collected were viewed in two ways: the <sup>fi</sup>rst was to cluster the data (following con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of its suitability) on scores created by PCA and rotation, while the second was to determine new clusters that were generated from the original variables. These two clusters were then compared to determine any differences. Following the steps already presented, the original variables were initially run through <sup>fi</sup>ve different AHC methods. Three clusters were again identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and this was used to seed the k-means algorithm. The differences between these clusters are illustrated in Fig. 4, and this shows that apart from two counties all the group members were the same. The ‘Follower’ cluster was increased by the addition of Portugal from the Laggards and Spain from the Frontrunners clusters. These countries are both positioned at the boundaries of their cluster, and such movement is supported by the increased level of detail, which is available when 12 variables are used rather than the two latent dimensions.

The decomposition of the k-means clusters for both the factored and original variables is given in Tables A5 and A6. Such similarity supports the existence of the country groupings when using the two latent dimensions that are given by our model.

## 6. Discussion

Comparing ICT infrastructure (access and availability) and health data (usage and sharing) provides some interesting insights on health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation and ICT in EU Member States. In particular, <sup>fi</sup>ndings show highly differentiated country pro<sup>fi</sup>les in ICT and health data with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant policy implications (inputs and outputs) for cross-country health. Population size does not make the collected data any less reliable because of the structured statistical process that had to be followed by each country – otherwise any EU comparison would be impossible. What these population differences do show is the complexity and scale of providing service within each country and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of being able to share cross-country facilities. The diversity of health and ICT country pro<sup>fi</sup>les falls within one of three distinct clusters: Frontrunners, Followers and Laggards. Each of these is discussed in the following sections.

## 6.1. The frontrunners cluster

The leading countries in the Frontrunners cluster include Denmark, Finland, Sweden, Netherlands, Spain and Estonia. It is of little surprise that Scandinavian countries are found in this category, particularly as this <sup>fi</sup>nding con<sup>fi</sup>rms prior studies on ICT, the digital divide and health/eHealth [57]. Denmark, Sweden and the Netherlands each score 92% for percentage of all individuals who have used a search engine to <sup>fi</sup>nd information (srchEng). Comparing this with only 50% in Romania and 62% in Greece for the same indicator provides an example of the wide variation between Member States in ICT infrastructure and availability among EU citizens.

The largest country in this cluster is Spain with over 46 million inhabitants. This country is divided into 17 autonomous regions, each with its own geography and culture. The region of Catalonia is widely known for its excellent health services, with many British, Russian and Eastern European tourists visiting each year. Health tourism to this region is increasing with a group of Barcelona hospitals and medical centres working with the city’s tourism consortium and the Catalan Government to form Barcelona Medical Destination as an international hub of excellence for medical treatment. Currently, around 20,000 foreign citizens travel to Catalonia annually to receive treatment at one of the private hospitals taking part in the initiative. The aim of the new proposal is to increase this by 20% in 2 years.

A regional analysis of cross-border healthcare offers a more nuanced approach as some regions within the same country are more advanced than others. Estonia, which also maps into the Leader category, is a good example of a country with a very small population (around 1.3 million), which has made substantial progress towards eHealth. The EHR is a nationwide system that integrates data from a variety of healthcare providers to develop an individual patient record.

The technological challenge is to ensure that health data retrieved from various providers using different systems is presented in a standard format. This provides access to patients’ records easily from a single electronic <sup>fi</sup>le as test results (including X-rays) can be read in real time as they are entered. These advantages are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to health professionals and patients within a particular region or country. The critical question for EU policy-makers in promoting cross-border healthcare is whether member states agree to share patient data with each other. While this aim may be embedded into the narrative of EU policy, the practice may be somewhat different and dependent upon countrylevel privacy and security laws that override other considerations.

## 6.2. The followers cluster

Countries in the Followers cluster demonstrate a range of opportunities and barriers in relation to cross-country healthcare. Legal barriers cover issues of data protection, health professional obligations and patients’ rights. Technical barriers include lack of interoperability in eGovernment and eHealth systems operated by physicians, hospitals, insurance funds and other stakeholders. The Followers cluster is occupied by the largest (Germany, UK and France) and the smallest (Luxembourg and Malta) member states. Countries from Eastern Europe also make up this cluster (Hungary,

Czech Republic Lithuania and Slovenia), which comprises 12 in total. Despite the position of France as having one of the best health systems worldwide [7], the country scores relatively low for some indicators, including only 40% for individuals using the Internet seeking health-related information (srchHth) and 39.4% for GPs exchanging medical patient data with other healthcare providers and professionals (exchPat). This compares with the Netherlands, which scores 61% (srchHth) and 76% (exchPat). One explanation for the French results is the excellent health service provisioning for citizens across the whole country. Therefore, French citizens can access high-quality healthcare where there are close links between the GP surgeries, hospitals and pharmacies.

The results suggest the need for citizens to use eGovernment and eHealth services for searching and exchanging health data is reduced in well-resourced and mature health systems with relatively high patient satisfaction scores. Countries such as Germany, UK and France with established institutional structures and practices apparently offer reduced incentives for health professionals and citizen to engage in cross-border health. In the UK, for example (which has voted to leave the EU in a referendum in June 2016), the National Programme for IT, introduced as a technological innovation to develop electronic health records, was burdened by con<sup>fl</sup>icting narratives that either supported or condemned the programme. Despite extensive efforts from the government to ensure the programme was a success, it was subsequently abandoned as several stakeholder groups remained unconvinced about the value of the programme to the health system and to patients [58,27]. A parallel can be drawn with cross-border health as the narrative in support of the policy needs to be weighed against the practical implications for health systems at the country level.

Legal constraints, for example, act as barriers in cross-border EU health. Germany has some of the most stringent data privacy laws in Europe. To promote further digitisation in the health sector, a patient data management system will give German doctors access to emergency patient data through electronic health (eHealth) cards in the event of an emergency. Patients will need to give their consent for the storage of their emergency patient data on the card. Doctors will be incentivised to input details of their decisions about patients into the new system but face <sup>fi</sup>nancial penalties for failure to do so. Data protection is a top priority in Germany where the law requires access to data on the data management system to be controlled and logged and for medical data to be encrypted. In common with other countries in the Followers cluster, Germany is making progress towards improving its ICT infrastructure and health data sharing in fostering the transformation towards realtime eHealth where it was described as ‘in the mid<sup>fi</sup>eld when it comes to the implementation of e-health solutions’ [59].

Technological infrastructure and applications have evolved considerably over several decades where GPTs are more widely available to citizens. Government and media publications review opportunities and barriers of trade in EU health services, with some overstating the ease of patient (citizen) access and availability of cross-border healthcare and others discussing the pitfalls of medical tourism. While technology is portrayed as the great leveller in policy documents for its potential to reduce health inequalities across rich and poor countries (and regions), our comparative data suggest that the wider implications of health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation will bring about considerable challenges to EU health integration policy.

## 6.3. The laggards cluster

The Laggards cluster describes those countries with relatively low scores across the two dimensions. This category includes 10 countries with substantially fewer resources in ICT infrastructure and healthcare. The <sup>fi</sup>nancial constraints over the past few years, broadly described as ‘austerity measures’, suggest further delays in achieving the EU policy objectives in extending cross-border healthcare. Our composite data show <sup>fi</sup>xed broadband subscriptions (fixedSub) as an indicator of ICT availability are much less for countries such as Romania (18%), Bulgaria (20%) and Poland (23%) than for the highest performer, Denmark (41.4%). The number of citizens actively seeking healthcare abroad or even using the Internet to <sup>fi</sup>nd out about local services is greatly restricted in countries with poor ICT infrastructure and availability. Combined with the large variation in the percentage of GDP each country spends on health [64], with France at 11.7% (highest) and Romania at 5.3% (lowest), additional barriers may also emerge where different tariffs for services, cost sharing and reimbursement arrangements and geographical restrictions on travel may all in<sup>fl</sup>uence a patient’s ability and motivation to seek cross-border healthcare.

Despite a lack of useful data on eHealth access and usage, indicators of GPs exchanging medical patient data with other healthcare providers and professionals (exchPat) and GPs using electronic networks to transfer prescriptions to pharmacists (exchPres) reveal some signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in country pro<sup>fi</sup>les. For example, Slovenia is the country with the lowest frequency, only 5% for exchPat compared with 91.8% for Denmark. Similarly for exchPat, Estonia reports a 100% score, with many other countries reporting <5% (Bulgaria, Hungary, Ireland, Portugal, Poland, Slovak Republic and Slovenia). While this study is limited by the lack of cross-country datasets, the data empirically support prior studies and anecdotal accounts of wide variation in health systems [28].

For cross-country healthcare to become a reality, our study identi<sup>fi</sup>es the need for more empirical data in a variety of thematic areas including (but not limited to) protection and patient rights regarding cross-country mobility, <sup>fi</sup>nancial provisioning for patients seeking treatment across member states, regional arrangements for public health insurance systems, development of the private insurance market, reimbursement mechanisms for health services, and availability and quality of healthcare.

## 7. Conclusion

This study uses multivariate statistical methods to analyse country pro<sup>fi</sup>les in health systems across the 28 EU Members States. As health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation becomes embedded in the narratives of EU directives and policies, the demand for more and better <sup>fi</sup>nancial indicators and metrics is growing among politicians, health professionals and citizens alike. Combining indicators that measure the percentage of citizens with access to, and use of, eGovernment and eHealth, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three groupings from our cluster analysis: Frontrunners, Followers and Laggards. The leading countries demonstrate strong pro<sup>fi</sup>les combining ICT infrastructure and eHealth, where some countries are making signi<sup>fi</sup>cant inroads in using and exchanging electronic patient health data with other service providers. Follower countries that tend to have mature health systems demonstrate fewer incentives to adopt cross-border health, contrary to the countries in the Laggards category, which have greater incentives to pursue cross-border health because of the systemic and economic constraints of their health systems.

Our study makes three observations to debate the surrounding health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation and ICT for policy-makers, health professionals, citizens, patients and many other health stakeholders. First, it extends comparative cross-national research using composite indicators to measure EU health systems. This study does not offer a detailed analysis of cross-country health at the member state level. Rather, it identi<sup>fi</sup>es and illustrates many complex issues and challenges underpinned by health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation that go beyond technology to include the legal, regulatory, <sup>fi</sup>nancial and cultural attributes of health systems. Diversity plays an essential part in unpacking how EU directives, which promote integrated healthcare, face dif<sup>fi</sup>cult challenges as national and regional policy-makers apply their own laws and policies that prioritise their own social and health needs. The variability of the different health systems across the EU raises many questions about systemic and contextual obstacles facing EU policy-makers in promoting cross-border health, with the result that policy narratives become challenged by <sup>fi</sup>nancial imperatives.

Second, our study contributes to interdisciplinary research on health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation and the digital divide [60]. While the vast majority of management research examines developed countries, our study using composite indicators to compare and contrast country pro<sup>fi</sup>les not only illustrates the complexity and uniqueness of diverse health systems but also raises questions about the robustness of EU-wide policy to engineer socio-political and cultural change, particularly in countries with under-resourced health systems. For example, the GDP spent on health varies greatly across the EU Member States. So against a background of challenging <sup>fi</sup>nancial conditions facing health systems [12], many EU countries continue to lag behind other countries not only in their ICT and eHealth capabilities but also in their capacity to invest in basic healthcare. Countries will thus need to make dif<sup>fi</sup>cult decisions to prioritise how they spend limited resources, with eHealth possibly not registering as the most important priority.

Third, as global health systems become increasingly <sup>fi</sup>nancialised, a paradoxical scenario may arise as EU policy on cross-border health is challenged by politicians and public. Member states will increasingly ration EU-wide citizens’ access to health services because of pressures to meet national and local health-related targets. ICT, promoted as an enabler in cross-border health, may also become a tool to restrict citizens’ access to health services. In a climate of continuing economic austerity, the country pro<sup>fi</sup>les

Comparative country differences in reimbursement arrangements, under-capacity or over-capacity in health services, bilateral cost-sharing agreements, variable tariffs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts packages are all examples of health <sup>fi</sup>nancialisation, which may adversely affect the EU policy for cross-border health. Member states will continue to prioritise ICT infrastructure and eHealth investments for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of their own health systems. Further work is thus needed to develop quality indicators, which measure citizens’ satisfaction with health services [28]; p. 347 and how ICT can facilitate data sharing and health tourism. Academic research will be further enriched by extending our geographical reach to include countries with less developed ICT pro<sup>fi</sup>les. Global health systems and technologies are changing fast. Future directions for research may consider the concept of leapfrogging [61], where health providers adopt mobile technologies, cloud computing and other electronic devices/applications to promote cross-border health. Leapfrogging in this context does not refer to one country overtaking another in its ICT capability. Instead, it illustrates how a technology may be selected to develop health services that have previously used earlier (legacy) technologies. Mobile health offers a contemporary example where evidence shows that developing countries are leapfrogging traditional technologies to offer health services to local citizens, which were previously not possible [62]. Taken together, the three country groupings identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study are evolving in a dynamic and changing socio-political and economic climate. Over time, countries with more advanced ICT infrastructure and eHealth may be slow to adapt to change, while those with less developed health systems seize upon new technologies to build digital health services at lower cost, without the restrictions of institutionalised structures.
