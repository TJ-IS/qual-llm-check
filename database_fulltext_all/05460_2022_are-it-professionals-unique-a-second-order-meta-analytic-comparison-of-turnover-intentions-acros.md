# ARE IT PROFESSIONALS UNIQUE? A SECOND-ORDER META-ANALYTIC COMPARISON OF TURNOVER INTENTIONS ACROSS OCCUPATIONS<sup>1</sup>

Sam Zaza Information Systems and Analytics, Jones College of Business, Middle Tennessee State University, Murfreesboro, TN, U.S.A. {sam.zaza@mtsu.edu}

Damien Joseph Information Technology & Operations Management, Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore {adjoseph@ntu.edu.sg}

Deborah J. Armstrong

Department of Business Analytics, Information Systems and Supply Chain, College of Business, Florida State University, Tallahassee, FL, U.S.A. {djarmstrong@business.fsu.edu}

Information technology (IT) professionals are a strategic human resource for enabling competitive advantage through the application of data and technologies. Yet, it remains a challenge for organizations to retain top IT talent as the business context and the nature of work change. Retention strategies that have worked with other business professionals have faced limited success with IT talent, leading some scholars to ask whether the latter are unique in terms of culture, personality, and tools. This study seeks to address this question by (1) undertaking a meta-analysis of studies investigating the turnover intentions of IT professionals, and by (2) conducting a second-order meta-analysis to compare findings between IT and non-IT professionals. The meta-analytic findings identify new antecedents more recently examined in information systems (IS) research while confirming enduring relationships between antecedents and turnover intention. The second-order meta-analysis provides intriguing findings regarding the potential uniqueness of IT professionals. We provide an integrative discussion on the state of turnover intention research within the IS discipline, start a dialog on interdisciplinary comparisons, and offer a forwardlooking agenda for future research. We conclude by calling on scholars to revitalize turnover intention research within the IS field by moving toward fresh theories, fresh constructs, and fresh approaches.

Keywords: IT professionals’ uniqueness, meta-analysis, second-order meta-analysis, meta of metaanalysis, turnover intention, IT workforce, IT talent management

## Introduction

Information technology (IT)<sup>2</sup> is a key strategic resource for enabling competitive advantage (Tyler et al., 2019). The centrality of the IT role is generalizable across industries and economies as firms embrace business process transformations through digitalization and automation (Fitzgerald et al., 2014; Hess et al., 2020). Therefore, while retaining IT talent is of increasing importance, it is a challenging task because of the persistent talent shortages in the IT labor market and the “turnover culture” within the IT field (Moore & Burke-Smalley, 2002). There is much evidence over the years that the churn in IT labor becomes even more pronounced when demand outstrips supply (e.g., Cetron et al., 1988; Challenger, 2003; Lerman, 1998; Xue & Larson, 2015). Hence, it behooves us to understand the antecedents of turnover intention (i.e., an employee’s desire to voluntarily leave a job,

Joseph et al., 2007) to aid organizational talent management initiatives, human resource (HR) managers, and managers of IT professionals.

Information systems (IS) research has a long and deep intellectual history of examining the antecedents of turnover intention (TOI). Still, a comprehensive review of the scientific evidence on TOI within the IS discipline has not occurred since Joseph et al. (2007). Since then, research on this topic has accelerated, technology-related innovation has exploded, and the global business landscape has changed significantly. For example, the emergence of new IT job roles such as UI/UX designer and data analyst, new technologies such as Web 2.0 and mobile computing, and new ways of working such as gig and human-robot collaboration are impacting the IT field. These substantive issues present an opportunity to examine the implications of these sea changes on the strength and direction of established and emergent antecedents that may drive TOI within the changing nature of IT work. And while technological change can impact all employees, we argue that not all employees will respond similarly.

There has been a long-standing question within the IS discipline about whether (and to what degree) IT professionals are different from other professionals. Recent research has argued that IT professionals are different (e.g., Brooks et al., 2011; Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021; Tambe & Hitt, 2014). However, empirical evidence to answer this question remains limited. We add to this limited but growing body of evidence by investigating the differences and similarities in the antecedents of IT professional TOI (RQ1) and the similarities and differences in the antecedents of TOI that exist between IT and non-IT professionals (RQ2).

Three research goals drive this study. The first research goal aims to establish the state of the IS TOI literature, including historical trends and more recent developments. We conducted a meta-analysis of primary studies on IT professional TOI to update and extend the only meta-analysis on the topic (Joseph et al., 2007), which synthesized 26 empirical studies. In our research, 139 studies are metaanalyzed, indicating that 82% of the IT TOI research occurred after the Joseph et al. (2007) meta-analysis. This technique is appropriate for three reasons. First, narrative literature reviews cannot empirically resolve inconsistent findings across studies (Hunter & Schmidt, 2004). Second, meta-analysis corrects for artifacts that may attenuate the effect size between the variables of each study. Therefore, we can correct the effect size reported in primary studies by accounting for sampling errors due to smaller sample sizes and measurement errors due to imperfect scales (Schmidt & Hunter, 2014). By doing so, we can compute precise estimates of the population effects or relationships between constructs. Third, a meta-analysis can examine attenuations that may be attributed to research design, methodological, or contextual factors (Schmidt & Hunter, 2014).

For the second research goal, this study intends to use the antecedent relationships of a phenomenon common to all professions—turnover intentions—to synthesize the TOI literature across disciplines. This is appropriate as metaanalyses have been published in the management literature illustrating many of the relationships with TOI identified in the current meta-analysis (e.g., Alarcon, 2011; Tett & Meyer, 1993). To accomplish this, we compare the meta-analysis results obtained from the IS literature with the meta-analyses available in the management literature through the secondary uses of meta-analysis (Schmidt & Oh, 2013). By doing so, we can determine whether the antecedent-TOI relationships are generalizable across workers or if some may be idiosyncratic to IT professionals.

Building on the findings of the previous two research goals, the third research goal aims to contribute to the TOI conversation by providing an integrative, future-oriented discussion of the TOI literature and recommendations for future work. A research agenda is provided so that IS research efforts might be directed toward theory development utilizing emergent (i.e., understudied) constructs and relationships with TOI that are idiosyncratic to the IT profession.

## IT Professionals’ Turnover Intentions

So, what is IT TOI, and why is it important for researchers in our discipline? TOI is an employee’s plan to voluntarily change companies and continue in the same job role (Schyns et al., 2007). IT TOI occurs when a worker within the IT field leaves one organization and is employed in an equivalent job role in a different organization (Joseph et al., 2012). IT professionals are a key resource because they are charged with acquiring, developing, and/or managing the IT resources and assets of a firm (Ang et al., 2014). According to Ferratt et al. (2005), “to the extent that IT human capital is rare, inimitable, and heterogeneously distributed, and therefore provides the possibility of bestowing competitive advantage upon the firm, the effective management of this resource represents a significant area of managerial concern” (pp. 237-238). IT TOI is a major concern because organizations risk losing 10-15% of their IT talent every year (Bean 2019), and projections suggest this trend will continue.

With the growing popularity of crowdsourcing platforms, consulting opportunities, and startup companies, there is a shift away from the traditional career progression, which adds to the already high turnover within the IT sector. In addition to the number of IT professionals turning over, the increasing integration of IT into organizational operations and the acceleration in IT innovation exacerbates concerns regarding the strategic impact of IT TOI. Organizations make significant investments in their IT assets and staff. The more organizations invest in and rely on technology to support their business processes (Kretschmer, 2012), the more critical the role of their IT assets and IT human capital. Thus, realizing the benefits of IT investments requires the strategic utilization of an organization’s IT human capital (Lee et al., 1995).

The IT sector is perceived as a high-pressure environment where it is challenging to keep up with the specialized technical skills required, making it harder for companies to recruit and retain individuals with the needed skills (Setor et al., 2015). In fact, “faster knowledge depreciation because of technological advances makes IT professionals who move from firm to firm and gain greater exposure to different IT applications more valuable than the professionals who work in a single environment” (Mithas & Krishnan, 2008, p. 428).

Managing IT professionals effectively requires an understanding of the IT occupational culture, the needs of individual IT workers, and the key practices that can influence retention. Research has established the value of strategic IT human resource (HR) management practice configurations for the organization and TOI. For example, IT HR management practices focusing on career development and security, compensation, and a balance of concern for the individual and productivity, provide substantial IT retention payoffs (Agarwal & Ferrat, 2002). Specifically, work arrangement preferences for IT professionals have been found to differentially influence job satisfaction (Enns et al., 2006; Prasad et al., 2007). A more recent study found that two configurations of HR management practices—human capital focused (HCF) and task focused (TF)—exhibit a synergistic relationship with the job search behavior (i.e., pre-TOI behavior) of IT professionals such that those with the HCF configuration had lower than expected job search behavior, and those with the TF configuration had higher than expected job search behavior (Ferratt et al. 2012). Taken together, we see that IT professionals are key organizational resources (assets), and unpacking the established drivers of IT-TOI is an important baseline to establish.

## Research Goal 1: Meta-Analysis

## Literature Search and Inclusion Criteria

To determine their correlational strength and direction, we conducted a first-order meta-analysis of the TOI antecedents examined in prior IS studies. We used multiple sources to collect studies on IT professionals’ TOI. First, we included all

26 studies examined in Joseph et al.’s (2007) meta-analysis of IT TOI. Second, we searched multiple electronic databases (e.g., PsycINFO, Business Source Complete, Academic Search Complete, ABI/INFORM Complete, AIS e-Library, proceedings of the HICSS, and SIGMIS-CPR conferences, Google Scholar, and Dissertation Abstracts). We systematically searched these databases using the following combinations of terms appearing in the title or abstract: (“turnover” or “turnover intention” or “intention to leave” or “intention to quit”) and (“information technology” or “information systems” or “IT” or “IS”) and (“professionals” or “workers” or “employees” or “personnel”).

Third, we used the Web of Science Cited Reference Search to identify articles referencing key papers about IT professionals’ TOI (e.g., Ahuja et al., 2007; Ghapanchi & Aurum, 2011; Joseph et al., 2007; Lo, 2015; Moore, 2000). We then conducted a forward/backward search of the literature for completeness. Fourth, we used the institutional library’s document delivery systems to collect publications and dissertations unavailable online. Finally, we looked for unpublished or research-in-progress studies on IT turnover by posting calls for papers on listservs such as AISWORLD, OCISNET, and SIGMIS. We did not receive any unpublished or work-in-progress studies through this channel. In total, we identified 191 studies through June 15, 2021, on IT professionals’ TOI.

## Inclusion Criteria and Coding Procedure

Articles had to meet four criteria to be included in this review (see Table 1). One, we included primary studies that reported measures of the associations between TOI and its antecedents. We, therefore, excluded conceptual, narrative, systematic reviews, and meta-analysis studies. We included studies where the unit of analysis was the individual IT professional and excluded articles that focused on all other levels of analysis (e.g., collective turnover, turnover rate). Two, we included studies that examined TOI and excluded studies that examined the actual turnover behavior of IT professionals without examining TOI. Studies that examined variations of TOI (e.g., a dimension of turnover) or other forms of job mobility (e.g., turnaway) were also excluded. Three, we included studies reporting the minimum necessary information to conduct a meta-analysis—a correlation between an antecedent and TOI and the sample size used to estimate that correlation. Four, we excluded studies in which the sample contained a mix of IT and non-IT employees or if the same sample was repeated (i.e., used for multiple studies). As such, we excluded dissertations and conference papers that were later published as journal articles to avoid double counting (e.g., Ahuja et al., 2002; Ahuja et al., 2007).

Of the 191 identified articles, 134 (70.2%) met all the criteria for inclusion. The 134 papers included 104 published papers and 30 manuscripts (e.g., published and unpublished dissertations). One paper (i.e., Korunka et al., 2008) contributed two independent samples, and another (Chang et al., 2020) contributed five independent samples. As such, we metaanalyzed 139 studies<sup>3</sup>. We coded each study’s sample size, the correlation between TOI and its antecedent(s), and the reliability statistics of the antecedent(s) and TOI. Two authors and two research assistants coded the studies that passed all inclusion criteria and attained an initial intercoder agreement of 87%. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

## Meta-Analytic Procedures

We used a random-effects meta-analysis (Hunter & Schmidt, 2000) because samples of IT professionals and their context may vary across studies. Hence, we assume that there was a distribution of effect sizes and that variations of effect sizes were attributable to random within-studies errors, random between-studies errors, and research-related artifacts. Using Hunter and Schmidt’s (2004) psychometric approach, we corrected each correlation for sampling and measurement errors using the sample size and reliability estimates. If the reliability estimates of the constructs were not reported in the paper, we computed the mean reliability using the Spearman-Brown formula (Van Iddekinge et al., 2012). The 95% confidence interval indicates significant effect sizes, not including the zero point (Whitener, 1990).

The Rosenthal’s failsafe calculations indicate that there were sufficient studies to minimize publication bias for most of the relationships. Rosenthal (1979, 1986) noted that studies with significant results were more likely to be published, i.e., a publication bias. Thus, the failsafe N estimates the number of studies with zero effect sizes that would increase the p-value above 0.05. The recommendation is for the failsafe N estimate to exceed 5k + 10, where k is the number of effect sizes in the analysis (Del Vecchio & O’Leary, 2004; Ma & Kishor, 1997; Rosenthal, 1979, 1986).<sup>4</sup> For instance, the threat of obsolescence-TOI relationship (#20 in Table 2) has a failsafe N of 6. This means that six studies with zero effect sizes are needed to nullify the effect of the threat of obsolescence on TOI. While we did our due diligence in finding all the articles on the threat of professional obsolescence and TOI for this study, we might have overlooked six studies with zero effect sizes. Therefore, we cannot draw a definitive conclusion about the effect of the threat of obsolescence on IT TOI.

## Are IT Professionals Unique?

Voluntary TOI is an evaluation that almost every worker makes at some point in a career and has been examined in many disciplines (e.g., Park & Min, 2020). Early IS research (e.g., Baroudi, 1985; Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1992; Thatcher et al., 2002) on the TOI of IT professionals was generally congruent with the relationships observed among non-IT professionals, particularly in the management literature. The unstated assumption underlying these early studies was that all workers evaluate TOI in a similar manner (Rubenstein et al., 2018). As the IS TOI research stream evolved, scholars began to explore more IT-focused variables and observed relationships that potentially deviated from those expected for non-IT professionals.

To address the second research question, this study compares the drivers of TOI between IT workers and workers in other occupations. Even in the management literature, there is a precedent for investigating similarities and differences across occupations (e.g., Johnson et al., 2005; Maruyama & Tietze, 2012). For example, Hom et al. (1992) compared the antecedents of TOI between military and civilian occupations, and between nursing and nonnursing occupations. Hom et al. found “dissimilar structural relations among turnover causes across these occupations” (p. 894). Similarly, another study found that IT professionals’ creative process preferences were similar to those in other occupations (e.g., engineers) but distinct from other occupations (e.g., finance) (Puccio et al., 2018).

The IT profession’s occupational culture is distinct from an organization’s culture (e.g., Ramachandran & Rao, 2006). The IT profession “is not bounded by any one organization but rather cuts across all organizations” (Jacks & Palvia, 2014, p. 20), and “only work experience allows for a better understanding of it” (Guzman & Stanton, 2009, p. 183). Prommegger and colleagues (2020) suggest that the unique aspects of IT fall into three categories—occupation (e.g., culture), human (e.g., personality), and technological (e.g., rapid technology change).

The empirical evidence supports the uniqueness of the IT profession: Jacks et al. (2018) confirmed that IT professionals rated a set of occupational culture values (particularly, autonomy in decision-making) significantly higher than non-IT professionals. Guzman et al. (2008, p. 33) pointed to “the use of technical jargon, primary value of technical knowledge, extreme and unusual demands … related to the constant change of IT, feelings of superiority and a general lack of formal rules” as unique to the IT profession. Wynekoop and Walz (1998) compared IT professionals to population norms on the California Psychological Inventory Adjective Check List (ACL). The scholars found differences on 19 of the 24 scales. Finally, Fu (2010) showed that IT professionals have lower social needs and a higher need for achievement than non-IT individuals and that the threat of professional obsolescence and professional self-efficacy are more salient for IT professionals compared to non-IT individuals. Hence, it is not surprising that Riemenschneider and Armstrong (2021, p. 1154) offered “perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession” as a multidimensional construct that “captures the characteristics of the IS work environment that differentiate the IS function from other functional areas.”

## Second-Order Meta-Analysis

Based on our literature review, the idea that IT professionals may be unique or distinct from other professionals is emerging as an area of scholarly interest. In addition, scholars have, on a limited basis, attempted to identify similarities and differences in the drivers of TOI between various occupations. Maier and colleagues (2015) found differences between IT professionals and non-IT professionals for several relationships, including work exhaustion-TOI. We continued this tradition in exploring the similarities and differences that may exist between IT and non-IT professionals in terms of the antecedents of TOI (i.e., RQ2) by conducting a second-order meta-analysis comparing the antecedent-TOI relationships for IT and non-IT professionals.

A second-order meta-analysis, also known as a meta-analysis of meta-analyses (e.g., Cooper & Koenka, 2012), is “a metaanalysis of a number of statistically independent and methodologically comparable first order meta-analyses examining ostensibly the same relationship” (Schmidt & Oh, 2013, p. 204). Secondary uses of meta-analysis (i.e., secondorder meta-analysis) produce new knowledge that cannot be directly obtained from primary studies or singular metaanalytic results (Oh, 2020). The secondary uses of metaanalysis have a history of advancing knowledge in the management discipline by “provid[ing] unique information that cannot be obtained using the more traditional methods of first order meta-analysis” (Schmidt & Oh, 2013, p. 216). Seminal management studies using this approach include a review of the selection (Schmidt & Hunter, 2004) and turnover literature (Hom et al., 1992).

According to Borenstein et al. (2009), there are three options for conducting a second-order meta-analysis: (1) conducting a full meta-analysis including all primary studies, (2) averaging the first-order meta-analyses effect sizes without taking into consideration the between-meta-analysis variance, and (3) combining meta-analyses mean effect sizes while taking into consideration the between-meta-analysis variance. We chose the third option as recommended by Schmidt and Oh (2013) since “primary studies from all relevant first-order metaanalyses are unavailable… (a second-order meta-analysis can be conducted using only first-order meta-analytic estimates), and there is a need to estimate the between-meta-analysis variance (for example, when each meta-analysis represents a random sample of a different population [e.g., country, occupation, type of industry, military vs. civilian])” (p. 205).

We followed the same procedure for the broader management literature search as in our meta-analysis of the IT literature. We searched key journals such as Journal of Applied Psychology, Academy of Management Journal, Personnel Psychology, Journal of Management, and Human Relations in addition to electronic databases (e.g., PsycINFO, Business Source Complete, Academic Search Complete, ABI/INFORM Complete, and Google Scholar) using the following terms appearing in the title or abstract: (“metaanalysis” or “meta analysis” or “meta-analyses”) AND (“turnover” or “turnover intention” or “intention to leave” or “intention to quit”). We identified 22 articles with 24 metaanalyses.

We reviewed the industry, the sample, and the data collection year for each of the 24 meta-analyses. To meet the assumption of sample independence, we ensured that “the primary studies or samples contained in a first-order meta-analysis [were] not also included in any of the other first-order meta-analyses” (Schmidt & Oh, 2013, p. 207). To be rigorous, we excluded meta-analyses that: (1) did not specify the job category or industry; (2) had samples drawn from multiple industries; or (3) had overlapping publication years of the primary studies included in the meta-analysis. By doing so, we conducted our second-order meta-analysis based on first-order meta-analysis investigating a specific job category or industry (e.g., nurses, hospitality) and the publication year of the studies included in each meta-analysis was mutually exclusive to ensure the studies were statistically independent (Schmidt & Oh, 2013). Applying our inclusion/exclusion criteria resulted in seven first-order meta-analysis articles spanning samples from social work, child welfare workers, health and social care, nurses, tourism and hospitality, community corrections, institutional corrections, and law enforcement.

We conducted the second-order meta-analysis following the guidance of Schmidt and Oh (2013). In addition to including the seven identified articles, in cases where only one metaanalysis $\mathrm { ( \sum k _ { i } < 2 ) }$ was identified per antecedent, we took two routes: (1) if the meta-analysis that we excluded in the initial step (e.g., meta-analysis not specifying the industry) provided information about that antecedent, we reincluded it; or (2) if no other information was found, we removed that antecedent from any further analysis and comparison. This led us to reinclude two previously excluded articles regarding perceived workload, colleague support, and supervisory support antecedents. Including these two articles that could contain IT workers in the sample does not jeopardize our results or our claim for two reasons. First, the number of IT articles used in these management metaanalysis studies is far smaller than non-IT articles. Second, IT professionals were not actively sampled within the management articles and therefore represented a far smaller proportion than non-IT professionals.

Table 4 presents the results of the second-order meta-analysis, in which the mean effect sizes representing non-IT workers are compared with the effect sizes obtained for the same antecedent-TOI relationships for IT workers. The “comparison of current study with management findings” column reports the comparison (i.e., t-test) of the relationships for the IT workers versus the average of other workers. Stronger indicates a stronger relationship between the construct and TOI for studies for IT workers as the primary referent. Similarly, weaker indicates a weaker relationship for IT workers. It is important to note that many of the relationships (e.g., career satisfaction, boundary spanning, promotability) from our first-order meta-analysis of IT professionals could not be compared to the second-order meta-analyses of other occupations due to an insufficient number of first-order meta-analyses in the broader management literature. Nonetheless, where we find differences in the effect sizes, these differences suggest that IT professionals may be unique compared to non-IT professionals.

We see in Table 4 that the occupational context seems to matter, as 10 of the 17 (58.8%) antecedent-TOI relationships were significantly different. The results reported here provide the first evidence that IT occupational context moderates the drivers of TOI. This study answers the call made at the turn of the century to address the missing context in IT workforce research (Ang & Slaughter, 2000). This study finds both generalizable relationships between the antecedents and TOI (noted as same under the “results” column in Table 4) as well as IT occupation-specific relationships. For example, we see that the strength of the negative relationship between affective commitment and TOI is weaker for IT professionals, but the strength of the relationship between overall organizational commitment and TOI is similar to that of other occupations. The weaker effect of affective commitment may be due to the unique nature of the IT field, which is characterized by a strong external labor market where there is a stronger affective attachment to the profession than to any one organization (e.g., Fu & Chen, 2015; Rashid & Zhao, 2010). A further possible explanation could be that the overall organizational commitment construct (e.g., Mowday et al., 1979) masks the occupational differences arising from conceptualizing organizational commitment as a multidimensional construct. An immediate implication for future research examining occupational differences would be to utilize the multidimensional conceptualization of organizational commitment.

Another interesting finding is that the negative relationship between occupational tenure and TOI is stronger for IT professionals than for other professionals. Given that the IT profession has a historically strong labor market, theory would suggest that individuals with longer occupational tenures would benefit from job mobility. However, recent thinking on the qualitative aspects of work experience suggests that long IT occupational tenures are associated with increasing complementarities between firm-specific and IT-specific human capital (Saldana et al., 2020), with firm-specific human capital potentially making turnover costlier for IT professionals (Slaughter et al., 2007). Future research might resolve this competing hypothesis of whether the value of an IT professional to an organization arises from a deep understanding of the firm’s context and applying appropriate IT to improve firm performance.

It also appears that IT professionals are more immune to role stressors than other professionals. Role stressors are an acknowledged part of the IT occupational culture (e.g., Ahuja, 2002; Guzman et al., 2008), and within the IT profession, individuals often expect role stress in terms of role ambiguity and role conflict (Igbaria & Siegel, 1992). As the nature of IT work is often boundary spanning (e.g., Deng & Chi, 2015; Dong & Gotz, 2021), it could be that IT professionals recognize that inconsistent or incompatible demands and uncertainty about which tasks and responsibilities are part of their role(s).

Interestingly, the level of perceived workload more strongly influences TOI for IT professionals than for professionals in other occupations. It may be that agile software development practices create pressures to complete tasks within unrealistic deadlines (Tuomivaara et al., 2017; van Oorschot et al., 2018) and require development teams to be more responsive to volatility in requirements (Lee & Xia, 2010; Maruping et al., 2009). In addition, agile methods require frequent interaction and informal communication across functional areas (Hummel et al., 2013), which may be perceived as more taxing “work” for some IT professionals.

IT professionals may be more extrinsically motivated as evidenced by a stronger salary-TOI relationship. Pay rates within the IT field are often widely reported in trade publications, are more standardized across job roles (i.e., software developer, project manager), and are consistently in high demand. Hence, IT professionals may react more strongly to IT salary comparisons with published benchmarks and become motivated to “job hop” for a higher salary.

Finally, IT professionals’ TOI appears more strongly influenced by relational factors, especially the level of perceived support available within the workplace (e.g., perceived organizational support and colleague support). The perceived support (i.e., assistance and encouragement) from the organization and colleagues produce stronger reactions to TOI in IT professionals. IT professionals are also more concerned than other business professionals about satisfaction with supervisors (satisfaction with supervisor) versus supervisors providing support (supervisory support). Supervisors are often considered one of the most influential organizational members in the lives of their employees as they communicate expectations, provide rewards, and disseminate policies (Kim et al., 2017). It appears that IT professionals see their supervisor’s role as providing a facilitative work environment (e.g., removing work blockages, providing resources) rather than relational support (DeConinck & Stilwell, 2004).

## Integrative Discussion

Here, we provide an integrative discussion to address Research Goal 3 by building on Research Goals 1 and 2 and providing a research agenda to direct IT TOI research efforts.

## Research Goal 1: The State of TOI Research Within IS

When we look at the landscape of the IS TOI literature over time, we see that the strongest predictors of IT TOI continue to be affective commitment, job satisfaction, and work exhaustion (Figure 1). The weakest predictors of IT TOI are gender and education. We suggest that scholars retire such enduring, well-explored antecedents that have been repeatedly studied and have produced consistent results across worker populations (e.g., job satisfaction). These antecedents have shown stable enduring effects across decades and a multitude of contexts (see Figure 2, the concepts with a large circular marker). It may be time to investigate these antecedents as controls rather than substantive antecedents.

About 43 antecedents are relatively new to the IS literature (42% of all antecedents identified) and have appeared in only 1-3 studies, and almost half (47%) of these emerged in Decade 4 (2011-2020). Of these newer constructs, the strongest predictors of IT TOI are normative commitment, training and development, job security, and perceived organizational support. Interestingly, normative commitment and training and development were of brief interest to IS scholars from 2001 to 2010. Presumably, the era of ubiquitous computing experienced a wave of entrants into the IT profession with little formal IT training (e.g., Niederman et al., 2016). Thus, one of the “burning questions” in this era was focused on the returns to training these new IT entrants. More recently, the increasing emphasis on work automation, digital transformation, and artificial intelligence has increased the demand (and perceived workload) for IT professionals (e.g., Meske & Junglas, 2020). The ensuing labor market churn may have nudged scholars to examine the role of perceived workload, work stress, and perceived organizational support in the TOI of IT professionals.

In Figure 2, the antecedents located within the circle should be of interest to IS scholars. We note these antecedents for their relatively higher variance in their correlations with IT TOI. These correlations with high variances result in a nonsignificant relationship that may be indicative of uncovered contextual moderators or relationships that are suppressed by mediators (Hayes, 2017). These antecedents (within the circle in Figure 2) are mainly those that reduce TOI (Table 1: career satisfaction, career anchors, supervisory support, leader-member exchange, normative commitment, and job/occupation tenure). Consequently, there is potential for scholars to enrich theories of turnover by uncovering these yet unidentified moderators or mediators.

## Research Goal 2: Comparing IT TOI Across Occupations

To synthesize the TOI literature, we considered the management literature as a referent because of its deep and broad intellectual history. Taking a panoramic view of the landscape of the TOI literature across occupations, we uncovered three important insights.

Insight 1: There are similarities and differences in the drivers of TOI across occupations. The management and IS disciplines are an amalgamation, with characteristics drawn from reference disciplines such as psychology and sociology (e.g., Churchman, 1971; Dubin, 1978). Expectedly, the IS and management literature streams converge on March and Simon’s (1950) desire to move and ease of movement categories as proximate antecedents of TOI. This is an example of where IS and management researchers share the motivation to refine formal theories in what Cronbach (1988) refers to as strong programs of construct validation.

One avenue for IS scholars might be to highlight where IT professionals do not differ from other professionals, potentially resulting in generalized models with occupational augmentation (e.g., occupation mediators or moderators). We anticipate similarities in the effects of proximate antecedents and differences in more distal classes of antecedents. For example, the relationships with TOI are relatively consistent across occupations for some of the antecedents in the desire to move category (e.g., job satisfaction). In contrast, technological trends and the issue of professional obsolescence should be more of a concern to IT professionals and IS scholars. IT human capital is prone to obsolescence faster than human capital in most other professions (e.g., management) due to the increasing rate of technological advancements (e.g., Joseph et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2012). We encourage scholars to operationalize the effects of contextual variables such as the rate of technology change and professional obsolescence. Such directions should allow IS scholars to test the boundaries of theories, refine them, and explain and predict IT TOI, thereby pointing to the potential uniqueness of the IT profession.

Insight 2: There are differences in the strength of the antecedent-TOI relationships between the professions. IT professionals appear to be less sensitive to the effects of jobrelated constructs such as autonomy and role stressors. Where the effects are more pronounced for IT professionals, these antecedents typically reduce TOI (e.g., support, salary, and IT tenure), except for perceived workload - which is the only antecedent with a stronger positive relationship with TOI for IT professionals. As noted above, with perceived workload, the emphasis on digitalization (e.g., Meske & Junglas, 2020) and automation of work processes (Sommer, 2015) may have increased interest in the role of perceived workload as a driver of TOI. The work demands (e.g., long work hours, tight deadlines, continuous learning) clearly produce a stronger reaction to withdraw from the organization for IT professionals than for other workers. Tasks that require more knowledge and diverse skills can exacerbate workload perceptions due to their cognitive complexity (Chilton et al., 2010). Future research might examine these factors as moderators in the perceived workload-TOI relationship. The “S<sup>3</sup>”—satisfaction, support, and salary—could be proximate levers for retaining valued IT professionals.

Insight 3: IS researchers have moved beyond traditional, established turnover theories to examine constructs of interest such as personality, career, and culture. The IS discipline has contributed to the reference discipline(s) by expanding the TOI nomological network (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955) and implicitly defining the positions of these constructs in relation to other constructs in the network. The placement of antecedents is, for the most part, deduced from theory and helps establish the discriminant validities of seemingly unrelated constructs (Campbell & Fiske, 1959).

Here, the IS discipline appears focused on weak programs of construct validation (Cronbach, 1988) that are largely based on less developed theories and more on exploratory empirical research. If IS researchers continue along a weak programmatic approach to research, IS TOI research becomes at risk of “sheer exploratory empiricism” (Cronbach, 1988, p. 12), which would threaten construct validation. We contend that IS researchers should continue to expend efforts on both strong and weak programs of construct validation, as these are complementary in developing theories and refining our collective understanding of the TOI phenomenon. In the context of our study, the comparisons of magnitude and variability of results contribute to research by refining and contextualizing theories of TOI to the IS field. This study identifies antecedents that ought to be moved to the background as control variables and highlights potentially unique antecedents that could contribute toward a more nuanced understanding of TOI and IT professionals uniqueness.

## Research Goal 3: Integrating Insights and Research Agenda

Integrating Research Goals 1 and 2, we develop a forwardlooking research agenda to guide IS researchers. When we integrate the findings of this study, we see two foci of interest that may assist the movement of TOI research toward more productive avenues.

Our first focus is on the impact of the changing nature of work (e.g., digitalization, gig economy, remote work) on the study of IT TOI. We are at a pivotal moment for the future of work. Employers are known to screen potential candidates by reviewing their activity on digital work and digital professional network platforms. From a retention perspective, companies need to accelerate their war for talent now that workers have publicly visible digital work profiles. Such platforms are double-edged. Online talent platforms provide firms access to specialized, expensive, and hard-to-find talent. Yet, these same platforms afford competitors the ability to lure away valued employees more easily. On LinkedIn alone, 49 million people search for jobs each week, and six people are hired every minute.<sup>6</sup> Online talent platforms enable labor market fluidity across organizational boundaries that have significant implications for IT TOI research.

Digital innovations are increasing and “competition is occurring between IT ecosystems that involve numerous organizations” (Grover et al., 2019, p. 167). The increasing popularity of remote work via digital platforms and newer forms of work arrangements arising from the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., hybrid work models) offers research opportunities for scholars interested in IT TOI. The concept of turnover is premised on full-time, long-term employment in an organization, with work primarily conducted on-site under organizational supervision (Schroeder et al., 2021). Turnover and TOI are organization-referenced concepts that require movement across organizational boundaries (see Joseph et al., 2012). To the extent that digital platforms and digital labor markets are characterized by some form of an organizational boundary, the results presented here hold.

We see a digital workforce emerging, comprised of individuals who utilize technology to complete their work (Colbert et al., 2016). The typical digital platform/digital labor market is based on contingent employment arrangements, with an emphasis on projects. Digital workers are often classified as self-employed independent contractors, lacking the protections and rights given within standard employment relationships (Prassl, 2018). Such employment relationships, referred to as “gig” work, transfer much of the risk (e.g., demand-side shocks) and responsibility (e.g., professional development) to the worker. Within this context, the concept of “organization” may not be emphasized; hence, the concept of “turnover” as historically defined may become less relevant. A first step to situating this phenomenon within the IT TOI agenda may be to study these platforms in terms of work standards (e.g., pay, conditions, contracts) and the impact of changes or breaches in these standards on IT labor market fluidity (i.e., IT TOI).

Our second focus is the notion of exploring employment-related exchanges from a longer time horizon (i.e., transactional vs. relational; job vs. career). Employees have had more than two years to reflect on their needs and aspirations, and many want a new work model (i.e., The Great Resignation). This change may influence our view of TOI and the context of TOI—in terms of the workplace, the work, and the worker.

In terms of the workplace, the increase in remote work raises the possibility that IT professionals may be less embedded in their jobs. Remote work weakens links to the work community (e.g., work teams, or department) as there are fewer opportunities to socialize at work (Bailey et al., 2015) and engage new hires in the organization’s socialization process. According to a 2021 PwC US Pulse survey, 36% of executives say the loss of corporate culture is the biggest challenge to new hybrid work models.<sup>7</sup> As IT professionals have adjusted their lifestyle toward remote work, many find it attractive (e.g., work-life balance, desirable locations), leading to possibly establishing a new normal (e.g., Zoom meetings as default, hybrid work schedule, and even early retirement). Also, there is increased demand as IT is a key resource to keep companies in business.

The underexplored but potentially interesting concepts identified in this study regarding interpersonal (e.g., supervisory support, leader-member exchange, normative commitment) and intrapersonal (e.g., career anchors, career satisfaction, occupation tenure) exchanges should be of interest to IS scholars. When we look at the change in terms of the work and the IT worker, we see the emergence of a gap regarding the dimension of time. Our integrated findings show that exploring the very nature of the employment relationship is needed.

With the pandemic and its mandatory move to remote work, the nature of the relationship between employer and employee has moved more toward a transactional arrangement. Transactional work arrangements tend to be short-term focused, while relational work arrangements are more longterm focused. We currently have more transactional work relationships (e.g., gig), but our research indicates that IT professionals want more relational-focused arrangements (e.g., supervisor support). As Wendy Duarte Duckrey, executive director of global technology recruiting at JPMorgan Chase, says, “You have to manage to each individual and invest time into discovering what each member of a team needs both at work and outside of work to do their job to the best of their ability” (Pratt & Florentine, 2021, np).

Possible future research could explore the concept of Xmember exchange, where X might be a leader, team, or organization. Such an avenue could be particularly fruitful for establishing multiple links to embed the individual within the organization. When situated within varying work models (face-to-face, remote, hybrid), potential equity issues between on-site and remote workers may occur (Samuel & Robertson, 2021<sup>8</sup>). As organizations make policy changes to how and where work gets done based on lessons learned from the pandemic, DEI (diversity, equity, inclusion) efforts should be revisited. For instance, what might be the impact of in-person group dynamics versus remote group dynamics on team building and leadership mentoring programs for diverse populations within varying work models? Will these new work models increase participation diversity or create a new hierarchical structure of workers?

From an intrapersonal perspective, the focus in organizations is the now (current job role and maybe the next job role), whereas this research indicates that IT professionals want a more holistic, future focus (career over time). IT professionals want to explore and/or work on emerging technologies and keep their skills current in a quickly advancing profession. “Making educational and career advancement opportunities available, even if they result in employees growing up and out of your organization, is a must because you never know when an employee might return to your organization [turnback], or when they’ll make a key referral” (Pratt & Florentine, 2021). This includes employees’ awareness of opportunities and resources that can add to and/or change their roles and encourage them to take on more or different responsibilities and spearhead new projects. Research that expands the idea of IT career movement (not necessarily promotion) within an organization could add to our understanding.

## Practical Implications

This study has two clear implications for practitioners. We know the enduring levers that influence TOI (e.g., job satisfaction, affective commitment). Given that these factors are adequately addressed within an organization, HR professionals and IT managers might focus attention on other key factors leading to TOI, such as perceived workload. Long work hours and tight deadlines produce a stronger reaction to withdraw from an organization for IT professionals than for other workers. But one potential solution is clear—supporting IT professionals. Our results show that providing support from various sources (organization, peers) produces a stronger negative reaction toward TOI for IT professionals than for other workers.

The implication is that the traditional human resource practices that focus on building attachments to the organization to retain valuable workers may not work with IT professionals but supporting them and their work does. Hence, human resource practices directed at retaining IT professionals may need to be different from those utilized in retaining other workers. In addition, direct supervisors need the training (and autonomy) to enable IT professionals to do work they enjoy and carve out a career development path that accommodates personal priorities.

Another implication for IT management may be in terms of employee selection. Figure 2 indicates that career anchors may be significantly related to TOI for IT professionals. While the relationship needs further study, hiring managers may nonetheless want to consider incorporating career anchor inventories into the selection process. A career anchor inventory might be useful in identifying clusters, rather than a single dominant career anchor, as research has indicated a cluster approach more accurately depicts IT professionals (Ramakrishna & Potosky, 2003). With this inventory, the hiring manager may determine the level of fit between what the individual wants from a career and what the organization can provide. In addition, meaningful career strategies can be jointly developed and activated within the organization.

## Limitations

We have endeavored to be as comprehensive as possible in the literature search process, but we may have overlooked some research studies. In addition, despite our efforts to cast the net wide, our search for IT TOI research in multiple sources led to a sample mainly based in North America. As such, the findings may not generalize as broadly as one would like. We encourage researchers to explore the TOI phenomenon across global regions. A second limitation is that some of the failsafe Ns reached the lower limit of five. Those constructs with a low failsafe N are not as robust as we would like, given the small number of studies. A third limitation is the number of studies identified for the second-order meta-analysis. The low number of studies in the management literature for certain antecedents constrained comparing the IT TOI antecedents identified in the primary meta-analysis of IT TOI studies. The fourth limitation involves the two meta-analyses per antecedent minimum constraint for conducting the second-order metaanalysis. Three variables (perceived workload, colleague support, supervisor support) required the inclusion of a meta-analysis that may have contained articles that were also included in other meta-analyses used in this study. While this is a limitation also found in other meta-analyses (e.g., Barrick et al., 2001; Cooper & Koenka, 2012), we believe it was the appropriate choice to allow a more holistic picture of the influencing factors. These limitations notwithstanding, we assert that this study’s findings and the associated proposals guide several fruitful avenues of TOI research within the IS community.

## Conclusion

This research asked: What lessons can we learn from reviewing the state of the IT turnover intention literature and synthesizing this review with those from other professions? Three broad research goals address this question: (1) to establish the state of the TOI literature for IT professionals, including historical trends and newer developments; (2) to synthesize the TOI literature across disciplines; and (3) to provide an integrative, future-oriented discussion of the TOI literature and recommendations for future research. The findings suggest that IS research efforts might be directed toward understudied constructs and relationships with TOI that are idiosyncratic to the IT profession. To that end, we encourage the revitalization of TOI research within the IS field—to move toward fresh theories, fresh constructs, and fresh approaches. We hope that future research builds on the methods and agenda provided and advances the field in exciting new directions.

Damien Joseph (ORCID 0000-0002-4632-660X) is an associate professor of information technology and associate dean (undergraduate education) at the Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University (Singapore). His research seeks to understand why and how IT professionals sustain themselves in the course of their work and careers. Specifically, his research focuses on examining themes relating to IT work, compensation, competencies, and careers. Underlying these themes are the contextual influences of subjective and objective cultures. Damien’s research has been published in top journals in management information systems (MIS) and management. He has published in MIS journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Communications of the ACM, Information and Management, and Communications of the Association for Information Systems. Damien has also published in top management journals such as Human Relations and the Journal of Organizational Behavior. Damien is a regular participant at major MIS and management conferences where his research has won Best Paper awards.

Deborah J. “Deb” Armstrong (ORCID 0000-0002-8824-045X) is a professor of management information systems at Florida State University. Deb’s research interests cover issues at involving the human aspects of technology, change, and learning. Many of the research problems that Deb finds interesting are situated within the contexts of IT work and IT careers. Deb has published articles in Management Information Systems Quarterly, the Journal of Management Information Systems, the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and the European Journal of Information Systems, among others. Deb has served as the AIS Women’s Network co-chair, and now sits on their Advisory Board. To God be the glory!
