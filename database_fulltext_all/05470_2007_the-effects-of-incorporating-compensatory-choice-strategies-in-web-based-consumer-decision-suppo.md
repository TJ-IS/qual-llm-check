# The effects of incorporating compensatory choice strategies in Web-based consumer decision support systems

Jaeki Song <sup>⁎</sup>, Donald Jones <sup>1</sup>, Naveen Gudigantala <sup>2</sup>

Rawls College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409, United States

Received 29 June 2006; received in revised form 22 September 2006; accepted 8 October 2006 Available online 14 December 2006

## Abstract

Given the importance to web retailers of creating satisfying experiences for website consumers, surprisingly little research has been done concerning the effects of Web-based consumer DSS. In this study, we draw on theories of decision making and user satisfaction to develop three realistic Web-based DSS that support different decision strategies, and conducted an experiment to compare them. As we hypothesized, results indicate that the two DSS supporting compensatory strategies (weighted additive or equally weighted), compared to DSS supporting non-compensatory strategies (elimination-by-aspects), were perceived to be more accurate, less effortful, more effective, more satisfactory, and had superior consistency with stated preferences. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision strategies; Web-based decision support systems; Accuracy; Effort; Effectiveness; Satisfaction

## 1. Introduction

Research in the area of decision support systems has focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of decision making. In 1970's the DSS tools started in the DOS and UNIX environments and then moved to Windows in the early 1990s. The advent of the Internet has helped the DSS technology in that the new Webbased DSS can take advantage of the opportunities provided by the World Wide Web, such as rapid dissemination of information to decision-makers [36]. On the other hand, the last few years have witnessed a rapid expansion of online retailing on the Internet (hereafter web retailing) and this growth has rivaled physical brick-and-mortar stores in share of the market. According to a report from the US Department of Commerce [46], searching for product or service information was the second most popular Internet activity after email or instant messaging in 2003. The fastest growing Internet activities between 2001 and 2003 were related to online transactions such as purchasing products and services and online banking [46].

In an attempt to establish an online presence, however, many e-commerce websites were developed quickly with little attention paid to the features to aid consumer decision making. Over 80% of web shoppers have left electronic markets without knowing what they want, and 23% of attempted transactions ended up in failures, and, furthermore, only about 2% of the users who visit a website end up making a purchase [38]. A bulk of these results has been attributed to the deficient functioning of the decision support systems and search software [38]. Nonetheless, the growth of web retailing is compelling the Web retailers to be competitive in making their websites better for consumers. Therefore, designing websites that facilitate consumer decision making process in an effective manner as well as satisfying the users with the decision support features is becoming an important concern for Web retailers.

A decision support system (DSS) can be defined “as an interactive information system that provides information, models and data manipulation tools to help make decisions in semi-structured and unstructured situations where no one knows exactly how the decisions should be made” [38]. There are a number of key differences between an offline DSS and a Web-based DSS that warrant research in the area of Web-based DSS. Webbased DSS are credited with reducing the technological barriers and making it easier and less expensive to make decision-relevant information available to managers and customers in geographically dispersed locations. Organizations can now use Web-based DSS to provide the DSS capability to managers over the intranet, suppliers over the extranets, and customers over the internet [36]. Contrary to an offline DSS that can be used only by a limited number of users, Web-based DSS can be used by a vast number of users over the web. Moreover, many of the technological developments are speeding up the query processing time and improving the display of results [36]. The integration of personalization software into the Web-based DSS provides a key feature of learning and remembering user preferences, often not feasible in off-line DSS. As a result, the end users are provided with suggestions that are specific to the individual customers, which in turn save their time considerably [38].

Decision makers have used the Web to improve their information input making it more current, easier to obtain, and cheaper [11,12]. To exemplify this distinction between offline and web DSS, consider a customer planning to purchase a digital camera who knows nothing about the product. Using an offline DSS, the customer is at a disadvantage concerning the inputs into the system, because the DSS may not be able to provide the required information about the available brands, features, and prices. On the other hand, the same customer astutely using Web-Based DSS could browse catalogues, go through electronic forums, and examine consumer reports to find about the product information. Making decisions on the Web, however, can be a frustrating experience because of the exponentially increasing information available about any product [20, 29]. For a decision maker, recognizing the relevance of numerous brands and attributes might be a difficult task. Therefore, improving our understanding of how the Webbased DSS can manage the information load on the decision maker becomes an important question.

Many previous studies examined the effect of DSS on decision performance [43]. The focus of these studies has generally been to examine the role of DSS as a contributor to efficiency and effectiveness [37,43]. Some studies investigated decisions strategies and found the trade-off relationship between accuracy and effort [5,17]. Sharda et al. [35] and Lilien et al. [21] studied DSS effectiveness. Vessey [48] proposed the cognitive fit perspective for understanding the use of information displays in decision making. Parikh et al. [30] and Silver [37] examined how DSS functionalities enlighten users' decision-making processes. However, as noted in Todd and Benbasat [43], the results of the studies vary due to different tasks and conditions. Todd and Benbasat [43] suggested that studies in DSS effectiveness and efficiency need to investigate relevant factors that influence decision performance. Furthermore, understanding how to satisfy customers who use decision support systems provided by websites will be critical for establishing long-term customer relationships, which consequently increases profitability [23]. Extending the previous research to understand how users' cognitive decision factors influence their satisfaction level is of great importance to Internet business. Nonetheless, based on our knowledge, there are very few studies that examined the aforementioned user perceptions of the features of DSS in a turbulent Internet environment. In addition, none of the Internet studies applied decision strategies in developing DSS.

This study attempts to address the following research question: In the context of Web-based decision support systems, how does varying levels of support for decision strategies incorporated into the Web-based decision support systems affect the user perceptions of accuracy, effort, effectiveness, and satisfaction, along with a measure of consistency of the Web DSS assisted decisions with stated preferences? In addressing our research question, we draw on user satisfaction theory [9,23], decision strategies [17,44], buyer behaving theory [36], multi-attribute utility measurement theory [11,12], and cognitive cost-benefit theory [17].

We conducted an experiment in which subjects were provided with a task to rent an apartment complex. The study was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, we identified alternatives based on a comprehensive web search of the local rental market, and elicited relatively important decision attributes based on a survey. These attributes were then used to apply decision strategies in developing three Web-based DSS. In the second phase, we developed an instrument to measure important constructs such as Web users' perception of accuracy, effort, effectiveness, and satisfaction levels with the three Web-based DSS. During the experiment, the users examined the three decision support systems in the context of renting an apartment complex. Repeated measures ANOVA was used for data analyses. In what follows, the next section provides literature review followed by theoretical background for the study. The subsequent two sections report research design and data analyses. In the final section, we provide the conclusion of this study.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

Users' decision making is based upon users' salient beliefs related to decision strategies. Decision strategies [32] and a host of other studies related to decision support systems provide the theoretical background for the conceptualization process in this study.

## 2.1. Decision strategies and Web-based decision support systems

Each decision maker has preferences concerning the amount of importance (s)he places on various attributes. For example one consumer may care mostly about price and little about features while another consumer may be willing to pay a high premium to get a certain constellation of features and still not be attracted to some other constellation of features. In addition, there is clear evidence that decision makers use decision strategies in their choice behavior [17]. To express their preferences, consumers execute decision strategies in assessing the features of products they are considering. These strategies can be non-compensatory or compensatory [32,44], and computer systems could give varying levels of support for each type of strategy.

Non-compensatory strategies include the simplification that they do not allow high values of one attribute of a product (or service) to “compensate” for low values of another attribute. One example of a non-compensatory strategy is satisficing, as “buy the first acceptable product” in which one or more attributes must meet some acceptable threshold. Another example of noncompensatory strategy is elimination-by-aspect, as “eliminate all cars from consideration that are not four-door” or “eliminate all cars from consideration that are not convertible.” Satisficing is very easy and may be accurate enough for certain situations such as stopping by the corner store to purchase a gallon of milk. Elimination-by-aspect is more accurate than satisficing because it considers an entire set of available options. Its execution may result in a winning alternative product, or it may be applied repeatedly until a winner is chosen, as in choosing a shirt off a sale rack. Computer support for non-compensatory strategies focuses on allowing the consumer have some control over viewing and manipulating features of interest. For example, a website could allow the consumer to specify the value of an attribute (e.g., enter text for searching a website or choosing from a list of criteria) to help select a set of products for further investigation. Even more support is given when the consumer can sort products based on the value of an attribute (as in sorting by price, weight, or category).

Compensatory strategies, on the other hand, enable desirable values of one attribute of a product or service to “compensate” for undesirable values of another. Compensatory strategies are difficult for consumers because they require computations or judgmental assessments that combine multiple variables for each product being considered. An example of a compensatory strategy is a weighted-additive strategy in which a weight (relative importance) is assigned to each attribute and multiplied by its value. The results of these multiplications are summed to provide a score for each product being considered. Then the product with the highest score is selected. Variations of the weightedadditive strategy use judgmental assessment of these tradeoffs rather than an actual calculation. Compensatory strategies are more accurate when potential tradeoffs exist between variables, such as when the consumer would say, “I would rather have a convertible, but not if I have to pay too much extra to get it.” Unfortunately, compensatory strategies are so difficult that they are rarely used except for small sets of products or if some sort of “calculator” is provided [17,44]. Computer support for compensatory strategies would make it easier for consumers to see several attributes for several products at a time, such as when a website displays an array of rows of products with columns for various attributes. Such an array makes it easier for the consumer to identify and assess the tradeoffs of compensatory strategies. Some websites provide the display of such an array. Support for compensatory strategies could also enable the selection of products based on multiple criteria. Still further support would be needed to assist the consumer in evaluating tradeoffs by providing some sort of scoring computation of the values of various attributes. Such a “model” would best serve the consumer if the consumers were allowed to say which attributes were important and how much weight they would assign to each attribute. Finally the consumer would need a way to easily find out which products or services obtained the highest scores, such as having the system sort or select products based on scores.

Many websites do not provide adequate support for comparing similar products. As a result, the users often have to note, print out, or remember necessary information as they visit several websites to compare features [27]. In a recent study, the comparison of products side-by-side allowed users to execute a better decision strategy such as a compensatory strategy [20]. Another study found some preliminary support for a method to generate alternatives for infrequently purchased products over the catalog-based systems [47].

Based on buyer behavior theory, Silverman et al. [38] provide a framework that describes the design options available to a shopping website developer when developing a DSS for online buyers. The framework is based on Miles et al. [24] three stage model of buyer behavior, which specifies that buyers initially identify the search criteria, perform the search, and finally compare the products leading to a final choice or to a decision to quit the search. The framework suggests that online users need more features in the DSS, such as the ability to compare products than just a feature to do a quick search. Specifically, the levels of DSS that can be integrated into a B2C or B2B website are categorized into level 1, 2, and 3. Level-1 DSS use off-the-shelf software and provide simple keyword-based search strategies. Level-1 DSS offer little support to a decision maker except providing catalogue based information. Level-2 DSS are an improvement in their ability to understand buyer mental processes and provide features to compare brands and products, online-help, and natural language-based search, etc. Level-3 DSS are advanced in that they support the long term relationship between the buyer and seller using knowledge-based prompting both within and across transactions [38].

Edwards and Falso [11] also provide a very good overview of how decision aids have been implemented within the websites. Usually the objective of a decision maker is to select 1 out of ‘N’ available alternatives. Therefore, the decision aids typically help the decision maker in two stages. During the first stage, ‘n’ out of ‘N’ alternatives are selected, and in the second stage, usually 1 or more out of the ‘n’ alternatives is (are) chosen by the decision maker. The first stage is called the ‘winnowing’ stage while the last stage is called the ‘comparison’ stage. Many DSS now distinguish between the winnowing and comparison stages. The aids provided by the DSS support winnowing in a fast, non-compensatory way and comparison being done in a compensatory way. The idea is to save time and effort on the part of the user. The examples provided in the Edwards and Falso [11] discuss four types<sup>3</sup> of compensatory decision technology employed in commercial websites. The first example discusses a website (www.personalogic.com) that provides support for the overall winnowing process, which selects 1 alternative among many that are available in a single stage process as opposed to the multi-stage process described previously. The website in this example provides support for making a decision about which city to move to. The website has alternative set (N = 353) and the list of attributes are pre-selected by the website which are considered relevant for the decision maker. The website asks the users to judge the importance of each attribute relative to the others and provides the ratings of the cities in the descending order of the scores. The second example (also from www.personalogic. com) discusses the case of considering user profiles to help users express their preferences. This example concerns the type of users who are less aware of the preferences and weights they would present as inputs into the system. For example, many people deciding to have a pet dog, may initially know little about the traits of specific dog breeds. In such cases, a possibility would be to learn about the users' preferences concerning the dogs, and to match the profile of such users with system stored profiles. Based on a similar profile, the system recommends decision inputs. The decision technology in the third example (www.frictionless.com) adjusts the range of attributes according to the individual user choice. For example, a student planning to buy a computer can provide weights to several criteria such as price, hard drive capability, memory, processor etc. Furthermore, he is allowed to edit a default range for price (if the range is between \$400–\$7000, he can make the range from \$400–\$1000) that is specific to an individual user. The fourth website (www.activebuyers. com) is an example of a DSS that elicits weights via tradeoff procedures. Given a scenario of choosing between two products, this websites asks users to provide ratings of a tradeoff between features of product A and product B. The decision technologies that employ non-compensatory DSS focus on helping decision makers choose important attributes, and set cutoff levels. These DSS simplify decision tasks by reducing the number of alternatives. In an example of such DSS for supporting users' decision to choose a cell phone, the DSS reduces the alternative set from 95 to 5 [11].

Research on information processing models of decision making [32] suggests that non-compensatory, multiattribute strategies such as elimination-by-aspects are preferred by normally unaided and pressured decision makers. Despite no objective reason to feel pressure, there is evidence that people on the Web act fast as though they are under pressure [25]. Because the users on the Web are normally unaided, non-compensatory DSS are argued to fit well with the preferences of such users [11]. On the other hand, studies examining the linkage between information load and information search strategies found that, when information load increases, decision makers would start with a noncompensatory strategy, and then shift to a compensatory search strategy. Because using a compensatory strategy involves higher levels of cognitive effort, as the information load increases, decision makers make greater use of simplifying search strategies such as non-compensatory strategies [7,31]. In a recent study by Jedetski and Yeo [16], participants, when presented with fewer than 30 alternatives used more compensatory strategies; but when presented with more than 100 alternatives increased use of non-compensatory strategies. In light of the competing outcomes predicted by the aforementioned studies, the present study investigates user preferences in the context of Web-based decision support systems that employ a non-compensatory strategy, and a hybrid strategy that begins with a non-compensatory strategy followed by a compensatory strategy.

## 2.2. Perceived effort and accuracy of using Web-based DSS

In the process of interacting with a Web-based DSS, users will spend considerable effort in evaluating the choices. Users' evaluation of the accuracy of a Webbased DSS is pertinent in the context of the user's decision to buy a product online. Previous research has shown that users as a group understand the effort and accuracy dimensions of decision strategies [6]. The existing research on multiattribute choice strategies indicates that decision makers adapt to differences in task features by selecting a strategy that is determined by a tradeoff between the goals of minimizing effort and maximizing accuracy, and these tradeoffs affect the quality of the decision outcomes [13,17,19,44]. Based on cost-benefit theory, most decision makers try to maximize their benefits (accuracy) while minimizing mental costs (effort) [17]. The theory posited that an individual's choice of decision making strategy is a function of accuracy and effort [17,19]. Decision makers use different strategies to make decisions, and the selection of a strategy depends on the accuracy of a DSS and the mental effort required interacting with it [17,42].

In this study, we incorporate decision strategies in Web-based DSS (hereafter Web DSS) as a decision aid. For example, Web DSS employing a non-compensatory strategy allows users to eliminate a portion of the possible option set by manipulating certain attributes (e.g, sort by brand name, or sort by price, etc.). Web DSS employing a compensatory strategy provides a more sophisticated decision aid in enabling users to express their own preferences concerning which attributes are important and (perhaps) how much weight should be given to the various attributes. In such environments, when users make the final decision after using these systems, a compensatory strategy based Web DSS is likely to produce a relatively smaller choice set compared to a non-compensatory based Web DSS since the possible choice set has already been screened based on the users' given preferences. Therefore, making decisions based on the possible choice set will be relatively easier with the compensatory strategy based DSS. The possible choices derived from noncompensatory based Web DSS do not fully take into account the users' preferences and is likely to produce a relatively large option set, which must still be processed (unaided) by the user. Therefore, it will be relatively easy to make a decision without spending lots of effort using a compensatory strategy based Web DSS compared to a non-compensatory based Web DSS. In addition, when users employ compensatory strategies, all available information is integrated into the choice process. This strategy also takes individual preferences into account in a sophisticated way by weighting attributes resulting in more accurate solutions [43,45]. This is in line with cost-benefit theory in decision making. Hence, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. The Web-based DSS incorporating a compensatory strategy will be perceived as requiring less effort than Web-based DSS incorporating a noncompensatory strategy.

Hypothesis 2. The Web-based DSS incorporating a compensatory strategy will be perceived as more accurate than Web-based DSS incorporating a noncompensatory strategy.

## 2.3. Perceived effectiveness of Web-based DSS and consistency with user preferences

While the prior hypotheses were concerned with the effort demanded of the systems and the accuracy of using them, it is still possible that a DSS could be judged favorable on these aspects but not actually lead to improved decision making. Indeed, prior studies concerned with the evaluation of DSS success point to performance as the most important outcome in decision making [21,30,35]. If DSS fails to improve performance in making decision in terms of effectiveness and efficiency, DSS can be considered as failures [43]. Many studies have examined how DSS capabilities influence DSS performance [21,35,43].

Todd and Benbasat [44] extended the previous literature by considering the mediating effect of decision strategy on decision performance. DSS that make certain decision strategies easier to execute increase the likelihood that those strategies will be executed. When DSS encourage the more accurate strategies by making them easier, then the overall performance of decision making is increased because the more accurate strategies are chosen. Finally, the level of performance is improved even further when parts of the more accurate strategies are automated (i.e., incorporated into the DSS) to reduce the likelihood of computational error [18]. For these reasons we hypothesize that Web DSS incorporating compensatory strategies will be perceived as more effective, as in the following,

Hypothesis 3. The Web-based DSS incorporating a compensatory strategy will be perceived as a more effective system than a Web-based DSS incorporating a non-compensatory strategy.

As argued, decision makers have much practice in assessing the tradeoffs between effort and accuracy of decision strategies [17] and are generally aware of the levels of effort and accuracy associated with strategies [6]. This means that users' assessments of DSS effectiveness would coincide with the reality of the situation, so that the superiority of the compensatory strategies in DSS would result in better decision being made by users of DSS incorporating compensatory strategies (compared to web DSS incorporating only non-compensatory strategies). In this case the “better decision” would the decision most consistent with the DSS user's preferences. This is hypothesized as follows,

Hypothesis 4. The Web-based DSS incorporating a compensatory strategy will be associated with decisions more consistent with the users previously stated preferences than a Web-based DSS incorporating a noncompensatory strategy.

## 2.4. Web-based DSS user satisfaction

Many researchers have investigated users' satisfaction in terms of end-user satisfaction, which is considered as an important factor in IS success and use [9,15,23,34]. After DeLone and McLean [9] provided guidance for studying end-user satisfaction using their D–M model, there are many attempts to verify and modify the D–M model [24,33]. Doll et al. [10] reviewed the literature of satisfaction and refined the satisfaction measure as a second-order construct of content, accuracy, format, timeliness, and ease of use. Particularly, McKinney et al. [23] proposed a comprehensive model for Web-customers' satisfaction. They argued that Web customers' satisfaction is a result of satisfaction with information quality and system quality. They decomposed information quality and system quality into various aspects of information (relevance, understandability, reliability, adequacy, scope, and usefulness) and system (access, usability, entertainment, hyperlinks, navigation, and interactivity). Satisfaction is also an important outcome in studies of DSS. Parikh et al. [30] examined the role of guidance in DSS in the decision making process. They found that guidance in DSS generally improves user satisfaction. Guimaraes et al. [14] found that among many perspectives of a decision support system's success, user satisfaction was regarded as a major criterion.

Compensatory strategies integrate available information into the choice process in a comprehensive fashion and also consider consumer preferences in a more detailed way by weighting attributes [43]. If the features provided by websites help consumers' choice processes by making the choices easier and more consistent with their preferences, then consumers would experience greater satisfaction [10]. While Web DSS are generally designed to help consumers make decisions in some manner, we posit that users are likely to be more satisfied with Web DSS that incorporate a compensatory strategy based on major salient cognitive factors in decision making, such as effort, accuracy, and consistency with user preferences. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 5. The Web-based DSS incorporating a compensatory strategy will be associated with a higher rating of satisfaction than Web DSS incorporating a noncompensatory strategy.

## 3.2. Development of Web-based DSS

The DSS systems used in this study were Web-based and designed to help customers select apartments to rent. We built three versions of the experimental systems based on different decision strategies; the first one was based on a non-compensatory strategy, the second one was based on a hybrid strategy that provided support for non-compensatory strategy followed by a compensatory strategy called equal weighted additive strategy, and the third one provides support for a hybrid strategy that initially follows a non-compensatory strategy followed by a differential weighted-additive strategy. The two Web-based DSS that use the hybrid strategy take the user provided weights into consideration while presenting the result list. The process of incorporating decision technology to capture user preferences is similar to the work done by Lee et al. [20], who developed intelligent agent-based systems in an effort to overcome the problem of information overload. These systems, based on user preferences, retrieve product information that is specific to an individual customer and recommend the products accordingly.

Once the user specifies the number of bedrooms and selects an attribute, the results are presented to the user in an alternative-by-attribute matrix. This website is similar to a website such as www.rentnet.com that asks users for rent and number of bedrooms.

Then the system displays the apartments having the number of bedrooms and within the rental price range as specified by the user, sorted from highest scoring to lowest scoring (Fig. 3).

For unaided decision makers the equally weighted additive strategy is easier than the differential weighted additive strategy but performs almost as well [3,17]. The crux of whether Web DSS 2 would be assessed differently than Web DSS 3 depends on whether decision makers would see an advantage in being allowed to specify weights in Web DSS 3 or whether they would see this requirement as a nuisance. If they perceived the requirement of specifying precise weights as a nuisance they would likely prefer Web DSS 2 which enabled them to express their preferred attributes without the requirement to give each one a weight. Since this issue is largely unexplored in the literature, we elected to include both Web DSS 2 and Web DSS 3 in our study as exemplars of incorporating compensatory strategies in Web DSS.

## 3.3. Experimental design and procedure

The experimental design was as follows. All participants visited websites for all three DSS, so Web

DSS was a within-subjects factor. There were six possible sequences (3-factorial) in which participants could be instructed to visit the websites. The assignment of Web DSS sequence was controlled by the experimental material and balanced across the experiment. Web DSS sequence was the only between-subjects factor in the experimental design, and participants were randomly assigned to this control factor.

The experimental sessions were conducted in a computer laboratory in which every computer had Internet access. Participants were seated at a computer and given verbal instructions about the experimental procedure, emphasizing the point that they were to work through the material in sequence without returning to prior pages. At this point, participants were handed out a task description and the questionnaire for the experiment. The participants were asked to imagine that they were in the market to look for an apartment. Prior to looking at any websites, participants were asked to give their weights for the importance of each of the apartment attributes included in the study. This was to get participants' impression of the relative importance of the attributes prior to seeing any websites.

At this point, participants were instructed to visit their first assigned website and to examine its contents with care. Such examination involved activities such as reading information about apartments, examining appropriate attributes, and checking availability, etc. The participants then listed their three preferred apartments as well as time spent for selecting three appropriate apartments. The participants were asked to answer questions concerning their perceptions about effort, accuracy, effectiveness, and satisfaction. This procedure was repeated for the second assigned website and the third assigned website. Subjects spent 4.3 min on average to find the appropriate apartments using Web-DSS 1, 3.87 min using Web-DSS 2, and 3.36 min using Web-DSS 3. At the end, participants were asked to complete a questionnaire concerning demographics and their overall impressions of the three systems.

## 3.4. Dependent variables

Five dependent variables, perceived effort, perceived accuracy, perceived effectiveness, consistency with user preferences, and satisfaction, were used in the analyses. The scales for measuring these constructs were developed based on an extensive review of literature to ensure their content validity, and the instruments are reported in Appendix B.

Perceived effort can be measured as consumption and investment of energy and time in acquisition of information [1,17,45]. Perceived accuracy can be measured by addressing how close the information is to the ideal choice [41]. We define perceived effectiveness as a person's belief about how worthwhile and productive it is to use the DSS. In the DSS realm, this definition is in line with previous studies in terms of decision quality or decision outcomes [21,35].

In this study, the constructs are measured as the average of the items. Many studies have pointed out the critical importance of instrument reliability in IS research [26]. As reported in Appendix B, the constructs for the each Web DSS have alpha values well above the cutoff point of 0.70 [28], indicating the high reliability of the items. The averaging approach has been applied to many studies and it has been argued that the averaging of items for a construct prevents the construct from losing information in case of ordered categorical measurement [39,40]. Since our study has ordered categorical measurement, this is the appropriate approach for item combination. Thus, our selection of items and using averaging as the means of combining them for a construct is deemed the appropriate approach.

In addition, one purpose of using DSS is to improve performance of decision making. The quality of decision outcomes is reflected in consistencies in preference [17]. A measure of performance, therefore, represents the extent to which decision outcomes are consistent with decision makers' stated preferences [17,41]. The optimal choice will have the highest expected utility value in that it uses all the available information whereas the other choices do not use the information as efficiently as the optimal choice and, hence, have lower expected values. The decision performance measure indicates how close the expected value of chosen alternatives is to the expected value of optimal choice. Decision consistency was computed as:

$$
\text { Decision   Consistency } = \mathrm{EV} _ {\text { OptimalChoice }} - \mathrm{EV} _ {\text { ActualChoice }}
$$

The measure of performance used in this study is consistent with the measure employed for performance by Johnson and Payne [17] and Tabatabaei [41] except that it does not consider how far the expected value of optimal choice is from the expected value of random or worst alternative. The latter component was not necessary in this study since all decisions were made from the same data set, while the other studies [21,48] included the worst situation as a benchmark for comparing decisions across various data sets.

Our experimental questionnaire asked each participant to give weights (relative importance) of apartments attributes before they looked at any DSS. Then after

Table 3

looking at a DSS, participants were asked to write down their three first choices for apartments. One of the authors computed the aforementioned decision consistency score for each of the three apartments. Since this computation would have the counterintuitive result of better (more consistent) choices having lower scores (with excellent choices close to zero), these scores were transformed by subtracting them from 100 so that higher scores represented better decision performances. The average of these scores for the three apartments was recorded as the performance score for the respective DSS.

As far as measuring satisfaction is concerned, there is no consensus in the literature. Some have measured satisfaction as user information satisfaction [15]; satisfaction with outcome [21]; or ease of use and format perspectives [10]. In this study, we measured overall Web users' satisfaction [23] rather than performing a detailed analysis of user satisfaction. Therefore, we measured overall satisfaction of using the system with “In general, after using the system in looking for information about apartments, I am very satisfied”.

## 3.5. Participants

Participants were MBA and senior undergraduate students at a large business school in a university in the U.S. The participants were randomly assigned to three different website sequences and data were collected from 130 participants. Participants were told that the experiments were intended to gain information to help websites improve their services. To increase the seriousness of participation, participants received extra course credit and a chance to participate in a lottery drawing for participating in the study. Table 2 shows the profiles of the participants.

Most participants were familiar with activities on the Web and had experience with decision functionalities provided on commercial websites. Using students as study subjects always generates the question of external validity. Many studies have found that there are no significant differences between student subjects and non-student subjects in individual behavior, organization psychology, and so forth [22]. In addition, participants in this study have the characteristic that defines the population being sampled, which is that they are web consumers. Hence the type of subjects in this study does not present a significant threat to external validity and could be considered as representative of the general customers who seek apartment information.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Prior research, seeking to better understand how consumers make choices among products and services, has catalogued choice strategies in terms of their relative accuracy and required effort under a variety of conditions [17], found empirical support that the effort/accuracy dimensions of various strategies occur in actual decision making [32], and affirmed that decision makers can perceive and predict tradeoffs of cognitive effort and accuracy in various choice strategies [6]. In the DSS literature, behavioral decision research has been extended by findings that changes in DSS features cause changes in the strategies executed by DSS users, and that users gravitate toward strategies that are made easier to use, lending support for the notion of building features into DSS that make the more accurate strategies easier to use [42–44]. Benbasat and Todd [2] speculate that the cognitive cost/benefit approach could be extended beyond the selection of choice strategies and into the realm of selecting or formulating models to use in decision making. They posit that decision makers could consider various approaches to problem solving and predict both the effort of formulating a model and the accuracy to be gained by using the model.

With this research, we extend the aforementioned research in several ways. First, we extend prior literature by shifting focus from the users' cognitive processing of strategies while using a DSS, to the users' perceptions of the experience of using DSS. Prior literature has found that compensatory strategies are more accurate than non-compensatory strategies, and that they are harder in an unaided mode but they can be made easier (and thereby encouraged) by DSS features that give support for compensatory strategies. Our research extends that prior research by hypothesizing that DSS incorporating compensatory strategies, compared to DSS incorporating only non-compensatory strategies would have greater perceived accuracy, lower perceived effort, greater perceived effectiveness, greater consistency of prior decisions with user preferences, and greater satisfactions. All hypotheses were supported. We found that DSS incorporating compensatory strategy are perceived by users as more accurate, less effortful, and more effective than DSS incorporating only noncompensatory strategies. These perceptions were validated by the DSS incorporating compensatory strategies having choices more consistent with user preferences than the DSS incorporating non-compensatory strategies. Moreover, the DSS incorporating compensatory strategies were rated by users as more satisfying than the DSS incorporating non-compensatory strategies. This later finding should be crucial for web retailers, who may not care much about the Web consumers' accuracy or effort, but would certainly wish their shoppers to have a satisfactory experience.

Second, our study highlights the importance of studying web-delivered DSS used by consumers. Understanding Web DSS is important because their usage often has a high threshold for user satisfaction when users have many website options to choose from, as is frequently the case. Indeed, web retailers' focus on user satisfaction, rather than on user decision performance aspects, may have led them to create websites that are aesthetically pleasing and easy to navigate, without attending to how the websites could support web consumers' decision strategies. Our informal review of consumer websites has found that many websites only allow users to scroll through the offerings (perhaps after selecting a product category), some websites allow sorting on certain attributes (which would support non-compensatory strategies similar to our Web DSS 1), and almost no websites enable the user to select or give weights for criteria that would enable the user to easily execute a compensatory strategy. The results of this study, however, indicate that attending to user preferences would have benefits.

Third, the systems we designed added a degree of external validity, with little or no cost to internal validity given our research objectives, by appearing like real commercial websites that were designed specifically to help consumers. We created three Web-based DSS, each of which was designed specifically to give a comprehensive level of support for a specific class of decision strategy, rather than being designed to trace the strategies that were used. Web DSS 1 gave assistance only for noncompensatory strategies such as elimination by aspect (use DSS to sort on an attribute), Web DSS 2 gave assistance for the compensatory strategy referred to as the equal weighted attribute strategy (use DSS to select which attributes would be included in an equally weighted score), and Web DSS 3 gave assistance for the compensatory strategy referred to as the weighted additive strategy (use DSS to enter user's weights for each attribute included in a weighted average score). We hurry to acknowledge that it would not have been possible for us to build these unobtrusive systems had it not been for our reliance on numerous other studies that capitalized on the somewhat obtrusive experimental methods of computerized process tracing and protocol analysis. To create the unobtrusiveness of fairly realistic website settings, we forfeited the ability to “trace” the strategies used by our experimental participants. Nonetheless, since the strategies are well understood from the prior literature (i.e., those more obtrusive systems), and because support for specific strategies are tightly integrated into our DSS, we feel our systems gained external validity through a high degree of realism without losing control of the strategies being studied.

Finally, this study gives preliminary empirical results concerning DSS that incorporate equally weighted strategies versus DSS that incorporate weighted additiv strategies. We included both types of DSS in this study because both are relatively accurate types of compensatory strategies, but place differing demands on users. For users who know which attributes are important but have not thought about the relative importance of the attributes, providing the capability to select the favored attributes in a Web DSS that supports the equally weighted strategy should provide a good task/technology fit. On the other hand, users who have distinct opinions about the relative importance of attributes, providing the capability to give weights for the favored attributes in a Web DSS that supports the weighted additive strategy should provide a better fit than only allowing selection of the attributes. Our participants, on average, found Web DSS 3 (weighted additive strategy) as more effective, more satisfactory, and having superior consistency with user preferences, than Web DSS 2 (equally weighted strategy). Our participants did not, however, perceive Web DSS 2 and Web DSS 3 to differ in terms of effort. This result can be interpreted in light of Benbasat and Todd's [2] two components of effort in model formulation. They posit that a certain amount of effort is required in model formulation (and that this effort would vary by model and task) and a certain amount of effort is required for executing strategies once the model is formulated. Our participants (on average) may have felt that more effort was required to give weights of attributes in Web DSS 3 than to select attributes in Web DSS 2 (model formulation). Once weights were given, however, the resultant option set from Web DSS 3 may have seemed easier to process than the option set resulting from Web DSS 2. While our research's purpose was to investigate differences in perceptions and performance concerning consumer Web DSS that support non-compensatory or compensatory choice strategies, future research may increase our understanding of the tradeoffs between the effort of model formulation and the accuracy of model usage. In the meantime, our results suggest that web designers would do well to provide consumer DSS that support any compensatory strategy.

One of the limitations of the study is that it considers only one type of product. Studies in future can examine frequent and infrequent purchase items. Another extension of the study could be to compare the Web DSS used in this study with a Web DSS that supports purely compensatory choices.
