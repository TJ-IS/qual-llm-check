## Abstract

We aim to have a nuanced understanding of the seemingly antithetical tension between discipline and anonymity in online settings. Using a rich set of qualitative data from a real‐life online forum, we find that anonymity does not necessarily eliminate discipline. Three forms of discursive discipline appear under conditions of anonymity: technological discipline, peer discipline, and self‐discipline. Thus, the online forum may be seen as an omniopticon in which the anonymous online self is monitored and disciplined in multiple ways. Information technology both enables and constrains how individuals balance the competing needs of anonymity and discipline. We suggest new ways of looking at the literature on discipline and anonymity in online environments and at the role played by technology.

KEYWORDS

anonymity, anonymous online self, discipline, omniopticon, power

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

As Foucault's influential work Discipline and Punishment demonstrates (Foucault, 1977), discipline is one of the defining features of modern society. Whether in prisons, schools, and other places, individuals are monitored and transformed in their behaviours and minds or, as Foucault puts it, rendered docile in bodies and souls. Such discipline can be effected by the panopticon, a key enabling structure from which watchers can continuously monitor the watched. While the exact design of the panopticon, as envisioned by the social theorist Jeremy Bentham, may have never been fully implemented and should be taken not literally but as a metaphor (Dobson & Fisher, 2007), it is part of today's social environment to the extent that we can be said to be living in a surveillance society. Unde the gaze of panopticons, individuals are made visible; they become “knowable men” (Foucault, 1977, p. 305).

Information about their behaviours, states of mind, and any improvements in their behaviours is continuousl collected and analysed.

Recent advancement of information technology in the past decades has facilitated and, to the greatest extent transformed surveillance. Whenever they go online or move around with their cellphones, individuals risk their infor mation being collected, consolidated, and analysed by others, so much so that the society in which individuals carr out their daily lives has been accordingly termed as superpanopticon (Poster, 1990), dataveillance (Clarke, 1988), or surveillance assemblage (Haggerty & Ericson, 2000). Similarly, information technology is used by organizations in seemingly legitimate ways to monitor, measure, and control employee behaviour and subjectivity (Brivot & Gendron 2011; Doolin, 2004; Elmes, Strong, & Volkoff, 2005; Leclercq‐Vandelannoitte, Isaac, & Kalika, 2014). Thus, individuals may be said to be powerless when they are subjected to panoptic surveillance and discipline

If discipline and its power require visibility and knowledge of the individuals to be controlled, then online anonym ity can be seen as its antithesis. Although perfect anonymity may be hard to achieve given that advanced technology can be used to follow any digital traces individuals may leave behind wherever they go (Ohm, 2009), anonymity nev ertheless makes them, to a large extent, invisible and unknowable, and therefore be able to retain some power vis‐à‐ vis discipline and surveillance. Because it reduces the risk of identification and retaliation, anonymity is an important factor for explaining behaviours such as online activism (Postmes, 2007; Postmes & Brunsting, 2002; Rohlinger & Brown, 2009), whistle‐blowing (Lowry, Moody, Galletta, & Vance, 2013), griefing (Chesney, Coyne, Logan, & Madden, 2009), and flaming (Reinig, Briggs, & Nunamaker, 1997). Indeed, anonymity is probably a hallmark of information technology in supporting individuals' freedom of speech (Poster, 2006, p. 150–153). But because of the apparent freedom from consequences and the resulting disinhibition and lack of accountability, anonymity may be said to risk “an incremental breakdown of the fabric of our society,” particularly when anonymity facilitates bad behaviours and crimes (Davenport, 2002, p. 33). A recent example is the use of fake accounts on social media such as Facebook and Twitter to present fake news to influence the 2016 election cycle in the United States.<sup>1</sup>

Does discipline have no effect on individuals under conditions of anonymity? This is the question we hope to answer. More specifically, we examine the tension and dynamics between discipline and online anonymity. Using the unification of the Canadian accounting institutes as the research context, we qualitatively analyse the postings on an online forum devoted to the issue. The results suggest that the antithetical tension between discipline and ano nymity may be not as clear as previously thought. Our findings suggest that individuals were still subject to disciplin even when anonymously participating in the online discussion. We suggest the online forum had the aspects of an omniopticon in which the anonymous online self was watched and disciplined in multiple, dynamic ways.

This paper is organized follows. We first lay the theoretical groundwork by reviewing the literature on disciplin and anonymity. Next, we provide an overview of the research setting, data collection, and analytical procedures Based on the rich set of qualitative data derived from postings on the contentious case of institutional change in the Canadian accounting field, we then present the main findings of some unlikely disciplinary effects that appear even under conditions of anonymity. We conclude by discussing the implications of the findings and the limitations of the study.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Discipline

Discipline as conceptualized by Foucault refers to any methods that “made possible the meticulous control of th operations of the body, which assured the constant subjection of its forces and imposed upon them a relation of docility‐utility” (1977, p. 137). In other words, discipline makes individuals not only docile and obedient but also useful and productive. Discipline, as Foucault sees it, is a technology of power over the body and the soul. It “differ entiates, hierarchizes, homogenizes, excludes. In short, it normalizes” (1977, p. 183). Discipline is everywhere in society, whether it was the plague‐stricken town Foucault examined, prisons, schools, or military. The widespread of discipline is perhaps best captured by the term “panopticism” (1977, p. 195).

A key characteristic of the panopticon is that it makes the watched visible. Visibility and the fear or preoccupation of being visible are seen as the common denominator of the variety of control forms (Brivot & Gendron, 2011). Whether it is the few watching the many or the many watching the few (Doyle, 2011), visibility plays a central rol in making discipline work. Those who are under panoptic‐like gaze may be able to attempt resistance by gaming th visibility (Brivot & Gendron, 2011).

If visibility is the manifestation form of the panopticon, knowledge is its latent effect. For Foucault, the use of the panopticon is not merely for the purpose for watching a criminal but for the purpose of building a whole body of knowledge about the criminal, which makes possible a whole set of assessing, diagnostic, prognostic, and normative judgments concerning the criminal (1977, p. 19). The prison functions as an apparatus of knowledge (1977, p. 126). It is such “biographical knowledge” that provides “a technique for correcting individual lives” (1977, p. 252) in not only prisons but also schools, hospitals, and the military.

Foucault's concept of panopticism has been extensively referenced as a defining characteristic of modern society in terms of power and control in sociology (Power, 2011). It has become even more prevalent thanks to the ubiqui tous adoption of information technology. At the society level, the panopticon as a prison design has evolved into what has been termed as superpanopticon (Poster, 1990), dataveillance (Clarke, 1988), synopticon (Mathiesen, 1997), or surveillance assemblage (Haggerty & Ericson, 2000). In these various panopticons, personal information can be easily collected, aggregated, and analysed as “data doubles” (Haggerty & Ericson, 2000), “digital personae” (Lyon, 2003), or “virtual selves” (Whitson & Haggerty, 2008) of real persons for various purposes such as marketing, control, and intervention. Such data, if misused, may cause troubles such as identity theft and privacy invasion fo individuals, so much so that individuals have the burden of “caring for their virtual selves” (Whitson & Haggerty, 2008, p. 591).

Similarly, the concept of the panopticon has been applied in organizational studies for understanding managemen issues. Different types of organizational information technology such as mobile devices, enterprise resources plan ning, and knowledge management systems have been widely implemented to facilitate the monitoring, measurement, and control of employee subjectivity, behaviour, and performance (Brivot & Gendron, 2011; Doolin, 2004; Elmes et al., 2005; Leclercq‐Vandelannoitte et al., 2014).

## 2.2 | Online anonymity

If knowledge of individuals makes discipline possible, anonymity is antithetical to discipline and will likely make it less effective. This is because anonymity de‐individualizes actors in a way that is the opposite of how panopticism works. It withholds or restricts knowledge of oneself, particularly the kind of knowledge that can serve the disciplinary power (Hooke, 1987). Individuals become “unknowable,” to paraphrase Foucault, to examination gazes; “the body would go missing under the condition of anonymity.

The effects of anonymity have been well recognized in social and organizational studies (Scott, 2013). Put in a positive perspective, anonymity reduces the potential costs, being it financial, psychological, or social, one may otherwise incur under non‐anonymous conditions. Thus, anonymity encourages individuals to do certain things they may not otherwise do without anonymity (Haggerty & Ericson, 2000; Hooke, 1987). For example, anonymity is an important factor for explaining such behaviours as online whistle‐blowing (Lowry et al., 2013), online activism (Courpasson, 2017; Postmes, 2007; Postmes & Brunsting, 2002), help‐seeking behaviour (Cleavenger & Munyon, 2015), and consumer review (Scott & Orlikowski, 2014). In sports, anonymity has been institutionalized as a key ele ment in the judging system of figure skating for the sake of fair scoring (Lom, 2015). In academia, blind review methods are often used by journals to ensure peer review quality. Generally speaking, anonymity facilitates the development of frank, collegial, and confessional interactions (Ross, 2007).

From the negative perspective, anonymity is said to have some disinhibiting effects (Joinson, 2001) on bad behav iours. For example, individuals may hide behind the mask of anonymity to engage in misbehaviours such as griefing (ie, intentional, unacceptable online behaviour) (Chesney et al., 2009), flaming (verbal attacks intended to offend others) (Reinig et al., 1997), cyber‐bullying (Lowry, Zhang, Wang, & Siponen, 2016), leaking confidential information (Zajácz, 2013), and hacking (Munro, 2016), among others. Given these negative effects of anonymity, there has been some debate as to whether it may weaken the essential social values of accountability, openness, and honesty that hold strong the very fabric of our society (Davenport, 2002).

Is the tension between discipline and anonymity as clear‐cut as the above studies demonstrate? What is the relationship between the people in front of the computer screen and their data doubles under the condition of anonymity? The answer to these puzzling questions may not be as straightforward as it seems. On the one hand, prior studies typically assume one way or the other, ie, examining discipline and panoptic effects on the assumption of visibility and non‐anonymous (Brivot & Gendron, 2011; Leclercq‐Vandelannoitte et al., 2014) or studying anonymity based on the assumption of disinhibition and lack of discipline (Davenport, 2002). To the best of our knowledge, there has been no study that examines discipline and anonymity together. On the other hand, prior studies have suggested that the very concept of anonymity—whether or not the real person is traceable and identifiable—requires further probing. For example, prior studies have suggested that many different types of information may be relevant to one's identification (Rains & Scott, 2007; Whitley, 1997). It is not limited to the commonly used individual identification information; it also includes other information such as geographical location, temporal information, attitudes and emotions, among others. A pseudonym may help create an online profile for building reputation capital even if the real person is unknown (Scott, 2013). Furthermore, there is a difference between “being” and “feeling” anonymous (Kennedy, 2006). These nuances raise questions about how discipline might work under the condition of anonymity. Hence, we set out to explore the tension between discipline and anonymity in the context of the Canadian accounting field.

## 3 | RESEARCH SETTING AND METHOD

## 3.1 | Research context

Before 2011, the Canadian accounting field had three national accounting associations or institutes, under which each provincial governing body would issue its own designation (ie, CA, CGA, or CMA<sup>2</sup>) to individual accountants. The three institutes started a unification process in early 2011 to merge into a single professional body—CPA Canada Under the unification proposal, all designated accountants would be called “CPAs” (Chartered Professional Accountants). The unification was initiated by the CA and CMA institutes and CGA joined later.<sup>3</sup>

Facing potential resistance from their members when the unification was launched, the institutes set up an onlin forum on their official website for members to discuss the proposal. The forum was open for about 1 year from May 2011 to May 2012 (after that the forum was read‐only for a while and then taken down). During that time period, th institutes carried out various marketing campaigns such as TV commercials, billboards, and town hall meetings.<sup>4</sup> After the so‐called consultation period, provincial institutes then asked their members to vote. According to the institutes voting results, over 90% of CGAs and CMAs supported the unification and about 40% of CAs opposed.<sup>5</sup> After that, the institutes then sought legislation in each province. The unification went through in the end and is considered t be completed at the time of this writing.

The online forum may be seen as a professional setting where members were expected to behave professionally. Indeed, the institutes published some written rules in terms of what members may or may not post. Participation on the forum was anonymous. Accountants were not required to provide any personal information in order to partici pate on the forum. The only information required was an email address, which could be from any free email service providers, to register a user name (pseudonym) if one wanted to post anything. Other technical features of the online forum include the following

## 3.1.1 | Posting flexibility

The forum allows posters to freely express their thoughts and feelings. Some accountants posted very long messages (some of which were close to 1000 words, like an essay); others, on the other hand, posted some messages with only one word. As quotations cited later in this paper show, even only one word may convey rich meanings.

## 3.1.2 | Moderation

When setting up the online forum, the institutes published some specific rules aiming to control what may be posted on the forum. One or more moderators were assigned to keep an eye on postings and make sure rules were followed. Depending on the nature of a posting, it may be partially edited or completed removed. In some cases, user names registered and used by posters were deleted altogether. Users could flag any postings by alerting the moderators to potential violations.

## 3.1.3 | Archival

Messages posted by accountants were permanently stored on the online forum, unless removed by the moderator. Furthermore, every message was assigned a unique number, which allowed posters to search and refer back to old postings if necessary. The online forum was open to the public. Interested readers could access the forum without registration. Although it is a common feature of most online communities, public access was apparently a factor that influenced posters' behaviours on the online forum. (Note that the online forum has since been shut down and no longer available at the time of this writing.)

## 3.1.4 | Timestamping

Related to the feature of archival, every message was time‐stamped. Although it appeared to be trivial, this timestamping feature allowed posters to keep track (loosely speaking) of when someone posted something. Some posters even explicitly talked about timestamps when they tried to make sense of the unification and engaged in debate with opponents.

## 3.2 | Data and analytical procedures

The current study uses members' postings as the main data source. The data set includes about 1600 pages of post ings or about 595 000 words. Based on the statistics disclosed by CPA Canada on the forum, there were 130 registered users (326 posting users and 980 nonposting users or “lurkers”). Among the posting users, there were 53% CAs, 21% CMAs, and 6% CGAs. The rest did not disclose or give hints as to their designations in their postings. Othe descriptive statistics are not available because of the very nature of anonymity of the online forum

A relative advantage of the online forum as a research source is what is termed naturally occurring and unob trusive data (Kozinets, 2010; Silverman, 2013). Participants were not influenced or led by any predefined scope of discussion or the researchers' own categories or expectations. A notable issue is related to privacy. While there has been much debate as to how privacy should be handled in such online research (Langer & Beckman, 2005), it is not a particular concern of the present study for a number of reasons. First, it is an observational study that utilizes archival data. We (the researchers) did not participate in any discussions on the forum or directly solicit comments from participants. Thus, it is not human subject research as defined by generally accepted criteria (Kozinets, 2010, p. 141). Second, the online forum was public. No registration was required for reading forum postings. Third, posters participated in online discussions to voice their support of or opposition to the unification They wanted their postings to be read by other people. They probably believed that the more readers they had the better. Last, the topic they discussed was not a private matter as sensitive as very personal issues such as divorce (Paechter, 2013). It was in fact a public matter that required legislation, which in turn would require publi comments and debate.

The overall data analysis method follows the general guidelines of abductive research (Dubois & Gadde, 2002; Richardson & Kramer, 2006; Timmermans & Tavory, 2012). Seen as a modified grounded theory approach, this method takes into consideration relevant theories in existing literatures. The data analysis of our study was a long, iterative process of two steps: The initial step was based on the data without any theoretical framing. The objec tive of this step was to have some general understanding of what posters talked about on the forum. The second step was guided by relevant theories. On the inductive‐deductive spectrum, the initial step was inductive (from data), and the second was deductive (from theory). But it is worth noting that the use of theories was only to the extent of refining and re‐examining the result of the first step. It is not a pure deductive analysis in the con ventional sense.

The review team for this paper also played an instrumental role in the abductive processes of data analysis. Afte the first round of review, we scrapped our initial theoretical perspectives (such as affordance), reread the data we had, and reframed our research model. In the end, we chose Foucault's discipline as the anchoring theory. A ke advantage of a Foucauldian approach is its focus on the mechanisms of how discipline takes effect. Such a focus fit this study well in terms of examining how posters had some freedom but were also subject to power and control on seemingly anonymous online forum.

Technically, we did not use any specific coding software in the process. We read through all postings, took notes and put relevant postings under different categories in a separate file. The process was very laborious and included back‐and‐forth reading and rereading between raw postings and our notes file. In the end, we had a notes file o about 170 pages, from which we then tried to make sense of and theorize about the phenomenon.

## 4 | FINDINGS

In line with prior qualitative studies' approach to reporting data analysis results (Suddaby, 2006), we present a theo retical overview of the data structure first and then more detailed data analysis along the structure. The findings are summarized in Table 1. Generally speaking, the first‐order coding and summary column reflects the result of the first step of data analysis (ie, reading and interpreting without framing by any particular theories); the conceptua categories (and subcategories) are further refinement of the first‐order summary in the second step of data analysis (ie, guided by existing theories).

In a nutshell, the findings suggest that participants of the online forum, given its anonymous nature, enjoyed much discursive freedom to voice their opinions about the unification. They sometimes used harsh and profane language that is not commonly seen in professional or business settings. There was also intergroup fighting. Despite th disinhibiting effects of anonymity, however, they were discursively disciplined to some extent. Their postings were moderated and controlled by the administrators (technological discipline) and monitored by peers (peer discipline); they monitored their own voices (self‐discipline). In this section, we elaborate in detail such tension between ano nymity and discipline.

## 4.1 | Discursive freedom

Not surprisingly, anonymity did offer posters some freedom to speak out on the forum—discursive freedom—withou worrying too much about potential repercussions. Many participants suggested that the forum should be anonymous otherwise, discussion would be limited.

## 4.1.1 | Voicing

Many CAs were not happy about the unification. They were apparently very emotional about what was going on and what implications the unification would have. The online forum became a place they could voice their opposition and relieve their anger. This effect is similar to cathartic consequences examined in prior literature (Da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008).

## Emotion sharing

Many posters went to the forum to share their feelings. In some cases they merely shared their unhappiness without targeting anyone. Take the following posting as an example:

I would be okay with the overarching institutes merging. However the designations and required competencies should not be touched. I find it obnoxious that on one end you have CA specialty designations like CA‐ITs [CA specialized in information technology] but then the institute is trying to bury everything under one generic and non‐descriptive CPA (which also, as a rip off of the undervalued American designation, makes me sick) acronym. It harms us all.

Here, the poster was apparently unhappy about the “obnoxious” unification proposal, feeling “sick” about the new designation but otherwise calm. Another poster was even blunter: “NO ONE WANTS THIS. GET THE WAY OUT OF HERE (capitalization in original).” Here, the poster's negative feelings about the unification were not only expressed through his words but also, perhaps more significantly, by all capitalized letters

In other cases, posters simply opted for obscene language to target someone. One participant posted: “Hey T Account King [referring to another poster]<sup>6</sup> you/‘rhymes with ducking’/idiot. Someone should kick you in the head a few times so you'd shut your face for a while.” The poster made an effort to convey the often‐banned f‐word per haps in an attempt to avoid being moderated but had no problem calling the other poster out as an “idiot.”

## Denouncing the institutes

The CICA<sup>7</sup> was a frequent target for posters to condemn. Many CAs posted emotional messages about the unification using some strong language to criticize the institutes. For example, in a somewhat lengthy posting (about 280 words), one poster expressed his or her anger by using capitalized letters for whole words such as “WHY” and “NO” and combined punctuation marks such as “?!”. The poster mocked the institutes as tyrants or dictators who had the “audacity” to ignore members' opinions. Like other posters, he or she also used words like “your members” and asked rhetorical questions, clearly targeting the institutes. Other participants were even more direct in targetin the institutes' administrators. One participant posted:

“The position paper outlines the case for uniting our profession … have we missed anything [quoting a

rhetorical question posted online by the forum administrator]?”

How about the fact the CA and CMA leadership that is proposing this stupid idea missing out on the fact

that this ridiculous idea has already been proposed years back ‐ and members have unequivocally and

resoundingly all said NO!

What do not they get? NO is no is no!

Can we fire the idiots that are wasting our time and memberships fees on this?

Such emotional outbursts of yelling and calling people “idiots” are not commonly seen in typical business setting. But in this case, the disinhibiting effects of anonymity are very clear. There was likely no need for the poster to control his or her emotion and unhappiness about the unification. Ironically, it is also an indication of powerlessness. The poster and other participants who opposed the unification, paid their membership fees, which were used by the institutes to fund marketing campaigns that worked against their goals

## 4.1.2 | Intergroup fighting

Other than simply expressing their opposition or support in some emotional manners, posters often engaged in inter group fighting. In many cases, they belittled each other.

## Degrading other participants

One of the possible reasons why many CAs objected to the unification was the seeming higher status they had in th Canadian accounting field. Thus, there were some arguments about who were better than whom, In many cases such arguments escalated to explicit disparaging. Take the following exchange as an example:

## (A CA Participant)

Unfortunately, I did know about CMA. That's why I will not consider my comments as lack of humility. Instead, I think was just commenting on the truth that people like you are trying to conceal—stealing the wealth but pretending morall innocent at the same time

## (A CMA Participant)

“Stealing the wealth but pretending morally innocent at the same time”???? (Question marks in original) What wealth would be stolen? Seriously, just what new found rights or man from heaven do you think is going to be bestowed upon anyone if a merger were to ever happen? …

Here, the CA poster was not self‐aggrandizing but rather mocking the other group (CMAs) as thieves who steal wealth and pretend to be moral. The CMA poster appeared to be somewhat angry about such condemnation. He or she fired back and charged (along with CAs in general) as arrogant, “puffed up egos,” and only “alleged” to be professionals. Such condemning that targeted other participants could easily drive up the tension and make people more rather less, angry.

## Forming social groups and showing solidarity

After some initial postings on the forum, posters were able to build some social bonds and form social groups based on their opinions as to whether they supported or opposed the unification. Such in‐group social bonds appeared to be clearer at the end when the online forum would be shut down. Take the following exchange as an example:

(T. Account King)

Bang on TAK [referring to T. Account King], this thing will hang on the provincial merger tree a few more weeks and fal and rot on the ground … with only two more days on this forum, well done and all the best to you

(Toastymaster)

Toasty – thanks. Good luck going forward.

(Proud in HK)

Toasty, TAK…and others that I have missed – All the best to you and stand firm on your belief. It has been my honou to fight alongside with you all.

Best,

A proud Canadian Chartered Accountant currently based in HK

As the above exchange indicates, some form of social bond has developed within the group. They appeared to feel that they were together to fight for the same cause (ie, either opposing the unification and defending their cherished professional identity as CAs or supporting the unification and promoting “CPA” as their common professional iden tity). They were able to build such social bonds because of the online forum enabled them to make the connection (by using anonymous user names), despite the fact that they likely did not know each other offline and that they were located far away from each other (eg, in Hong Kong and Canada). It is interesting to note that they used acronyms (eg, “TAK” for “T. Account King”) and seemingly diminutive names (eg, “Toasty” for “Toastymaster”), despite the fact that all names are just screen names. Such practice may be seen as an indicator of solidarity.

## “Identity theft”

Despite the fact that the online forum was anonymous, user names or pseudonyms registered by participants were subject to somewhat creative abuse, akin to the popular concept of identity theft. Of course, pseudonyms are not “identities” per se. However, the very nature of anonymity allowed participants to register with different pseudonyms as many times as they wanted. In some cases, a poster may intentionally choose a pseudonym very similar and almost identical to others. Such similarity caused some confusion and led to some posters' call for discipline. Take the follow ing postings as an example:

I presume the author of post #6803 picked the screen name “alisonji” in an attempt to create confusion with the creator of this thread, “alysomji.” Readers should be cautioned that these are different posters.

In this case, the poster (T. Account King) had to remind readers of the seeming identity theft. T. Account King, one of the most active participants from the opposing camp, was also a victim of such identity theft. Someone else regis tered with “T. Account\_King” and “T. Account.King” in apparent attempts to troll and confuse people.

To prevent potential theft of their online “identities,” posters also suggested registering the same pseudonyms on other forums when this forum was set to be shut down. Consider the following exchange:

(Random\_Act)

“Proud in HK” and TAK [two posters], we should continue this discussion on the website [that another poster mentioned]. The only question will be whether or not we get new user IDs or keep our current ones???

(Proud in HK)

Random\_Act – Great! I will try to register as “Proud in HK“.

Do you know who is going to monitor it, if you know what I mean?

(Random\_Act)

I think that we'll figure out if the monitor is good or not very quickly. The website does not look like the handiwork of the CICA or a similar institute. We could always create a new e‐mail account to sign up so that we would remain anonymous while registering. I guess I'll stick with Random\_Act then.

(Proud in HK)

Just to be clear, I will try to register as “Proud in HK” but someone may have used this user name to create some noise in the forum.

It was apparent that both Random\_Act and “Proud in HK” were eager to continue their discussion on the unification issue on a different website. It is interesting to note that they both wanted to make themselves “identifiable” (at least with their current pseudonyms) by using the same user names on the new website, even though they also preferred anonymity. They worried about the possibility of “identity” theft, which may damage the consistency and integrity of their continued discussion. For this reason, some forms of “good” monitoring or discipline may b deemed necessary.

## 4.2 | Discursive discipline

Some participants appeared to be well aware of the nature of the anonymous forum and, therefore, were not surprised by the emotional outbursts, scolding, degrading, and denunciation that occurred on the forum. For example a poster quoted earlier suggested that discussion in a non‐anonymous setting would be “more civil” but “limited.” Another poster commented that “over a beer would make [discussion] go better.” Others were apparently offended and unhappy about the seemingly unprofessional postings and called for professionalism and de‐anonymization. In the eyes of these participants, there was an urgent need for discipline

## 4.2.1 | Technological discipline

## Being moderated by forum administrators

Participation in the online forum was moderated by one or more designated forum administrators. A posting may b partially edited or completely removed. Although there were published rules about what may be posted on th forum, moderation was at the discretion of the forum administrators (and, by extension, the institutes). Take the fol lowing posting as an example:

Let us be honest, it was better than the last attempt in 2004.

(This comment was partially edited to comply with the forum guidelines. View the forum guidelines at/forum‐guidelines/)

Here, the posting was partially edited by the forum administrators. The editing rendered the posting incomplete in terms of what the poster wanted to say beyond his apparent praise of the unification. An important characteristic of such discursive punishment is visibility. As the preceding quotes show, there is a clea indication of monitoring and moderation in most cases. ## Being confined to the online forum

The irony of the online forum is that posters were trapped in and appeared to be unable to do anything meaningfu bevond the forum, It should be noted that the online forum was not the only communication channel between the insti: tutes and their members. Other channels included town hall meetings, phone calls, and emails. Yet the online forum appeared to be the only feasible and meaningful way for members to express their opinions and hope to hear back from the institutes. As previously mentioned, many members had considerable faith in the use of the online forum. Posters appeared to be motivated to participate in online discussion, believing their voices were being heard. But that the institutes were listening may have been too bold an assumption. In fact, many posters questioned how the online forum might be used. For example, one poster was suspicious about the institutes' intentions to set up the online forum. To him, posters like himself might have been wasting time “debating air” and being trapped by what the institutes had “thrown…at the wall.”

Thus, it may be said that such discursive confinement is perhaps the most effective form of discipline, whereb posters were unable to go anywhere else or do anything other than expressing themselves, emotionally or rationally on the online forum. Leaving the virtual space risks one being revealed too much, thereby defeating the objective of remaining anonymous. Take the following posting as yet another example:

If anything, the almost desperate plea, “What we need is a hero…We need a voice” says it all. The poster appeared to be powerless, unable to use the online forum in meaningful ways to make any difference. Their “voices” appeared to be rather silent, not heard by their intended audience. But most of all, the posting shows the ultimate discipline of th forum despite the freedom of expression enabled by online anonymity.

## 4.2.2 | Peer discipline

Posters were also powerless because they were monitored by opponents on the online forum. There were severa ways in which peer monitoring could happen. First, a posting could be flagged by anyone to alert the forum admin istrators, who would then edit or remove the posting. For example, poster “Proud in HK” expressed his frustration about being flagged and moderated:

CAs opposing the merger as we are drawing closer and closer to 31 May when this forum will be shut down.

Other than simply flagging a posting, opponents could also post a message to criticize a poster and alert the forum administrators. Take the following posting as an example:

That is racist…[referring to a poster], you should apologize immediately.

I am very serious. I hope we can identify you by email/IP address, because I have every intention of

Moderators I hope you are paying attention.

Here, the poster tried to not only draw the attention of the moderators, perhaps trying to assist them to discipline th other poster, but also threatened to disclose the other poster's identity. Although it was unclear as to whether h actually had the means to do that, the attempt to discipline the other poster was clear. Given the anonymous natur of the forum, he was most likely bluffing. But such threatening, however unrealistic, may have made the other poster think twice. After all, the institutes had full control of the forum and might have been able to trace the poster's Inter net address as the poster threatened.

Other than alerting the forum administrators, opponents could also track and question a poster's posting record Take the following somewhat lengthy posting as an example:

Here, the poster questioned, in an “auditing” manner, the integrity of another poster by examining his or her seemingly inconsistent statements, thereby bringing the latter under the gaze of peer monitoring. It was not just about what the other poster said but also about when he or she said it (“in a matter of 18 hours”). It is interesting to note that the peer monitoring shown in this example was made possible by two technical features of the online forum namely, archival and timestamping. The archival feature afforded the poster the possibility to track and repost one by one the many postings made by the other; the timestamping function, on the other hand, afforded the poste the means to chronicle the other poster's postings and use the time frame to question his or her integrity.

Last, posters' behaviour (not just what they posted) was also under surveillance by peers. Take the following post ings as an example:

Here, the two posters were questioning the online behaviour of “Proud in HK.” Both of them seemed to doubt whether “Proud in HK” was actually in Hong Kong. Poster A appeared to have examined the timestamps of the post ings “Proud in HK” made. Poster B, on the other hand, appeared to have paid much attention to the sheer number o postings by “Proud in HK.” The commonality is that both of them questioned the online behaviour of “Proud in HK” by utilizing the timestamping and archival features of the forum. In other words, these features of the forum enabled the posters to do the discipline work

## 4.2.3 | Self‐discipline

In many cases, posters became self‐disciplined, thereby appearing to reduce their own power. In this case, the mod eration by the forum administrators and monitoring by other accountants (ie, their opponents) appeared to be unnec essary. Instead, posters disciplined themselves by watching and monitoring their own behaviours and postings. Take the following posting as an example:

By carefully choosing his words and attempting to make clarifications, th poster was essentially recognizing the power of the forum administrator and putting himself under continuous control or discipline. Otherwise, his postings would risk being removed. In this sense, the empowerment of free expres sion was diminished to some extent. He may in some way have become less emotional and less critical of the institutes on the online forum. His self‐disciplining was further indicated in another posting:

Here, the poster was apparently frustrated about being overly moderated and treated unfairly by the forum admin istrators. He complained half‐jokingly and half‐sarcastically. He attempted to control himself in his way of dealing with his frustration about being treated unfairly, whereby he opted for self‐deprecating (“train ing shortcoming of mine”) instead of emotionally targeting anyone else. ## 5 | DISCUSSION

To have a better understanding of the various concepts and their relationships discussed so far, we theorize a mode of anonymity and discipline in anonymous online communities (as shown in Figure 1). In this section, we elaborat this model in more detail in terms of the nature of the tension and the role of technology (the online forum in this case) and the implications for the very concept of Foucauldian discipline

## 5.1 | The tension between anonymity and discipline

The model highlights the tension between anonymity and discipline when individuals utilized information technology such as the online forum to interact and debate with each other. On the one hand, anonymity allowed individuals to keep themselves (the real persons) from being identified and, therefore, to remain unknowable to a certain extent. As a result, they enjoyed some freedom of online speech (the discursive freedom box in Figure 1). On the other hand the individuals were constrained in the sense that they self‐censored and were disciplined by others, including those who set up and controlled the very technology that they used and the other users of the same technology (the dis cursive discipline box in Figure 1).

One cause of tension was the fighting between various groups of users. There was a clear change of discursive forms when posters freely discussed online. Initially, they were mostly voicing their disapproval or support of the uni fication. But as the process continued, the discussions escalated to fighting, sometimes in angry and abusive language (the arrow within the discursive freedom box in Figure 1). The fighting and seeming lack of professionalism—and per haps the desire to make one's voice heard and not buried in the mess—drove some individuals to call for and engage in discipline (the far‐right bold arrow in Figure 1).

There was also change in disciplinary forms in the process (the arrows within the discursive discipline box in Figure 1). Initially, the disciplinary action was largely done by the site administrators to edit or delete inappropriate postings; posters were essentially confined to the online forum. But later on it escalated to peer monitoring (eg, flagging and reminding the forum administrators for any postings that were deemed inappropriate). It was further escalated to self‐discipline, whereby posters watched and monitored their own behaviours and postings, where in some cases they asked the forum administrators to remove their postings.

To a certain extent, the online forum may be seen as an omniopticon,<sup>10</sup> which depicts a more complex picture than a Foucaudian panopticon or synopticon (Mathiesen, 1997) does. Whereas the Foucaudian panopticon can be summarized as the few watching the many and synopticon as the many watching the few, the online omniopticon here is about the many watching the many. But it is not just about the sheer number of the watcher or the watched but also more importantly its complex nature and the multiplicity and fluidity of the mechanisms through which discipline works.

## 5.2 | Technology‐enabled mechanisms

Given the tension between anonymity and discipline, it may be said that, in contrast to the Foucauldian panopticon (where inmates are knowable and identifiable), the online forum examined in this study enabled anonymity (the dot ted arrow in Figure 1) and, at the same time, an antithetic discipline (the solid bold arrow in Figure 1). Here, we high light two important, nuanced technological mechanisms and elaborate the differences compared with the Foucauldian panopticon.

## 5.2.1 | Visibility

The Foucauldian type of panopticon centres on the visibility of “the body” with an emphasis on behaviour. Through infinitesimal observations, a specific case may be built, and knowledge may be accumulated for changing an individual's behaviour. In recent studies on organizational information systems (Brivot & Gendron, 2011; Doolin, 2004 Elmes et al., 2005), the body is also the focus, whereby what individuals do is captured and made visible for management control. But in an anonymous online setting, the body is not observable. What is observable and subject to scrutiny is what individuals say, ie, discourse that reflects what they think. Thus, in this sense, bodily or behavioura visibility is replaced with discursive visibility. Anonymity in this regard hides the body but not the discourse or mind

As prior research has indicated (Whitley. 1997), some form of knowledge may be inferred or accumulated about an individual through discourse, Such knowledge may be just about one's point of view, an argument or any specific factual matters. But more importantly, it may be about one's emotions and social status. For example, with some leve of reliability, we can make sense of individuals' emotions based on their postings. They expressed their emotions either in words explicitly signifying emotions or somewhat indirectly in the ways they expressed their opinions. The also disclosed their designations, which are social identities that represent their social status in the professiona accounting field. Such visibility of emotions and social identities is important because it facilitates discipline. Being emotional may be criticized as “unprofessional." thereby degrading the social identity an individual represents ever if he or she remains anonymous. Such knowledge about emotion and social status has not been examined in Foucault's theorization or those recent IS studies cited above.

Another aspect of visibility is the very display of discipline. In his study of the history of penal systems, Foucaul suggests that panopticism has replaced public execution, which entailed “spectacle” or “ceremonial of torture” for people to watch, with hidden punishment. Most recent IS studies that employ the panopticon concept have largely focused on the visibility of the body and ignored the punishment element, which is typically outside the systems studied. Punishment in the online forum illustrated in the CPA Canada case, however, not only played an important role but also depicted a different mechanism. Punishment was made public; visitors to the online forum would know, and the punished would be reminded of, the punishment and who was punished. As general deterrence theory (Nagin & Pogarsky, 2001) suggests, such punishment visibility may have some impact on disciplinary effects. Individuals wil likely think again about what they do if they see other people being punished for similar behaviour

## 5.2.2 | “Little things”

The Foucauldian panopticon emphasizes “little things” or “petty mechanisms” of discipline and the accumulation of knowledge about the disciplined. It is through little things that make discipline work. The online forum illustrated in this paper relies on some little things in similar ways. Each posting, sometimes as little as one word, was intended for posters and others to read but was also subject to meticulous discipline. It may be deleted by the administrators; it may be flagged by anonymous posters using, literally, “little flags.”

Another good example examined in this study is the seemingly trivial function of timestamping. On a typica online forum, it may be used just for organizing postings. But those participants in the online forum utilized the timestamping function in many different ways. In one case, it was used by a participant to chronicle and discredi another poster's postings. In other cases, the same function was used by others to monitor another poster's online behaviour. In this sense, participants in the online forum exercised certain levels of human agency (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) in terms of how a technical feature or function is used.

Indeed, it may be said that there was nothing “big” on the online forum and that small things may make big differences. It was through such small things that individuals were punished and made aware of them being unde continuous watch, which may not be exactly the publicity or readership they wished for. But more tellingly, it was through some little things individuals were able to escape discipline, albeit to a limited extent. For example, some individuals were able to avoid moderation by using vague acronyms and substitutes for profane words (such as “rhyme with ducking”).

## 5.3 | The anonymous online self

While they matter in terms of how the online forum enabled both anonymity and discipline, the technology‐enabled mechanisms do not answer the question of why the seemingly contradictory anonymity and discipline coexisted. We argue that the why question may be answered by the concept of anonymous online self, which reflects and mediates the tension between anonymity and discipline and, relatedly, between the unknowable and the knowable. In this regard, the anonymous online self may be seen as an oxymoronic portmanteau of anonymity and identification On the one hand, one desires to be anonymous, unknowable to others; yet, on the other hand, one wants to be iden tifiable and knowable to the extent that others know “it's me” rather than someone else

An anonymous online self in this sense is different from data doubles (Haggerty & Ericson, 2000) or virtual selve (Whitson & Haggerty, 2008) because there is not a direct, visible link between the online self and the person behind it. But it is not just a trivial matter. Posting history may be put together by observers to build a profile of the online self without knowing the real person. Although there is a real person behind it that is ultimately disciplined, the punishment may be mediated by the seemingly anonymous online self. To a certain extent, it may be said tha the anonymous online self has its own ontology. Its postings may be edited or deleted; it may be flagged or punished But the real person can continue to talk by posing as a different, anonymous online body

Take, for example, “T. Account King” and “Proud in HK,” two of most active posters on the online forum. Although it was the postings that were subject to discipline, who the poster was is also important. Thus, “T. Account King” (the person) worried about the “theft” of his online identity by those behind “T. Account\_King” and “T. Account.King.” Fo him, it was necessary to make sure “T. Account King” (the online self) was consistent and did not confuse readers about “its” opinions. Thus, it is as if it was the virtual “T. Account King” but not the real person that was speaking on the forum. To a certain extent, then, the online self became the focal point rather than the discourse (what was said) and the real person (who said it).

Similarly, the online self was also important for “Proud in HK.” Although he did not worry about identity theft as much as “T. Account King” did, potential “impersonation” did concern him. Such impersonation was not about himself —the real person—but about his online self. For him, there was a need to maintain the integrity of “Proud in HK”—th online self—across different places on the Internet in which accountants may gather to discuss the issue on hand. It was also necessary to maintain a good image of the online self by watching “its” language in a self‐disciplinary manner, because even though it was anonymous, the online self has a social identity—the group it was supposed to represent—the seeming value of which was the very reason that accountants like “T. Account King” and “Proud in HK” (the real persons) came to the online forum to debate

Overall, the concept of anonymous online self sheds some fresh light on the very meaning of anonymity. On the one hand, anonymity (and the related concept pseudonymity) has often been seen as objective and binary: whethe one (the real person) can be identified or not. We argue that such objective and binary conceptualization may not be adequate for explicating the complex nature of anonymity. It is also subjective, and there is a difference betwee “being” and “feeling” anonymous (Kennedy, 2006). Objectively, if tracing and identifying the real person behind an anonymous online self is difficult (although not impossible), then in this sense, the real person can be said to “be” anonymous. Subjectively, the online self is identifiable to the extent of its own ontology with discourse, emotions and integrity. In this sense, anonymity is more subjective where the real person may be said to “feel” non‐anonymous and expect a unique identity to be maintained and protected.

On the other hand, anonymity has largely been conceptualized at the individual level. But as shown in this paper, the anonymous online self also implicates one's social identity. The link between the anonymous online self and socia identity is in line with what is termed as social identity model of deindividuation effects (Reicher, Spears, & Postmes, 1995), which suggests that anonymity leads to a shift in focus from one's individual identity to one's social identity a a member of a group. In this sense, the social identities or the shared professional identities of those accountants constituted the nexus between anonymity and discipline.<sup>11</sup> The importance of social identity, which is by definition visible, helps explain why calling for professionalism can serve as a disciplinary device.

Taken together, the unique characteristics of the anonymous online self helps shed some light on the multiplicit and fluidity of identity in the digital age.<sup>12</sup> One's self is not limited to or fixated on the body. When something is hidden, something else may become visible and, subsequently, become salient in one's conception of the self

## 5.4 | The dynamics of discipline

This paper also offers some nuanced understanding of power and discipline. The Foucauldian panopticon features what is termed as anonymous power, whereby the dominant become anonymous while the dominated become indi vidualized and identifiable. In Foucault's words, the modern panopticon does not have a king, which was the person that held the ultimate power in pre‐modern society. However, there is a clear structure of power hierarchy in terms of the dominant (eg, warders, teachers, and military officers) and the dominated (eg, prisoners, students, and soldiers)

We also see anonymous power in the online forum. There were three sources of disciplinary power on the online forum examined in this paper: the administrators or moderators, peers, and participants themselves. They were al anonymous to the extent that the real persons were not identifiable by observers. For example, a posting may be flagged; but it would be almost impossible to find out who posted it. But the disciplinary effect was clear and signi fying, however, minute the flag was. Thus, it is not just about lateral visibility (ie, everyone can see everyone) (Elmes et al., 2005) but also about lateral discipline (ie, everyone can, to some extent, discipline everyone).

Anonymous power should not however, be overstated in the case of the online forum In the case of CPA Canada's unification, there was perhaps a “king”—the power centre of the institutes represented by their executives The “king” was in full control of the online forum: how it was set up, who could participate, and ultimately when it was to be taken town. As Lyon (2003) puts it, information systems like the online forum are not self‐financing; they are set up by those with special interests in control and influence. Ironically, the “king” was elected and funded (through membership fees) by rank‐and‐file accountants. This structural arrangement adds another dimension to the dynamics of discipline and resistance between the dominant and the dominated

Self‐discipline is of particular interest here. In the Foucauldian panopticon, discipline “makes” individuals, who were regarded as “objects” and as “instruments” of the exercise of power (Foucault, 1977, p. 170). Thus, individuals subjected to Foucauldian discipline lacked agency. In this sense, self‐control may be superficial and half‐hearted (Los 2002) or become “dressage‐as‐response” (Berente, Gal, & Yoo, 2010) in the Foucauldian panopticon. It is, however, different in the online forum examined in this paper. Posters demonstrated their agency in several different ways. First, they participated in the forum voluntarily, therefore subjected themselves to the gaze of the institutes (admin istrators or moderators) and peers. Second, in their intergroup fighting, posters appeared to be assisting the moder ators to exercise their power. For example, they flagged other posters' apparently objectionable postings and “talked” to the moderators. Last, individual posters cared about the perceived image of their online “selves” and the socia identity they represent and, perhaps more importantly, the effectiveness of their resistance to or promotion of the unification—their greater cause. In this sense, it may be said that their self‐discipline was wholehearted. From this agential standpoint, then, it is arguably the human agency of individuals that makes effective the power to which they are subjected. The effects of discipline are not unilaterally exerted on but co‐constructed by individuals.<sup>13</sup>

## 5.5 | Boundary conditions, limitations, and future research

There are several boundary conditions and limitations that must be considered for interpreting the findings of this paper. First, as with typical qualitative research, this study does not necessarily support the direct generalization of findings to other settings in the statistical sense. The context of this study should be considered when interpreting the results. This study focuses on a specific occupational group (professional accountants) during a special time period (institutional change). The unique setting of the accounting field may have some influence on how people use technology in daily life and professional career and how they deal with discipline. These settings may be different from those with clear organizational boundaries or Internet censorship in general.

Second, while we believe that the theoretical model can be applied to other anonymous online communities, a likely boundary condition is that there exist some meaningful interpersonal and intergroup differences. If there are no such salient differences, then self‐identity and social identity may not be relevant. A particular dimension of such differences discussed in this paper is the political nature of the unification. Those accountants who participated in online discussior had some vested interest in the unification. It was apparently a loss for those who opposed it; but it was likely a gain fo those who supported it. The technology in this case (ie, the online forum) was like a virtual political arena for them to fight for their vested interests. As a consequence, their emotions and power may be involved in some dynamic ways Thus, future research may examine others settings to strengthen the findings of this study.

Third, the data we used are limited to what participants posted on the forum. Although we have tried to quote post ings as completely as possible so that readers can have a better understanding of the data, our interpretation may be biased or not fully reflect the participants' views. Thus, future studies might use other research methods to collect data to corroborate the findings. For example, interviews may help researchers get a better understanding of subiects' back ground and allow follow up with questions to probe their thinking. For example, are they really emotional? Do they really feel freedom and exercise restraint? The interview method would enable learning more about users

Fourth, the role of the moderators—like the guards in the Foucauldian panopticon—is worth further investigation for our theoretical understanding of discipline.<sup>14</sup> While we have focused on forum participants' postings, the moderators have had an effect through the deletion and modification of postings. Due to the lack of complete data (beyond the indication of deleted or modified postings), we were unable to examine in detail the thinking of the moderators and how the interaction between the moderators and participants may have played a role in discipline. Future studies with interview data may offer new insights into the role of the agents of the powerful and dominant

Last, the relation between what happened online and offline is worth investigating.<sup>15</sup> In this paper, we zoomed in to focus on what happened online while treating offline events as the background research context. We suspect that the online forum may have been used as political tool by the institutes to push through the unification. Thus, it ma be fruitful to theorize about the relation between online and offline from a political perspective. Such consideration may advance our understanding of power (discipline) and resistance (anonymity).

## 6 | CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this paper deepens our understanding of the Foucauldian sense of power and discipline in anonymou online spaces. Despite the condition of anonymity, however, discipline can still be present in multiple forms. In thi sense, the Foucauldian panopticon is still a useful metaphor for understanding power and discipline in the Internet age.
