# Enterprise social media use and overload: A curvilinear relationship

Xiayu Chen<sup>1</sup> and Shaobo Wei<sup>2</sup>

Journal of Information Technology 1–17 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2018 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621880272DOI: 10.1177/0268396218802728 journals.sagepub.com/home/jinf SAGE

## Abstract

Considerable research has focused on the positive effects of information technology use. However, emerging research and practice commentary highlight the importance of considering the negative side of information technology use. The current study investigates how enterprise social media use (i.e. work- and social-related use) influences employees perceived overload (i.e. information and social overload), which in turn affects enterprise social media-related strain. In addition, we posit that communication visibility moderates the nonlinear relationship between enterprise social media use and overload. Using a survey of 282 enterprise social media users in the workplace as a basis, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between work-related enterprise social media use and information overload and between workrelated enterprise social media use and social overload. Moreover, a U-shaped relationship is found between social related enterprise social media use and information overload and between social-related enterprise social media use and social overload. Communication visibility positively moderates the inverted U-shaped relationships between workrelated enterprise social media use and information overload and between work-related enterprise social media use and social overload, but negatively moderates the U-shaped relationship between social-related enterprise social media use and information overload. The theoretical and practical implications are also discussed.

## Keywords

Work-related use, social-related use, information overload, social overload, communication visibility, strain

## Introduction

Enterprise social media (ESM) has attracted the attention of contemporary organizations because it can support interpersonal communication, coordination and collaboration among employees (Leonardi, 2015; Mäntymäki & Riemer, 2016; Schlagwein & Hu, 2017). ESM consists of digital platforms that incorporate bundled social media tools, such as microblogs, wikis and social networking services; thus, ESM allows information sharing and social interactions among employees within organizations (Rode, 2016). ESM use is commonly restricted to the employees of an organization (Leonardi, Huysman, & Steinfield, 2013). The literature on ESM is unequivocal on the linear effect of ESM use (Mäntymäki & Riemer, 2016; Odoom, Anning-Dorson, & Acheampong, 2017). ESM scholars have suggested that ESM use can facilitate knowledge sharing among employees (Ellison, Gibbs, & Weber, 2014; Treem & Leonardi, 2012). These scholars have highlighted that employees’ work- and non-work-related ESM participation can engender different effects on job performance. Clearly, the studies on ESM have hitherto mainly focused on the positive linear effect of ESM use.

However, further attention should be given to the potential negative consequences associated with ESM use. Scholars of information systems (IS) have recently warned us of the negative side of information technology (IT) use (Tarafdar, Gupta, & Turel, 2013). The negative side of ESM use has important managerial implications because of considerable investment in ESM from companies. ESM use may lead to employee distraction (Ali-Hassan, Nevo, & Wade, 2015) and poor task execution (Lu, Guo, Luo, & Chen, 2015). The indiscriminate promotion of ESM use may increase employees’ perceptions of overload. As a consequence of this concern, some companies have banned ESM use in the workplace (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015).

We take the literature on ESM beyond the negative side of ESM use. Consistent with prior research (e.g. Lee, Son, & Kim, 2016), we accept that ESM use can initially lead to employee overload. Nevertheless, we provide additional theoretical precision to this argument. Information and relationship processing is a dynamically complex procedure that may be influenced by an individual’s past experiences (Hsu & Liao, 2014). This deduction means that ESM use initially increases employees’ perceived overload because of limited information and relationship processing capacities. However, employees’ accumulated ESM use enhances their experiences, thereby increasing their information and relationship processing capacities. We postulate that ESM use leads to overload up to a point at which increasing benefits offset the drawbacks and that employee overload decreases beyond this point. Thus, we suggest that the relationship between ESM use and overload is curvilinear rather than linear. This curvilinear relationship also helps explain the paradox of positive and negative effects associated with IT use (Tarafdar et al., 2013).

However, the effect of ESM use on overload is not independent of the specific ESM context. Compared with other technologies developed for use in organizations, ESM provides a forum for open communication among employees (Leonardi, 2015; McAfee, 2009). In particular, ESM enables the visibility of communications between two employees to everyone in the organization (Leonardi, 2014, 2015). Communication visibility may play double-edge roles in the workplace (Gibbs, Rozaidi, & Eisenberg, 2013). On the one hand, communication visibility provides employees with quick opportunity to locate experts in a particular domain and find common interests and hobbies (Ellison et al., 2014). On the other hand, communication visibility promotes employees to access too much information and to give too much social support that is beyond employees’ processing capability (Stohl, Stohl, & Leonardi, 2016). This situation suggests that the effect of ESM use on overload is dependent on the critical contextual factor of communication visibility. However, no study has examined the moderating role of communication visibility in the relationship between ESM use and overload.

This study addresses the above-mentioned issues in literature by investigating (1) the inverted U-shaped curve in the relationship between ESM use (work- and social-related use) and overload (information and social overload), (2) the communication visibility that moderates the curvilinear linkage between ESM use and overload, and (3) the effects of information and social overload on ESM-related strain.

## Literature review

## ESM use

ESM is defined as

web-based platforms that allow workers to (1) communicate messages with specific coworkers or broadcast messages to everyone in the organization; (2) explicitly indicate or implicitly reveal particular coworkers as communication partners; (3) post, edit, and sort text and files linked to themselves or others; and (4) view the messages, connections, text, and files communicated, posted, edited, and sorted by anyone else in the organization at any time of their choosing. (Leonardi et al., 2013, p. 2)

Compared with conventional collaborative technologies, such as knowledge management systems and USENET, ESM provides new social features in the workplace (Kane, Alavi, Labianca, & Borgatti, 2014). Researchers have theorized the positive outcomes of ESM use, such as facilitating knowledge sharing among employees (Leonardi, 2014; Treem & Leonardi, 2012) and increasing social capital (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015).

Lu et al. (2015) argued that ESM allows employees to engage in work- and non-work-related activities. In one case, employees can read work-related posts or exchange opinions and advice through ESM (Lu et al. 2015). Employees’ work-related activities may positively influence their task execution and performance. In another case, ESM can be used for building and maintaining relationships with coworkers (Leidner, Koch, & Gonzalez, 2010; Leonardi & Meyer, 2014). Employees’ non-work-related activities can sometimes negatively affect their task execution and performance. On the basis of a case study informed by boundary theory, Koch, Gonzalez, and Leidner (2012) divided ESM use into work and social aspects. Thus, in this study, we consider two types of ESM use, namely, workand social-related use.

To date, most studies on social media use have been conducted in the human–computer interaction communities and the computer-supported cooperative work contexts and have focused on specific technologies (Leonardi et al., 2013). Scholars of IS and organizations have recently explored ESM use (Lu et al., 2015; Sun & Shang, 2014). Although some recent studies have described employees’ ESM use in detail (Mäntymäki & Riemer, 2016), only a few have focused on the implications of ESM use for employees.

## Perceived overload on ESM

Overload indicates an individual’s evaluation and perception of the kinds of things that exceed one’s ability to handle (Zhang, Zhao, Lu, & Yang, 2016). This concept has been applied to different research areas, such as role overload (Tarafdar, Tu, Ragu-Nathan, & Ragu-Nathan, 2007)

work overload (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011) knowledge overload (Hunt & Newman, 1997), and communication overload (Stephens et al., 2017). The development of information and computer technology (ICT) has facilitated the research on technology overload, which describes that ICTs force individuals to work faster and longer than before (Tarafdar et al., 2007; Tarafdar, Tu, & Ragu-Nathan, 2010). Technology overload is inversely related to employees’ work productivity (Karr-Wisniewski & Lu, 2010; Tarafdar et al., 2007).

The proliferation of social media has induced new types of overload, including rapid changes in technological features of social media, involuntary extensions of social networking and excessive information (Lee et al., 2016). Zhang et al. (2016) proposed three dimensions of technology overload in social networking site contexts, namely, system feature, information, and social overload.

System feature overload occurs when the features provided by the social media technologies are too complex for a given task (Zhang et al., 2016). System feature overload may impede employees’ productivity. This can be explained by cognitive load theory, which indicates that the cognition of an individual is bounded by the limited working memory (Huang, Eades, & Hong, 2009). If the system becomes too complex, then information loss and memory overload will occur, thereby leading to poor performance (Karr-Wisniewski & Lu, 2010).

Information overload occurs when the information provided by social media technologies exceeds individuals’ processing capabilities (Lee et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2016). Bounded rationality asserts that individuals’ deci sion making is constrained by their cognitive limitations (Karr-Wisniewski & Lu, 2010). Thus, individuals make optimal decisions because of information processing capacity. When individuals seek more information than necessary, their decision-making performance may be decreased (O’Reilly, 1980).

Social overload is defined as individuals’ perception that they are giving too much social support to others in social media and that they are out of duty to respond to social support requests (Maier, Laumer, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2015; Zhang et al., 2016). Social overload was initially proposed by McCarthy and Saegert (1978) to emphasize the negative sides of population crowding. McCarthy and Saegert (1978) described that individuals have to invest much time to maintain relationships with the increase in regional populations, thereby possibly generating psychological distress. Joinson, Houghton, Vasalou, and Marder (2011) developed the concept of digital crowding and defined it as excessive social contact and self-disclosure. ESM is similar to a virtual society. Thus, social overload can also exist in this context.

Different from the technological features provided by public social media, those by ESM are not too complex. In addition, ESM designers continuously develop user-friendly interfaces to improve user experience. Our interviews with actual ESM users have also suggested that system feature overload is not their concern. Thus, this study focuses on two types of overload in the context of ESM, namely, information and social overload.

## Communication visibility theory

The adoption of ESM enables visibility of workplace communication among employees to a broader organizational audience (Leonardi, 2014, 2015). Workplace communication among employees, which is an aspect of the work environment, has long remained private (Leonardi, 2015). Nevertheless, Treem and Leonardi (2012) proposed that ESM promotes employees’ awareness regarding their coworkers’ activities at unprecedented rates by providing employees’ visibility into the occurrence of routine communications among their coworkers. The novelty of ESM from other preceding communication technologies is that ESM allows an employee to see the communicative activity among their coworkers even if that employee is not directly involved with that communication (Leonardi & Meyer, 2014). For example, the communicative exchanges between two coworkers on an ESM technology often appear on the newsfeed or wall of an employee outside the communicative activity (Jarrahi & Sawyer, 2013). That employee can articulate the social networks and tag documents produced by his or her coworkers within ESM, thereby providing outsiders with further visibility into the communication partners of his or her peers (Kane et al., 2014; Kietzmann, Hermkens, McCarthy, & Silvestre, 2011; Leonardi, 2014).

An emerging theory of communication visibility indicates that, once invisible communication occurring among coworkers becomes visible for third parties, the third parties can improve their knowledge of “who knows what” and “who knows whom” (Leonardi, 2014, 2015). Communication visibility theory further suggests that ESM enables communication visibility because “they provide a transparent exchange of messages of people with their communication partners and translucent network connections” (Leonardi, 2015, p. 749). While the theory of communication visibility makes intuitive sense (Flyverbom, Leonardi, & Stohl, 2016) relatively few researchers have empirically verified this theory.

## Hypothesis development

## Effect of work-related ESM use on information and social overload

Work-related ESM use initially increases information overload. When employees use ESM for work purposes, they need to share, disseminate and access a large amount of information rapidly (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015; Sun & Shang, 2014). The employees cannot process the information efficiently and may thus make decision errors, which raised the costs in terms of effort and time. This indicates that work-related ESM use can lead to information overload because of employees’ cognitive limitations.

However, as the extent of work-related ESM use increases, information overload declines because employees are becoming familiar with using ESM to manage the daily work. Previous studies have demonstrated that experienced users can process information effectively and efficiently with less perceived information overload compared with novice users (Dunbar, 1992; Hsu & Liao, 2014).

Therefore, as work-related ESM use increases, information overload increases initially. However, heavy ESM use can reduce the level of information overload. Consequently, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 1. An inverted curvilinear relationship exists between work-related ESM use and information overload.

Work-related ESM use initially increases social overload. When employees use social media for work-related purpose, they are simultaneously engaged in multiple communications with their coworkers at the beginning (Ou & Davison, 2011). Work-related ESM use may therefore interrupt employees’ daily life. This is because employees have to deal too much with their coworkers’ problems and perceive a general loss of control over the interaction on the others in ESM.

However, as the extent of work-related ESM use increases, employees can better know each other via exchanging task-related information and interacting problem-solving. Maier et al. (2015) indicated that an individual’s perception of giving too much social support and social overload can be reduced by enhancing the familiarity with others. This implies that the more employees know each other, the more they are likely to deal with others’ problems on ESM rapidly. Thus, when employees are highly involved with ESM use to process work, they spend less time on social-related activities. Consequently, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 2. An inverted curvilinear relationship exists between work-related ESM use and social overload.

## Effect of social-related ESM use on information and social overload

Social-related ESM use initially increases information overload. Social-related ESM use creates a lifelike and two-way communication channel that is highly interactive in work environments (Sun & Shang, 2014). Employees may thus receive a variety of information via such communication channel and need to spend much time to deal with irrelevant information. The exponentially increasing amount of message generated through social-related ESM use may lead to information overload.

Beaudoin (2008) indicated that individuals’ heavy social resource motivation for media use can reduce their perceptions of information overload. As the extent of social related ESM use increases, personal interaction and quality communication improve. In such a quality communication process, mutual understanding among employees is likely to be developed (Quan-Haase, Cothrel, & Wellman, 2005). Mutual understanding facilitates the information being easily exchanged with each other (Huang, Chen, Ou, Davison, & Hua, 2017). Thus, employees who heavily use ESM for social purposes are likely to confront fewer obstacles and perceive fewer problems in terms of information overload than their counterparts. Consequently, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 3. An inverted curvilinear relationship exists between social-related ESM use and information overload.

Social-related ESM use initially increases social overload. Social use of technology results in users accessing a large pool of dissimilar individuals (Wellman, Haase, Witte, & Hampton, 2001). Thus, when employees use ESM for social purposes, employees need to pay continuous attention to their coworkers, which urges them to be exposed to an overwhelmed volume of social demands. Maier et al. (2015) noted that exposure to social requests is an important precondition of users’ perceptions of social overload.

However, as the extent of social-related ESM use increases, employees may obtain high social support from interactions with other coworkers in ESM (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015). Heavy social-related ESM use makes employees believe that they are valued, cared for and helped by other coworkers (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015). This situation enhances their willingness to provide social support and valuable social resources to others. In addition, heavy social-related ESM use enables employees to accumulate social aspect experiences. Accordingly, their relationship processing capacity increases, which is vital for reducing social overload. Therefore, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 4. An inverted curvilinear relationship exists between social-related ESM use and social overload.

## Moderating role of communication visibility

Communication visibility prompts employees to be exposed to routine communication, including questions about problems and work assignments among other coworkers (Leonardi, 2015; Treem & Leonardi, 2012; Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, & Hu, 2016). Under high communication visibility, work-related ESM use drowns employees in a sea of boundless and unstructured information that overwhelms their interpretive and cognitive capabilities and thus renders information confusion. By contrast, when employees’ perception of the level of communication visibility is low, the influence of work-related ESM use on information overload is weak because employees may face less taskrelated information when using ESM for work.

Communication visibility serves as a lightweight method to know what colleagues are doing and with whom they are talking (Ellison et al., 2014; Leonardi, 2015). Under the condition of high communication visibility, employees who use ESM for work-related purposes are likely to have access to a large social network. Such uncontrolled contacts may increase the probability of receiving some form of social support requests. Conversely, low communication visibility enhances employees’ confidence to control the interaction with other coworkers. Thus, employees who use ESM for work-related purposes are less willing to offer support to each request.

Hypothesis 5. Communication visibility moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between work-related ESM use and information overload, such that when communication visibility is high, the rate of information overload increases associated with increasing workrelated ESM use is faster.

Hypothesis 6. Communication visibility moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between work-related ESM use and social overload, such that when communication visibility is high, the rate of social overload increases associated with increasing work-related ESM use is faster.

High communication visibility causes employees to be anxious about the exposure of their incompetence and deficiencies (Arazy & Gellatly, 2012), and employees may consider that ESM use for social-related activities needs much time and effort, thereby increasing employees’ degree of loading perception. In other words, when communication visibility is high, the influence of social-related ESM use on information overload should also be amplified. The reason is that communication visibility forces employees to maintain their positive social image on the ESM that may require employees to share and process considerable information with other coworkers. Conversely, in situation of low communication visibility, employees may need less time and effort to maintain their social image when using ESM for social-related activities.

Visibility can also inevitably generate a normative pressure from peers because of employees’ inclination to comply with other coworkers’ views (Hester, 2010). As a consequence, employees feel obliged to offer benefits to their social network. Thus, high perceptions of communication visibility indicate high perceptions of social pressure for employees to perform their behavior. When the level of communication visibility is high, employees who use ESM for social-related activities may provide too much support to their networks because of normative pressure. Thus, the influence of social-related ESM use on social overload should be reinforced under the condition of high communication visibility. By contrast, in low levels of normative pressure, social-related ESM employees may be reluctant to give too much social support to others.

Hypothesis 7. Communication visibility moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between social-related ESM use and information overload, such that when communication visibility is high, the rate of information overload increases associated with increasing socialrelated ESM use is faster.

Hypothesis 8. Communication visibility moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between social-related ESM use and social overload, such that when communication visibility is high, the rate of social overload increases associated with increasing social-related ESM use is faster.

## Effect of information and social overload on ESM-related strain

Strain represents an individual’s potentially harmful and unconscious psychological response to stressors, such as perceiving information overload when using ESM (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Information overload can distract employees from other important activities in the workplace (Karr-Wisniewski & Lu, 2010). The high speed of producing and diffusing information on ESM inflates the negative consequence. Wurman (1989) indicated that the typical outcomes of information overload incorporate feelings of anxiety, helplessness and stress. Zhang et al. Hypothesis 9. Information overload is positively related to ESM-related strain.

## Research methodology

## Measurement development

We used the survey method to test the proposed model. All the measurement items were adapted from existing validated scales. We slightly modified some items to ensure their suitability for our context. We used a 7-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”), to measure all items. Specifically, work-related use was measured using a five-item scale adapted from Ali-Hassan et al. (2015). Social-related use was assessed with a five-item scale adapted from Ali-Hassan et al. (2015). Information overload was assessed with four items adapted from Zhang et al. (2016). Social overload was measured using a six-item scale adapted from Maier et al. (2015) and Zhang et al. (2016). Communication visibility was measured with a three-item scale adapted from Leonardi (2015) and Rice et al. (2017). ESM-related strain was measured with four items adapted from Ayyagari et al. (2011). Appendix A lists the measurement items.

Because this research was conducted in China, following Van de Vijver’s (1997) approach, we translated the English questionnaire into a Chinese version. Then, we hired a professional translator, who knew nothing about our study, to translate the questionnaire from Chinese back to English. When we compared the translated English questionnaire with the original English version, we found no semantic discrepancies. Furthermore, three IS PhD students were invited to review and critique the questionnaire to ensure content validity.

Some demographic variables (i.e. gender, age and education) and ESM use hours that may affect ESM-related strain were included in the research model as control variables.

## Data collection

To identify appropriate respondents for this research, we cooperated with Chinese Software (a pseudonym), a large software development firm with over 7000 employees headquartered in Southern China. In late 2011, this firm used an ESM technology, called “circle,” for internal communication among employees. The circle looked nearly the same as publicly available social media technologies, such as Twitter and Weibo.

We conducted the survey by using a user list offered by the Chinese Software for about 2 months in 2016. On the basis of this list, we randomly selected 1000 users and then sent out the online survey hyperlink. To increase the participants’ response rate, we provided a payment of RMB 15–20 (approximately US\$2.18–US\$2.91) as an incentive for each respondent. A total of 282 valid responses were received. Table 1 shows the demographic information of the respondents.

To assess non-response bias, we used the approach suggested by Armstrong and Overton (1977). We compared the first 25% and last 25% of respondents for all the constructs. The tests indicated that t-statistics for the difference in all construct means were insignificant, suggesting that non-response bias was not a serious issue for this study.

Given that the data were collected from a single source at the same time and were perceptual, we further tested common method bias. We followed Harman’s single-factor method (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986) to evaluate the six conceptual variables in our model. The results revealed that four constructs had eigenvalues greater than 1, explaining 71.73% of the total variance. The first factor accounted for 22.16% of the variance. Therefore, the threat of common method bias for the results was minimal.

## Analysis and results

We used partial least squares (PLS), particularly, SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle, Wende, & Will, 2005) to test the research model. First, PLS, which is a second-generation component-based structural equation modeling (SEM) technique, can evaluate measurement model and structural model (Chen, Huang, & Davison, 2017). In particular, PLS can explain complex relationship because it avoids inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy issues (Fornell & Bookstein, 1982). Second, compared with covariancebased (CB) SEM, PLS is robust with fewer issues, such as statistical identification (Chen et al., 2017). Third, PLS and CB-SEM can offer very similar parameter estimations, but CB-SEM is particularly suited for theory development (Chen et al., 2017).

## Measurement model

The measurement model was evaluated by testing construct validity and reliability. As shown in Table 2, the loadings of all items were above the recommended level of 0.7 (Carmines & Zeller, 1979). The values of average variance extracted (AVE) ranged from 0.738 to 0.838, and all exceeded the desired value of 0.5 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Hence, these results demonstrated that our measurement model had adequate convergent validity. ## Discussion and implications

## Discussion

This study has several key findings. First, an inverted U-shaped relationship is found to exist between workrelated ESM use and information overload and between work-related ESM use and social overload. However, the relationship between social-related ESM use and information overload and between social-related ESM use and social overload is a U-shaped curve. One possible explanation is that social-related ESM use initially reduces information and social overload because of the potential benefits associated with social-related ESM use such as stimulating the development of close social connections. Nevertheless, too much social-related ESM use may result in information and social overload because too many social connections among employees reduce the boundaries between work and social roles (Koch et al., 2012).

Such reduction increases nonwork communication and gossip (Leonardi et al., 2013).

Second, communication visibility is found to positively moderate the inverted U-shaped relationships between work-related ESM use and information and social overload. However, we find a negative moderating effect of communication visibility on the U-shaped relationship between social-related ESM use and information overload. The reason is that high levels of communication visibility may result in social comparison, which increases employees’ feeling of burdensome (Fox & Moreland, 2015). High feeling of burdensome makes employees who use ESM for social-related activities less likely to spend additional time to process information. Thus, the rate of information overload decreases associated with increasing socialrelated ESM use and is slower under high communication visibility.

Third, social overload is found to positively affect ESMrelated strain, whereas the effect of information overload is insignificant. A possible explanation is that information overload may indirectly influence ESM-related strain through social overload. Our additional mediating test also confirms that social overload $( \beta = - 0 . 0 3 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ fully mediate the relationship between information overload and ESM-related strain.

## Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations that open up research opportunities for future research. First, this research considers communication visibility as a moderating factor. Future research should explore additional factors that exert different effects on the relationships between work- and social-related ESM use and information and social overload. For example, different individuals may have dissimilar abilities to process information and social requests.

Second, this study focuses on internal social media (i.e. ESM) for empirical evaluation. Although researchers and practitioners have become interested in investigating internal social media, employees’ external social media use (e.g. WeChat, Twitter, Facebook) is also worthy of further research and discussion. Unlike ESM that is restricted to be used only by employees within the organization, external social media can be used not only with coworkers but also with friends and family members (Moqbel, Nevo, & Kock, 2013). Thus, future research should consider how external social media use induces different overload perceptions.

Third, while we focus on work- and social-related use based on the existing ESM literature, other types of ESM use behaviors may need further attention. Examples are reinforced use that emphasizes social media use in enhanced and repetitive ways, and varied use that emphasizes social media use in innovative ways or with various new features (Pan, Lu, Wang, & Chau, 2017). Therefore, future research should investigate the influence of these types of ESM use on employees’ perceived overload.

Fourth, the data collected in this study is cross-sectional. The significant relationship between ESM use and overload is correlational based on the statistical perspective, and it can only be explained as causal from theoretical inferences. Future research can conduct more rigorous design, such as longitudinal data collection, to further verify the causal effects and capture the dynamic and complex relationship between ESM use and overload.

Finally, this study collects data from China. Given that China has unique national culture, our findings may be influenced by the cultural characteristics of the respondents. For example, previous research has indicated that Chinese employees may be unwilling to share their knowledge openly in a publicly accessible system (Davison, Ou, & Martinsons, 2018). This may also explain why communication visibility exerts a negative effect in this study. Thus, researchers should be cautious when generalizing our finding to other cultural contexts. Future research can explore how national cultures interact with communication visibility and influence social media use behavior.

## Theoretical implications

This research has several important implications for theory. First, previous studies have long focused on the positive linear effect of ESM use (Mäntymäki & Riemer, 2016; Odoom et al., 2017). For example, Kuegler, Smolnik, and Kane (2015) indicated that intra- and inter-team ESM use can improve employee task performance and innovation. Ali-Hassan et al. (2015) argued that ESM use contributes to social capital building among employees. Recently, scholars have called for future IS research to explore the negative side of IT use (Tarafdar et al., 2013). The present study responses to this call in the IS literature by empirically theorizing and verifying the role of ESM use in influencing employees’ perceived overload. Moreover, the findings of this study represent an important advancement in the IS use literature by challenging the conventional wisdom of a negative linear side of IT use. Our study theorizes the paradox of ESM use and offers empirical evidence of a curvilinear relationship between ESM use and overload. Thus, we address the following question proposed by previous researchers: “What theoretical frameworks can be developed to understand the paradox of positive and negative impacts associated with IT use?” (Tarafdar et al., 2013, p. 271).

Second, we identify communication visibility as a moderating factor and thus consider specific social media function characteristics. Previous studies have considered social interaction as a moderating factor influencing the relationship between overload and stickiness in the context of public social media (Hsu & Liao, 2014). However, no study has recognized the specific social media function characteristic factor that moderates the curvilinear linkage between social media use and overload. The current results show the interesting effects of communication visibility on the curvilinear relationships between work- and social-related ESM use and information and social overload.

Third, this study offers a novel view of overload. Prior research on overload has focused only on either information overload (Jones, Ravid, & Rafaeli, 2004) or social overload (Maier et al., 2015). Although Zhang et al. (2016) considered information and social overload in the context of public social network services, the importance of differentiating information and social overload in the organizational setting (particularly, ESM context) is still unknown. The current findings empirically demonstrate the validity of dividing overload into information and social aspects in ESM contexts given that the two types of overload behave differently in terms of their outcomes. Thus, this study provides a fine-grained understanding on the role of overload in the ESM context by specifying information and social overload.

Another contribution of this study is that it bridges a gap in existing social media literature by identifying two kinds of social media use behaviors, namely, work- and socialrelated use. The distinctions and consequential effects of the two important social media behaviors need to be determined. Social media use is considered a critical factor in the causal chain from social media implementation to individual and organizational success (Karahanna et al., 2018; Kuegler et al., 2015). Most studies on social media use have commonly regarded it as a broad behavior construct (Moqbel et al., 2013; Trainor, Andzulis, Rapp, & Agnihotri, 2014). A single conceptualization or measure cannot fully capture a complex construct such as social media use. In fact, when using a single measure of social media use, different findings regarding outcomes of use can emerge (Moqbel et al., 2013; Odoom et al., 2017). In this regard, distinguishing between the two types of social media use provides valuable insights into the current research on social media use. To follow up on this contribution, we suggest future work to determine the change in social media use patterns over time.

Finally, this study contributes back to communication visibility theory. Prior research has mainly focused on the positive side of communication visibility (Ellison et al., 2014; Leonardi, 2014). For example, Leonardi and Meyer (2014) found that communication visibility enables information seekers to gather information simply by watching others’ actions through technology, thereby facilitating information sharing and transfer. However, the negative side of communication visibility is overlooked. Our find ings demonstrate that communication visibility inevitably enables employees’ access to too complex and diverse information, thereby increasing employees’ barriers to collaborate. Therefore, the openness enabled by communication visibility may backfire by generating unproductive organizational behavior. Clearly, these responses violate the tenets of effective information sharing. Consequently, this study provides a comprehensive view of communication visibility theory.

## Practical implications

This study provides some valuable guidelines for practition ers. First, our study suggests that heavy work–related ESM use may reduce employees’ perceived information and social overload. Thus, managers should encourage employees to use ESM for work purposes by providing simple and lightweight tools that limit the degree of social interaction and allow employees to exchange task-related knowledge effectively and efficiently while minimizing distractions.

Second, our results indicate that social-related ESM use initially can reduce information and social overload. Some companies have banned the social functions when implementing the ESM technologies out of fear that the negative side will be associated with social-related ESM use. However, we recommend that appropriate ESM use for social purposes can help employees reduce their perceptions of information and social overload.

Third, we suggest managers to decrease employees’ perceptions of communication visibility of ESM for mitigating the curvilinear relationship between social-related ESM use and overload. For example, managers can enable modification of the default settings for the ESM technology. In this way, any employee who uses it can choose to see or not to see other coworkers’ profiles and posts.

Managers should also be aware of the indirect negative effect of information overload on ESM-related strain, considering that “content is king” in social media. Managers should provide some content management functions on ESM, such as showing a summary of posts or recommending content depending on different employees’ interest. This way allows employees to have a good experience in processing information on ESM.

Finally, managers should notice the negative impact of social overload. We suggest managers to provide effective filtering tools for reducing social network complexity and helping employees manage social requests and relationships. For example, a sophisticated recommendation function for friends can be provided to reduce social network complexity. Providing tools that can be used to help employees manage different groups in social networks is also necessary.

## Conclusion

This study examines the nonlinear relationship between ESM use and overload, which in turn influences ESMrelated strain. In addition, we consider the moderating effects of communication visibility on the curvilinear relationship between ESM use and overload. The results indicate that the relationship between work-related ESM use and information overload and between work-related ESM use and social overload is an inverted U-shaped curve, whereas the relationship between social-related ESM use and information overload and between social-related ESM use and social overload is a U-shaped curve. Communication visibility positively moderates the inverted U-shaped relationships between work-related ESM use and information overload and between work-related ESM use and social overload, but it negatively moderates the U-shaped relationship between social-related ESM use and information overload. The findings of this study provide suggestions for academics and managers in reducing ESM-related strain. Investigating the negative side of social media use is an interesting direction for future research.

## Author biographies

Xiayu Chen is an instructor in the School of Management at the Hefei University of Technology. She obtained her PhD degree from University of Science and Technology of China and City University of Hong Kong. Her research focuses on the electronic commerce and social media. She has published papers in journals such as International Journal of Electronic Commerce (IJEC), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), International Journal of Information Management (IJIM), Information Technology & People (IT&P) and Computers in Human Behavior (CHB).

Shaobo Wei is an assistant professor in the School of Management at the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). He obtained his PhD degree from USTC and City University of Hong Kong. His research focuses on supply chain integration, IT business value creation and social media. He has published papers in journals such as Journal of Operations Management (JOM) and International Journal of Logistics Management (IJLM), as well as in conference proceedings, including those of the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS).
