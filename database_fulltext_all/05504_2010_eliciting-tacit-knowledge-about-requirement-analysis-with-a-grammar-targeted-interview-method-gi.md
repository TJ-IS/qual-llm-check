# Eliciting tacit knowledge about requirement analysis with a Grammar-targeted Interview Method (GIM)

Michele Zappavigna<sup>1</sup> and Jon Patrick<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

The assumption that tacit knowledge cannot be articulated remains dominant in knowledge elicitation. This paper, however, claims that linguistic theory does not support such a position and that language should not be factored out of accounts of tacit knowledge. We argue that Polanyi’s (1966, p. 4) widely cited notion that ‘we know more than we can tell’ uses a folk model of language. This model does not acknowledge the linguistic patterns that competent language speakers deploy without direct awareness. This paper draws upon Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) to propose a Grammar-targeted Interview Method (GIM). The GIM uses SFL to unpack linguistic patterning, which we refer to as ‘under-representation’, to reveal tacit assumptions. It is a strategy that can be applied within a traditional interview method when the interviewer feels that there is confusion resulting from assumptions, such as those often embedded in terminology, that have not been directly expressed. This paper reports findings from an empirical study of tacit knowledge about requirements analysis in a Content Management System redevelopment. We compared the GIM with a Content-motivated Interview Method (CMIM) and show that, when the GIM is used, interviewees respond with less nominalised talk, that is the less nominalised content has more meaning unpacked as verbs and agents rather than hidden tacitly in nouns.

Keywords: tacit knowledge; knowledge management; requirement analysis; knowledge elicitation; Systemic Functional Linguistics

## Introduction

Tacit knowledge allows us the experiential agility to be at once efficient and creative, to assimilate the novel and the familiar: in essence, to develop expertise. Capturing and sharing the knowledge held by experts in organisations is a consistent problem in Information Systems and Knowledge Management research (Boisot, 1995; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Wenger, 1998). However, language has been factored out of accounts of tacit knowledge and Polanyi’s maxim (1966, p. 4), ‘we know more than we can tell’, remains the dominant perspective. This paper seeks to move beyond folk characterisation of what it means ‘to tell’ by applying a mature linguistic theory to knowledge elicitation. We demonstrate a Grammartargeted Interview Method (GIM) that uses Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) to elicit tacit knowledge from individuals by analysing the patterns in their talk in real time by a human analyst.

This paper is structured to give the reader an insight into the flaw of construing tacit knowledge as unspeakable. We begin by critiquing the linguistic basis of Polanyi’s assertion that tacit knowledge is not carried in language. We go on to propose an extension to Polanyi’s model that accounts for the way knowledge may be ‘packed-up’ in spoken discourse due to the linguistic patterning employed by a speaker. We propose a GIM that uses questioning technique aimed at unpacking such patterning in order to elicit tacit knowledge from interviewees. The latter sections of this paper detail a field study that uses this GIM interview technique and compares the interview responses against those generated with a Content-motivated interview method (CMIM) in the requirements analysis phase of a CMS redevelopment.

## Background: Polanyi’s Theory of Tacit Knowing (TTK)

The model of tacit knowledge applied in this paper is a development of Polanyi’s TTK. Polanyi (1958) suggested that tacit knowledge is inherently personal, underlying our ability to perform tasks we find difficult to explain such as facial recognition. His model of tacit knowing is cited in most serious investigations of tacit knowledge (Baumard, 1999; Richards & Busch, 2003; Castillo, 2002; Tsoukas, 2002; Tsoukas & Mylonopoulos, 2004). Polanyi’s TTK describes two kinds of awareness that he argues are part of the structure of consciousness: focal awareness and subsidiary awareness. Focal awareness refers to conscious attention, while subsidiary awareness is below-view in the sense that the person does not directly attend to the given phenomena. Tacit knowing is the process by which elements in a person’s focal awareness are integrated with elements in their subsidiary awareness. For example, in stereovision, a person is not directly aware of the two images that come together to make the image that they perceive with the illusion of depth. Polanyi refers to this as tacit integration, the fundamental configuration in TTK.

The present paper adopts the tenets of Polanyi’s TTK with the exception of his principle of ineffability, that is, the principle that tacit knowledge cannot be expressed in language. This is the attribute most consistently ascribed to tacit knowledge (Reber, 1993; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Baumard, 1999; Collins, 2001). The strong position is that tacit knowledge cannot be articulated in any linguistic form, while the weak position holds that it is difficult to articulate or only subsets may be articulated (Busch, 2004). We would locate Polanyi’s TTK at the stronger end of this continuum. Given that Polanyi’s (1966, p. 4) concept that ‘we know more than we can tell’ asserts the epistemological significance of tacit knowledge in terms of its inability to be articulated with language, it should be assessed using a coherent linguistic theory.

Exactly what Polanyi meant by ‘telling’ remains unclear but it is key to understanding his maxim. While he acknowledged that language production is itself an act of tacit knowing, he did not exploit the implications of this conceptualisation. If we allow that ‘telling’ involves patterning of which a speaker is not necessarily aware, and that such patterning is meaningful, language is reinstated as relevant to understanding tacit knowledge. We might argue about whether meaning is continuous with knowledge, but this is more likely a disciplinary distinction with ‘meaning’ preferred in Linguistics, and ‘knowledge’ used in Information Systems to refer to equivalent ideas.

Interpretations of Polanyi’s theory from the perspective of knowledge elicitation have argued that a reason that tacit knowledge cannot be carried in language is that once something is articulated it is codified and therefore no longer tacit. This, however, is a misinterpretation of Polanyi who has himself argued that language production is a tacit process and not intrinsically a codification. Thus we assert ‘talk’ can be mined for tacit knowledge because articulation is not the same thing as codification. When we say something we articulate it, in the sense that we say the words, but we do not codify it. We view codification as a post-articulation process of interpreting language according to a structural schema that in production is confounded by processes of interpretation. So, when we talk, the linguistic patterns that occur are not directly apparent until linguistic analyses of the kind undertaken in this paper are performed.

## Tacit knowledge elicitation

There has been extensive research into the acquisition of what is termed ‘explicit knowledge’ despite Polanyi’s (1969, p. 144) objection that ‘all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge’. However, methods for eliciting tacit knowledge remain problematic. General knowledge elicitation methods such as protocol analysis (Ericsson & Simon, 1993) and interviews (Agarwal & Tanniru, 1990), even though they do not specify explicit strategies for uncovering tacit knowledge, do obtain discourse from subjects. This may be due to assumptions, such as the kind made by Ambrosini (2001, p. 815) that ‘instruments such as surveys and structured interviews are likely to be inappropriate [for eliciting tacit knowledge] insofar as individuals cannot be asked to state what they cannot readily articulate’.

The small subcategory of knowledge elicitation approaches aimed specifically at uncovering tacit knowledge typically consider scenarios (Sternberg, 1985, 2000) and narratives (Linde, 2001). Again, while eliciting discourse is the focus of these methods, they do not leverage the value offered by close linguistic analysis for justifying their assertions about the discourse. As Halliday (1994, p. xvi) notes ‘discourse analysis that is not based on a grammar is not an analysis at all, but simply a running commentary on a text’. If we, however, draw on research from Linguistics, there is a rich tradition of uncovering below-view patterning in language. For example, tacit assumptions and ideological positions that characterise various technical domains such as scientific discourse (Halliday & Martin, 1993; Martin & Veel. 1998). technocratic discourse (McKenna & Graham.

2000) and administrative discourse (Iedema, 1997) have been uncovered using SFL.

There is, however, a body of work within domains such as organisational semiotics (Stamper, 1997) that approach knowledge in organisations in terms of how meaning-making is enacted (for an overview see Clark, 2001). These perspectives draw on a range of semiotic theories, that is theories that consider meanings in terms of systems of signs, and may be considered fellow travellers to the research undertaken in this paper. There is also a range of work that considers language in action from a social perspective, applying an information systems viewpoint (Winograd & Flores, 1986; Suchman, 1987; Hirschheim et al., 1996; A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk et al., 2006).

## An overview of SFL

The present study aims to unpack linguistic patterns that under-represent meaning in spoken discourse. The theory of language employed to identify these patterns is SFL. SFL is aligned with the functional tradition in linguistics manifest in the Prague School ( Jakobson, 1971) and arose out of the linguistic school known as Firthian Systemics (Firth, 1957). It is a functional theory because it is targeted at answering questions about how meanings operate within the particular contexts in which they are created.

SFL stratifies language into patterns of emergent complexity as shown metaphorically in Figure 1. According to this perspective, discourse semantics may be considered patterns of lexicogrammatical patterns and lexicogrammar patterns of phonological patterns. SFL uses the technical term ‘realisation’ to refer to this relationship between strata. The lexicogrammatical stratum in Figure 1 is highlighted in grey to indicate that the kinds of linguistic analysis undertaken in this paper are at this level of language. SFL adopts the term ‘lexicogrammar’, as opposed to ‘grammar’, to accommodate at this strata two types of ‘words’: content words (the lexicon) and function words (function words). Function words are a closed set of words (e.g. pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions etc.) that have a grammatical purpose, and thus best considered part of lexicogrammar rather than lexis (Halliday, 1989).

SFL is systemic because it models meaning as selection from a network or system of choices. The actual choice a speaker (or writer) makes in a particular context is referred to as the instantiated meaning, whereas the other possible choices they could have made is referred to as the meaning potential. Linguistic choices are represented by using system networks. These are decision tree-like structures that are arranged in terms of increasing delicacy, that is, from most general to most specific meaning (Matthiessen, 1995, pp. 14–15).

The key functional unit in SFL is the clause. A clause corresponds to the notion of a simple sentence. For example, I built the system is a clause. A complex sentence can contain multiple clauses. For example, I built the system and they didn’t use it. This sentence contains the additional clause and they didn’t use it. Halliday (1994, p. 106) proposes the clause is central in construing experience, claiming that ‘it embodies a general principle for modelling experience – namely, the principle that reality is made up of PROCESSES’. A process might be thought of as the heart of the clause. In lay terms, a process is a verb, that is, something that happens such as ‘eating’, ‘thinking’, ‘saying’ and ‘playing’, or something that ‘is’ such as ‘being’ or ‘existing’. Processes are important to this study as they are the common content that is made tacit by various lexico-grammatical constructs such as nominalisation.

## Nominalisation

The present study attempts to explicate what we label ‘under-representation’ in spoken discourse. Under-representation refers to instances in which knowledge has been packed-up through particular grammatical choices. In this study we focus on the case in which a process undergoes nominalisation. Using traditional grammatical terms, this is the rendering of a verb as a noun. This is only one way in which meaning can be ‘under-representation’ grammatically (for an explanation of other grammatical features associated with under-representation, such as effaced agency, modality choice and generalisation, see Zappavigna, 2007).

According to SFL, nominalisation is a structural feature ‘whereby any element or group of elements is made to function as a nominal group in the clause’ (Halliday & Matthiessen, 1999, p. 41). In simple terms, a verb is rendered as a noun. When a procedure or course of action is nominalised, it becomes less available for analysis as its components are obscured. However, we do this because the process under reference is more easily reconstrued as part of other happenings in the discourse that we wish to give emphasis or attention to. The process converted into an entity can be readily used to combine with other processes to explain more complex ideas. This, for example, is common in constructing labels for scientific taxonomies (Rose. 1998).

An important consequence of the nominalised form is that it is less contestable, as Halliday says ‘you can argue with a clause but you can’t argue with a nominal group’ (Halliday & Martin, 1993, p. 39). The nominalisation camouflages what SFL refers to as the agent in a clause. The agent is the participant that is the external cause of the process, should one exist. For example, in the clause, she developed a useful system, the agent is she. The nominalisation, system development, embeds both the process of developing and the agent who does the developing. As the results section will discuss, analysing patterns of agency in talk can make visible the assumptions a speaker makes about the roles of various participants in the process of system development.

Nominalisation is a useful feature through which to compare the knowledge elicitation methods as it is a grammatical feature that is commonly used by speakers but difficult for them to consciously manipulate should they become aware that the interviewer is focusing on their grammar. This is due to the distance of grammatical categories from everyday experience. However, nominalisations are not so cryptogrammatical that an interviewer cannot identify them in real-time discourse. They typically sound longer than more congruent forms: for example, compare, ‘This is what I require’, with, ‘The requirements analysis states that’ (in which ‘requirements analysis’ is a nominalisation).

## Introducing the GIM

The purpose of the GIM is to define a method for unpacking under-representation in an interviewers discourse. Making a distinction between content and process is important for understanding the GIM. Put simply, content is ‘what’ we say, while process is ‘how’ we say it. Most interview methods used in knowledge elicitation employ content-motivated strategies that deal with the topics that occur in the conversation. The focus of the GIM method is, however, on process, and asking questions that probe the grammatical choice a speaker has made (Table 1). Necessarily the question arises about when it is appropriate within an interview to focus on process. We argue that the GIM is best employed in instances in which there is some confusion relating to

\- assumptions embedded in terminology;

\- interpersonal conflict about which the participants are unclear;

\- practical knowledge that the interviewee finds difficult to explain.

Under-representation will occur throughout an interviewee’s discourse; however, many instances will not be relevant to the interviewer’s objectives. Over-use of the questioning technique will interrupt the interpersonal flow of the interview and thus the GIM should be used in a targeted fashion.

Questions about process are questions targeted at grammatical form. This means that they are questions that address how a person speaks. This ‘how’ is tacit because we do not typically think about the grammar we use when we talk because, unlike lexical items, ‘grammatical categories are far removed from experience (Halliday & Webster, 2002, p. 303). Grammatical features used in our model of under-representation are shown in Table 2. The aim of the GIM is to probe instances of such under-representation.

In order to probe under-representation the GIM requires two steps of the interviewer. First, the interviewer has to attempt to identify in real-time conversation an instance of the features presented in Table 2. The instance selected should be relevant to the issues that the interviewee is trying to understand in the interview. After identifying the instance, the interviewer should ask a GIM question aimed at unpacking this feature. Examples of the type of question required by each type of feature is summarised in the final two columns of Table 2.

It should be noted at this point that we are attempting not merely to elicit a more detailed account from the interviewee in terms of precision, but an account that uncovers meaning that has been obscured through under-representation of process. Consider, for example, Table 3. In this table the interviewee was not aware of the way in which she was talking about the Content Management System (CMS) on which she was working. As she admits, she was not aware that she was referring to the system in a very general way as an ‘it’. The ‘it’, though unspecified, was involved in processes of possession (having), action (going) and organising (managing). In fact, in her language the ‘it’ is an agent causing these processes to occur. The speaker endows the ‘it’ the power to precipitate processes even as she is unaware of her usage of the term. Tacit knowledge is below-view in the sense that the speaker does not attend to such usage. The interviewer thus went on to attempt to unpack a description of how the interviewee understood the system. Examples of this type of probing are given in the results section of this paper. In this example an interviewer using a content-based strategy would have asked ‘what are the different types of processes’ and received an enumerated list for an answer.

Our hypothesis is that interview responses to the GIM will contain less instances of nominalisation than responses to the CMIM. A decrease in nominalisation represents a shift in the grammatical choice that a speaker has made. For example, saying the user needs to do X with the system is very different to saying the requirement was X. The former is a proposition that can be probed, while the latter embeds the proposition in a nominalisation. The former is a less under-represented instance of discourse because it elaborates the latter, the nominalisation (the noun ‘requirement’), as processes (the verb ‘to do’) and participants (the nouns ‘user’ and ‘system’).

## Field study site

This study was conducted in an Australian media organisation. The organisation had recently undergone restructuring of its content management processes. A project team had been assigned the task of deciding whether to buy a new CMS, to develop in-house a new system or to re-work an existing system. This team consisted of four people: a project manager and three other employees: two technologists from the Information Technology (IT) department, Technologist 1 and Technologist $^ { 2 , }$ and an information architect from the New Media department. The project stakeholders were drawn from both the IT and New Media departments. The CMS project team were in the requirements analysis phase of the project, involved in activities such as interviewing users and stakeholders, and assessing the usability of the existing system. They were also investigating buying a commercial CMS. The failure of the project brief to specify whether to make, buy or reuse the CMS had problematised the requirements analysis process. As this field study will show, the problems in the project were largely tacit in nature.

## Subjects

Four project team members were interviewed in the study using two different interview methods: GIM interviews conducted by one interviewer and CMIM interviews conducted by a different interviewer. The interviewers were independent and blind to each others’ interviews and the interviewees were blind to the interview strategy used in each interview. Four pairs of grammar-based and content-based interviews were undertaken with each of the interviewees over 5 months. Each interview was approximately half an hour in duration. Multiple interviews with the interviewees were necessary in order to build up a body of knowledge about which topics to cover and to accumulate enough discourse to show statistical characteristics. At the end of each pair of interviews, the researcher developed broad topics that were provided to both interviewers to guide the content of the next pair of interviews. These topics provided a stable platform of content upon which to compare the two interview styles.

## Data analysis

The interviewee responses in the 12 GIM (average length 24 min) and 12 CMIM (average length 19 min) interviews were transcribed and divided into clauses. The first interview with each interviewee was not included in the corpus as these interviews were introductory and did not exemplify the interview techniques. A sample of 150 clauses was selected from each interview: 50 clauses from the beginning, 50 clauses from the middle and 50 clauses from the end. Where the interview contained less than

150 clauses the entire interview was used. The 24 samples formed a total corpus of 3096 clauses.

We investigated the effect of interview type on the tendency of interviewees to use nominalisations in their interview responses. We tagged ‘nominalisation present’ or ‘no nominalisation’ using Systemic Coder (O’Donnell, 2002), a linguistic annotation application. We then tabulated the level of nominalisation for each interviewee in each interview. Nominalisations that occurred in a question and subsequently in the response were not included in the count. This was to minimise the effect of interviewees repeating the words the interviewer had used. In these instances the interviewee’s repetition of the nominalisation is an intervening variable. We performed a repeated measures ANOVA, with the three rounds of interviews forming the repeated observations for each interviewee. Our interest was in determining the within-subjects effect. We also wanted to account for the possibility that there would be a level of nominalisation that varies according to the person rather than the interview type.

## Results

We present our results in two ways. First, after a brief description of the semiotic context, we provide quantitative findings about the amount of nominalisation present in the interview responses by each interview type. We then proceed to more qualitative grammatical analyses of particular instances of nominalisations in the interview responses to show how the GIM methods elicit tacit knowledge.

The GIM interviews with the CMS development project team uncovered each person’s tacit understanding of the requirements analysis activity in which they were involved. The team adopted a Rational Unified Process methodology for managing this activity. While this methodology specified terminology that the team used when talking about requirements analysis, their tacit assumptions about what these meant differed considerably between staff. These assumptions by staff involved competing notions of what it meant to need (stakeholder & user requirements), to organise (systems), to write or speak (documents) and to act (workflow). However, the CMIM interviews conducted by the systems analyst did not address these different assumptions about requirements analysis that were embedded in the language used by the project team members. These interviews instead focused on eliciting content-level descriptions of that activity. The following sections focus on the first aspect of the team’s requirement analysis activity: what the team believed it meant for a user to need to use the system to do something. This was important to the team as the answers set the context for deciding the information to be collected in preparing their requirements analysis report.

## The amount of nominalisation by interview type

The results of the repeated measures ANOVA show there was significantly less nominalisation in the GIM responses compared to the CMIM responses [F(1, 11) ¼ 48.8, P ¼ 0.0002]. Table 4 presents the means for the counted nominalisations by interviewee and interview type.

Figure 2 is a visualisation of the difference in the amount of nominalisation by interview style. The results show that the GIM interview technique has successfully unpacked instances of nominalisation that instead appear in the discourse as propositions involving processes and participants.

Present in the interviews were the two major kinds of nominalisation, unsurprisingly, technical nominalisation and managerial nominalisation. Technical nominalisations were nominal groups about artefacts and procedures to do with technical systems. Managerial nominalisations were nominal groups about artefacts and procedures to do with managing phenomena in organisations (Table 5).

## Unpacking the nominalisation ‘requirement

The technical discourse that the team produced when talking about requirements analysis nominalised the process of users needing to do things with a system in the term requirement. The GIM interviews sought to unpack this nominalisation when it occurred in the interviewees’ talk. The responses that were elicited contained a particular patterning of agency: users were the least frequent agent (7%), while technical and managerial artefacts (33%) and the technologists themselves (54%) appeared much more frequently in the agent role (Figure 3).

As mentioned earlier, an agent in a clause is the entity causing the process to occur. An example in which users’ agency was maximally effaced is the description by Technologist 1 in the third GIM interview of requirement as a capability of the system rather than something owned by users.

The minimal agency ascribed to users suggests that the project team’s tacit conception of a requirement was not necessarily a user requirement. In other words it was not necessarily about empowering users in the sense of accounting for their needs. This is a significant finding as the interviewees all explicitly stated that their requirements analysis activity was ‘about users’. The contradiction points to knowledge that is tacit because the interviewees were not aware of their construal. This lack of awareness had the potential to derail the requirements analysis process and result in costly post-production problems such as user resistance. The implications of their talk are seen in their work practice: the technologists had interviewed a minimal number of users from a very large user base during their requirements collection phase. This created tension among team members about insufficient collection of user opinions. The interviewers believed the coverage of users opinions was dangerously inadequate.

## Unpacking the nominalisation ‘tracing

The interviewees employed the nominalisations, tracing and traceability, to describe the relationships they created between technical artefacts. The nominalisation camouflages that something or someone can trace something to something or someone. If we look at how tracing was unpacked across the corpus, we see that there is systematicity to the interviewees’ responses in that they use relational processes (verbs about states) that are metaphorically material (verbs of action) (Table 6). SFL categorises processes into types: material (doing), mental (thinking or perceiving), relational (having or being), existential (existing) and behavioural (behaving). For example, consider the process to cover. In its material sense, to cover means to physically lay one thing over another, as in the following:

I covered my eyes with my hands.

![](/api/attachments/B9FQ4HA9/fulltext/images/3300f7ae7509ed7a75e8635fcf8c6fc066c315fffe950f05f4e627682ce911a2.jpg)  
Figure 3 The distribution of agency in the interviewee’s talk.

The meaning is, however, relational when cover is used as follows:

It really means that one requirement artefact covers off or covers the scope of the one it is tracing to.

The clause above is the third example to cover in Table 6. The meaning of cover here is to do with one participant ‘dealing with’ or ‘addressing’ the other. This is relational in the sense that it is about an abstract relationship rather than a tangible action: the requirement does not literally place itself over the scope. The requirement in this clause is the users’ opinions about their needs constructed as a thing. The implications of this tacit patterning are that the technologists are dressing up the inactive as active. By talking in this way as if ‘tracing’ is an action and the users’ requirements are things, it is easier for them to get into the situation in which they do not fully engage with their user community.

Figure 4 shows how the team conceives of tracing as a binary mapping between users’ requests and features in the system. However, as this figure visualises, the requests and features occupy different domains of meaning-making by different communities (users and technologists).

The construction of users’ opinions as things and use of metaphorically relational processes to talk about tracing is part of the way the project team’s grammar operates to tacitly solidify their analytical approach. It is part of their genuinely held belief that they are engaging in work activity that is analytical and rigorous. By tacitly rendering the perspective of the users as objects they provide themselves with stabilised immutable artefacts to manipulate.

An alternative way of approaching the activity of tracing would be to construe the user’s opinions as dynamic or fluid. Instead of relational processes of abstraction, this would necessitate verbal and mental processes of negotiation (verbs about talking and thinking). For example, the technologist might talk about what the users say or believe. They would then be more likely to acknowledge the importance of the users’ role and negotiate with them.

## Uncontested meaning in the CMIM interviews

Most instances of under-representation remained uncontested in the CMIM interviews. For example, Table 7 is the phase in the fourth CMIM interview with Technologist 2. The interview topic guide required the interviewer to elicit a description from the subject of the work they had done until that point in the project. The interviewer begins with a broad question about the work that has been happening on the project. The interviewee’s response contains a number of nominalisations in accord with the shorthand style of summarisation. These include market analysis, requirements, proof of concept and implementation. The failure of the interviewer to unpack the nominalisation requirement means that the issues identified through the GIM (described in the previous two sections) are not raised in the interview. The nominalisations are naturalised in the interview and are not foregrounded as possible sites of tacit knowledge. The interviewer has missed the opportunity to uncover the problems the technologists had in their relationship with the user community that were discovered through the GIM method. The portrayal of the CMS project is of a project that has been successful in it requirements analysis and is ready to go and there is no indication that there are problems in the level of communication between the technologists and users.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper we have been able to report on tacit knowledge in two ways: in terms of linguistic patterning that can be assessed quantitatively across a corpus, and in terms of more qualitative grammatical analyses of instances of under-representation. Such a two-pronged approach is required due to the complexity of human language. We have departed from Polanyi’s principle of ineffability to show how a GIM can uncover underrepresentation in talk such participants and processes embedded in nominalisation, and camouflaged agents.

Future research will need to consider the problem that while the interview process allows for a rich description of interviewees’ tacit knowledge, it requires the interviewer to possess a degree of expertise in linguistic analysis. The GIM interviewer, while not a trained linguist, was experienced in linguistic analysis. Since organisations do not typically draw upon this kind of analytical method, research will be required into how to best teach these skills to non-linguists so that they are able to identify under-representation in talk. While linguistic analysis is typically a labour-intensive activity, our method of focusing on a limited set of linguistic features in real time means that we leverage the value of manual analysis while profiting from the relative speed of targeted interviewing.

The implication of our findings is that making a person aware of the meanings embedded in their grammar may improve the way in which they work. For example, in response to a questionnaire about their experiences of the GIM, the interviewees suggested that the questioning process had been beneficial to improving their work practice:

The GIM interviewer’s questions made us better assess the way we worked as a team, and the fundamentals behind the day-to-day requirements our project attempts to address.

They said that it caused them to ask questions about material that they assumed was ‘beyond question’. This was phenomena seen as too obvious to deconstruct,
