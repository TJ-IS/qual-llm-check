# FAIRNESS IN THE INSTITUTIONAL VALUATION OF BUSINESS JOURNALS<sup>1</sup>

Gary F. Templeton

Department of Management and Information Systems, College of Business, Mississippi State University, Mississippi State, MS 39762 U.S.A. {gtempleton@business.msstate.edu}

Bruce R. Lewis School of Business, Wake Forest University, 360 Farrell Hall, 1834 Wake Forest Road, Winston-Salem, NC 27106 U.S.A. {lewisb@wfu.edu}

The fairness of performance evaluation is a concern for all professions, and the appraisal of research output is of particular interest to business scholars and academic administrators. We describe research assessment as a process of social construction that is heavily influenced by journal valuation in business schools. Using journal quality data from multiple sources, we empirically investigate whether the journals in each of eight business disciplines (Accounting, Economics, Finance, Information Systems, Management, Marketing, Operations Management, and Quantitative Methods) are treated evenly across the board. Specifically, we explore whether each business discipline exhibits recognition fairness (i.e., actual institutional journal evaluations are the same as market expectations) and inclusion fairness (i.e., actual availability of publication space in top journals being the same as market expectations). Our findings indicate that faculty in some disciplines enjoy an advantage, while faculty in other fields are disadvantaged. Consequently, we offer recommendations to ameliorate this inequity.

Keywords: Journal valuation fairness, institutional and public business journal lists, citation metrics, research evaluation, social constructionism

## Introduction

As business schools have become more focused on research, the question has naturally arisen as to the objectivity of the methods they employ to assess faculty scholarship (Hitt and Greer, 2012). Business faculty members are sensitive to the even-handedness of their research appraisals (Saunders and Benbasat 2007) and the academic business literature suggests that perceptions of a lack of fairness can lead to faculty dissatisfaction (Dennis et al. 2006; Valacich et al. 2006). Deans are, therefore, motivated to establish research review processes that are fair across disciplines within their schools. These assessments of academic research are typically influenced by the value of the venues (i.e., journals) in which the products (i.e., papers) appear.

Issues of this nature justify the examination of fairness in journal valuation as a direction for research, a topic that has heretofore received scant attention across the business disciplines. In this investigation, fairness is defined as the unbiased judgment of the stature of journals. We examine this subject via data drawn from each of eight business disciplines: Accounting, Economics, Finance, Information Systems (IS), Management, Marketing, Operations Management (OM), and Quantitative Methods. When evidence shows that unfairness exists within a group, it indicates that the members of that group are not being treated justly in the market relative to the others (Arrow 1973).

Apprehensions that current business research assessment systems may not be as fair as they could be (Kock and Davison 2003) highlight the question of how journals in the various business disciplines are valued. Modern organizations compete in the Analytics 3.0 era, which means organizations “have the ability to embed analytics and optimization into every business decision” (Davenport 2013, p. 67). One of the conclusions in this paper concerns the usefulness of analytics in helping establish and improve perceptions of journal valuation fairness. To practice what we preach, we use an empirical approach to objectively assess the status of journal valuation fairness in business academia and to justify specific recommendations. We do so by systematically examining the differences between institutional evaluations of business journals and indicators of their market value and availability in the major business disciplines. As such, the findings of this research should be of significant interest to the entire business academic community as “there is every reason for professional disciplines to have an inherent interest [to understand] themselves better” (Straub 2006, p. 242).

This study addresses questions relevant to the two forms of fairness that may be of concern to business scholars: recognition and inclusion. Because compensation (salary and raises) and career progression decisions (annual reviews, tenure, promotion, awards, and honors) are often at stake, both forms of journal fairness in business research institutions are significant issues. Recognition fairness is concerned with the existence of impartiality in the appraisal of an individual or group (EEOC 2012). For the purposes of this research, recognition fairness means that institutions value journals in a way similar to the academic market. To establish recognition fairness, legal practitioners statistically assess differences between the amounts of acknowledgment subjects receive in organizations and a standardized assessment in a market (Steward 2009). In any discipline, if institutions value journals less than the market, the research of faculty in that discipline is undervalued. Conversely, journals within a discipline having greater recognition than market evaluations are overvalued. Where there are no significant differences between institutional and market-based evaluations, fairness exists. Thus, the first question is:

Research Question 1 (RQ1): Across business disciplines, is there journal recognition fairness when

## comparing institutional journal evaluations to market expectations?

Inclusion fairness is the equitable representation of a group (Wodak 2008). In this study, inclusion fairness exists when the availability of high quality publication space (journals and their articles), as determined by the journal rating practices in institutions, is in sync with market expectations. If a discipline has a significant publication space deficit or surplus relative to the number of scholars active in the field, there is an inequitable state of publication opportunities. Perfect jour nal inclusion fairness occurs when institutions have exactly as many highly regarded journals and article slots in proportion to numbers of faculty in a given discipline. Although dependent on institutional journal evaluations, as is recognition fairness, inclusion fairness can be more serious than recognition fairness, because it addresses outright barriers to entry for individuals regardless of production capacity. Accordingly, the second question is:

Research Question 2 (RQ2): Across business disciplines, is there journal inclusion fairness when comparing availability of publication space in leading journals to market expectations?

This paper begins with a background explication on fairness in the treatment of business journals. We then present the methodology, analyses and findings, summary and recommendations, and conclusions.

## Background

We describe journal valuation fairness following the tradition of applying social constructionist theory (Berger and Luckmann 1966) to the creation of knowledge. The social construction of knowledge explains how different groups of scholars assign value to research at different stages of development toward the socially mediated goal of publication (Mantere and Ketokivi 2013). The next three sections address the challenges and significance of journal evaluation, how journal value and fairness are socially constructed, and the effects of journal valuation biases in business research.

## The Social Construction of Journal Evaluation

Published journal ratings represent at least four ways in which journal evaluations are socially constructed (Berger and Luckmann 1966). First, opinion surveys are the outcomes of many years of dialogue in publishing and interpersonal conversations among peers and mentors with similar interests aimed at publishing in high-quality journals. Second, citations are the result of reading the cited publication, which was written and reviewed by peers who often cite many prior publications that were also peer reviewed. Third, institutional journal lists<sup>2</sup> are socially constructed through various forms of faculty collaboration (e.g., face-to-face meetings, e-mail, blogs, etc.) that culminate in general group agreement as to the results. Fourth, business journal lists publicized by trade publications (e.g., Financial Times 2012) and scholarly leaders (Consortium of Senior Scholars 2011) reflect the aggregated views of discipline members.

In each of these ways, published journal rankings represent both the outcomes and inputs to social construction processes in business research institutions. Journal evaluations have a reciprocal relationship with social processes in pertinent disciplines. For example, Trieschmann et al. (2000) has had an influence on journal evaluation, but has also been met with controversy (Kozar et al. 2006). This example of the dual relationship between journal evaluation and social constructionism shows the importance of establishing fair evaluations of journals. Rightly or wrongly, journal evaluations influence research assessment in the field and consequently shape disciplines and institutions.

## The Social Construction of Journal Valuation Fairness and Bias

The concept of fairness has a significant tradition in the economics, sociology, and psychology literatures, has affected public policy, and may be based on any group or individual characteristic. Valuation fairness is applied to a wide range of phenomena, from tacit communication (Sia et al. 2002) to the more tangible concept of monetary income (Becker 1971). The modern view holds that valuation fairness variations and their motivations are highly context-dependent for each “specific time, place, and space” (Figart and Mutari 2005, p. 481). Therefore, establishing fairness in any profession is complex since assessment measures should be appropriately generalizable across contexts and standardized throughout the market.

In the business research community, fairness is dependent on the outcomes of journal evaluation processes. Therefore, achieving journal valuation fairness is challenging due to potential “biases and political manoeuvring of those who create the rankings" (Harris 2008, p. 375). Academic decision makers often make judgements based on a self-serving bias (Heider 1958; Starbuck 2005), which serves as a filter in many aspects of the social construction of knowledge. Such biasing factors in journal evaluation include self-interests, predispositions, memory bias, society and editorial board membership, previous authorship in journals, methodology, topic of specialization, academic function (e.g., administrative or faculty), and geography (Chan et al. 2009; Holsapple and Lee-Post 2010; Reinstein and Calderon 2006). Important facets of forming these journal prejudices are the faculty interactions and decisions that form each discipline. Deviations from fairness are not always the result of a desire to exclude or even negative feelings toward an affected group (Figart and Mutari 2005). However, prejudices encoded in members about each journal may cause institutional evaluations to deviate from market norms.

## The Consequences of Discipline Journal Biases in Business Research Institutions

When empirical evidence shows that biases exist for a group (e.g., faculty of a discipline in a given institution), its members are treated differently than the community norm (Arrow 1973). Institutions and faculty incur costs if journals in a given discipline are not fairly valued in an institution relative to how it is valued on the market. Institutions may undervalue a journal and compensate faculty marginally less than the market determines they should. At the faculty level, being under-rewarded for premium work can harm morale and discourage production. Faculty may skirt responsibilities as a form of self-compensation, especially if they are tenured. Business disciplines have had a particular problem with faculty leaving academia and this is a reasonable concern in the context of under-appreciating research in a profession growing in complexity. At the institution level, transaction costs associated with open positions and hiring new faculty may be far greater than savings associated with fairly applied faculty rewards.

Institutions overvalue research when they expend associated rewards that are greater than the market justifies. When a journal is shown to be overvalued, institutions are likely overcompensating faculty who publish in that journal, making the institution less fiscally efficient. Among faculty in a discipline, this can lead to a sense of entitlement and partisanship among general business faculty.

The goal of this study is to assess fairness in institutional journal valuations within the business disciplines. We do this by comparing the way in which institutions evaluate journals to market-based expectations (RQ1) and by relating the availability of publishing opportunities in leading journals to market prospects (RQ2).

## Methodology

## Data Sources

The study employed three data sources to investigate journal fairness in the business disciplines. We used institutional journal lists obtained from AACSB-accredited schools. Such an approach has been used in other business journal studies (e.g., Beets et al. 2015; Beets et al. 2013; Meredith et al. 2011; Steward and Lewis 2010). These tiered journal lists are indicators of institutional standards for faculty performance because they impact research appraisal practices affecting hiring, promotion, compensation, and career progression (Beets et al. 2013). Van Fleet et al. (2000) noted that an institutional target journal list “provides an explicit measure of how a department values research outlets” (p. 340). Tiered journal lists created in schools are appropriate for representing the institutional perspective in assessing fairness because they (1) reflect the general academic agreement about the standing of a journal in a discipline when aggregated (Meredith et al. 2011) and (2) can be used to produce a standardize measurement across disciplines to facilitate equivalent comparisons with the market. Further details and results of the institutional journal list collection from AACSB-accredited schools utilized in this study are described in Appendix A.

Second, we employed citation databases, which have been the basis for journal research in various disciplines, including Accounting (Chan and Liano 2009), Economics (Palacios-Huerta and Volij 2004), Finance (Arnold et al. 2003), IS (Katerattanakul and Han 2003), and Management (Podsakoff et al. 2005). Using citation metrics is useful in establishing market-based journal evaluation because they represent material events external to institutions that signify the use of an article in the development of subsequent research. To the extent that citation metrics represent references to legitimate prior findings, they capture the authentic influence of a journal (Garfield 2006; Lynch and Sills 2012), and thus represent the real-world value of the journal in the market. Because citation rates are computed using the same type of data and procedures, journals from different disciplines are treated the same.

Citation metrics are applicable for our purposes since they are positively and significantly related to the practical relevance of research (Baldridge et al. 2004) and are being increasingly accepted by the Association to Advance Collegiate Schools of

Business (AACSB International 2008).<sup>3</sup> We employed multiple sources for citation data in order to take the comprehensive approach advocated in Lowry et al. (2013) and Vanclay (2012). We used the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) database to collect 2-year and 5-year impact factors, the Article Influence (AI) score, and the Eigenfactor. We accessed Scopus to collect the Source Normalized Impact per Paper (SNIP) metric to account for any subject-specific biases (Moed 2011) associated with any journal. All of these metrics were collected for the year 2011. We also employed h-index scores from scimagojr.com (SCImago 2013). In addition, from the JCR database we collected article counts for each journal from 2011.

The third data source was a custom report provided by request to the authors that included the results of a 2011 AACSB survey of its accredited institutions. Following the precedent set by Valacich et al. (2006), we used the number of faculty in each discipline to establish norms for inclusion fairness tests since these data represent a market-based proxy of the expected distribution of journals that should be present in the market in all business disciplines. All faculty in the 2011 AACSB survey were categorized by discipline using the same classification scheme as journals.

Appendix B provides a look at our data for school list, citation, publicized list, and article count metrics for the top 100 journals ranked by weighted average percentile (WAP)<sup>4</sup> and organized by discipline.

## Measures

We use journal evaluation metrics as surrogates to investigate the fairness of research assessment between business disciplines. A valid journal quality metric must have the same meaning across all disciplines and accurately show differences between disciplines (Lowry et al. 2004). This is appropriate since business researchers publish in disciplinary silos (Valacich et al. 2006), which means scholars generally tend to publish within their own fields. Choosing measures to represent journal value and availability should be done based on a fit with the context (Straub and Anderson 2010). Based on this logic, we operationalized the differences between institutional and market conditions differently for each of our two research questions.

## Measures to Investigate Recognition Fairness (RQ1)

Recognition fairness is shown when institutions value a journal the same as the market and can be demonstrated by investigating the presence of biases for or against a group that are not attributable to market considerations. Assessing recognition fairness, therefore, requires capturing and comparing institutional and market-based journal evaluations.

For the institutional perspective, we surveyed AACSBaccredited institutions and collected 72 tiered journal lists (herein referred to as the AACSB72) to establish an institutionbased measure of journal value. To produce a journal quality measure that mitigated exclusivity biases (Chen and Huang 2007), we aggregated across all institutions for the journals contained on the AACSB72. Each journal was then ranked on the WAP as described in Appendix A.

A form of market-based journal assessment is socially constructed when scholars “justify new knowledge in a field” (Mantere and Ketokivi 2013, p. 70) by consuming, contemplating, and citing the concepts and methods found in articles published in journals. Therefore, to assess recognition fairness (RQ1), market-based evaluation measures for each journal were determined by rankings based on six citation metrics (2-year impact factor, 5-year impact factor, SNIP, h-Index, AI, and Eigenfactor). The use of multiple measures and data sources allowed us to triangulate results, following Hult et al. (2009). In all journal metrics pursuant to RQ1, ranks were produced across all disciplines and the smaller the rank value, the better the quality.

In this research, deviations from recognition fairness are assessed by subtracting institutional from market journal evaluations. For each journal, six recognition fairness scores were computed (one for each citation metric) that each capture deviations from fairness. Based on this conceptualization, if a difference is negative, institutional bias against the journal is indicated. The private sector business community commonly uses this approach, where an expected recognition value based on social norms is subtracted from the recognition awarded amount (EEOC 2012).

## Measures to Investigate Inclusion Fairness (RQ2)

Inclusion fairness is commonly judged by inspecting the difference in the number of subjects in a group compared to a fair representation (Wodak 2008). It is examined here by investigating the availability of publication space in leading journals because of their primary role in faculty research decisions (Brusa et al. 2010). For each discipline, publication space is represented by both (1) the number of leading journals and (2) the aggregated number of annual articles published in those journals.

In order to operationalize the institutional perspective on publication space availability in the various disciplines, all journals from the sample of school lists were first ranked based on their WAP scores across the eight major business disciplines. This omnibus list was then grouped into tiers of the top 25, 50, 75, and 100 ranked journals. If institutional journal inclusion fairness exists, one would expect the percentages of globally participating faculty to be approximately the same as that of the journals and articles in each defined tier within each discipline (Kozar et al. 2006; Valacich et al. 2006).<sup>5</sup>

The above process was replicated with three public lists serving as the means for creating the subsets of journals. The Financial Times (FT45) list includes 45 journals for rating MBA programs. Similarly, the Businessweek (BW20) list encompasses 20 business journals used to rank MBA programs. The University of Texas at Dallas (UTD24) list contains 24 business journals used to rank schools of business on research productivity. Public journal lists drive faculty perceptions of journal quality (Meredith et al. 2011) and, at some business schools, are utilized in the official evaluation process. Therefore, we employed them, along with the school lists, as proxies for institutional indicators of quality in assessing inclusion fairness of journal valuations.

## Analyses and Findings

## Recognition Fairness (RQ1)

To capture discipline-level indicators of recognition fairness, the journal recognition fairness scores (the journal rank on the citation measure minus the journal rank on the school-list WAP) were summarized with the median of rank differences for each discipline, as reported in Table 1. A negative sign on these rank difference medians indicates that the journals in that discipline are undervalued on that citation metric; a positive sign shows overvaluation of the journals in that discipline. A zero difference represents fair valuation. The size of the rank difference median expresses the number of rank positions the discipline is under- or overvalued.

The Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test was employed to determine if these differences between the ranks on each of the six market criteria (citation metrics) and the institution-based ranks for the journals in each discipline were statistically significant. The results of the 48 tests for recognition fairness (RQ1) in business research institutions are presented in Table 1. Thirty-six (75%) of these tests were found to be significant at α = .05, providing evidence that recognition fairness is problematic in the business disciplines.

In order to assess the practical importance of the recognition fairness differences, we computed the effect size $( | z _ { \mathrm { W i l c o x o n } } | / \sqrt { \mathrm { N } } )$ for each of the Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Tests (Corder and Foreman 2014), as shown in Table 1. These effect sizes for the significant tests vary from large to small, according to Cohen’s (1988) heuristic (provided in note c of Table 1). Baroudi and Orlikowski (1989) note that moderate effect sizes indicate differences that are sufficient to be conspicuous. Hess et al. (2014) posit that even small effects can be of importance. The right-most column of Table 1 provides the statistical power of each test. Reporting power is recommended to aid in the judgment of nonsignificant tests (Baroudi and Orlikowski 1989).

The results indicate that the journals in Accounting are clearly overvalued since all six of the tests were significant, all differences were positive, and all effect sizes were large. There is also evidence that the journals in Finance are overvalued (all six significant, all differences positive, with two large effect sizes and the other four moderate). Likewise the journals in Marketing appear to be overvalued (five of six significant and positive, with two large effect sizes and the other three moderate). Conversely, the results of the recognition fairness analysis indicate that journals in Information Systems and to a slightly lesser extent Operations Management and Quantitative Methods are undervalued. In Information Systems, five of the six differences were statistically significant and the differences were negative; four of these exhibited moderate effect sizes. The results for Operations Management also show undervaluation: four of the six tests were significant, with three of the effect sizes moderate. In Quantitative Methods, the results are similar: three significant differences were negative, two exhibited large effect sizes and the third one was moderate.

Therefore, with respect to RQ1, we conclude that recognition fairness is not present across the business disciplines when considering the way in which institutions grade journals. These results imply that to bring about recognition fairness in the undervalued disciplines, their institutional journal standings should be improved.

In order to examine whether school list size influenced journal recognition fairness, we tested the differences between largeand small-list schools using the recognition fairness scores for each discipline. We also performed tests to investigate the influence of mission orientation by splitting the sample into teaching- and research-oriented schools. A discussion of these tests is reported in Appendix C. The results suggest that list size and mission orientation are both related to recognition fairness. In addition, we conducted an examination of how disagreement within each discipline about journal evaluation was related to recognition fairness (see Appendix C). The most consistent findings were in IS and indicated that recognition unfairness is related in part to a lack of consensus about journals in the field.

## Inclusion Fairness (RQ2)

To assess discipline-level inclusion fairness, we again compared institutional data to market data. In this case, the market data, which represented the expected outcome if inclusion fairness was present, consisted of the percentages of faculty in each discipline. The institutional data entailed the discipline percentage of publication space in tiered-sets defined as leading journals by schools. The comparison was made by examining the difference between the percentages (institutional minus market) in each discipline. A negative sign on this difference indicates a lack of availability of publication space in the given discipline; a positive sign shows a surplus. The magnitude of the difference expresses the percent of excess- or under-availability for the discipline. The ratio of the percent of availability to the percent of faculty provides the amount of publication space relative to the faculty sizes of the disciplines. For example, in a given set of top business journals, a discipline having an articles-to-faculty ratio of 0.8 indicates its leading journals have only 80 percent of the articles that should be expected based on its number of faculty. A journals-to-faculty ratio of 1.5 shows an excess of 50 percent more journals than needed; perfect fairness, neither over nor under, is indicated by a ratio of 1.0.

## Inclusion Fairness Based on Public Journal Lists

Similar to the previous analyses using institutional tiers of leading journals, we examined both the journal and article distributions for the public lists (FT45, BW20, and UTD24) to assess inclusion fairness. With respect to journal availability (Table 4), three of the 24 statistical tests were significant at α = .05 level, all indicating an excess number of leading journals in the lists. The results show that almost twice as many

Management journals are on the Financial Times and Businessweek lists as would be expected. The UT Dallas list contains nearly three times as many Operations Management journals. All of three of these tests exhibit effect sizes that are approaching large effects.

The results of examining inclusion fairness based on article space availability in journals on the public lists are reported in Table 5. Twenty-one of 24 tests for differences between article and faculty percentages were statistically significant (at α = .05) across all three public lists. The three comparisons that were not significant were all on the UT Dallas list. Articles-to-faculty ratios ranged from 0 to 3.18, providing evidence that inclusion fairness with regard to article space varies among business disciplines in public journal lists. These findings for the availability of article space for the public lists generally agree with the same analyses of the institutional top-100 tier. Again, the Accounting discipline fared the worst in representation, with under-availability in all three lists and effect sizes approaching moderate. Operations Management and Quantitative Methods both exhibited excess capacity in article space in these lists.

In considering the findings for inclusion fairness, we note that the availability of leading journals is not an issue and that journal inclusion fairness is mostly at acceptable levels. However, the availability of articles in leading journals is clearly not equitable; inclusion fairness is lacking with regard to article space. Hence for RQ2, we conclude that institutional inclusion fairness does not exist among the business disciplines when considering the publication opportunities among top journals. These results imply that to bring about inclusion fairness in the underrepresented disciplines, the availability of article space in top journals should be improved.

## Summary and Recommendations

This study assessed whether journal valuation fairness exists in academic institutions by comparing institution- and marketbased evaluations of journals to address research questions concerning recognition (RQ1) and inclusion (RQ2) fairness. For RQ1, institutional valuations were represented by a sample of internally developed journal lists from AACSBaccredited business schools and market norms were determined using six citation metrics. For RQ2, we associated the global faculty distribution provided by AACSB as an estimate of market expectations with the actual availability of publishing prospects in leading journals for each discipline, as defined by institutional and public journal lists. In these results, we found deviations from market-based anticipations while pursuing each of the research questions, and conclude that institutional fairness does not exist across business disciplines with respect to journal assessment. Among others, Information Systems is specifically disadvantaged. Thus, publication in business journals is not a level playing field in academia.

While no particular individual or other entity can be directly blamed for journal recognition or inclusion disparity, the downstream consequences are inefficiencies throughout the business academic industry. It has been long established that markets punish unfair actors (institutions in this case) commensurate with the degree of unfairness (Becker 1971). Consequently, when journal evaluation fairness is lacking, the overall market for ideas in the discipline has been harmed. In addition, faculty morale is at stake. Faculty members are quick to notice differences between the disciplines in terms of publication contents, impact, popularity, and acceptance difficulty (Swanson 2004). Because research institutions pay higher salaries for prominent scholars (Gill and Bhattacherjee 2009), largely defined by where they publish, relative market wage discounts for faculty occur in disciplines experiencing journal under-appreciation. In the global marketplace, with these constraints in place, deviations from journal valuation fairness could encourage some faculty to exit the profession altogether. In the context of publication, overvaluing a journal means the institution is expending marginal rewards that are greater than the marginal institutional benefit. By overpaying for lesser performing faculty, the institution is less economically effectual. On the other hand, when individuals are lesser compensated for superior output, they are discouraged from producing. Fairness in organizations is essential to group cohesion and performance (Wodak 2008), which should be of paramount importance to academic administrators. Conscientious academic leaders will want to alleviate concerns over either real or perceived journal unfairness.

Overvaluation on recognition fairness and over-availability on inclusion fairness are both manifestations of inequity since they represent a positive preference and thus give the scholars in that discipline an unfair advantage. However, we believe that undervaluation and under-availability are the more serious conditions and hold the most potential to be rectified. Table 6 provides a recap of the major problematic issues and the disciplines that are the most disadvantaged. As has been noted previously, IS, OM, and Quantitative Methods journals are undervalued, and adequate article space is not available in top Accounting and Marketing journals. Accordingly, we offer the following recommendations to remedy these problems.

## Promote Higher Level Recognition of Journals in IS, OM, and Quantitative Methods

The findings from the analyses focusing on recognition fairness (RQ1) provide evidence that the journals in IS, OM, and Quantitative Methods are undervalued by institutions (see Table 1). One issue that these three disciplines have in common is that there is ambiguity in exactly what constitutes a journal specific to these fields (Peffers and Ya 2003; Theoharakis et al. 2007). Published works in both the IS (Lewis et al. 2007) and OM (Holsapple and Post 2010) areas have called for a focus on the journals centric to the discipline in journal quality research, and recent studies have made a concerted effort, and progress, in definitively addressing this issue (Lowry et al. 2013; Meredith et al. 2011). However, since an understanding of which journals are clearly in the field is the first step toward improving recognition fairness, this trend must continue and we reiterate this proposition.

In IS, where the journals are the most consistently undervalued compared to the market (see Table 1), the leaders in the field, called the Consortium of Senior Scholars (CSS), have promoted a list of top IS journals in an attempt to moderate this problem (Consortium of Senior Scholars 2011). Lowry et al. (2013) provided evidence of the efficacy of the

CSS journal basket and further suggested that this list be divided into two tiers. We strongly support this idea of a tiered list, and we also advocate additional enhancements. First, we suggest that the CSS list be expanded and include three or possibly four tiers. Not all academics publish in top journals and standards for journal quality are not as stringent at non-research schools (Beets et al. 2015); multiple tiers would provide valuable guidance for these faculty and their administrators. Second, we recommend that the CSS list encompass several tiered versions: one to present research journals, such as MIS Quarterly (ranked 5<sup>th</sup> among business journals in Appendis B), one for niche journals, such as Decision Support Systems (44th), another for practitioner journals, such as Communications of the ACM (36<sup>th</sup>), and the final one for pedagogy journals, such as the Journal of Information Systems Education. Not all versions of the list would necessarily need to have journals in all tiers. In its 2008 report, AACSB encouraged business schools to put more emphasis on practice and pedagogy research, particularly in light of their specific mission priorities (Walker et al. 2010). Different versions of the CSS list would be of great benefit in this effort. In addition, we encourage the CSS to widely publicize their revised list. The target should be deans and non-IS faculty, as well as IS faculty. Multiple sources could be utilized, including an article in the quarterly magazine of the AACSB, BizEd, Association for Information Systems (AIS) venues, letters specifically to deans and IS faculty, and conceivably even social media networks.

Another issue, which seems to be having a negative impact on the valuation of IS journals in particular, is disagreement within the IS discipline (see the second section in Appendix C). Hopefully, a clear enumeration of IS-centric journals and the revised SSC list will have a mitigating effect on this situation. A panel discussion addressing this issue at a major IS conference would also be in order.

Even though we advocate that the Financial Times journal list not be used as an exclusive set of the top business journals (see below), a journal’s presence on this list does have an influence. Consequently, we suggest that the CSS request the Financial Times to add Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS) to their journal list, per the findings of this study (ranked 13<sup>th</sup> in business in Appendix B) and those of Lowry et al. (2013).

Further, we concur with Straub (2010), who recommends that IS journal editors should focus on publishing articles that have extra-disciplinary utility, such as methodological and integrative research. Editors may also publish articles on topics much broader than just the IT artifact (Orlikowski and Lacono 2001; Weber 2003). Having IS journals successfully lead on streams intended to serve not only IS, but other business areas as well (e.g., design science, innovation diffusion, and big data topics) is a healthy way to encourage interdisciplinary citation, and thus better journal recognition.

## Increase the Number of Articles Published by the Journals in Accounting and Marketing

The results of the inclusion fairness (RQ2) analyses indicate that both of these disciplines are lacking high-quality article space in proportion to the number of faculty in their area (see Table 3). The problem is not a deficit of prestigious journals in these fields, in fact the opposite is the case, since the journals in these disciplines are overvalued (see Table 1). The issue is that the prominent journals in Accounting and Marketing do not publish enough articles to adequately service the scholars in their fields. Accounting faculty are disadvantaged the most, consistently in their journals across all four levels from the school lists, as well as in their publiclist journals. Accounting journals in the top 100 school-list tier have less than half of the available article space that is needed (see Table 3). In the 100 school-list level Marketing journals, article space is short by approximately 40 percent. The solution to address this situation, in both disciplines, is for the journal publishers to increase the number of articles that they publish. This recommendation is aimed specifically at the 12 marketing and 17 accounting journals listed in Appendix B. The manifestation of this problem is not new, and the remedy is entirely feasible. In the early 2000s, IS faced a similar difficulty (Dennis et al. 2006; Valacich et al. 2006). In response, the two top IS journals (MISQ and ISR) significantly increased their article output, thereby addressing the problem (Dean et al. 2015). Our results show that the seven IS journals listed in Appendix B should continue to address this issue.

## Do Not Rely on Public Journal Lists to Determine the Top Journals in Business

Even though some schools utilize the Financial Times, Businessweek, and UT Dallas lists to identify the leading journals in business, their purpose is not to provide a definitive nor comprehensive set. At best, they offer a subset. Lowry et al. (2013) note these lists were not created based on a scientific methodology and their intended usage was not for academic purposes. As can be seen in Appendix B, the journals on these lists differ in quality, within and between disciplines, sometimes markedly so. For instance, the Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS) is ranked (13<sup>th</sup>) overall much higher on the school lists than is the Journal of Business Ethics (JBE) (100<sup>th</sup>). An examination of the citation metrics of these two journals confirms this relative comparison. Yet, JBE is on the Financial Times and Businessweek lists, and JMIS is not. In addition, the available article space in the journals on these public lists is by no means proportionate to the distribution of faculty in the various business disciplines (see Table 5). The use of these lists will, thus, exacerbate the issue of inclusion unfairness. Business deans, please take note: just say no to the use of public journal lists.

## Conclusion

Given our inference that journal publication is not a level playing field among business disciplines, we encourage business school deans to take a critical look at their processes for determining journal quality and prestige. Likely, some revisions are in order, in light of our findings. In this endeavor, a sound empirical methodology, utilizing market measures, will bring about the best results (Van Fleet et al. 2000). Analytics have become the basis for competition in many organizations operating on a global scale (Davenport 2006), and analytic approaches have shown great promise in controlling the extent of perceived fairness and job satisfaction in organizations (Long et al. 2011). Their use can also enhance journal evaluation fairness, thus aiding in the improvement of scholarly performance in the academy.

In particular, we suggest that institutional journal lists, if they are used, be aligned with the global distribution of faculty, available from AACSB. The reason the global distribution should be used (and not the local distribution) is because each faculty member competes on a global scale when submitting their work to journals. Further, the ratings of journals on these lists should be determined objectively, preferably by triangulating across multiple sources (Lewis 2008). If schools do not employ their own internal lists, the next best approach would be to rely on studies published in academic journals or citation metrics or both. Articles that have published journal standings have been shown to be valid (Lewis et al. 2007; Steward and Lewis 2010), and the merits of citation metrics have been demonstrated (Lowry et al. 2013). For certain, there is no reason to use external journal lists (e.g., Financial Times), since other, much more credable, sources are available.

This study has limitations regarding potential sample bias, definitional assumptions, and measurement. In the examination of recognition fairness (RQ1), the journal basket depended on matching pairs of records containing values for both market and institutional journal quality. If only one of these values existed, that journal record was not included in the final set of data for that analysis. Further, analyses of recognition and inclusion differences assume that the institutional assignment of, and real market value of, journals can be standardized. Our approach to this challenge was to rank both measures and use the differences in ranks for the tests. We also assumed that citation rates uniformly portray actual productivity. Whereas JCR-based citation metrics have been criticized for having differences between disciplines, we used the SNIP from Scopus to negate this influence.

Equity in academic publishing, as well as the associated rewards, is an indispensable ingredient to motivate good scholarship. This study reveals inconsistencies pertaining to business journal valuation fairness, and offers remedies. We hope this information will encourage efforts for a more equitable approach in terms of the dissemination of ideas and to equalize the publication opportunities in all business disciplines.

## Establishing the AACSB72 Journal List Sample

In this study, institutional journal lists were collected from AACSB-accredited business schools by e-mail solicitation. Each AACSB-accredited school was asked to submit the target journal list used at their institution for evaluating faculty publications, if such a list existed, or to indicate that their school did not employ a list. At the time of the request, 545 institutions from around the world held AACSB accreditation, and 206 (38%) of them responded to the request. The demographics of the responding schools are reported in Table A1.<sup>1</sup> The sample predominantly represented certain types of institutions: public (75%), North American (91%), offering both undergraduate and graduate degrees (87%), and having a teaching orientation (53%). In order to determine representativeness, sample demographics were compared to those of the population of all AACSB-accredited schools. One-sample chi-square tests were employed on categorical demographic measures (affiliation, geographic region, degree level offered, and mission orientation) to determine whether the sample differed from the population. Only one of these tests was significant at α = .05, and only barely so. For the school size variables, one-sample t-tests were utilized for the same purpose and no significant differences were found at α = .05. Consequently, the sample appears to exhibit demographic characteristics similar to the population and we conclude that it is representative of the population of AACSB-accredited schools (see Beets et al. 2015; Beets et al. 2013; Meredith et al. 2011; Steward and Lewis 2010).

## Computing the Weighted Average Percentile (WAP) for Journals from the AACSB72

Using the AACSB72, a weighted average percentile (WAP) was computed for each journal. This metric takes into consideration the relative tier placement of each journal across schools, as well as the number of schools that listed that journal. The 3,839 journals from the sample of institutional journal lists, spanning all business disciplines, were ranked by their WAP scores for our analyses. In this study, these school-list WAP ranks represent the collective institutional perception of journal value.

The number of graded tiers in the journal lists from the AACSB-accredited schools differed (ranging from 1 to 6), as did the number of journals rated at each school (ranging from 7 to 1,359), as well as the number of journals in the individual tiers at different schools (ranging from 5 to 641). In order to standardize across these differences in school lists, a percentile score was employed for each journal at each school based on its placement among the school’s graded tiers. This score was computed by the following method: for a given tier at a given school, the percentage of journals below the tier (Below%) at that school was determined. Likewise, the percentage of journals in the tier (Tier%) at that school was found. The percentile score for the journals in that tier at that school was then calculated based on the following formula:

## PercentileScore = Tier% ÷ 2 + Below%

As an example, consider the school that rated 20 journals in 3 tiers, where the first tier contained 8 journals, the second tier contained 7 journals, and the third tier contained 5 journals. The PercentileScore for the journals in the first tier at this school would be computed as follows:

The Tier% for the top tier would be: $8 \div 2 0 = . 4$

The Below% for the top tier would be: $( 2 0 - 8 ) \div 2 0 = . 6$

The PercentileScore for the top tier would then be: $. 4 \div 2 + . 6 = . 8$ (interpreted as the 80<sup>th</sup> percentile)

The PercentileScore for the journals in the second tier at this school would be computed as follows:

The Tier% for the second tier would be: $7 \div 2 0 = . 3 5$

The Below% for the second tier would be: $5 \div 2 0 = . 2 5$

The Percentile Score for the second tier would then be: $. 3 5 \div 2 + . 2 5 = . 4 2 5 ( { \mathrm { t h e } } 4 2 . 5 ^ { \mathrm { t h } } { \mathrm { p e r c e n t i l e } } )$

These computations were repeated for each journal at each school. All journals in the same tier at the same school were given the same percentile score for that school. The percentile score for a given journal takes into account the numbers of tiers, the number of journals in each tier, and that journal’s tier placement at all sample institutions. For each journal, these percentile scores were then aggregated across the schools by computing the arithmetic mean, which represented the average tier placement (the “AT” column in Table B1 in Appendix B) of the journal at the schools in the sample. The final WAP score for each journal was calculated by multiplying the average percentile for the journal by the number of schools listing (the “Times Listed” column in Table B1) that journal in one of their tiers. Multiplying by “Times Listed” adjusted the scores for quantity, assuming more prominent journals are listed on more institutional lists. These WAP scores were then ranked to produce the final overall journal rankings from the school-list data.

## Validating the WAP Measure

In order to make a quick assessment of the validity of the WAP-based rankings, we correlated them with published perception rankings for the last 10 years in each discipline. Although published perception rankings were not available in all areas, there were 14 such studies in 6 disciplines.<sup>2</sup> The results are presented in Table A4. All of these correlation coefficients were statistically significant and only one was slightly less than .5 with an average correlation across all 14 studies of .68. We conclude from this analysis that rankings based on school-list WAP scores exhibit acceptable validity.

With the aim of determining if there were similarities among the AACSB72, we correlated the WAP journal rankings between groups defined by demographic characteristics. As reported in Table A5, these correlations indicate that the school lists were similar when grouped by institution size (r =.89), journal list size (.73), research versus instructional emphasis (.89), and U.S. versus non-U.S. affiliation (.88). All of these correlation coefficients were statistically significant and exhibited strong association between the demographic subgroups. This seems reasonable, given that institutional list development from different schools will probably be influenced to some extent by the same sources, such as published journal perception studies and citation metrics. Our interpretation of these findings is that our school-list rankings are not unduly influenced by demographic subgroup disparities.

## The Roles of School List Size and Mission Orientation

## School List Size and Mission Orientation Influence on Journal Recognition Fairness

In order to examine whether school list size influenced journal recognition fairness, we tested the differences between large- and small-list schools using the recognition fairness scores (market minus institutional) for each discipline. The results of these tests are shown in the top half of Table C1. Because the findings were virtually the same for all of the citation metric ranks, we only report the tests for the three citation metric rankings that showed the most sensitivity in the overall recognition fairness analyses. Using two-year impact factor and SNIP, seven of the eight disciplines showed significant differences (α = .05) between schools with large and small lists. Using the h-index, five of these tests were significant. Overall, 79 percent (19 of 24) of these tests showed significance, indicating that schools with larger lists differed from the schools with smaller lists. This suggests that list size is related to journal recognition fairness.

We performed tests to investigate the influence of mission orientation by splitting the sample into teaching- and research-oriented schools. Shown in the bottom half of Table C1, exactly half (12 of 24) of the tests (at α = .05) revealed differences in journal recognition fairness between teaching- and research-oriented schools. Using the two-year impact factor, five of eight tests were significant; using SNIP, four were significant; and using h-index, three were significant. While institutional mission influences levels of journal recognition fairness in most disciplines, two (Finance and Economics) were consistently different, while mission orientation had no effect in two other disciplines (Accounting and IS). This shows that mission orientation has some influence on journal recognition fairness, although this association was not consistent across all business disciplines.

## Internal Discipline Disagreement and Journal Recognition Fairness

We conducted an examination of how disagreement within each discipline about journal evaluation was related to recognition fairness. For each journal, we calculated the standard deviation of the WAP scores across the 72 school lists. These standard deviations represent the aggregate amount of discipline disagreement on the relative evaluation of a given journal. The larger the standard deviation, the larger the disagreement is within a discipline. We then correlated these journal standard deviations against the journal recognition fairness scores. This procedure was repeated for each discipline on the fairness scores based on each of the six citation metrics. As shown in Table C2, few of these correlation results were significant. However, a majority (5/6) of these tests were significant for the IS discipline and all were negative. This finding indicates that the more disagreement on journal standing within IS, the more recognition unfairness is present for IS journals, suggesting that the recognition valuation of IS journals is related in part to incongruity in the field.
