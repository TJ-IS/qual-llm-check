# An empirical investigation of socio-cultural factors of information sharing in China

Seung Kyoon Shin <sup>a,\*</sup>, Michael Ishman <sup>b,1</sup>, G. Lawrence Sanders <sup>c,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business Administration, University of Rhode Island, Kingston, RI 02881, United States <sup>b</sup> Department of Commerce, College of Business Administration, Niagara University, NY 14109, United States <sup>c</sup> Department of Management Science and Systems, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY 14260, United States Received 26 August 2005; received in revised form 27 October 2006; accepted 15 November 2006 Available online 12 January 2007

## Abstract

Understanding information sharing is an important challenge to modern organizations, and is likely to be increasingly considered when IT investment decisions are made world-wide. Our research study investigated the influence of cultural factors on information sharing in China. It was postulated that social network structures such as guanxi, Confucian dynamism, and collectivism could explain the degree to which information sharing took place between people in China. It was found that guanxi, Confucian dynamism, and collectivism all had a significant influence on information sharing. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information sharing; China; Culture; Collectivism; Confucian dynamism; Guanxi

## 1. Introduction

Technologies, such as intranets, extranets, and the Internet, have substantially increased the potential for information sharing within and between organizations and enhanced firm efficiency. The phenomenon of information sharing, however, is usually associated with cultural factors beyond information technology. An understanding of information sharing across cultures is still a major challenge and is likely to be more important as global systems are developed.

In some local cultures, such as highly competitive organizations having trade secrets, information sharing is highly controlled because of fear of industrial espionage, diversion of people’s attention from their work, and role conflict. Cultural differences may affect individual or organizational attitudes towards sharing information. Our study explored information sharing in China, where socioeconomic background and family origin are important factors in interpersonal relations; indeed, relationships depend on the idea of guanxi, the existence of direct particularistic ties between people [40].

China’s economy has experienced phenomenal growth over the past two decades. It has become an economic giant among the nations of the world, and has recently grown to become one of the largest consumer economies in the world. However, the problems that Western businesses have had in doing business with China is challenging for Western businesses due to their lack understanding of cultural barriers. Although the Chinese have changed over the past few decades, traditional socio-cultural values have remained a major influence [3] and while American and European businesses have acknowledge the importance of guanxi, many still do not fully understand the meaning and subtle forms that it can take. We therefore decided to investigate the influence of guanxi on information sharing with internal and external groups as well as two other cultural factors that were postulated to have an influence on information sharing in China.

## 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1. Culture

Culture becomes important in a global environment where members of organizations come from more than one nationality. It has been defined as the ‘‘collective programming of the mind’’ that differentiates people of different societies [12]. Hofstede [11] delineated four dimensions of national culture: power distance, individualism–collectivism, masculinity–femininity, and uncertainty avoidance. These dimensions were distilled from a seminal study involving more than 120,000 respondents from more than 50 countries. Cultural differences between members of different nations and societies were found to be stable over the years. In addition, Confucian dynamism<sup>3</sup> was identified as an important variable in a study by The Chinese Culture Connection.

Scholars in social psychology and management have successfully used these dimensions to account for empirical observations [38]. In the field of information systems, Straub et al. [33] used uncertainty avoidance to explain why the diffusion of IT differed in the United States and Japan. Watson et al. [44] used individualism– collectivism to account for differences in the way group support systems affected group decisions in the United State and Singapore. Hence, individualism–collectivism has not only been shown to have useful explanatory power but also to be a factor in information sharing.

We postulate that a unique factor in Chinese culture, guanxi, has a strong impact on information sharing. The word guanxi refers to the concept of drawing on connections in order to secure favors in personal relationships [21]. Tsui and Farh examined the relationship between it and relational demography, and suggested that the two ideas were conceptually related but not equivalent. In business settings, guanxi has been found to have four variables describing business practices: intermediary, social investment, back door, and mutual trust [6]. It has been shown to have a direct impact on market expansion and sales growth of Chinese firms by affecting resource sharing and social, economic, and political contexts in inter-firm transactions [15]. Some view guanxi as simple corruption, while others argue that it can be a legitimate alternatives to the western free market systems [19].

## 2.2. Social network theory

A central idea in social network theory [18] explains the differences of information exchange based on the strength of relationships between the two parties. In essence, the development of strong ties based on mutual trust is a function of the resources employed, including the amount of time, the level emotional intensity, mutual intimacy, and reciprocal services. In a comparison of strong and weak ties, Freeman [8] considered strong ties as those in which individuals were closely affiliated, while weak ties had less intimate connections.

The theory of social networks posits that the efficiency of information transmission depends on the strength of the ties between people. Thus, strong ties offers two primary advantages: they involve the exchange of high-quality information and knowledge and they operate as part of a social control mechanism that normalizes partnership behavior.

## 2.3. Interdependence theory

Interdependence theory [16] was originally formulated by Kelley and Thibaut in 1959. It has been applied in the study of a wide range of dyadic and inter-group phenomena such as bargaining behavior, conflict resolution, and the evolution of relationships. The theory implies that the stronger the cultural context in support of friendship, altruistic attitudes, good citizenship norms, and polite behavior, the more likely it is that an individual will willingly share personal resources. In information sharing between two individuals acting alone, rational self-interest and simple reciprocity theoretically predict behavior. The resulting behavior would be that A helps B if B helps A, and A withholds help if B acts destructively. However, the reciprocation of negative behavior does not necessarily happen when there are strong social norms supporting the harmony of society. Thus, those who are future-oriented and tend to enjoy social harmony should show a greater willingness to participate in information sharing.

## 3. Model development

Our model of information sharing was based on the theory of communicative action [10], which refers to the interactions involving speech and actions that relate to the establishment of interpersonal relationships. One of the central concepts of communicative action is that it situates information sharing behavior within a hierarchy of resources in the context of social norms and cultural values. In addition, the theory offers explanations of people’s information sharing behavior when people share information to achieve organizational goals.

According to the theory, information sharing can be explained in terms of three domains: person, culture, and society. These together form the setting that provides an information sharing context. In many contexts, the behavior is anchored in the individual’s social responsibility to prove the validity of their claims. In addition, these claims may be based on the social/ group norms of the context. That is, cultural factors and social relationship may influence the people’s information sharing behavior in a variety of settings. Te’eni [34] presented a simplified form of the theory of communicative action to illustrate how domains constitute a communication setting (Fig. 1). Goals, values, and norms are the corresponding products of the four communication domains.

In our study, information was viewed as a resource that could be shared among members of a social group, and information sharing as a societal behavior to share resources. Te’eni defined cultural values as the stock of knowledge that guided behavior of communicators belonging to that culture. He viewed individualism– collectivism as a major cultural dimension for explaining similarities and differences in communicative behavior. In addition, Confucian dynamism was investigated as a cultural factor that influenced information sharing. In this context, guanxi was thus viewed as a strong societal relationship involving mutual trust-based relationship that influenced information sharing in China.

## 3.1. Model of information sharing and cultural factors

Information sharing is the extent to which critical or proprietary information is communicated to one’s partner [22]. From a comprehensive perspective, researchers extended this definition to sharing and transferring information within/across the structure of an organization, focusing on providing a context for efficient sharing of this valuable intangible capital [41,43]. Today’s organizations generally have policies and methods that encourage employees to help others and share information [1]. Western researchers have concluded that increased information sharing could improve organizational efficiency, learning, innovation, flexibility, and understanding of organizational goals [42]. Indeed, information exchange and communications are heavily influenced by an individual’s relationships with others [26].

A key challenge has been in transferring information from the source to the locale where it was needed and used. Some organizations discourage information sharing because there are cultural disturbances due to the distance between individuals and also due to environmental and societal distrust [9].

We therefore posited that three cultural factors (guanxi, collectivism, and Confucian dynamism) had positive influences on information sharing in China. A research model including the three cultural factors is presented in Fig. 2. We further investigated the influences of the factors on information sharing in two disparate contexts: in-group information sharing where the sharing takes place across internal organizational boundaries beyond the peer-to-peer sharing, and external sharing, in which sharing occurs with familiar and trusted business partners.

## 3.2. Guanxi: a strong tie social network

Guanxi refers to personal and interpersonal relationships and places high values on social networks, trust, commitment, favor, mutuality, reciprocity and long-term benefits [46]. It has been regarded as the lifeblood of personal relationships and business conduct in Chinese society [14] and requires implicit mutual obligation, assurance and understanding. Thus, guanxi is more than an interpersonal relationship or friendship; it is a mutual obligation to respond to requests for assistance [39]. This type of relationship leads to the development of strong ties social networks among people based on trust and guanxi and has played a critical role in organizational performance in China.

In an organizational context, a guanxi-based network is part of an organization’s core competency and provides competitive advantage [20]. In the rapidly changing economic and social environment of China, guanxi has become even more entrenched with strong and direct implications for business practices [27]. Understanding and applying the notion of guanxi is necessary if western firms wish to be successful when engaging in business in China.

Sharing information through guanxi often follows successful transactions: intimate relationships result from frequent information exchanges among partners [17]. Guanxi provides a valuable entrepreneurial tool to bridge gaps in information flows between unlinked firms and between firms and important outside stakeholders: firms develop guanxi to broker structural holes and alter the existing network.

For any two individuals to develop guanxi, they must be well acquainted and participate in some sharing as part of a so-called good deal. Guanxi develops between persons who are strongly tied [2] and it plays a more prominent role in influencing behavior than does role obligation and friendship. Information in China often flows through guanxi because it is the most important social network. It is the strength of ties that are particularly important when people pass information across organizational barriers [32].

A person who refuses to return a favor and does not follow the rules of reciprocity is viewed as untrustworthy. However, exchanges often favor the weaker partner. At the individual level, a guanxi network often links people across uneven ranks, with the weaker party calling for special favors without an equal level of reciprocal obligation. The situation can be theoretically explained by interdependency theory. Based on the strong tendency of people in China towards reciprocation and to freely share their resources, we hypothesized.

Hypothesis 1. Attitude toward guanxi will positively influence attitude toward information sharing.

## 3.3. Collectivism

Individualism–collectivism has been identified by many researchers as the most important dimension of cross-cultural psychology and is consistently featured in cross-cultural studies. Collectivism is the degree to which people prefer to act as members of groups rather than as individuals. Collectivism has been used to explain why people are more willing to adhere to group norms in their country rather than others [5].

In a collectivistic society, people favor and maintain harmony and relationship. Thus, collectivism has been viewed as the subordination of personal goals to those of the group with an emphasis on sharing and harmony [23]; this is the in-group, which includes family, friends, or work associates. A collectivistic culture consists of people integrated into cohesive groups with strong ties and thus they base their self-understanding on the reactions of others.

Members of collectivist cultures show great concern for the welfare of in-group members but relative apathy to outsiders’ needs [37]. This attitude has roots in benevolence and conformity. Benevolence focuses on the concern for the welfare of close others in everyday interaction. People in a high-benevolence group therefore tend to share their information and resources. Conformity is derived from the requirement that individuals inhibit inclinations that might be socially disruptive. According to the interdependence theory, the stronger the cultural context supporting friendship and polite behavior (or altruistic attitudes), the more likely it is that an individual will willingly share resources. We, therefore, hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2. There is a positive relationship between collectivism and attitude toward information sharing.

## 3.4. Confucian dynamism: a cultural dimension

Confucian dynamism [30] emphasizes work ethics and the enjoyment of prestige without the loss of face and the saving of others’ face. It is the degree to which an individual associates with such values as persistence, ordering relationships by status, thrift, and having a sense of shame [13]. Confucian dynamism identifies a core set of values which include time orientation, and is partially related to the work of Confucius. However, Robertson found that, in the U.S., Confucian dynamism is related to other cultural dimensions.

Ralston et al. [29] found three sub-dimensions of Confucian dynamism, based on Schwartz’s work: societal harmony, virtuous interpersonal behavior, and interpersonal harmony. Many values are related to social harmony and supporting people. As such, individuals from higher Confucian dynamism societies tend to social harmony and exhibit a greater willingness to share information.

The stronger the cultural context in support of friendship and polite behavior, altruistic attitudes, and of good citizenship norms, the more likely it is that an individual will willingly share individual resources. Thus, people from a high Confucian dynamism society should be more willing to share information with other members of the group. Also, people are more likely to share information when he or she wishes for good outcomes not only for himself or herself but also for others. Thus we should find that people are more willing to share information when they are happier with their coworkers and with the organization.

Murnighan et al. [24] argued that willingness to share information should be greater under conditions that increase social concern for the information seeker: i.e., helping people and social harmony in Confucian dynamism. As an expression of self-consistency, sharing information may result in personal benefits such as heightening of self-esteem and pride, increased self-efficacy, increased personal identification with coworkers or the work group, more respect from others and a better reputation, and reduced alienation or stronger feelings of commitment. Thus, our third hypothesis was:

Hypothesis 3. There is a positive relationship between Confucian dynamism and attitude toward information sharing.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Operationalization of research constructs

The data was collected using a survey technique. Whenever possible, appropriate scales with good psychometric properties were used. For the remaining constructs, sets of items were generated based on reviews of previous relevant literature.

A short form of Confucian dynamism measure developed by The Chinese Culture Connection [35] was used to assess Confucian dynamism of respondents. Six items from the Schwartz’s Value Survey were used to measure collectivism of respondents [31]. After conducting a pilot test, the scale was modified to include only six items: three for benevolence (honest, loyal, and true friendship), and three for conformity (self-discipline, politeness, and honoring of parents and elders).

Park and Luo [27] argue that guanxi plays an important role in information sharing with the business community and the government authorities. Based on prior studies, a seven-item scale was developed to measure the attitude toward information sharing among individuals in a work group (in-group information sharing), and with business partners (external information sharing). Guanxi within the business community and with the government authorities constitute a multifaceted construct. The former consists of horizontal connections with buyers, suppliers, and competitors, while the latter includes vertical connections with political governments, industrial departments, and other regulatory authorities, such as taxation bureaus, banks, and commercial administration bureaus. As such, the scale was developed to measure information sharing with in-group members and external business partners, respectively. Finally, a four-item scale assessing individual managers’ perception of guanxi was developed, based on a review of existing measures in the literature. All of the measures are presented in Appendix A.

Assessment of the research model was conducted using the Partial Least Squares (PLS), which is a good approach because of the minimal demands on measurement scales, sample size, and residual distributions [45]. PLS helps in screening out factors that have a negligible effect on the dependent variables. As this study involved new scales, an additional benefit of PLS was its ability to assess both measurement and research models at the same time [36].

## 4.2. Research setting and sample

Data collection took place in the People’s Republic of China. A preliminary pilot study questionnaire was administered to 31 Chinese managers studying in an English language Executive MBA program. The resulting data were analyzed and used to modify the questionnaire items for the full study. These subjects were excluded from later analysis.

The main study involved administrating the validated survey to 140 high-level managers, general managers, and deputy general mangers. The age of the respondents ranged from 27 to 48 years with an average of 35.6 years. 69.1% of subjects were engaged in organization management or employed in managerial positions. Average work experience for the subjects was just over 13 years, and they had spent about 7 years on average with their current employer. They represented all functional areas of business and a broad range of industries, including manufacturing, services, retailing, wholesaling, healthcare, education/research, communications, advertising, and retailing. Approximately 79% of the subjects were male, and 21% were female. The demographics for this sample are presented in Table 1.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Measurement model assessment

Table 2 reports the result of factor analysis with all independent variables. While some measures showed some weak loadings, they were adequate for an exploratory study (>0.6) [4]. The factor loadings of Confucian dynamism were essentially consistent with the Chinese Culture Connection study.

The reliabilities of all multi-item scales were above the 0.7 level, which is generally deemed sufficient for field work [25]. The internal consistency reliabilities and Cronbach’s a for all constructs were greater than 0.7 (see Table 3). The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was also calculated. This measures the variance that a construct captures from its indicators relative to the variance contained in measurement error. This statistic is generally interpreted as a measure of reliability for the construct and as a means of evaluating discriminant validity [7]. All AVEs for the constructs in our study were greater than or approximately equal to 0.50. This indicated that more than half of the variance of the indicators could be accounted for by the latent variables.

The AVE can also be used to assess discriminant validity. They should be greater than the square of the correlations among the constructs; that is, the amount of variance shared between a latent variable and its block of indicators should be greater than the shared variance between the latent variables. ## 5.2. Structural model assessment

With an adequate measurement model, the hypotheses were tested by examining the structural model. The $R ^ { 2 }$ value was used to assess the proportion of variance in the endogenous constructs that could be explained by the antecedent constructs. Approximately 29% of the variance in information sharing was explained by the three cultural factors, making interpretation of the path coefficients meaningful.

All hypotheses in the research model were statistically supported. The results also confirmed that guanxi is one of the most influential cultural factors affecting information sharing in the Chinese business environment $( \beta = 0 . 4 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). Confucian dynamism, on the other hand, appeared to have relatively less effect $( \beta = 0 . 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

The path coefficients from the PLS analysis are shown in Fig. 3. The coefficients in the model represent standardized regression coefficients. The suggested lower limit of them for regression coefficients is 0.05 [28]. In a more conservative position, path coefficients of 0.10 and above are preferable. All hypotheses were supported. Guanxi (H1), collectivism (H2), and Confucian dynamism (H3) were shown to exert a significant positive influence on in-group information sharing. After computing path estimates in the structural model, the PLS software was used to perform jackknifing to obtain the corresponding T-values. Support for each hypothesis can be determined by examining the sign (positive or negative) and the statistical significance for the T-value for its corresponding path. All hypotheses were accepted with a significance level of either 0.001 (guanxi and collectivism) or 0.05 (Confucian dynamism).

## 5.3. Guanxi and external information sharing

The influence of guanxi on external information sharing was also investigated (see Fig. 4). The $R ^ { 2 }$ for the research model using external information sharing was 0.035. It is notable that the results were quite different from those for in-group information sharing $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 9 )$ . Although guanxi still showed a statistically significant impact on external information sharing $( \beta = 0 . 1 4 , p < 0 . 5 )$ , the other cultural factors (collectivism and Confucian dynamism) did not show significant causal relationship with the dependant variable.

## 6. Discussion and concluding remarks

We investigated the impacts of three cultural factors on information sharing and concluded that all three (guanxi, collectivism, and Confucian dynamism) influenced information sharing within the organizational boundary. However guanxi had a stronger impact on in-group than on external information sharing. This is not surprising; according to social network theory, interpersonal ties based on transmitting work information, advice, and friendship are less successful with external social groups.

However, guanxi was the only statistically meaningful factor that operated across group boundaries, having a moderate influence on external information sharing. Enormous opportunities for improved efficiency, increased flexibility, and timeliness can be realized by the free sharing of information in a supply chain. However, this same information can be used to exploit the party that provides the information, for example, by demanding lower prices when the supplier has found ways to reduce costs. Thus, information sharing requires that an atmosphere of ‘‘trust’’ be created. The results, therefore, showed that guanxi plays a particularly critical role in both in-group and external information sharing.

Collectivism had a direct influence on in-group information sharing but not with an external organization, consistent with cultural studies. Triandis et al. argued that collectivists were willing to self-sacrifice for their in-group but not for outsides. Confucian dynamism appears to have even less effect on in-group information sharing. The Confucian spirit has possibly been diluted to such an extent that the younger generation knows little about or is not influenced by Confucian dynamism, as per Ralston et al., who compared today’s to previous generations of Chinese managers.

The findings of our study should be important to managers for several reasons. First, executives and managers involved in commerce with Chinese firms need a deeper understanding of how their own value sets diverge from those of their business partners. Western business people are faced with an urgent need to adapt to and operate within non-western systems. Insufficient understanding of culture can become a barrier to understanding social relationships and lack of understanding can lead to business failure. The bottom line is that foreign companies will be more successful if they understand how to use Guanxi to develop business relationship in China.

A trust-based relationship between two parties is an enabler of information sharing; information systems, therefore, should be designed to foster sharing of proprietary information through peer-to-peer communication that facilitates trust. In addition, Confucian dynamism was found to have a positive impact on ingroup information sharing.
