# Doing critical information systems research – arguments for a critical research methodology

## Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic

School of Information Systems, Technology and Management, Australian School of Business, University of New South Wales, Australia

Correspondence: Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic, School of Information Systems, Technology and Management, University of New South Wales, Kensington Campus, Sydney, NSW 2052, Australia. Fax: +612 96624061: E-mail: dubravka@unsw.edu.au

## Abstract

Critical information systems (IS) research, it is argued, does not have a distinct methodological identity. While some research methods are closely related to the positivist research paradigm (experiments, surveys, and structural equation modelling) and others to the interpretivist paradigm (field study, ethnography, and action research), the critical paradigm is not identified with specific ‘critical methods’ and typically relies on the appropriation of interpretivist methods (such as critical ethnography). The criticism of the critical research paradigm in IS has often focused on the lack of distinctly critical research methods and even the neglect of methodological issues (Klein; McGrath). This paper questions the notion of and the arguments behind the quest for ‘critical research methods’ defined in contrast to positivist and interpretivist methods. Instead, the paper argues that it is a critical research methodology – understood as an overall strategy of conceptualizing and conducting an inquiry, engaging with studied phenomena, and constructing and justifying socially relevant knowledge, which distinguishes critical from other research paradigms. Building on a Kleinian argument regarding the need for common principles across diverse critical IS inquiries (Klein; Myers & Klein) this paper proposes a framework that describes key dimensions of a critical research methodology that distinguish critical from other research paradigms and provide methodological guidance in the doing of critical research.

## Introduction

What is critical information systems (IS) research and how does the doing of critical research differ from positivist and interpretivist research? To ask these questions does not necessarily indicate ignorance (by the mainstream researchers) but may also indicate confusion arising from the increasing diversity of research ‘that aspires to the name critical’. In his unfinished chapter ‘Critical Social IS Research Today’, published in Carole Brooke’s book Critical Management Perspectives on Information Systems (2009), Heinz Klein prompts us to think about the nature and identity of critical IS research in light of the diversity evident in critical IS researchers’ concerns and orientations, theoretical foundations, research questions, research methods and relations to practice. Diversity, Klein suggests, makes critical research exciting, innovative and lively and hence is something we should be proud of and nurture. While diversity and the rejection of any cannon on how to do critical research are something most critical researchers cherish and celebrate Klein warns that we are at risk of diluting the meaning of critical and thus also losing a sense of distinct identity of critical research.

Reflecting on the Kleinian legacy in critical IS research and especially the theoretical foundations that he and his collaborators have developed, it feels as an obligation as well as a challenge to further develop a distinct identity of critical IS research while appreciating and stimulating the diversity and richness of critical inquires. The last paper Klein co-authored ‘A Set of Principles for Conducting Critical Research in Information Systems’ (Myers & Klein, forthcoming) designates an interesting direction for reflecting on and expressing critical research identity. While recognizing the tremendous diversity of critical IS research they provide a convincing argument for a set of general principles for conducting critical IS field research that capture the commonalities of its diverse strands. The general principles – they hope – ‘will stimulate further reflection and debate on the important subject of grounding critical research methodology’ (p. 2).

In this paper, I draw from a range of Kleinian explorations of methodological issues in critical IS research (1999, 2009) and in particular take the proposed principals for conducting critical research (Myers & Klein, forthcoming) as a metatheoretical grounding for exploring a critical research methodology. It is important to note that ‘research methodology’ and ‘research method’ are not synonyms as sometimes assumed (Neuman, 2006). The distinction between the two terms adopted here follows the tradition in the philosophy of social sciences (Gebhardt, 1978; Morrow & Brown, 1994). The term research method refers to a set of techniques and procedures for selecting subjects (and objects), measuring or observing phenomena, and collecting, processing, and analysing data. For instance survey, laboratory experiment, and field study are among research methods often used in IS research (Mingers, 2003; Yogesh & Kuljis, 2008). Research methodology, on the other hand, is a broader term that denotes an overall strategy of conceptualizing and conducting an inquiry, engaging with phenomena studied, and constructing and justifying knowledge claims. Importantly, research methodology is concerned with the assumptions of methods and their links with theory and with the selection, justification and appropriation of method(s) in a particular research context (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). It indicates that research methodology involves much more than a method or a combination of methods. Research methodology entails the linking of theory with overall research strategy and methods, explicitly considering the ways knowledge claims are produced and justified.

The aim of this paper is to argue for and propose a distinct critical research methodology. More specifically the paper identifies and describes key distinguishing dimensions of critical research methodology together with illustrations of their relevance and implications for conducting critical IS research. Demonstrating that the critical research paradigm can be characterized by a distinct critical methodology is important given that methodology is one of major determinants of the identity of a research paradigm (Neuman, 2006). Understanding the dimensions of a critical research methodology along which key choices need to be made is relevant for assisting researchers and advancing the practice of critical IS research as well as for assessing critical research results. Furthermore, by contributing to the methodology debate the paper intends to enhance the understanding, appreciation, and legitimation of critical IS research within the broader IS community.

To achieve paper’s aim and develop my argument I first remind the reader of the nature of critical research and summarize the diverse critical research traditions as they emerged in IS (Section ‘Critical IS research and Critical Theory’). Next, I discuss and question the common conception of ‘critical research methods’ as something to be defined in contrast to positivist and interpretivist methods (Section ‘Methodology discourses in IS). My claim is that critical research inquiry cannot and should not be distinguished from a positivist and interpretivist inquiry in the same way these two research paradigms are distinguished among themselves. Given the critical theoretic and ethical concerns and normative foundations of both knowledge claims and proposals for transformative social change that characterize the critical research paradigm, its distinctness cannot be understood at the same discursive plane at which the distinctness of the positivist and interpretivist paradigms is argued. However, it is the dichotomy between positivist and interpretivist assumptions and methods – for example, realism vs relativism; nomothetic vs ideographic explanations; extensive vs intensive inquiry – that preoccupies the prevailing methodological discourse within IS (see Mingers, 2003). Hence, this paper disrupts the discourse and draws attention to a broader view of methodology within which the question of a distinctly critical research methodology can be fruitfully explored and convincingly argued.

Instead of seeking distinctly critical research methods, I put forward a proposal for a distinctly critical research methodology understood as an overall strategy of motivating, conceptualizing and conducting an inquiry, engaging with studied phenomena and subjects/objects in their contexts, as well as constructing and justifying socially relevant knowledge claims. In Section ‘A set of principles for conducting critical research in IS’, I summarize Myers and Klein’s principals for conducting critical research (2011) that provide grounding for the proposal of four key dimensions of critical research methodology presented in Section ‘Distinctive dimensions of critical research methodology’. While acknowledging diversity of critical enquiries, the four dimensions of critical research methodology identify commonalities across different strands of critical research and thereby exemplify the identity of critical IS research. The proposed critical research methodology enables critical researchers to explore and better argue their methodological choices and justify their socially relevant knowledge claims. I conclude with a discussion about the relevance and implications of such a critical research methodology for advancing the critical IS research project, improving practice and appreciating contributions of the critical IS research paradigm.

## Critical IS research and Critical Theory

One of the problems with the broader understanding of the critical research paradigm in the IS community has been the very term ‘critical’. Every research is critical, the argument goes, and hence to call a research paradigm ‘critical’ is not helpful. ‘Critical scrutiny of knowledge claims is a trademark of Academia’ (Alvesson et al., 2009, p. 8) – is an argument for the claim that the mainstream research is already critical. Denying the difference between critical and mainstream research, the authors maintain, is a simple strategy for rejecting the (potentially dangerous) critique of the mainstream.

Critical research, as Klein has often emphasized, means socially critical research, which challenges established social conditions and institutions and oppressive forms of control, often enabled and supported by IS, which prevent the realization of humane, just and free organizations and society (Klein, 1999, 2009; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2008). Critical IS research challenges the prevailing view of IS as an important instrument for enabling and strengthening the economic-rationalist and managerialist view of organizations that prioritizes the interests of capital (i.e., of stockholders and managers) over all other interests (other stakeholders, community, society, environment, etc.). Critical researchers

(co)create knowledge with transformative and emancipatory intent by revealing how IS serve particular interests, by developing a situated understanding of positions and experiences of people affected by the systems, and by linking such understandings with broader conditions, power relations, and social structures. They strive for changing consciousness and counteracting detrimental human and social consequences. Similar to other critical social studies, critical IS research seeks to serve democratic purposes and strives to become practically relevant. (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2008, p. 127).

They suggest that critical IS researchers not only aim to explain and understand phenomena – such as IS development and deployment and their organizational and social implication – as positivist and interpretivist researchers do (although in different ways). Critical researchers also aim to reveal hidden interests and agendas guiding IS development and deployment and expose the assumed roles that IS are expected to play in strengthening existing social structures and increasing control. Such critical insights and deep understanding allow critical researchers to question the taken for granted purpose and roles of IS in organizations and society and articulate alternative views and conceptions of IS to help transform current conditions and disrupt the social order (Klein & Huynh, 2004; Klein, 2009). Unlike their positivist and interpretivist peers, critical researchers take explicit ethical and value positions. As Stahl emphasized, it is an explicit concern with ethics and morality that distinguishes critical social research (2008).

The origins of critical research can be traced back to the enlightenment ideal and Kant’s view of the human potential and responsibility to achieve enlightenment and emancipation (Brocklesby & Cummings, 1996). Early critical researchers derived their inspiration from Kant’s rationalistic ethics, Weber’s theory of bureaucracy, critique of western rationality and moral philosophy, and Marx’s critique of capitalism. These social theories certainly inspired social theorists of the Frankfurt school, including Theodor Adorno, Eric Fromm, Max Horkheimer, Herbert Marcuse, and Jurgen Habermas, who were credited for the development of Critical Theory, the most influential philosophical tradition in critical management and critical IS research (Scherer, 2009).

Among the key objections of Critical Theory towards the mainstream social science are the notions of valuefree research and value-neutral knowledge:

The success of the natural sciences in explaining natural phenomena and the associated capabilities of bringing nature under technical control has seemed to demonstrate a superiority of the rational, scientific method. At the same time. the normative ethical dimension of reason has been systematically excluded from scientific analysis. Values and normative judgments are considered subjective and, seen from a scientific perspective, cannot be generalized and verified. Therefore, science is obliged to refrain from value statements or normative theory (value-free thesis). (Scherer, 2009, p. 30)

The scientific method and the positivist social science are, as Scherer and other critical theorists demonstrate, fundamentally deficient as a model for social sciences primarily because they cannot explain the subjective, socially constructed and normative character of social phenomena. In contrast, interpretivist approaches to social sciences assume and aim to understand social phenomena, social actions, processes and institutions, as subjectively meaningful and socially constructed. They acknowledge that values permeate all meanings and actions. But they refrain from taking a value position or adopting a normative theory.

The two contrasting views of social sciences and their limitations have been illuminated by Habermas’ theory of knowledge-constitutive interests (1972) (see also Klein & Huynh, 2004). This theory links the nature of human knowledge to some fundamental cognitive interests. The positivist social sciences that aim to explain and predict phenomena to enable effective technical control in the domain of labor (including material production and systems, people and objects) are guided by the empirical-analytical interest (also called the technical interest). The interpretivist socials sciences that aim to interpret and understand the social world – the domain of social interaction and forms of communication aimed at achieving understanding – are guided by the hermeneutic-historical interest (or the practical interest). These two cognitive interests in Habermas’ theory express significantly different orientations and imply different methodological strategies thus producing different types of knowledge: (i) the objective and general knowledge consisting of nomothetic explanations (driven by technical interest); and (ii) the ideographic interpretation and hermeneutic understanding in a particular social and historical context (driven by practical interest). Both these views of social sciences aim to find out what is the case in the real world (albeit based on different ontological and epistemological assumptions) but neither is interested in critiquing it. Proposed theories may be criticized for not explaining accurately or for not providing plausible interpretation and understanding of the existing forms of life, work systems or social interaction. But the existing forms of life, work systems or social interaction are not subjected to scientific critique.

Habermas (1972) defines the third critical-emancipatory interest that guides critical social sciences oriented toward revealing and overcoming hidden and taken for granted constrains and conditions resulting from social forms of domination and distorted communication. A criticalemancipatory cognitive interest aims at the pursuit of reflection on both ‘the conditions of knowledge and a critical-reflective dissolution of dogmatic forms of life’ (McCarthy, 1996, p. 96). In contrast to the technical and practical interests, ‘the emancipatory interest in knowledge has a derivative status’ (Habermas, 1974, p. 176; emphasis in the original). This means that the pursuit of emancipation, practiced through critical self-reflection, is focused, on one hand, on the domain of labor, material production and conditions of work systems, and on the other, on the domain of social interaction and forms of communication. In other words, the critique and normative reasoning are applied as part of examination and understanding of the two domains of empirical research. An important implication is that ‘a unique aspect of the critical-emancipatory interest is that a cognitive activity unites both empirical and normative theorizing’ (Morrow & Brown, 1994, p. 149).

This suggests that the critical social sciences pose substantially different methodological problems. While adopting methods to explain, interpret, or understand phenomena critical social researchers require normative theoretical foundations and a different research methodology to deal with moral and normative-ethical questions to pursue emancipatory interest.

Critical Theory has provided such normative and methodological foundation for critical research in IS and other management and business disciplines (see, e.g., Alvesson et al., 2009). This is identified as the neo-humanist strand of critical IS research that is largely dominated by Habermas’ critical social theory (Klein, 2009; Myers & Klein, forthcoming). Early publications were conceptual proposing Habermas’ theory of communicative action (1984, 1987) as a theoretical foundation to study the nature and role of IS in organizations and society (Mingers, 1981; Lyytinen & Klein, 1985; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1988; Lyytinen, 1992).

Furthermore, Hirschheim & Klein (1994) propose emancipatory IS development to overcome communicative distortions. Communicative distortions in an IS development context typically occur due to oppressive power structures and unwarranted power exercise, peer pressure, lack of motivation, and biases and technical language (jargon) used by information technology (IT) professionals. They identify methodological challenges in revealing communicative distortions in IS development processes and difficulties in enacting potentially emancipatory practices. Later studies adopted Habermas’ theory of communicative action (1984, 1987), especially the notions of social actions, instrumental and communicative rationality, colonization of lifeworld, and rational discourse to critical empirical investigations of IS in organizations and society (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997; Klein & Huynh, 1999; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2002; Heng & de Moor, 2003; Janson & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2005). These studies illustrate major methodological challenges of the neo-humanist strand: revealing and denaturalizing restrictive and oppressive forms of IS development and use (characterized by dominant instrumental rationality and colonization of lifeworlds), and envisaging and adopting emancipatory processes of IS development (characterized by communicative rationality and rational discourse) leading to greater autonomy and freedom.

The second strand of critical scholarship is mostly critical of the enlightenment ideal, a belief in reason and the alleged ‘rationalist bias’ in Critical Theory. Very broadly it was seen as post-humanist, influenced by poststructural and post-modernist discourses (Kincheloe & McLaren, 2000; Alvesson et al., 2009). An important criticism by post-structuralists and others refers to the question of the subject (Jones, 2009). The problem is that a humanist assumption of a unified autonomous subject behind an alienated and deprived individual essentializes the subject. The subject, it is claimed, is ‘a terrain of conflict’ (Jones, 2009). It is produced by different and conflicting discourses and is thus uncertain. How can an individual that is subjected to and thereby constructed through the regimes of meaning be autonomous, reflect critically and undermine those very regimes of meanings that created him/her?

This second strand of critical scholarship is focused on the role of power and the relationship between knowledge and power. Habermas’ (1984) view that rational discourse governed by communicative rationality under (near) symmetrical power relations will enable the realization of an autonomous and responsible subject is questioned and contrasted with Foucault’s (1980) view of the pervasiveness of power and the nexus between power and knowledge. Building on a Nietzschean argument Foucault (1980) demonstrates that power is everywhere and within everyone and hence there is no escape from power relations. As power and knowledge are seen as mutually reinforcing and not opposing, he reveals a potentially productive aspect of power. Subjectivity is created through power/knowledge relations and is also a condition that makes these relations possible. This draws attention to the production and utilization of knowledge and the effects on transforming individuals into subjects that acquire meaning and identity through practices invoked by power/knowledge relations. Such Foucauldian analysis enables a subtle examination of IS and their role in exploitation as it ‘draws attention to subjugation through subjectification and self-discipline’ (Knights, 2009, p. 160). In another example, Doolin (2004) draws on Foucault to examine the implementation of a large hospital information system to monitor and scrutinize clinical activity through increased visibility. While the use of the information system engendered self-disciplining behaviour in organizational participants it also produced resistance by doctors, gradually weakening its role.

Methodological challenges of the post-modernist strand of critical IS research include archaeological and genealogical analysis of IS–organization relations, revealing and critiquing regimes of truth and the underlying transformation of power/knowledge nexus due, for example, to IS implementation and use, and understanding the discursive formation of subjectivity, subjugation, and self-disciplining or resistance through IS use.

Furthermore, feminist and gender research that focuses on fundamental gender inequalities associated with appropriation of ITs by modern organizations has been inspired by both neo-humanist and post-humanist critical research. In their study of the traditional view of women’s place in IT work, termed the ‘women in IT problem’, Adam et al. (2006) adopted the critical feminist research approach seeking ‘to challenge the status quo, to reveal asymmetries in power relations and y hear the voices of groups of people who are not in a dominant position in their organizations’ (p. 368). Critical feminist research is responding to important needs for critical examination and action regarding the gender inequalities largely missing in critical IS research (Howcroft & Trauth, 2008).

This brief overview of some major concerns and different strands in critical IS research literature indicates that conducting a critical empirical IS inquiry poses novel methodological challenges, not experienced by positivist or interpretivist researchers, that we discuss next.

## Methodology discourses in IS

Critical social research has traditionally been preoccupied with theoretical analysis (for instance. Marxist and neo-Marxist theories, Critical Theory of the Frankfurt school, Foucauldian inspired theories, post-modernist theorizing). The preoccupation with theoretical analysis has changed however, and in the last two decades critical empirical research has gained in prominence (Howcroft & Trauth 2005). The pressing and distinct needs of critical empirical research, and the desire for recognition of the critical research paradigm, have motivated critical researchers from different disciplines to examine and contribute to the empirical dimension of critical research and the development of critical research methods (Crotty, 1998; Klein, 1999; Kincheloe & McLaren, 2000). These developments can be seen to emerge in two major directions simultaneously.

## Conventional methodology discourse

The conventional direction, which characterizes the dominant methodology discourse, follows the debate between the positivist and interpretivist research paradigms: it assumes that a distinctly critical research paradigm needs to employ and be branded by distinctly critical research methods. A paradigm or an approach as a system of thinking is defined as ‘a general organizing framework for theory and research that includes basic assumptions, key issues, models of quality research, and methods for seeking answers’ (Neuman, 2006, p. 81).

It has been claimed that much empirical critical research in IS – neo-humanist and post-humanist – has adopted variants of interpretive and hermeneutic methods (Klein, 1999; Avgerou, 2002; McGrath, 2005; Mitev, 2006). While this assertion is not without controversy, critical researchers do typically infuse critical theoretic concerns and intents with hermeneutic acts of interpretation thereby adapting and transforming interpretivist methods to serve critical emancipatory purposes. This is the case, for instance, with critical ethnography (Thomas, 1993; Myers, 1997), critical hermeneutics (Thompson, 1981; Vatimo, 1994) and critical discourse analysis (Fairclough, 1995; Alvarez, 2005, 2008).

While these are considered distinctly ‘critical methods’, this just proves the point that the critical research paradigm has relied on the appropriation of interpretivist and hermeneutic methods without having methods of its own. The charge is that the critical research paradigm does not have a clear methodological distinction which will ensure its identity and also contribute to its legitimization as a valid option for IS research.

This charge is particularly damaging for the critical project when it is contrasted with the other two established and credible research paradigm in IS: positivism (the most established and most credible) and interpretivism. For instance, the positivist research methodology is closely related to variable-based quantitative research methods using statistical modelling with survey research being the most popular method in the IS discipline (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). The interpretivist paradigm, on the other hand, is identified with qualitative research methods such as interpretive case study, ethnography, discourse analysis, and action research. Such associations reflect distinct and mutually exclusive ontological and epistemological assumptions and different cognitive interest in knowledge.

Following the same logic, it is expected that the critical research paradigm should also be closely associated with distinctly critical methods. If the critical paradigm – it is argued – is to be a viable and respectable option for empirical IS research, critical researchers should be much more concerned with methodological issues and distinctly critical research methods (Klein, 1999; McGrath, 2005). This would make it (the critical paradigm) a more attractive alternative to positivism and interpretivism and most importantly improve the legitimacy and credibility of critical research in the IS community.

Another motivation for the emphasis on research methods is their importance for the justification of research findings and knowledge claims. There is a tendency in the current methodological debates (mostly coming from positivist quarters) to equate justification and robustness of methods with justification of findings and knowledge claims: ‘it is assumed that if we can justify the methods used in terms of underlying theoretical assumptions and apply them properly, we have also justified the validity and relevance of the research results, which often involve evaluations, change proposals, new designs, interventions, etc’ (Ulrich, 2006, p. 14).

While positivist and interpretivist researchers considerably differ in their understanding and attribution of justification power to proper method selection and application they both leave out and exclude from justification value judgements and normative implications of the findings and recommendations. They can do so as they remain committed to ‘value-free research’ (positivist) and ‘value-neutral research’ (interpretivist). The former excludes values due to their ‘subjective’ and ‘irrational’ character, thus seeing no place for normative theorizing in strictly scientific social science. The latter assumes that everything, including research, is valueladen and is satisfied with description and interpretive understanding while avoiding normative argumentation or moral judgments as part of knowledge claims and theorizing. Both positions can be questioned as problematic and untenable (see McCarthy, 1996; Ulrich, 2006) but such a critique is beyond the purpose of this paper. The important point for the argument in this paper is to reveal the grounds on which claims to distinctive nature and justificatory power of positivist and interpretivist research methods have been and continue to be made.

This brings me to the key question regarding the alleged necessity to develop distinctly ‘critical research methods’ in contrast to positivist and interpretivist methods. In arguing against such a position, I first question the argument that the critical research paradigm should be distinguished from the positivist and interpretivist paradigms in the same way these two are distinguished among themselves, that is on ontological and epistemological grounds. As discussed above critical research, while diverse in its theoretical underpinning, is distinguished from positivist and interpretivist research primarily by its critical-emancipatory interest and its aims to be explanatory, practical, and ethical (on normative-ethical grounds) at the same time (Horkheimer, 1982; Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2005; Mingers & Walsham, 2008; Stahl, 2008; Bohman, 2009; Klein, 2009; Myers & Klein, forthcoming).

This suggests that the same arguments behind the distinction between positivist and interpretivist research methods cannot be used to justify the expectation for distinctly critical research methods. Critical research is explicitly concerned with social critique and the production of knowledge that changes consciousness and engenders transformative praxis towards emancipation, democratization, justice, and liberation. The distinctiveness of critical research cannot be understood at the level of the positivist vs interpretivist dichotomies. Ontological and epistemological assumptions are certainly important (and extensively debated), but the distinctive nature of the critical research methods cannot be articulated and discussed at the same discursive plane (see, e.g., Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Klein & Myers, 1999; Myers & Klein, forthcoming).

## A critical view of the conventional methodological discourse

The second direction of critical methodological discourse is concerned with methodological choices and freedom to make these choices within social, material, historical, and political contexts in which these choices are made. Critical research methodology is explicitly concerned with the purpose of inquiry, moral and ethical issues, social and normative theories, and how these are linked with research design and methods in any given research context. Despite notable (and certainly plausible) attempts to develop distinctly critical research methods (e.g., critical ethnography, critical discourse analysis) critical research need not be and is not limited to those methods perceived as critical. This is consistent with the position by Morrow and Brown who claim that Critical Theory is eclectic with respect to data collection techniques and methods (1994, p. 200):

critical theory has no basis for a priory rejection of any particular methods or techniques as such. But critical theory does require a critical pluralism in that it directs attention not only to how the type of theoretical problems shapes the choices of methods but also to the political and ideological contexts of methodological choices as part of the process of non-empirical argumentation. (Beardsley, 1980)

Galtung (1977), in particular, points to the political and ideological aspect of methodological choices given that ‘to work with any methodology y is a political act y the choice of a methodology is implicitly the choice of an ideology’ (p. 40). A critical approach to the question of research methodology broadens the discourse and emphasizes the social, historical, and political dimensions of conducting research projects. From this perspective, critical IS researchers should be vitally interested in the methodological question. This is not so much the issue of distinctly critical methods (while their value is undisputed) as it is the question of conscious methodological choices, that are also political and ideological choices, and the freedom of making these choices.

Furthermore, given its critical-emancipatory interest the uniqueness of critical research methodology is associated with reflexivity and the dialectic relation between research and practice (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). Ulrich, in particular, emphasizes the importance of critically reflective research practice:

y what matters in the end is not the methods we use but the validity claims we associate with them and how critically we handle these claims in our argumentative practice. Critically reflective research practice is thus a much richer concept than the proper choice and application of methods. It requires a strong discursive culture, that is, an argumentative practice ready to deal with different kinds of validity claims in an open, self-critical, and participatory mode. (2006, p. 16, emphasis added)

These requirements cannot be reduced to a choice of research method(s) but instead need to be integral to broader methodological considerations of conceptualizing and conducting critical research inquiries. It needs to be reiterated that critical social concerns and the orientation towards liberating and emancipatory social change, which characterizes the critical research paradigm pose distinct and indeed considerable methodological problems loaded with moral and ethical questions. However, these distinct methodological problems cannot be reduced to choosing appropriate methods for data collection, analysis, or interpretation. They involve broader and more substantive questions regarding an overall strategy of conceptualizing and conducting an inquiry, the linking of critical theoretic and ethical concerns with empirical methods in concrete social practices; this may further involve engaging with studied phenomena and subjects/objects in their contexts, as well as constructing and justifying valid and socially relevant knowledge claims and transformative ends.

Following the arguments for the critical approach to research methodology, critical research faces the question of the nature and grounding of critical research methodology. In the next section, I briefly describe Myers and Klein’s principles of conducting critical research (2011) that provide grounding for the proposal for a critical research methodology.

## A set of principles for conducting critical research in IS

In an early attempt to develop a framework for critical management research, Alvesson & Deetz (2000) propose three elements of critical research: insight, critique, and transformative redefinition (pp. 139–153). These elements are important in examining the methodological question and have been appropriated for determining the principals of conducting critical research by Myers & Klein (forthcoming).

Insight is a type of practical knowing achieved through hermeneutic, interpretive, or archaeological understanding of real people and situations. Research focuses on social and power relations, micro, and macro-aspects of phenomena, corporate culture and forms of domination and control. The element of critique is closely related to insight and suggests deeper investigation beyond the observable, revealing hidden interests in seemingly objective knowledge production and workings of power. As a third element transformative redefinition denotes the necessity not only to understand and critique but also to envisage alternatives and stimulate change (Alvesson & Deetz, 2000).

Building on these ideas Myers & Klein (forthcoming) propose six principles for conducting critical research, three for enabling critique and three for enabling transformation. They maintain that principles for conducting interpretive research (Klein & Myers, 1999) are applicable to the element of insight. Myers & Klein’s (forthcoming) principles for critical research related to critique are:

1. The principle of using core concepts from critical social theories: ‘critical researchers start with a priori theoretical concepts derived from one or more critical theorists’ (p. 10) based on their relevance to the problem at hand.

2. The principle of taking a value position ‘recognizes the importance of taking value position for motivating and grounding research project’ (p. 10); critical projects typically take an explicit value position in relation for instance to human rights, freedom, elimination of alienation, democracy, equality, justice, and fairness.

3. The principle of revealing and challenging prevailing beliefs and social practices directs attention to ‘habits, customs, and conventions that are typically passed on by venerable tradition and sacrosanct sources of authority’ (p. 11). This principal encapsulates what Foucault calls the ‘critical genealogy of knowledge’ that ‘looks at knowledge in two ways: how it is conditioned by exercising power through “regimes of truth”, and how knowledge can be use as a tool of power once it has become available’ (p. 11).

The three principles related to the element of transformation are:

4. The principle of individual emancipation reflects a fundamental idea about freeing individuals from repressive social and ideological conditions, realization of human needs and potential, critical self-reflection and selftransformation; critical researchers have the important task of identifying possibilities of change and assisting the dominated, powerless and exploited to have a voice and emancipate themselves.

5. The principle of improvements in society suggests that improvements beyond the individual, at organizational, institutional and societal levels are possible; these improvements are contingent upon each other and should thus be considered as mutually related; the direction of improvement has to be determined by those affected, by internal self-governing processes.

6. The principle of improvements in social theories consists of two parts: ‘explicit procedures of evidence giving and the acceptance of the idea of fallibility, y and selfcorrection through criticism and debate’ (p. 12); and subjecting ‘our theoretical apparatus y to change, partly in response to new historical discoveries or empirical data on current social changes, and partially in response to new theoretical reasoning and debate’ (p. 12).

While relating to two elements – critique and transformation in critical research – the proposed principles for conducting critical research go beyond the elements as defined by Alvesson & Deetz (2000). Myers and Klein’s principles further differentiate the critical IS research paradigm from the positivist and interpretivist paradigms. Most importantly the principles clearly demarcate a discursive plane at which questions regarding the distinguishing nature of critical research methodology can be debated.

## Distinctive dimensions of critical research methodology

In this section, the distinctive dimensions of critical research methodology are proposed grounded on Myers & Klein’s (forthcoming) principles for conducting critical IS research and informed by a significant body of critical research literature in the IS and other disciplines. Four dimensions of critical research methodology are proposed to identify key methodological choices regarding the conceptualization and conduct of critical IS research inquiries. The four dimensions are described in Table 1. The framework depicted in Figure 1 indicates how the four dimensions are grounded on the six principles and also shows how they are mutually intertwined.

## 4. Reflexive–dialectic argumentation

Reflexive–dialectic argumentation is a defining dimension of critical research methodology that infuses and impacts on decisions in all other dimensions. Given critical researchers’ belief that all research is part of the process of social (re)production, the uniqueness of critical research methodology is associated with reflexivity and the dialectic relation between research and practice.

Reflexive and dialectical argumentation may be seen as a discursive examination of ontological and epistemological assumptions behind methodological choices and grounds of knowledge claims (made in other dimensions) in pursuit of freedom from any form of dogmatism, guided by an interest in emancipation and greater autonomy.

Examples from the literature

Rosio Alvarez in her paper ‘Examining technology, structure and identity during an enterprise system implementation’ (2008) adopts critical discourse analysis to show the implications for the restructuring of work relations and the links between technology and organizational structure. By exploring tensions between managerial discourses and professional identity and presenting ‘genealogy of resistance’ she demonstrates how ERP implementation not only changes organizational structure but also the identities of professionals. Transformative redefinition of ERP use and implications may be relevant beyond the research site.

A reflexive–dialectic dimension of critical methodology is exemplified in Klecun and Cornford’s paper ‘A critical approach to evaluation’ (2005), in which they acknowledge how researchers ‘activity exposes, expresses, challenges, or enforces some interests, while downplaying others’ (p. 230). After completing their empirica study the authors reflect on and question their own and ‘participants interests, beliefs and assumptions, and consider how these might influence the choices and methods, the evaluation process and its findings’ (p. 239). These reflective accounts enable the authors to critically assess, refine, and deepen their critical approach to IS evaluation.

Unlike interpretive researchers, they are concerned with unquestioned assumptions about the role of IS as instruments for enhancing managerial control, increasing rationalization and enabling efficient domination in the workplace that underlie these processes. Critical researchers reveal and challenge such taken for granted assumptions and concepts of IS, so as to assist actors in developing alternatives and envisaging and enacting change. Similar to interpretivist researchers, critical researchers engage in a close relationship with actors and their situations to achieve an understanding of these phenomena through in-depth examination while situating them into the wider social, economic, cultural, historical, and political contexts. Alvesson & Deetz (2000) refer to this component of critical methodology as insight defined as a type of practical knowing that reflects importance to people studied. This explains why interpretivist methods such as ethnography and hermeneutics, have been appropriated by critical researchers.

However, it is important to emphasize the difference vis-a\`-vis the interpretivist approach: insight, in-depth examination and deep interpretation are used by critical researchers as ways of achieving critical understanding. Pursuing practical interest and achieving interpretive understanding are necessary but not sufficient to pursue emancipatory interest. Critical understanding and indepth examination is a dimension of critical methodology that requires an essentially new quality including:

(a) argumentation based on critical theoretic concerns and the adoption of concepts from critical social theories (following principle 1);

(b) motivation guided by emancipatory interest and an explicit value position in articulating desirable social changes (e.g., empowerment, democratic workplace, gender equality) (following principle 2); and

(c) methods that enable revelation and the challenging of taken for granted assumptions and beliefs underlying social and power relations, work and management practices and (re)production of domination, inequality, alienation, etc. (following principle 3).

To achieve critical understanding and in-depth examination critical researchers in different critical traditions would adopt different theoretical foundations and also different methods. Some may, for instance, investigate increasing economic-rationalist management in organizations by studying systematically distorted communication enabled by IS in a Habermasian sense, or through deconstruction in a Derridian sense or by exploring genealogy of knowledge in a Foucauldian sense. Hence, different research methods might be chosen and adapted in order to link critical-emancipatory interest to research design and empirical data collection and making and justifying knowledge claims.

Apart from selecting appropriate methods, the achievement of critical understanding and critical in-depth examination involves difficult choices in a field setting and requires sensitivity to specific situations and positions, interests and meanings as well as organizational history, culture, market, and competitive position, etc. Questions to be answered include: How does a researcher come to know the Other? How does s/he motivate the Other to speak and open up? How does s/he respect the view of the Other and express a critical attitude towards the Other? How can a researcher critically engage with the Other, explore alternative interpretations and co-create knowledge relevant to the Other? (Fuchs, 1993; Kincheloe & McLaren, 2000). Researchers need to address these questions in particular circumstances and conditions while making choices in this methodological dimension.

Critical researcher has a benefit of distance and the wealth of theoretical and conceptual knowledge to draw from, but s/he cannot determine critical understanding nor assume the authority in the matters studied. One way to achieve critical understanding is to engage in participatory relations and open discourse whenever possible (e.g., through participatory action research or critical ethnography). As an engaged participant, the researcher may contribute to dialectics of discourses in the field and thereby get exposed to opposing and contradicting views and explanations. This indicates how achieving critical understanding is closely linked with decisions along other dimensions (e.g. open discourse and reflection).

An interesting example of methodological choices in developing critical understanding and in-depth examination of IS planning processes is presented in Cordoba & Midgley (2008). By questioning narrow approaches to IS planning, typically limited to organization boundaries as given, they argue for a critical review of boundaries of IS planning and for the adoption of wider social and ethical concerns. Using the concept of ‘boundary critique’ (coined by Ulrich, 2002) they demonstrate the necessity for justification of boundary judgements in IS planning. It involves critical understanding of and reflection on a situation, revelation of taken for granted boundaries that determine analysis and decisions and investigation of alternative boundaries and their consequences. To practically challenge normative content of boundary judgements they adopt Ulrich’s (1987) framework of 12 questions asking about 'what is the case' and ‘what ought to be’. These methodological choices enable them to address major questions regarding the ethics of IS planning.

## Critical explanation and comparative generalization

A dimension of critical explanation and comparative generalization across sites and situations – historically, spatially, or genealogically developed – involves methodological choices that enable searching beneath the surface and revealing hidden mechanisms and structures that can explain phenomena studied. Critical explanation aims at ‘empirically lifting into view the underlying semantic, sociocultural, and structural relations that are constitutive of historically unique actors, mediations, and systems, respectively’ (Morrow & Brown, 1994, p. 212). Critical explanation goes beyond in-depth examination and critical understanding of local meanings and phenomena (IS in organization or across organizations or society) informed by critical theorizing. Critical explanation re-contextualizes critical understanding of local meanings within the broader social and power relations that constructed them. In doing so, critical explanation may seek to reveal deep structures and generative mechanisms underlying the social production of meanings, people and their identities, and especially the role of IS in these mechanisms. Depending on their affinity to a particular critical tradition or stream of critical IS research, researchers may seek different types of critical explanations that may range from explicit quasi-causal explanations of underlying mechanisms and structures (e.g., linking user participation in IS development to user empowerment) to more critical-hermeneutic or deconstructive explanations.

Comparative generalization is complementary to critical explanation. Comparative generalization aims to identify patterns, structures, and mechanisms underlying social and technological phenomena across cases but not to find universally valid laws. Unlike statistical modelling that deals with decontextualized variables across large aggregates that remains on the surface of social phenomena, critical explanation, and structural generalization dig deeper beneath the surface in search for underlying, often hidden mechanisms that can explain phenomena of interest. Limited comparative generalizations are typically endeavoured based on several comparative cases or situations in a single case over a period of time in which observed phenomena are exemplified. Researchers seek to develop a critical explanation and derive limited generalizations that contribute to theory development or improvement. In such a way, critical explanation and comparative generalization add an important layer of intelligibility and validity to critical argumentation.

For example a critical study of micro-emancipation during the course of a participatory IS development project provided a critical explanation and some comparative generalizations across situations throughout the history of a retail company (a chapter ‘Participatory and Emancipatory IS Development: Principles, Practices and Pitfalls’ by Cecez-Kecmanovic & Janson, 2009). Empirical evidence is provided for different types of micro-emancipatory processes based on a framework adopted from Alvesson & Willmott (1992). Critical explanation of micro-emancipatory incidents and the theoretical understanding of emancipatory opportunities within IS development may play the role of an ‘emissary’ between research and practice, and between different organizations. ‘This might involve “translating” (Czarniawska & Sevon, 2005) micro-emancipations from one context into other contexts. It might also involve drawing out links of “articulations” between a series of micro-emancipations in order for them to gain greater collective power’ (Spicer et al., 2009, p. 554). The chapter by Cecez-Kecmanovic & Janson (2009) shows a range of methodological choices made to achieve not only critical explanation and comparative generalization (dimension 2), but also critical understanding (dimension 1), and open discourse and transformative redefinition (dimension 3).

## Open discourse and transformative redefinition or action

Open discourse implies non-distorted communication that allows questioning, criticizing, and problematizing taken for granted meanings, assumptions and values, as well as aims and objectives in resolving problematic situation and undertaking change, for example, through IS development and deployment. Such a discourse opens a possibility of different and contradicting views, perceptions, and explanations to be argued and subjected to public scrutiny. An open discourse would then (ideally) lead to transformative redefinition of a situation and a course of actions to achieve desirable ends. It is important to emphasize that ‘both the researcher and the actor y enter symmetrical discourse in which the consensus (as long as it is achieved) will determine what is true or what is right and what can be considered ethically sound (Habermas, 1984, 1987, 1990)’ (Scherer, 2009, p. 37).

Transformative redefinition and action in critical research is a broad concept:

To transform means to change fundamentally, to recognise basic structures and to breach current limits. The perspective goes beyond a surface level of reality to realign subjective understandings with external reality, and then uses renewed consciousness as a basis for engaging in actions that have the potential to modify external conditions and future consciousness. The relevance of knowledge is its ability to connect consciousness with people engaging in concrete actions, reflecting on the consequences of those actions, then advancing consciousness to a new level in an ongoing cycle. (Neuman, 2006, p. 100)

For instance, of particular concern for critical IS researchers are increasing control, power centralization and managerial domination over employees, enabled and supported by IS. By being focused on functional and technical issues, or more precisely, by being made to believe that this is what IS are all about and what they should be concerned with, employees assist in and contribute to the design and implementation of systems that work against their own interests. Employees seem to wilfully consent to increased control, domination, and loss of autonomy. They are, critical researchers claim, socialized into organizational culture, subjected to managerialist ideology and technological determinism that together impose particular social meanings, representations, and rituals (including IS development and deployment methodology) that produce consent to increased control and domination. Employees are thereby misled and exploited, made to act against their own interests.

How is it then at all possible to come up with a critical insight and transformative redefinition that can engender changes? How can employees change the very conditions that shaped them and undertake actions to realize their potential? Principles 3, 4, and 5 suggest that improvements are possible ‘not only at individual level but in society as a whole.y in organizations, institutions, y and that improvements at all levels must go hand in hand, because their success is contingent on each other’ (Myers & Klein, forthcoming, p. 11). Existing conditions in organizations and society, however, pose serious problems and make methodological choices for critical researchers with emancipatory interest, seeking transformative redefinition and change, very difficult.

Methodological choices along this dimension include the forms and means of open discourse in concrete situations and practical ways to attract and engage relevant parties in debates and transformative redefinition and action. As Ulrich (2006) argues critical research practice needs to establish a strong discursive culture and an argumentative practice that enables participants to deal with different validity claims in an open and power-free atmosphere. To stimulate transformative redefinition of existing situations and conditions and to engender change critical researchers need to be highly innovative to create conditions of non-distorted communications that enable a broader base of stakeholders affected by an IS development and use to engage in an open dialogue free from the coercive use of power and based on rational argumentation.

Researchers need to make choices regarding the forms that such open dialogue can take and methods of facilitating the dialogue so that it produces acceptable outcomes for all involved. Such an open dialogue, for instance, may be designed to reveal illusions and taken for granted assumptions in an IS development, and also arbitrariness of so-called objective measures of IS project success. It can also be designed to explore broader questions regarding the adoption of IS to support and strengthen existing practices and power structures that limit innovation and instead explore alternative innovative forms of work and management. Critical researchers need to find ways to assist organizational participants to deal with different, often conflicting views and interests and reconcile opposing requirements of the IS implementation. Transformative redefining and action may need to be negotiated rather than achieved by consensus.

By co-designing and engaging in an open dialogue critical IS researchers aim to create conditions for liberating actions (for instance, by taking part in IS development and critically assessing its implications may lead to more active engagement of stakeholders in the development process). This is where critical IS researchers see their role and the purpose of their engagement. By stimulating open discourse they assist actors in exposing assumptions and beliefs behind an IS implementation. By revealing how power structures and dominant discourses shape consciousness and thereby produce employees’ subjective experiences and acculturate them to feel comfortable in relations of domination and subordination, critical researchers motivate them to (at least) question their position and assist them in undertaking transformative, liberating actions.

In the paper ‘Examining Technology, Structure and Identity During an Enterprise System Implementation’,

Rosio Alvarez (2008) adopts critical discourse analysis to show the implications for the restructuring of work relations and the links between technology and organizational structure. By exploring tensions between manage rial discourses and professional identity and presenting ‘genealogy of resistance’ she demonstrates how an Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) implementation not only changes organizational structure but also the identities of professionals. Transformative redefinition of ERP use and implications may be relevant beyond the research site. This paper also makes explicit methodological choices along the dimensions of critical understanding and critical explanation.

## Reflexive–dialectic argumentation

Reflexive–dialectic argumentation is the fourth dimension that involves modes of non-empirical argumentation in relation to methodological choices and practices in other three dimensions (see Figure 1). Given critical researchers’ belief that all research is part of the process of social (re)production, the uniqueness of critical research methodology is associated with reflexivity and the dialectic relation between research and practice (Morrow & Brown, 1994; Neuman, 2006). It could be claimed that positivist and interpretivist researchers also adopt (self-consciously or not) some form of reflexivity and dialectic reasoning. The key difference however is that critical researchers consider reflexive-dialectic argumentation central to their social inquiry and integrate it within empirical methodological choices and strategies in other dimensions. It is therefore proposed here that the reflexive-dialectic argumentation is a distinct and defining dimension of critical methodology that infuses and impacts on decisions in all other dimensions and their mutual linkages.

A reflexive and dialectical orientation of a critical methodology has been interpreted in a number of ways, from Hegel, to Marx, to Horkheimer (dialectical methodology) and Habermas. Referring to Habermas, McCarthy suggests:

The unifying thread seems to be ‘the insight that the research process organized by subjects belongs – through the very act of knowing itself – to the objective complex that is supposed to be known’ [Habermas, 1974, p. 12]. This is a reference to the fact that the critical theorist is himslef part of the social reality that he wishes to analyse, that his problems and his interests, as well as his concepts and judgements, issue from and belong to the very traditions and institutions under investigation. Critical theory is doubly reflective: it is self-conscious of its origines in the historical development of society, and it is selfconscious of its role in the further development of society. (1996, p. 135)

This is highly relevant for IS research generally while a key concern for critical IS research. Once we realize that IS research is part of reality that is researched and criticized, our critique should also be directed to the assumptions and interest behind IS research as part of the social, economic, political, and historical reality. How much are we self-conscious of IS theoretical origins in the historical developments of IS and their roles in organizations and society? How much are we selfconscious of the impact IS research has had in the past and might have on the future of organizations and society? Have we ever attempted to develop an IS theory that is ‘at every step guided and permeated by the self-consciousness of its own relationship to practice’ (Habermas, 1974, p. 81)?

Reflexive and dialectical argumentation as a nonempirical methodological practice is necessary for critical researchers to confront, question, and deconstruct the dogmatic nature of knowledge and knowledge production (in IS research); it is necessary to reveal and demonstrate the ways we (both researchers and researched) ‘habitually naturalize’ and reify dominant meanings and explanations (Alvesson & Aschroft, 2009) (involved, for instance, in IS development, adoption, use and evaluation); it is necessary for us to identify, demystify, and expose dogmatic forms of organizational life and IS practices, and their detrimental consequences. Reflexive and dialectical argumentation may be seen as a relentless discursive examination of assumptions behind methodological choices and grounds of knowledge claims (made in other dimensions) in pursuit of freedom from any form of dogmatism, guided by interest in emancipation and greater autonomy.

Following topics illustrate the type of issues discussed along the reflexive–dialectical methodological dimension:

\- Reflexivity in relation to the economic, social, cultural, and historical contexts and conditions of empirical inquiry, the production of findings and valid knowledge claims, and the dialectic relationship between knowledge and practice;

\- Self-reflection of researchers and researched on their assumptions, positions, experiences, and biases; this may involve reflection on how knowledge claims are (and can be) grounded in the lifeworld and everyday experiences of social groups with often different standpoints and interests; this may also involve reflection on historically suppressed discourses and reconstruction of what has been suppressed;

\- Reflection on normative argumentation, value claims as subjects of rational argument and the ways and degrees to which they are consensually validated; normative argumentation may refer to any aspect of empirical study in other three dimensions;

\- Researchers’ self-reflection regarding their participation in the institutionalized research processes and their dialectical understanding of knowledge production and the politics of the academic publishing, reward structures etc.

A reflective dimension of critical methodology is exemplified in Klecun & Cornford’s paper ‘A Critical Approach to Evaluation’ (2005) in which they acknowledge how researchers’ ‘activity exposes, expresses, challenges or enforces some interests, while downplaying others’ (p. 230). After completing their empirical study the authors reflect on and question theirs and ‘participants interests, beliefs, and assumptions, and consider how these might influence the choices and methods, the evaluation process and its findings’ (p. 239). These reflective accounts enable the authors to critically assess, refine and deepen their approach to IS evaluation.

Another lively example can be found in Walsham’s chapter on ‘Critical Engagement: Why, What and How?’ (2005). By drawing from three longitudinal research projects in South Africa, India and Brazil, Walsham reflects on examples of researchers’ critical engagement that ‘implies challenging the status quo and in some cases therefore, opposing existing vested interests.y [that] can sometimes be a painful and even on occasions a dangerous process’ (p. 227). Walsham first explores selfreflective accounts of these researchers and then reflects on his own research and orientation. For instance researchers in a district-based health information system project in the South Africa express their political stance and motives:

It is important to know that the original key members of the HISP team have background as social/political activists in the antiapartheid struggle and other social movements, and that we have always explicitly and implicitly seen ourselves as political actors in a larger development process. (Braa & Hedberg, 2002, p. 114, cited from Walsham, 2005, p. 231)

This self-reflective identity of researchers is important for the justification of research strategy, research methods, and research outcomes. Walsham himself reflects on his own ‘journey’ and discusses how he bacame more and more ‘openly critical’ (p. 241).

While it has been acknowledged by some (see, e.g., Alvesson & Skoldberg, 2000; Alvesson et al., 2009), reflexive–dialectic argumentation of critical research methodology needs much more attention and exploration as a key dimension of critical IS research. Reflexivity and dialectic reasoning deserve to be added to Myers & Klein’s (forthcoming) general principles for guiding critical IS.

## Concluding remarks

In this paper, I argue that it is not research methods but a research methodology that distinguishes critical from other research paradigms. I proposed a framework for a critical research methodology that includes a configuration of four analytically distinguished but intertwined dimensions: (1) critical understanding and in-depth examination, (2) critical explanation and comparative generalization, (3) open discourse and transformative redefinition or action, and (4) reflexive–dialectic argumentation. They are grounded on Myers & Klein’s (forthcoming) principles for conducting critical IS field research and informed by a broad critical research literature. The paper provides a theoretical argument for the claim that these dimensions express the defining character of a critical research methodology. As such the framework may contribute to better understanding and appreciation of the critical IS research paradigm and its identity. It may also contribute to the advancement of the critical IS research practice.

Specific contributions to the critical IS research and practice include:

1. The framework for critical research methodology identifies key dimensions along which vital methodological choices need to be made; the methodological dimensions express unique characteristic of critical IS research while assuming that the specific choices made within the diverse research strands may significantly differ.

2. The proposed methodological dimensions and suggested choices that need to be made in each will assist and guide critical IS researchers in designing and conducting their empirical studies and justifying results and knowledge claims; through their wider adoption in critical research practice the framework and the methodological dimensions will be also exposed to criticism and reflected upon, hopefully leading to further development and refinement.

3. The framework for critical research methodology may help journal editors and reviewers in assessing and evaluating critical research studies and justification of their knowledge claims.

4. The framework may be also used to attract and open more opportunities for potential researchers to pursue critical IS research; it may be usefully adopted for training new critical IS researchers and for providing methodological guidance for conducting critical research.

5. The framework is intended to stimulate and broaden the debate about critical research methodology and its adoption in the critical IS research practice; it can thereby assist critical IS researchers in sharing their experiences and reflecting on their practices and building their identity (McGrath, 2005; Klein, 2009).

6. The proposed critical research methodology can be also seen as a response within critical IS research to quests in positivist and interpretivist research to advance research methods and increase their justificatory power; as argued in the paper distinct methodological issues in critical IS research cannot be resolved by advancing methods alone, they need to be debated primarily at the level of methodology; this does not
