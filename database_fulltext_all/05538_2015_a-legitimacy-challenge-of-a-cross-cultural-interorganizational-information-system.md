# A legitimacy challenge of a cross-cultural interorganizational information system

Carol Hsu<sup>1</sup>, Yu-Tzu Lin<sup>2</sup> and Tawei Wang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>College of Management, National Taiwan University, Taiwan; <sup>2</sup>Department of Business Management, National Kaohsiung Normal University, Taiwan; <sup>3</sup>School of Accountancy, University of Hawaii at Manoa, U.S.A.

## Abstract

This paper studies the adoption and diffusion of a cross-cultural Interorganizational Information System (IOS), which is used to streamline the processing of financial transactions between European investment fund companies and Taiwanese banks. Drawing from institutional and organizational legitimacy theory, we argue that the adoption and implementation of technological innovation is contingent upon its alignment with three institutional pillars in different countries and the deployment of legitimation strategies by stakeholders. Departing from classical innovation diffusion theory, our empirical investigation reveals that the implementation of a cross-cultural IOS is a dynamic process involving the recognition, understanding, and management of the regulative, normative, and cognitive challenges arising in two different institutional settings. This paper contributes to the growing body of research that highlights the significance of social and institutional influences on the adoption of IOS in a global environment.

Keywords: Interorganizational Information System (IOS); institutional theory; crosscultural study; interpretive case study

## Introduction

Interorganizational Information Systems (IOSs) have been an important element in the standardization and automation of processing <sup>fi</sup>nancial transactions over the past two decades. Popular examples of such technologies include electronic data interchanges (EDI), electronic trading networks used by <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions, and the RosettaNet. In view of its perceived value in streamlining interorganizational business activities, studies on the adoption of IOS among organizations have been of strong interests to many information systems (IS) researchers. Roger’s innovation diffusion theory has been dominant in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of research for identifying the factors that affect the adoption process. This theory involves the development of variations in the organizational determinants of adoption (Robey et al, 2008). These determinants encompass organizational readiness, perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and innovation characteristics (Ramamurthy & Premkumar, 1995). Against this deterministic model, other researchers have focused on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of societal and institutional in<sup>fl</sup>uences (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 2001; Teo et al, 2003). These scholarly works have individually shown that a variety of economic, organizational, and institutional considerations in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of IOS by organizations. Nonetheless, in the arguments below, we propose that, as IOSs expand to connect organizations that are more globally dispersed (Robey et al, 2008), a more dynamic and alternative approach is required to explain the interplay of institutional and cultural in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the adoption process.

Globally dispersed organizations operate in different institutional settings in which social, political, and business practices may vary extensively. We see these variations as challenges to the diffusion of a cross-cultural IOS. The argument is that institutional constraints and societal differences have important implications for the diffusion process. To achieve collective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from a cross-cultural IOS, organizations from one sociopolitical environment need the collaboration of, and the connectivity with, others within a network. Drawing from institutional and organizational legitimacy theory, we argue that the success of an IOS adoption lies in the degree to which it is considered legitimate in each relevant institutional setting. The idea of legitimacy is an important element as it reflects embededness in a system of institutionalized beliefs and action scripts (Schuman, 1995, p. 574). In other words, organizations need to comply with the regulatory rules, normative values, and cultural practices (i.e., the institutional pillars) that govern organizational practices in a given setting. The concept of institutional legitimacy is commonly applied to IS studies in areas of, for example, IS innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997; Kaganer et al, 2010), organizational change (Orlikowski & Robey, 1991; Robey & Boudreau, 1999) and electronic markets (Grewal et al, 2001). However, it is seldom examined in any great depth and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as far as we are aware there has not yet been applied in the context of the diffusion process of a cross-cultural IOS.

For the purposes of examining the complexities and interplay of institutional factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of IOS by organizations across different countries, we undertook a longitudinal interpretive case study of the diffusion of a processing system between Europe and Taiwan used in connection with the sale of mutual funds. This system, known as straight-through-processing (STP) technology, is a type of IOS used in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry. It has mainly been a U.S./European initiative used by <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions and regulatory agencies since the late 1990s. The objective of STP is to streamline interorganizational transaction processing by minimizing manual intervention and maximizing automation in order to reduce human errors, increasing processing speeds, and reducing the incidence of failed trades. STP was <sup>fi</sup>rst developed and used among investment fund companies and distributing agents to automate the order and settlement of fund transactions in Europe. In the early 2000s, with the growing demand for Taiwan-based investment funds, European investment fund companies faced an increasing need to request that banks in Taiwan replace their existing practice of placing orders via fax with STP technology. Compared with the broader acceptance and adoption of the technology in Europe, only <sup>fi</sup>ve banks in Taiwan had opted for adoption in the period up to 2010.

Our research objective is twofold. First, through an interpretive case study, we aim to identify the set of institutional conditions that facilitate or inhibit the diffusion of STP in two different institutional settings. In particular, drawing from the institutional theory, we attempt to explore how variations in the three institutional pillars affect the introduction of STP from Europe into Taiwan. Second, responding to Kaganer et al’s (2010) call for the need to study the temporal progressions of innovation discourse and legitimation strategies, we undertake a longitudinal study that ascertains the manner in which investment fund companies and STP vendors from Europe make sense of, manage, and navigate through the conditions supporting the legitimacy of STP in Taiwan. An analysis of the results from this empirical investigation can advance our understanding about the legitimacy challenges that arise in implementing a successful crosscultural IOS in a global context.

In the next section, we review the existing IOS literature with a focus on the institutional and cultural implications of IOS adoption. This is followed by a description of the institutional and organizational legitimacy framework that serves as our theoretical lens. We then present our research methodology and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our empirical investigation. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section concludes with the theoretical and practical implications of this study.

## Literature review

Our review of the existing literature shows that research on IOS adoption has primarily relied on the innovation diffusion theory and other concepts related to the technology acceptance model (Iacovou et al, 1995; Premkumar et al, 1997; Chwelos et al, 2001; Quaddus & Hofmeyer, 2007). These models consider that the process by which IOS adoption takes place is mainly determined by the attributes of the innovation itself. Although these studies have been valuable to our initial understanding of how decisions are made at the organizational level, we argue that this dominant approach is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient in explaining all the complexities arising from a cross-cultural IOS adoption.

First, as Rodon & Sese (2010) point out, most studies have applied the innovation diffusion model to make predictions about the adoption process at the organizational level, thus overlooking other levels of analysis – such as community and industry (p. 638). Given that the purpose of IOS is to facilitate transactions among organizations, the adoption and diffusion process requires communication, negotiation, and coordination beyond the boundary of each independent organization. Scholars have supported the need to develop an alternative approach that can synthesize across multiple adoption levels and explain the emergence and dynamics of IOS adoption in a broader context (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2010; Reimers et al, 2014). However, our review of the literature indicates that these arguments have mostly remained at a conceptual level, and there remains a dearth of empirical studies incorporating a multiple-domain-based approach.

Second, we argue that the underlying deterministic view of technology adoption is inadequate because this approach ignores the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of institutional properties and the market structure. Other research has looked at the process of information technology (IT) adoption and implementation as the shaping and reshaping process among IT artifacts within the contextual environment in which the organization is situated (Walsham & Waema, 1994; Avgerou, 2001). This approach considers institutions primarily as stabilizing, and inhibiting social structure change, and recognize how technologies are embedded in complex interdependent social, economic, and political networks, and how they are consequently shaped by broader institutional influences (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001, p. 154). Several studies have also attempted to address the social context of IOS adoption. For example, Hsiao (2003) uses the issue of trust to analyze how a society’s sociocultural structure affects the adoption of an e-marketplace in Singapore. Teo et al (2003) adopt the institutional theory to investigate the adoption of a <sup>fi</sup>nancial EDI in Singapore. On the basis of a longitudinal in-depth case study of an industry IOS, Rodon et al (2008) conducted a sociotechnical, process-oriented, and multi-level study on IOS diffusion. Their paper shows how a diverse team of actors performs to keep different interests aligned, and how different stakeholders struggle to inscribe their interests into textual descriptions, training programs, rules, hardware, and software. Robey et al (2008) also conclude with a suggestion that future IOS research overcome the pro-innovation bias that permeates much prior research and consider alternative determinants (e.g., managerial fashion) and more critical evaluations of IOS adoption and diffusion (p. 512). In this study, we contribute to these perspectives by attempting to make a theoretical contribution in demonstrating the value of proposing an alternative approach for analyzing and explaining the IOS adoption process. A summary of the literature review is given in Table 1.

In summary, we believe that the organizational level analysis and the innovation-determinist approach to technology adoption have their limits in explaining the phenomenon of cross-cultural IOS diffusion. To address this research gap, we draw on the organizational and institutional legitimacy theory to gain some insight into the importance of institutional settings to organizational structures and actions (Teo et al, 2003) and offer a conceptually rich model for observing the non-linear routes of IT adoption and diffusion across different social contexts (Currie, 2009).

## Theoretical framework

Institutional theorists believe that institutions are social structures based on taken-for-granted, formal, or informal rules that restrict and control (or support) social behaviors (Biorck. 2004). Scott (2008) states that institutions are comprised of regulative, normative and cultural–cognitive elements that, together with associated activities and resources, provide stability and meaning to social life (p. 48). According to this view, each institutional pillar (regulative, normative, and cultural–cognitive) corresponds with each of the three different mechanisms of isomorphism: coercive, normative, and mimetic (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991; Meyer & Rowan, 1991; Scott, 1995), and these mechanisms can shape and in<sup>fl</sup>uence how organizational practices or innovations are diffused within an institutional setting. In a similar vein, institutional scholars af<sup>fi</sup>rm that organizations thus become more legitimate when they behave or take actions in order to comply with regulatory requirements, normative structures, and cultural values embedded within the institutional setting (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009). In analyzing the elements in the institutional framework, while agreeing these three factors as contributing, in interdependent and mutually reinforcing ways, to a power social framework (p. 50), Scott (2008) argues that more progress and valuable insight can be achieved by identifying their different underlying assumptions, mechanisms, and indicators (p. 51). For the purpose of this study, we will explain the nature of each isomorphism and their relevance to technology adoption in cross-cultural settings.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst institutional pillar is about rules and regulations. These serve as the basis of coercive isomorphism to control and constrain organizational behaviors through the power of enforcement and the power to impose sanctions and other penalties for non-compliance. King et al (1994) explain that government agencies can often take action that may have an impact on IT innovation. For example, they can develop policies or pass laws that provide <sup>fi</sup>nancial support for a selective set of technological research, develop a standard to be attained through technological development, or establish professional institutions that support the knowledge development for a particular innovation. DiMaggio & Powell (1991) cite as an example a government’s ability to compel the adoption of new pollution control technology by issuing appropriate mandatory environmental regulations. Kaganer et al (2010) <sup>fi</sup>nd that vendors of computerized physician order entry (COPE) systems try to encourage the adoption of such systems by emphasizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of COPE in helping organizations comply with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States. Most of the regulatory requirements and rules that may affect cross-cultural IOS adoption tend to be country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c. Different governments are likely to formulate different policies and rules. This might have an unfavorable or challenging impact on the legitimacy of cross-cultural innovation because of the number of different authorities sovereign over it and by the diversity or inconsistency of their accounts of how it is to function (Meyer & Scott, 1983, p. 202; as cited in Kostova & Zaheer, 1999, p. 71).

The second institutional pillar relates to compliance with the set of norms and values held within a social system that can determine the appropriateness of organizational actions (Scott, 2008). Norms describe how things should be done and represent the appropriate ways to achieve the de<sup>fi</sup>ned goal and objectives. DiMaggio & Powell (1991) argue that the process of professionalization contributes to normative legitimacy and forms the basis of the social responsibility of an organization. Professionalization through networking, training, or socialization allows members of a particular industry to communicate and exchange industry-related information and experiences. As a result, there is a convergence of thoughts, beliefs, and conduct among professionals within an institutional setting. These beliefs and values then can be developed in the form of standards operating procedures or organizational policies to impose or empower professional activities. Teo et al (2003) <sup>fi</sup>nd that managerial engagement and participation in trade or business associations that endorse <sup>fi</sup>nancial electronic data exchanges (FEDI) generate normative pressure upon an organization’s decision to adopt IOS in Singapore. Gosain (2004) argues that the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations in enterprise IS used by organizations tend to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the current attitudes of IS professionals. In a cross-cultural context, we anticipate that the extent to which normative isomorphism affects IOS adoption will depend on the existence of endorsement from related trades or business associations in each country as well as the congruence in values of IT innovation perceived and interpreted by association members in these countries.

The third institutional pillar is cultural–cognitive. In addition to conforming with regulatory rules and normative values, organizational actions also need to comply with the cultural–cognitive beliefs rooted in an institutional environment (Schuman, 1995). This cultural– cognitive dimension highlights the importance of symbolic representation, norms, and meanings shared in a broader social context (Zucker, 1983). The distinction between normative and cultural–cognitive dimension is that the former focuses on the social obligation and binding expectation normally speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by standards or industry policies, while the latter is about the construction of a common framework of meaning embodied in social routines or industry culture. Scott (2008) suggests that organizations can achieve cultural–cognitive legitimacy when they imitate the structure or actions of other organizations, especially those perceived as successful or powerful peer organizations. This is because of the likely perception by a social collectivity, such as a country, that any structure or act that is similar to a pre-existing one is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and hence would be more easily supported or accepted. In the context of IT innovation, cognitive legitimacy relies on the spread of knowledge regarding that innovation (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994) and the ability of such innovation to respond to environmental uncertainty (Gosain, 2004). A number of studies have discussed how cognitive-cultural legitimacy and mimetic isomorphism can in<sup>fl</sup>uence organizational adoption. For instance, Tingling & Parent (2002) found that mimetic pressure could lead the decision maker of an organization to choose an inferior technology during the process of technological product evaluation. The impact of mimetic in<sup>fl</sup>uences is also evident in a study of FEDI adoption in Singapore (Teo et al. 2003). in particular the influence of competitors and successful peer organizations. Kaganer et al (2010) further reveal how vendors of the CPOE system and potential adopters endeavor to establish cognitive legitimacy by emphasizing the alignment between the CPOE system and existing institutional practices prevalent in the health-care industry, and publicizing the implementation success stories of peer organizations. Within a cross-cultural setting, we believe that the conformance and maintenance of cultural– cognitive legitimacy is likely to be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and challenging since diffusion needs to take place across different countries. As Scotts (2008) states: differentiated roles can and do develop in localized contexts as repetitive patterns of action gradually become habitualized and objectified (p. 59). Walsham (2o02) provides a valuable study that illustrates the implication of cross-cultural con<sup>fl</sup>ict and contradiction in software production and use. In the context of our empirical study, we anticipate that the differences in common corporate cultural beliefs and values between European and Taiwan would shape and affect the organizational adoption of STP.

In short, by drawing on institutional and organizational legitimacy theory, we argue that in the context of crosscultural IT innovation diffusion, whether such innovation is aligned or in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with relevant regulations, normative values, and cultural beliefs can have a profound impact on an organization’s decision to adopt, as well as the overall diffusion process.

## Research approach and case description

## Methodology

This study employs the institutional perspective as the interpretive lens to analyze the adoption and diffusion pattern of STP innovation from Europe into Taiwan. We conduct an interpretive longitudinal study into the adoption and diffusion of STP projects in the fund industry in Taiwan taking place during the period between 2000 and 2011. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author became aware of the development of STP in Taiwan’s fund industry in 2004 when she was working in a <sup>fi</sup>nancial institution in Taiwan. During this time, she has been collecting industry news and became acquainted with the managers from mutual fund companies and SWIFT at industry forums and social meetings. Although the <sup>fi</sup>rst author was not involved in any STP-related projects in her own company, the continuous interactions with professionals within the mutual fund industry allows her to follow the STP development in Taiwan.

The formal <sup>fi</sup>eld study started in July 2008 when the <sup>fi</sup>rst author took an academic position in a university in Taiwan. Because of the strong network established and the industry knowledge acquired in her previous work environment, she can quickly identify the key participants and collect our primary data via semi-structured interviews. Our interviewees include banks in Taiwan, European investment fund companies’ of<sup>fi</sup>ces in Hong Kong, STP solution providers in Hong Kong and Taiwan, Taiwanese government bodies and banking associations in Taiwan.

The focus of the data collection was to inquire about discourses related to adoption decisions, implementation incentives and barriers for STP, banking system usage, fund trading operations, and data exchange associated with fund transaction. Most of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork was carried out in the interviewees’ of<sup>fi</sup>ces in Hong Kong or Taiwan, with a few meetings conducted at nearby cafes or cafeterias, as requested by the informants. Managers were interviewed or followed by e-mails several times when there was a new development of STP adoption activity. The majority of the earlier interviews were recorded by taking extensive <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, to allow for openness and trust building. Majority of interviews afterwards were tape recorded and subsequently transcribed. The <sup>fi</sup>eldwork was temporarily suspended between early 2010 and late 2010 where there was no STP adoption taking place. The interview resumed in late 2010 when a global infomediary entered the market with the proposal of new STP solution.

During the course of the study, over 60 face-to-face interviews were conducted. In addition to face-to-face interviews with individuals, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author was also allowed to participate as observant in a number of regular meetings in which managers from mutual fund companies discussed and shared their thoughts on STP development in Taiwan. Secondary data used in this study include data collected from various market sources, such as industry conferences, presentations, workshops, and newsletters. We also reviewed the memorandum of understanding signed by the Taiwan Fund Operation Group (TFOG) relating to the implementation of STP by participants in the Taiwan funds market, and copies of various Service Level Agreements used by different STP vendors.

This broad range of sources allowed us to gain multiple perspectives on the topic (Eisenhardt, 1989).

For data analysis, in early stage, we started with the development of major events, such as dates of industry forum or workshops, formation of industry working group, and dates of adoption by banks in Taiwan, as indicated in Table 2. Once the key events were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, we began with the development of narratives from our textual data including interviews and secondary data with a focus on the contextual elements and other factors relevant to the STP adoption at different point of time. In the analytic phase, although we consider the value of qualitative analysis software, we decided to follow the approach suggested by Walsham (2006) to categorize and code manually the empirical data in accordance with three institutional pillars and their corresponding mechanisms of isomorphism. Following that, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings were organized into two different cultural settings, that is, Europe and Taiwan. This process allowed us to compare and contrast the different institutional factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of STP between Europe and Taiwan. To further validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, another co-author independently reviewed the empirical data, and ensured the validity and reliability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings generated from the coding process. The <sup>fi</sup>nal step was to discuss the case <sup>fi</sup>ndings and analysis with interviewees to ensure the validation of our interpretation of the case. We consider that this is essentially an iterative process with the purpose of meeting the principles put forward by Klein & Myers (1999). ## Case description

Investment funds As illustrated in Figure 1, a mutual fund is a form of investment scheme set up by fund companies to raise money through the sale of shares in the fund to the investing public. Fund managers would then invest the money raised in a variety of <sup>fi</sup>nancial products such as stocks, bonds, and other money market instruments on behalf of the shareholders. There are three main distribution channels through which the public can purchase shares in a mutual fund: (1) from the fund companies directly, (2) via a ‘supermarket’ platform offering a variety of funds to investors, or (3) through brokerage <sup>fi</sup>rms, insurance companies, or banks. Investors can place orders to either purchase or redeem shares in a fund based on fund performance and their individual risk appetite. Different funds may be offered for sale in different countries and so investors may be located in or outside the country in which a fund is established. In this case study, we focus on mutual funds domiciled in Europe that are available for subscription to investors located in both Europe and Taiwan. To the extent that funds are domiciled outside Taiwan but are available for subscription by Taiwanese investors, they constitute ‘offshore funds’ for the purposes of Taiwanese regulations. The majority of European investment fund companies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed Taiwan as one of their most important offshore markets. According to the Taiwan Securities Investment Trust & Consulting Association, the offshore fund market in Taiwan comprised 1018 funds with an aggregate asset value of U.S.\$72 billion as of November 2011. More recently, an article in the Financial Times (of 3 April 2011) reported that Taiwan was considered the most pro<sup>fi</sup>table and the strongest funds market in Asia.

Order routing between distributing agents in Taiwan (e.g., banks, insurance companies, or supermarket platform providers) and investment fund companies have traditionally been heavily reliant on manual processes via fax. In the case of a normal order routing from a Taiwanese bank and a European investment fund company, the bank would normally have a cut-off time at 3:30 pm for receiving instructions from local investors in order to allow for suf<sup>fi</sup>cient time for its back-of<sup>fi</sup>ce to aggregate and process those orders. The bank’s staff would have to manually enter each order into its systems and then to fax the orders to the investment fund company or its representative transfer agent for further processing. Depending on the daily transaction volumes, the time required to complete this process can vary widely. Having received the orders, some investment fund companies may provide the bank with an order con<sup>fi</sup>rmation the next day, while others may require the bank to make a follow-up phone call to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the transmission of orders. Indeed, the mounting cross-border trading volumes brought pressures on participants to implement STP in the securities market, including those in the investment fund industry, who are on the buy-side of the securities market. The pursuit of STP adoption in the investment fund industry also resulted in various institutional changes and technological developments in Europe and the United States. For example, global custodians, investment managers, and broker–dealers set up the Global Straight through Processing Association (GSTPA) in 1998, while the European Fund and Asset Management Association (EFAMA) established the Fund Processing Standardization Group (FPSG) in 2003. Following these developments in Europe, investment fund companies and SWIFT began discussions with Taiwanese banks concerning the possibility of implementing and extending the STP solution to automate cross-border fund transactions since the existing practice of using fax for order routing was inef<sup>fi</sup>cient and error-prone. European investment fund companies and SWIFT began promoting STP to Taiwanese banks in the early 2000s. In contrast to the high adoption rate in Europe, efforts in Taiwan only saw the <sup>fi</sup>rst adoption in 2006, followed by the second adoption in early 2007, two more in 2008 and another one in early 2009. Since then, there was no further STP adoption among Taiwanese banks. It was not until late 2010, interests in Taiwan for adoption began to emerge again when a global infomediary entered the Taiwanese market with the proposal for adopting a hub-based STP model based on the ISO 20022 standard. This new STP model attracted several banks to begin the testing phase. However, a supervisory intervention in mid-2011 caused a temporary suspension of the global infomediary’s services in Taiwan. Nevertheless, as of the time when this empirical study was completed in early 2012, the supervisory authority had already published guidelines that allowed the global infomediary to resume services in the provision of offshore fund processing, provided it worked with a local infomediary for the provision of offshore fund processing. Currently, additional <sup>fi</sup>ve banks have implemented this system in 2012 and 2013. Our contact from the global infomediary informed us that a number of other banks are under discussion for future implementation.

## Case findings and analysis

## The institutional environment for initiating STP adoption in Europe and Taiwan

As discussed above, there are differences between the business environments in Europe and Taiwan that have affected the STP adoption rate seen in each location. Here our analysis demonstrates how variances in regulatory rules, normative values, and cultural beliefs in these two different institutional settings in<sup>fl</sup>uence the early organizational adoption of STP innovation, as summarized in Table 4. In doing so, we can explain why the rates of diffusion within the European investment fund industry and cross-culturally between European investment fund companies and Taiwanese banks have been so different.

First, with respect to the regulatory pillar, we observed that European investment fund companies face much more supervisory pressure compared with their Taiwanese counterparts. Of regulatory concerns, one is the implementation of the Basel II framework and the other is the adoption by the European Parliament of a resolution on asset management. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, formed by a group of central banks, is responsible for the coordination of prudential banking supervision issues across the globe. The Committee has implemented a supervisory framework to determine, among other things, the minimum regulatory capital requirements for banks. The objective was to ensure that banks have an adequate capital to cushion the effects of unexpected losses. In the early 2000s, the committee published a revised standard known as ‘Basel II’ that introduced a minimum capital requirement for operational risk. Basel II’s guidelines on operational risk management required banks to reduce risks arising from the delivery, execution, and process management stages associated with <sup>fi</sup>nancial transactions. In Europe, European banking regulators have included other <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions, such as investment banks or major brokerage <sup>fi</sup>rms in enforcing the Basel II compliance. These European <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions consider STP as the technological solution to mitigate operational risk by automating the transaction process and eliminating any manual intervention.

Separately, the investment fund industry in Europe also faced speci<sup>fi</sup>c regulatory pressure from the European Parliament. The Parliament adopted a resolution on asset management in 2007, in which it clearly states that the commission should take action if the industry does not substantially progress in greater use of electronic and standardized fund processing by the end of 2009 (EFAMA, 2008). As one representative from an investment fund company observed, these (regulatory requirements) can be an important driver for STP, especially for back-office operation.

In contrast to the proactive role of supervisory authorities in Europe, our observations show that there was a lack of a comparable in<sup>fl</sup>uence from Taiwanese supervisory authorities in two respects. The <sup>fi</sup>rst relates to the Basel II minimum capital requirement for operational risk. The Basel committee had provided guidance on three different methods of capital calculation. Regulators in different countries have the discretion to make an independent decision regarding which method is required for <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions under its supervision. In Europe, majority of investment fund companies are required to comply with Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA). Institutions adopting AMA will need to undergo an exercise of collecting internal loss data and evaluating the effectiveness of existing controls in operational risk mitigation. As discussed above, this led to investment fund company in favor for STP innovation with the objective of operational risk reduction. By contrast, supervisory authority in Taiwan opts for either Basic Indicator Approach or Standardized Approach for banks’ minimum capital requirement calculation. Although these two approaches still recommend the implementation of sound internal governance, there is a less stringent requirement on internal loss data collection. As a result, banks in Taiwan did not see the urgent need to deploy STP innovation. In addition, in comparison with active support for STP from European regulators, our interviews with bank managers and members from supervisory bureau indicate that Taiwanese regulator seems having held a more neutral attitude towards STP innovation. As one interviewee from the funds association told us:

In general the supervisory authority has a positive attitude towards any practices that increase market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, but it seems impossible for the supervisory authority to go any step further by giving a formal endorsement on fund automation for offshore funds.

Second, on the issue of normative values, our analysis of empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveals several different practices between the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industries in Europe and in Taiwan. As described in our ‘Theoretical Framework’ section, the normative pillar relates to the compliance by an organization with the social obligations under which it operates. One important aspect is the process of socialization and networking activities. Our empirical data shows that within the European investment fund industry, certain professional associations and networks focusing on the development of STP for fund transaction automation were established, such as the GSTPA in 1998, the FPSG in 2003, and the ISSA Mutual Funds Working Group in 2007. For STP technology standardization, SWIFT had invited its members to form the ISO 20022 Advisory Group with the aim of developing data standards for fund transactions. Members of these working groups consisted mainly of European and U.S. <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions. These working groups published numerous reports, guidelines, and surveys. At the same time, there was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant media coverage promoting STP for fund transaction automation, such as that provided by the Financial Times and the Wall Street Journal. These events collectively generated high publicity for STP and contributed to the building of knowledge and consensus among professionals within the European investment fund industry.

While there was active professional engagement in discussions over STP in Europe, less activity was observed in Taiwan. Our empirical results demonstrate that the majority of activities on STP were in fact initiated by the European investment fund companies and SWIFT. For instance, SWIFT together with some of the larger European investment fund companies invited several major Taiwanese banks to form the TFOG in 2003, and European fund investment companies arranged for their Asian of<sup>fi</sup>ces to set up the Asia Fund Automation Consortium (AFAC) in 2006. SWIFT also held a number of Fund Automation Masterclass conferences between 2004 and 2008. The then regional head of SWIFT told us,

I realize that we need to establish a platform where everyone can come together to discuss the issue of standardization and implementation. As for the conference, it’s another platform that we’ve established to educate our users about the concept of STP ... [for them] to realize the importance of STP.

When we searched for coverages on STP innovation in the local media in Taiwan, we found little information available other than press coverage of the times during which the Fund Automation Masterclass conferences were taking place. This was very different from the wide extent of coverage in the European media.

Finally, the third pillar focuses on compliance with the cultural–cognitive domain of an institutional environment. In our analysis, we focused on the business culture associated with investment funds and SWIFT. First, we saw a variance in the investment culture for this particular type of <sup>fi</sup>nancial instrument. In Europe, as a representative at one investment fund company summarized, people in Europe tend to consider mutual funds as the investment option for retirement or long-term purposes. Thus, the common practice in Europe is for funds to be purchased based on investment advice from <sup>fi</sup>nancial advisors at brokerage <sup>fi</sup>rms or banks, or through a pension fund scheme. This constitutes the wholesale asset management side of fund investment, which is characterized by higher values for each transaction and lower turnover rates. In contrast, our interviewees from the investment fund industry in Taiwan revealed that there is a perception locally that funds are for short-term trading, with individual retail investors dominating the market. One research report in the Asian Investment Managers Guide in December 2010 highlights in the following words the business culture in Taiwan associated with funds:

Because of the appetite for risk among Taiwanese investors, they are happy to support new fund launches. However, they have a tendency to treat funds more like stocks and actively buy and sell them as they would shares. They also prefer to manage their own investment portfolios rather than delegating the responsibility to an adviser.

Furthermore, the investment fund industry in Taiwan has a larger number of participants. Together with the short-term trading culture and the retail nature of interests in these funds, we see higher volume of fund transactions spread across a large number of investment funds in Taiwan.

The second aspect of business culture is associated with the organizational use of the SWIFT system. As mentioned earlier, the SWIFT system has been the backbone of <sup>fi</sup>nancial messaging services in providing automation and standardization to transaction processing amongst <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions. To replace the reliance on fax, SWIFT worked with a group of European and U.S. <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions to develop the ISO 20022 standard for funds. Because of the wholesale nature of the asset management business in Europe, fund managers and other <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>rms naturally came to consider SWIFT as a good candidate to collaborate with for the development of an STP solution. On the other hand, the retail market in Taiwan did not need to use much the usage of SWIFT and that generated the lack of understanding of SWIFT for bank managers who is responsible for the handling and processing of fund transaction. As one local manager working at an international bank commented,

This has to do with how local banks use and perceived SWIFT. The system was only used and located in international banking department. Only a handful of people can access system and the system is mainly used for remittance. So when people at the local bank heard about SWIFT, they can associate it with money, not as messaging standard! In Europe, SWIFT message is a common thing for users in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions. For example, for us working in the international bank (but located in Taiwan), we just think SWIFT as an encrypted e-mail message for business communication.

Put together, our analyses above present the discrepancy and difference for each institutional pillar in Europe and Taiwan conditioning the STP adoption and diffusion. Our empirical results reveal that in Europe, there was a strong presence of all three institutional pillars in supporting the STP diffusion. This was evident with tougher <sup>fi</sup>nancial regulatory requirements from Basel II and the push from the European Parliament for automation; the closer network of <sup>fi</sup>nancial professionals involved in STP development; and the wholesale asset management culture and the market’s familiarity with the SWIFT system. In contrast, the force of the institutional pillars in Taiwan is relatively weak as evidenced by the lack of regulatory backing; the thin press coverage and little professional interest in STP; and the culture of retail investors, together with unfamiliarity with the SWIFT system.

Furthermore, we found during our research that, in addition to institutional differences, the variations in the economic costs of adoption, in particular the disparity between rental fees and labor costs, might also help to explain why adoption rates in Europe were higher than in Taiwan. In Europe, the high labor costs and rental fees for manual processing facilitates the offer of an additional incentive for implementing STP innovation since the automation and standardization of fund processing can ultimately increase processing ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and achieve cost reduction. The already extensive use of the SWIFT system also meant that STP adoption was much less costly since it was a mere extension to their existing systems, and not an investment in entirely new IT. On the other hand, for Taiwanese banks, the lack or infrequent use of the SWIFT system and relatively cheap local labor costs made STP a relatively unattractive investment. One Taiwanese bank manager explained the cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts concerns as follows:

It would cost us NTD5 million to install the [STP] system plus the messaging fees, but our fax system only cost NTD10, 000. We ask whether it is a worthwhile investment.

As a result, while the STP adoption rate for fund transactions was much higher in Europe, the adoption and diffusion rate for offshore fund transactions between European investment fund companies and Taiwanese banks remained low and sluggish.

## Institutional isomorphism to generate cross-cultural STP adoption

As we pointed out in the ‘Theoretical Framework’ section earlier, institutional theorists argue that in order to increase the legitimacy of innovation in an institutional setting, organizations can deploy three different mechanisms (coercive, normative and mimetic isomorphism) to achieve greater compliance with each respective institutional pillar. In our analysis of empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we have indicated that European investment fund companies and SWIFT have attempted to increase the strength of these three mechanisms in order to encourage cross-cultural STP adoption. While some have been more successful, others have failed, as we detail below.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst look at the coercive factor. Our discussions with investment fund managers, SWIFT, and fund associations in Taiwan indicate that, in their view European fund managers consider that a stronger local regulatory regime would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in boosting the adoption rate among Taiwanese banks. To achieve this, both European investment fund managers and SWIFT tried at an early stage to arrange various meetings with regulators and industry selfregulatory associations to explain the nature and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the SWIFT system. One local bank manager who had been a member of the automation committee of one such association stated that although the market generally supported the concept of automation, no formal action was being taken by the committee or supervisory authorities, and they failed to consider the implementation of STP as an important and mandatory matter for their members. In other words, there was no material change in the attitude of regulators and in the industry despite the efforts of the European investment fund companies and SWIFT.

The second strategy to increase institutionalization and legitimacy is through normative isomorphism. This is the process of social networking and education among organizational professionals. As seen in our analysis in the <sup>fi</sup>rst section under ‘Case Findings and Analysis’, for educational purposes, SWIFT had organized a series of Fund Automation Masterclass conferences and invited speakers from European investment fund companies and STP service providers to share their implementation experiences and, in particular, to highlight the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and advantages of adoption. In addition, they also made numerous visits to individual banks to promote and discuss the potential adoption of STP. In the context of normative isomorphism, such actions paid off in both increasing awareness and paving the way for STP adoption. The manager of the <sup>fi</sup>rst bank to adopt STP told us that their decision to adopt STP was primarily the outcome of visits from managers at SWIFT. Another bank manager mentioned that he <sup>fi</sup>rst received information about STP from AFAC. To champion the cause further, SWIFT also subsequently engaged managers from the <sup>fi</sup>rst adopting bank to share their experiences and their bank’s success story of STP adoption through workshops and conferences. This strategy of bringing in local advocates helped to create a positive image for STP, to give more credence to the possible benefits of STP for Taiwanese banks. and to alleviate concerns about the practicalities of implementation in the local context. Similarly, we also observed that the global infomediary took an active approach in networking with local banks and educating the bank managers on the nature and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a hub-based STP model.

In addition, the company made a strategic decision to appoint a manager who is able to communicate in Mandarin. This has worked well in engaging the discussion from bank managers for the development and adoption of this hub-based STP model.

Finally, organizations can also follow a strategic approach in order to increase legitimacy in the cultural– cognitive context. Our empirical analysis indicates that European investment fund companies and SWIFT attempted to make use of mimetic isomorphism to establish a sense of familiarity with STP innovation within the Taiwanese banking environment. The approach was <sup>fi</sup>rst applied through the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and selection of a leading group of local banks that were perceived to be most receptive to STP adoption. The objective was to create an imitation effect such that rival banks would seek to compete by also adopting STP. Mimetic in<sup>fl</sup>uence appears to have played an important role in shaping the early stage of cross-cultural STP adoption. As the manager from one adopting bank commented, Once we saw the successful implementations from both A and B banks, we decided to go ahead with such adoption. Besides creating an imitation effect, European investment fund companies and SWIFT also learnt that in order to promote STP successfully, and because of the retail-oriented investor base in Taiwan’s investment fund market and the intensity of competition among leading banks, there was a need to use alternative strategies which focused on better client services and increasing market competitiveness.

This view is supported by a manager at another adopting bank, who observed that since our routine work is now automated after STP adoption, we now have time to think ahead. We are planning for future ventures, not just catching up with current market trend.

In the late 2010, the global informediary took a similar tactic by identifying and approaching the leading banks among the existing non-adopting banks in order to generate the second wave of imitation effect. The mimetic pressure was evident in our observation, as the manager from the <sup>fi</sup>rst adopting bank for a hub-based model informed us that there were a number of enquiries from other banks about the progress of this STP project.

In summary, our analysis shows that together with normative isomorphism, the mimetic in<sup>fl</sup>uence also contributes to the organizational decision to adopt cross-cultural STP in Taiwan.

## Interplay between the institutional pillars and technological change

While our analysis focuses on the institutional pillars and the process of institutionalization through isomorphism, we have also discovered a dynamic relation existing between institutional conditions and technological development.

First, as we mentioned earlier, the SWIFT system had been utilized extensively among European <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions. Furthermore, SWIFT charges on a <sup>fi</sup>xed fee per message basis, which meant that the proportional cost for institutional trades was less since these trades tended to be higher value transactions. In contrast, the use of locally built IT systems in Taiwan was much more prevalent than use of the SWIFT system, which meant that Taiwanese banks faced higher costs in setting up a SWIFT-based STP system from scratch. Examples of such costs include costs relating to the consolidation of hardware and software with front-of<sup>fi</sup>ce applications, the implementation of SWIFT software, the establishment of a new server connecting to the SWIFT system, and testing. In addition, since the retail-oriented nature of the fund business in Taiwan saw a high volume of small-sized transactions, many banks found the <sup>fi</sup>xed fee structure set by SWIFT for the use of its system to be disproportionately and prohibitively high. In other words, technological barriers together with institutional resource constraints exacerbated the prospect of wide adoption of cross-cultural STP innovation beyond the <sup>fi</sup>ve banks that were able to surpass these challenges. Indeed, these <sup>fi</sup>ve banks were generally considered to be either more IT-savvy than the others, or to had more resources to invest. Since the <sup>fi</sup>fth adoption in 2009, we have been unable to <sup>fi</sup>nd any further signs of adoption among other banks until late 2010.

Second, we also see the development of international messaging standards having an impact on cross-cultural STP adoption. SWIFT developed a tactical solution in using existing ISO 15022 FIN messages to facilitate fund order placing and con<sup>fi</sup>rmations in 2002. At the same time, the ISO 20022 Advisory Group implemented a more comprehensive solution using the ISO 20022 standard, which is a XML-based message standard that covered not only the entire fund transaction order <sup>fl</sup>ow, but also transfers, statements management, pricing reports, and cash forecasting. Implementing this solution for cross-cultural fund transactions required a great amount of groundwork to be done in de<sup>fi</sup>ning, negotiating, and testing the common data format. A manager from the <sup>fi</sup>rst adopting bank in Taiwan recalled that the STP project took more than 1 year to become functional and most of the time during the set-up phase was spent working with investment fund companies in Europe on data format requirements and standards. Once the data format for cross-border fund transactions had been de<sup>fi</sup>ned, the availability of the data format and standards meant that the development and testing costs for subsequent adopting banks were greatly reduced. However, the removal of this cost barrier was evidently not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently material to persuade more banks in Taiwan to adopt STP.

The situation took a turn in late 2010 when a global infomediary began to offer a hub-based model for fund processing, providing services for data translation, transformation, and transmission. While the pre-existing technology meant using a one-to-one connection through the SWIFT network, this hub-based model worked by converting previously fax-based order <sup>fl</sup>ows into ISO 20022. Moreover, message transmissions to the hub are free of charge for the banks. In this new business model, the global infomediary agreed to cover the costs of initial technical development and implementation at local banks. In addition, the global infomediary has also made an agreement with major European investment fund companies that the transmission fees will only be applicable to the fund companies, not from local banks. As a result, it was reported that in 2011 several banks had already begun the testing phase for this new STP solution. However, it was at this time when adoption was gaining momentum that the Taiwanese supervisory authorities began to raise concerns about the security and privacy of data that was being transmitted via this hub model. Consequently, the supervisory authorities called for a temporary suspension of its use while they evaluated the model to see whether it should be permitted, and if so, whether it was necessary to impose relevant guidelines on its use. After several months, the supervisory authority published new regulatory guidelines that created a twotiered infomediary service. From the viewpoint of the supervisory authority, since the designated local infomediary has already been providing STP service for the processing of domestic mutual fund transactions, it would be a natural extension to include the local infomediary in the business model of offshore funds. Under these new guidelines, the hub model could be used by banks for automating offshore funds transactions, provided that they designated a local infomediary responsible for contracting with the global infomediary for the purposes of data transformation and transmission out of Taiwan. There are no new development requirements or costs for local banks since the global infomediary would work with the local bank to standardize the technical infrastructure and data format. As at the time of completion of this study, <sup>fi</sup>ve banks had begun to embark on the testing phase of this new STP solution, together with their local and global infomediaries, and expect it to become functional in the course of 2012 and 2013.

## Discussion and implications

Drawing on the analysis of the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings above, this section discusses their theoretical and practical implications.

## Implications for research

The results of this study contribute to the areas of research in the cross-cultural adoption of IOS, as well as the areas of research in the institutional perspective on innovation adoption more generally.

Cross-cultural adoption of IOS First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings lend support to other studies that consider the need to analyze cross-cultural adoption beyond Hosftede-type studies (Myers & Tan, 2002; Walsham, 2002) and beyond the innovation-centric approaches to IOS adoption (Robey et al, 2008). Walsham (2002) adopted a structural analysis to allow for a deeper examination of cross-cultural con<sup>fl</sup>ict and contradiction arising in the context of software production and use. In line with his arguments, we have demonstrated in this study the importance of structural properties and institutional pillars in shaping the crosscultural adoption of technology. Drawing on the institutional theory, this study provides empirical evidence to show that an organization’s decision to adopt innovation is subject not only to the social-cognitive belief in a social collectivity but also to the dimensions of regulatory pressures and normative values. That is, the reliance on a simplistic model of natural culture on technology adoption overlooks other structural properties that govern organizational practices in a society. Undertaking an analysis based on institutional theory allows us to gain a richer and more comprehensive understanding of ‘institutional distance’ in the context of cross-cultural IOS adoption, in comparison to a single-dimensional discussion on cultural homogeneity or heterogeneity. Kostova & Zaheer (1999) use the concept of institutional distance to explain the difference/similarity between the regulatory, cognitive and normative institutions of the two countries (p. 71). Although Kostova & Zaheer (1999) focused on the implications for the operational aspects of multinational enterprises, we believe that such a concept can similarly be applied to IOS adoption in multiple domains of institutional environments. As shown in this study, we can see that in Europe, where there are less institutional differences among countries, this is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in a higher STP adoption rate. Therefore, it is much easier to establish and maintain the legitimacy of STP solutions among investment fund companies and banks within Europe. The greater institutional distance between Europe and Taiwan means that European investment fund companies <sup>fi</sup>nd it more challenging to understand the structural properties and legitimacy requirements in Taiwan. Consequently, this larger institutional distance results in a slower adoption rate for crosscultural STP.

Second, previous studies have criticized Hosftede’s cultural typology for neglecting the dynamic process of intercultural interaction and negotiation (Brannen & Salk, 2000; Walsham, 2002). Indeed, institutional theorists recognize the dynamic and <sup>fl</sup>uid nature of the legitimation process in an institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld (Schuman, 1995; Zimmerman, 2002). Our analysis of institutional isomorphism indicates that, although institutional variances can present challenges for cross-cultural STP adoption, organizations can deploy various legitimation strategies to meet or alter the legitimacy requirements in a given institutional setting (Kaganer et al, 2010). Kaganer et al (2010) conclude in their study and suggest that there is a need to study the core legitimation strategies and the temporal patterns across different types of IT innovations and different industry settings (p. 26). Findings from our longitudinal case study reveal that at the outset, European investment fund companies and SWIFT had attempted to increase legitimacy in all three institutional pillars. However, the lack of formative responses from supervisory authorities in Taiwan led to the concentration of effort on normative and mimetic isomorphism. Our observations indicate that there was a wave of intensive application of normative isomorphism to build up the network of <sup>fi</sup>nancial professionals in Taiwan, which was gradually followed by the deployment of mimetic in<sup>fl</sup>uences among the leading group of Taiwanese banks. Within the domain of normative isomorphism, our empirical results further lend support to the movement of innovation discourse from ‘know-what’ and ‘know-why’ (e.g., the nature and bene<sup>fi</sup>t of STP) to ‘know-how’ (e.g., the implementation challenges and success of STP) (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Overall, from a theoretical perspective, our study highlights how organizations utilize legitimation strategies for crosscultural IOS adoption.

Third, through this study we have addressed the limitations of current IOS research which has predominantly been based on an organizational level of analysis (Rodon et al, 2008; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2010). Lyytinen & Damsgaard (2010) propose a theoretical framework of con<sup>fi</sup>guration analysis to bring order and clari<sup>fi</sup>cation to the multilevel and dynamic elements of research into IOS. They argue that for an IOS to be adopted, it must pass through a series of alternative adopter con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. In a similar vein, drawing on institutional theory, this study provides evidence to show that organizational decisions to adopt IOS are highly contingent on the structural properties of the institutional setting and the degree of success of legitimation strategies implemented by stakeholders or vendors in favor of STP adoption. In other words, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate how the structural properties at the national level can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the diffusion of a crosscultural IOS adoption at the industry level. For instance, the differences of regulatory requirements at the national level have a great in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the perceived needs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of adopting STP solution at the industry level in Europe and Taiwan. Furthermore, differences in industry structure and competitiveness affect the organizational decision to adopt IOS. As mentioned earlier, different business practices in Europe and in Taiwan have led to different interpretations of managers about the urgency and importance of STP adoption. At the organizational level, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that given the variances in national and industrial conditions in Europe and in Taiwan, <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions in these two contexts have thus reached different decisions for STP adoption. These empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to the theory explaining the dynamics and robustness of IOS diffusion across multiple levels.

An institutional perspective on technology innovation In their review of institutional perspective in IS research, Mignerat & Rivard (2009) call for the contribution from longitudinal studies on technology adoption and implementation research. They point out that longitudinal studies are particularly relevant to the study of institutional forces, since…pressures may different from one phase of the implementation process to another (p. 388). As shown in our case, the regulatory forces were different in early and later stage of STP implementation, and thus had different implications on the STP development in Taiwan. Furthermore, the longitudinal approach allows us to unfold the process of how different institutional force was developed and evolved. We believe that our process-oriented and historical research design made a theoretical contribution to the extant literature where most studies using cross-sectional methodologies with an objective to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the relevance or signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of institutional forces.

Furthermore, while the results of this study offer support to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of institutional pillars in shaping technology innovation, we also extend support to the argument that organizations have a choice to manage actively their relation with the institutional environment in which they operate (Pfeffer, 1982; Oliver, 1991). Other studies have highlighted the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of economic factors in moderating the institutional effect of an organization’s decision to adopt innovation (Ang & Cummings, 1997; Zinn et al, 1998; Hsu et al, 2012) such as the availability of resources or perceived gains in competitive advantage or production economics. In our analysis of the institutional pillars in the context of STP adoption in Europe and Taiwan, we found that, in addition to the strong presence of institutional pressure in Europe, the perceived gain in operational cost savings provided additional incentive for European investment fund companies to move STP adoption. We also see the importance of economic factors in distinguishing the group of the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>ve adopters from the rest of the banks in Taiwan. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that the <sup>fi</sup>ve adopting banks were considered to be either more IT-savvy or having more slack resources. IT-savvy banks portray themselves as being more innovative and competitive in supporting ITenabled business initiatives, and hence an investment in STP is perceived as the pathway to increasing responsiveness to client needs and gaining a competitive advantage. Those with more resources tend to be the banks with a higher degree of state-ownership, and can afford to invest in innovation more than others do. Similarly, we observe signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic incentive for local banks to adopt the hub-based STP model since there are no development costs and message transmission fees. By saving the costs for local banks, the willingness of the potential adopters within the Taiwan banking community increases greatly.

The empirical analysis for this study further illustrates that the adoption of a cross-cultural IOS is a process that involves strategic response to the institutional and technological changes. Barley & Tolbert (1997) posit that institutions do change over time, thus they create forces for and against change in a social setting. Others also suggest that IT can enable and facilitate structural changes (Orlikowski & Robey, 1991; Orlikowski, 2000). Davidson & Chismar (2007) demonstrate the interplay of institutional and technology-triggered change on the social structure of a hospital after the initiation of the CPOE project. They conclude that while their observations on the mutual in<sup>fl</sup>uence of institutional and technology-triggered change are at the organization level, they anticipate a similar interaction to exist across the U.S. health sector. They call for further investigation into the possibilities of such interplay at the industry level. As shown in the ‘Case Findings and Analysis’ section, we offer evidence to illustrate that both institution-triggered and technology-triggered change can shape and reshape the cross-cultural IOS diffusion process at the industry level. For technologytriggered change, we have shown that standardization in <sup>fi</sup>nancial messaging, such as ISO 20022, in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry and later the development of STP via hub model offer business opportunities for the global infomediary in the Taiwan offshore fund industry. As shown in Table 5, comparing with the model of direct connection to SWIFT network, the global infomediary reduces the technical barriers and costs to implement fund order automation system. This led to an increase in adoption for STP. Nonetheless, the growing popularity of global infomediary brought concerns for regulators regarding the security and privacy of data transmission. As a result, the regulator triggered an institutional change by enforcing the requirement of including a local infomediary to the original hubbased connection. Given that both health and <sup>fi</sup>nancial sectors are heavily regulated, we anticipate that additional studies in other less regulated industries can further deepen our understanding of the interaction between institutional-triggered and technology-triggered changes during the process of technology innovation.

## Practical implications

The empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study show that managers need to evaluate their organization’s decision to adopt cross-cultural IOS, and to do so in ways that go beyond the ones advocated under the dominant innovation theory and Hosftede’s culture typology. In the context of crosscultural IOS diffusion, elements of institutional properties, such as regulatory requirements and local industry norms, can represent a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenge, con<sup>fl</sup>ict, or contradiction to adoption. In this case, technology vendors and early adopters need to be mindful of the dynamic institutional structures, and be ready to react appropriately in light of these institutional variances.

Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that supervisory authorities play an equally important role in the crosscultural IOS adoption process. The results from this study demonstrate that differences in the expectations and standards of governments, and the nature of the guidelines they implement, can in<sup>fl</sup>uence an organization’s decision to adopt and the steps an organization is required to take during the technology diffusion process. Such differences can be more problematic and challenging for IOSs that connect organizations in a number of different countries. Indeed, it would seem highly bene<sup>fi</sup>- cial to a cross-cultural IOS adoption process if there is a greater discussion and collaboration among the regulatory agencies in different countries. ## Conclusions

This study contributes to a less researched, yet increasingly important, area of cross-cultural IOS adoption. We see limitations and shortcomings in the existing literature on innovation adoption in explaining the multifaceted challenges faced by organizations desiring to expand connectivity to their counterparts located in different geopolitical settings. Drawing on institutional and organizational legitimacy theory, our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of institutional properties in an organization’s adoption of a cross-cultural IOS, and the strategic response of organizational action in the presence of institutional variations in different countries. For future research, our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of institutional properties and legitimation strategies for crosscultural IOS adoption. We encourage further studies to be undertaken on the nature and temporal pattern of legitimation strategies for other types of cross-cultural IOS adoption in different industry settings. In addition, we found the dynamic interactions between institutional and technological change during the innovation diffusion process. Further studies can contribute to our understanding of the linkage between these forces at the industry level. We also see limitation of this research, we acknowledge that continuing a longitudinal research will further enrich our understanding of how institutional forces shape, and are reshaped by, IOS adoption in a crosscultural setting. Furthermore, our empirical setting is on <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry in which regulation plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cance role in adoption decision. We believe that a relevant study would be applying the institutional theory in other industry settings, and examine the extent to which potential adopters consider regulatory pillar and coercive isomorphism as the criterion for adoption.
