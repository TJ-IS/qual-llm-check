# A study of information systems issues, practices, and leadership in Europe

Leon Kappelman<sup>a</sup>, Vess Johnson <sup>b</sup>, Russell Torres<sup>a</sup>, Chris Maurer<sup>c</sup> and Ephraim McLean<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of North Texas, Information Technology & Decision Sciences Department, Dallas, USA; <sup>b</sup>University of Arkansas, Business Information Systems Department, Little Rock, USA; <sup>c</sup>University of Virginia, Information Technology Department, McIntire School of Commerce, Charlottesville, USA; <sup>d</sup>Georgia State University, Computer Information Systems Department, Robinson College of Business, Atlanta, USA

## ABSTRACT

In the summer of 2017, an online survey was administered to European IT executives, asking them about their concerns, spending, investments, cloud usage, security, workforce, reporting relationships, and other issues of importance. A total of 276 organisations responded. Their top-<sup>fi</sup>ve most important IT management concerns were: Alignment, Digital Transformation, Cybersecurity, Cost, and Business Agility. Similarly, their top-<sup>fi</sup>ve largest IT investments were: Analytics, Software Development, Cloud, Cybersecurity, and ERP.

The average age of the CIOs was 49, 41% reported to their CEO, and nearly two-thirds interacted with their CEO at least weekly. Almost three-quarters came from previous IT positions, but only a 16% from IT within their current organisations. On average, CIOs spend about 20% of their time interacting with members of the C-suite, about 26% with their IT employees, and about 16% with other corporate employees. IT spending was up nearly 5% over the previous year and averaged almost 7% of corporate revenues. Over twothirds of companies reported the number of IT employees and salaries were both up and spending on Cloud also increased.This paper explores these and other <sup>fi</sup>ndings and their implications for IT management practice. Comparisons with US -based organisations are also included.

## 1. Introduction

The long-running dialog within the information systems (IS) academic research community regarding the virtues of both rigour and relevance point to the need for high-quality research <sup>fi</sup>ndings that can be applied to problems encountered by IS practitioners (Sidorova, Evangelopoulos, Torres, & Johnson, 2013). While conceptually easy, the practical di<sup>fi</sup>culties associated with gaining access to IS executives often limits our ability to provide normative guidance to practitioners. However, well-designed and executed practitioner-oriented research, that can successfully engage a large number of IS leaders, can provide the IS academic community with insights to help us better prepare our students and bene<sup>fi</sup>t the practitioners and organisations they serve and that we study.

This paper reports the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a study of information technology (IT) management issues and practices as reported by European IT executives. This study was conducted as an extension of a practice-oriented research study, conducted in the United States (US) since 1980, which reports on a wide range of IT concerns, investments, skills, management practices, and leadership activities.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we provide a brief discussion of the research method employed. Next, we present our <sup>fi</sup>ndings organised by issues and concerns, skill needs, investments, IT spending and practices including workforce, performance measures, and Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) demographics and activities.

Please note that (1) all comparisons to the US use Kappelman et al. (2018) as their source, and (2) “IS” and $^ { \mathfrak { c } } \mathrm { I T } ^ { \mathfrak { p } }$ are used as synonyms in this article.

## 2. Research method

This research was conducted by means of an online <sup>fi</sup>eld survey of senior IS executives. Each participating executive served as a key informant, providing factual information and personal perspectives about themselves and their organisations. The use of key informants is consistent with current research practice and is a commonly-used strategy for gaining insight about organisational issues (e.g., Queiroz, 2017).

Two strategies were used to contact prospective respondents in Europe. First, two organisations with strong connections to European IT leaders, CIONET and the Business & IT Trends Institute (BITTI), agreed to distribute the survey within their networks. CIONET is a community of European IT executives and BITTI is a research and benchmarking institute that studies IT issues in Europe. Second, a snowball sampling technique (Goodman, 1961) was employed in which the members of the Society for Information Management (SIM), a US-based organisation of IT leaders, were asked to distribute the survey to members of their professional networks located in Europe.

## 3. Research <sup>fi</sup>ndings

## 3.1. Top IT management issues and concerns

Participants were presented with a list of 41 IT management concerns from which they selected up to <sup>fi</sup>ve issues they considered most important to their organisation. The list was initially developed using a Delphi-group comprised of senior IT leaders in the US (see Kappelman, Mclean, Luftman, & Johnson, 2013 for details). In subsequent years, the list was modi<sup>fi</sup>ed through additions based on suggestions from study participants and the removal of items with very low selection rates (Kappelman, Mclean, Johnson, & Gerhart, 2014; Kappelman, Mclean, Johnson, & Torres, 2016; Kappelman et al., 2018, 2017). Figure 1 presents the ten most frequently selected by the most senior IT leader in each of the 276 European organisations.

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variation among the top-ten most important IT management issues is indicative of the complex environment in which IT leaders must operate. As expected, there are issues related to IT operational e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness such as “Cost Reduction/Cost Controls-IT” and “Agility/ Flexibility-IT”. However, these IT management issues extend far beyond the boundaries of the IT function and re<sup>fl</sup>ect the use of IT to increase organisational productivity, reduce time to market, and fundamentally alter the business. The top rank of “Alignment of IT with the Business” along with the other businessoriented issues in the top ten, points to the organisational importance of IT. To examine how important IT management issues might vary with organisational size, the data were separated into quartiles based on revenue. Table 2 presents the rankings for the top-<sup>fi</sup>ve items by quartiles.

While no discernible pattern is evident for some items, “Alignment of IT with the Business” appears to be a less pressing concern for larger organisations. Conversely, large organisations appear to be more concerned about cybersecurity and privacy issues than smaller ones. This makes sense given that large organisations are more appealing attack targets. It is noteworthy that “Digital Transformation” ranked high across all quartiles. Because the research questionnaire did not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally de<sup>fi</sup>ne “Digital Transformation”, it is unclear to what extent respondents had or transformation in mind, or some combination thereof (e.g., Besson & Rowe, 2012).

## 3.2. Technology investments and worrisome technologies

Each IT leader was also asked to select up to <sup>fi</sup>ve technologies, from a list of 37, in each of three separate categories: (1) the organisation’s largest current or near-term IT investments; (2) technologies that should get more investment; and (3) technologies of greatest personal concern $( ^ { \infty } \mathrm { { i . e . } , }$ they keep you up at night”).

## <sup>3.2.1.</sup> Organisations’ largest IT investments

Figure 3 presents the ten largest IT investments as identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by 219 IT executives. Large expenditures in “Business Analytics” and related technologies are consistent with expanding global interest in data-driven decision making. The relatively high rank of “Application Software Development/Maintenance” was unexpected given the growth of cloud and commercial products intended to reduce the need for custom development. However, 57 participants provided additional insight revealing that “Integration” and “Legacy System Enhancement” received the largest share of these investments. Overall, investments appear to favour operational over strategic concerns.

## <sup>3.2.3.</sup> IT workforce skills: the most important and most di<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd

Workforce skills are critically important to the success of the organisation and its IT initiatives. Participating IT leaders were asked to select up to <sup>fi</sup>ve skills they deemed most di<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd and the most important to their organisation from each of two separate lists: “technical skills or capabilities” and “soft skills or personal attributes”. As shown in Table 6, there is considerable agreement between the skills identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as most di<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd and those believed to be most important for organisational success. The correspondence between these lists suggests a critical shortage of quali<sup>fi</sup>ed IT talent. In general, technical skills on both lists align well with both IT issues and spending (Tables 3 and 5), with considerable importance placed on speci<sup>fi</sup>c skills related to “Security/Cybersecurity”, “Analytics”, and “ERP”. However, more generally applicable technical skills related to “Architecture”, “Business Analysis”, “Agile Development”, and “Project Management” are highly sought after too.

These results are consistent with those in the US, with security, analytics, and business analysis occupying the top three positions and a heavy interest in architectural skills too. ## <sub>3.3.4.3.</sub> IT workforce turnover and retirements.

Human resource concerns are of critical importance to many IT leaders (Figure 2). Employee turnover can result from an organisation’s action or an unexpected employee departure. To gauge this, each participant was asked the percentage of voluntary and involuntary turnover their organisation experienced in the prior 12 months. For the 70 reporting organisations, 68.7% of the departures were voluntary, resulting from quitting, retirement, or death; and, 31.3% involuntary and related to organisation actions such as downsizing, layo<sup>f</sup>s, and terminations.

Participants were also asked to estimate the percentage of IT employees that will retire within the next <sup>fi</sup>ve years. The 116 organisations responding indicated that, on average, 5.4% of their IT workforce is expected to retire within that timeframe. Approximately 87% of responding organisations estimated that less than 10% of their employees would retire within the next <sup>fi</sup>ve years. Surprisingly, almost 34% of the responding organisations claimed that they expected no retirements at all within this period.

In terms of reporting relationships, 91.2% of European CIOs report to individuals at the C-level or above; 40.9% report to the CEO, 20.4% to the CFO, 15.3% to a Board member, and 14.6% to the COO. Only 89.0% of CIOs in the US report at the C-level or above (41.4% to the CEO, 28.1% to the CFO, 19.0% to the COO, and only 0.5% to a Board member. The much smaller percentage of US CIOs reporting at the Board-level (0.5% vs. 15.3% in Europe) is at least in part a function of the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the role and makeup of boards between the two regions (a very interesting subject, but beyond the scope of this article)(Kappelman et al., 2018). Nevertheless, in both the US and Europe only about 10% of CIOs report below the senior executive level (i.e., C-level or Board), indicating a high-degree of organisational visibility for the IT function.

## <sup>3.5.1.</sup> CIO previous employment

Before becoming the top IT executives in their current organisations, 72.8% of 148 responding CIOs were in a previous IT position. Only 16.3% came from within their current IT organisation, which is less than the 17.7% that came from non-IT positions in another organisation. Overall, 74.2% of CIOs came from outside their current organisation. Interestingly, these percentages are very similar to those found in the US (Kappelman et al., 2018). While this may indicate a lack of “bench strength” or well-developed succession plans in these organisations and their IT departments, but it may also point to a general dissatisfaction by senior management and members of the Board with their prior IT leadership.

## <sup>3.5.2.</sup> With whom CIOs spend their time

The job of the CIO is complex and involves interaction with people both inside and outside the organisation. Figure 14 shows the average percentage of a CIO’s time spent with di<sup>f</sup>erent groups of people. On average, with 85 responding, these European CIOs spent well over twice as much of their time with employees inside their organisations as with those from other organisations (61.1% vs. 25.7%). Moreover, on average, they spent a total of 43.5% of their time with IT people and 60.7% of that with IT personnel from within their own organisations (26.4% of their time). This is similar to CIOs in the US.

CIOs were also asked about the frequency of their interactions with C-level executives and Board members; speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, if they met daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annually with them. A majority of those responding have at least weekly interactions with their C-level peers (i.e., CEO 64.2%, CFO 71.7%, COO 73.0%, CMO (52.8%), with the exception of the Chief Legal O<sup>fi</sup>cer (33.3%) and a Board member (49.3%).

## 4. Conclusion

This article reports on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of practitioneroriented research intended to document contemporary IT management issues and practices from the perspective of European IT executives. Using survey results from 276 unique organisations, each represented by a senior IT leader, we describe the complex environment in which IT professionals work.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT leaders in Europe must simultaneously balance the needs of their own IT organisation, often much of it outsourced, against those of the greater organisation, and make both IT operational and enterprise business decisions, while performing to a set of often contradictory metrics. Our data also suggests that the role of the CIO is both more complicated and more business focused than one might think. Consider that CIOs play an important part in the overall functioning of the business, spend almost all their time with a variety of people in their own and other organisations (Figure 14), meet quite frequently with their C-suite peers (Section 3.5.2), perform a complex assortment of tasks (Section 3.5.3), and are evaluated by a plethora of both IT and business performance measure (Table 16). Moreover, almost all CIOs on both sides of the Atlantic report at the C- or Board-level; a clear indication of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the CIO’s role and the high degree of importance and value placed on IT by the organisation (Section 3.5).

Notwithstanding this and many other similarities between European and US CIOs, there are some noteworthy di<sup>f</sup>erences. Cloud spending (Section 3.3.3) and cloud utilisation (Section 3.3.5.1) are both lower in Europe than the US. Despite the fact that organisations in both locales spend about half their IT budgets on people, European companies spend more of it on contractors and consultants (Section 3.3.3). European organisation appears to be ahead of the US in many cybersecurity-related practices (Section 3.3.6); and yet cybersecurity is rarely considered in evaluating the performance of European CIOs (Section 3.4). Moreover, cost-related performance metrics are more common than strategic ones for CIOs in Europe, while quite the opposite is true in the US. Better understanding <sup>fi</sup>ndings like these points to an important need for in-depth comparative research into the cultural, economic, legal, and other di<sup>f</sup>erences involved (e.g., Preston, Karahanna, & Rowe, 2006).

Nevertheless, we believe all this bodes well for IT leaders and their IT organisations, which are coming to be looked upon as partners their businesses rather than simply service providers their busi-<sup>to</sup>nesses. We hope that this report provides a sharper picture of, and perhaps in some small way helps, the IT executives who are successfully leading major European organisations as they face the challenges and the opportunities, both today and in the future.
