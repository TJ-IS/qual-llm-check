## Abstract

The mobile ecosystem has recently experienced a transition in platform leadership from network operators to mobile operating system providers. In each system the platform provider exerts effort in order to attract other <sup>fi</sup>rms for generativity and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. In this paper, we identify and analyze the working mechanism of one business practice that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences the ecosystem’s generativity and platform provider’s pro<sup>fi</sup>tability via value appropriation. Revenue sharing has become a common practice in the mobile ecosystem following NTT DoCoMo’s radical revenue-sharing model contributing toward mobile service success in Japan. Studies further argue that offering a wide portfolio of services through an attractive or innovative revenue-sharing model is one of key success factors in the mobile ecosystem. However, app developers have continuously claimed that they do not receive their fair share and the press reports a substantial number of disputes concerning revenue sharing between the platform provider and app developers. We propose a new bargaining model, the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed apex game, that investigates how value is likely to be appropriated between the platform provider and app developers within a given mobile platform mediated network. We support our theoretical predictions using data collected from the early mobile ecosystem by a network operator as well as the iOS and Android mediated networks.

## Introduction

A ccording to the layered modular architecture perspective of Yoo et al. (2010) in which there are four layers of devices, networks, services, and content such that a digitized product can be simultaneously a product in one layer Aand a platform in another, the mobile ecosystem has experienced at least two distinct digital product platform leadership models. Network operators (telecommunications operators) dominated the birth and evolution of the early mobile ecosystem via both logical transmission and physical transport layers. On the other hand, recent mobile ecosystems are represented by two contrasting digital product platforms: Apple’s iPhone serves as a platform at both the logical capability (iOS) and physical machinery (iPhone handset)

layers whereas Google’s Android serves as a platform at the logical capability layer only (Android mobile operating system).

Platform providers strive to attract heterogeneous actors in order to produce novel layer components outside of its digital product platform, directly affecting the generative potential of the entire architecture. Firms accordingly seek to build effective incentives for different <sup>fi</sup>rms to join their product platforms (Yoo et al., 2010). On the other hand, anecdotal evidence from the aforementioned representative mobile platform con<sup>fi</sup>gurations <sup>fi</sup>nd that a <sup>fi</sup>rm that successfully controls key digital product platform components is rewarded with the lion’s share of the created value.

Revenue sharing has become a common practice in the mobile ecosystem following the radical NTT DoCoMo revenue-sharing model’s contribution to the success of mobile services in Japan (Ahonen et al., 2004; Rajala et al., 2007). Studies further argue that offering a wide portfolio of services through an attractive or innovative revenue-sharing model is one of the key success factors in the mobile ecosystem (Olla and Patel, 2002). However, app developers continuously claim that they do not receive their fair share and there have been a substantial number of disputes regarding revenue sharing in the mobile ecosystem.

Although app developers occasionally claim that Apple should cut its share, Apple’s 30–70 revenue split model with app developers is relatively well accepted (Boudreau, 2008). As of February 2015 there are more than 370 thousand active app developers and more than 1.5 million apps are available in the App store.<sup>1</sup> However, several disputes concerning revenue sharing between Apple and app developers have been reported since Apple’s announcement that it would take the same 30% cut of the revenue generated from in-app subscriptions in 2011 (Pegoraro, 2011). App developers argue that Apple should take either a smaller or no cut since Apple’s contribution in selling a subscription is different from selling an app. As one of the largest app developers Microsoft refused to pay 30% of its SkyDrive subscriptions (within the app for additional storage space) and Apple responded by blocking SkyDrive updates (Reisinger, 2012). After approximately 10 months of dispute Microsoft chose to remove the option to purchase additional storage space within the app and released an update (Lardinois, 2013). The Financial Times also refused to pay 30% of its subscriptions and pulled its iOS apps from the App store completely (Ahmed, 2011).<sup>2</sup>

As illustrated by the NTT DoCoMo and Apple cases, a revenue-sharing arrangement often becomes a key component of a digital product platform that underlies a mobile ecosystem in both providing incentives for <sup>fi</sup>rms to join the mobile ecosystem as well as maintaining cooperative relationships. More fundamentally, ‘how the value appropriation among participants within an ecosystem is determined’ has been a fundamental research question in business strategy and organizational governance (MacDonald and Ryall, 2004). However, there is relatively little study investigating how this value is appropriated (or revenue is shared) among participants in the mobile ecosystem. Liu and Tang (2006) analyze the impact of a revenue-sharing model between a network operator and a content provider on prices, market shares, and social welfare. Chakravarty and Werner (2011) study the optimal structure of mobile portals on the revenue <sup>fl</sup>ows between mobile phone companies and service providers. Following the literature on platform economics, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the platform provider, app developers, and the MPMN as follows. With the continuous development of mobile technologies numerous new businesses have entered the mobile ecosystem, and some of these new businesses have substantially reshaped the ecosystem’s structure (Basole, 2009). While the early mobile ecosystem was built based on the network infrastructure including the gateway to mobile services (e.g., NTT DoCoMo’s i-mode), the recent mobile ecosystem heavily relies on the mobile operating system (e.g., Apple’s iOS) (Basole and Karla, 2011; Tilson et al., 2012). The mobile ecosystem has undergone a transition from platform leadership by players at the network layer to leadership by players at the device layer according to the layered modular architecture perspective. A platform is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a delivery system that enables value-added services to reach a consumer (Gawer and Cusumano, 2002; Gawer and Henderson, 2007).<sup>4</sup> The value of a platform without services is little or often zero (Cusumano, 2010), and is extended via services provided by third-party providers (Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005; Eisenmann et al., 2006, 2011). We accordingly de<sup>fi</sup>ne the network infrastructure including the gateway to mobile services as a platform in the early mobile ecosystem, the mobile operating system coupled with the handset device as a platform in Apple’s mobile ecosystem, and only the mobile operating system as a platform in Google’s mobile ecosystem. The network operator was also the platform provider in the earlier mobile ecosystem, and the mobile operating system developers are the platform providers in the recent mobile ecosystem. Notice that the platform provider leadership for the early mobile ecosystem (the network operator) comes from its dominance of the network layer, including both the logical transmission and physical transport layers, whereas the platform providers for the current mobile ecosystem derive their leadership from dominance of either the logical capability layer (mobile operating system) alone (Google) or both logical capability (iOS) and physical machinery layers (iPhone handset) of the device (Apple).

Apps refer to mobile services designed to support a platform. This includes mobile applications as well as various mobile content. Any entity that provides an app supporting a platform is therefore an app developer. Finally, we follow Eisenmann et al. (2008) in referring to the platform network (or platform provider) and apps (or app developers) designed to support the platform as the MPMN.<sup>5</sup> For instance, the iOS mediated network indicates the network of iOS devices (or Apple) and various iOS apps (or app developers) that support these iOS devices. We would like to note that the MPMN is a network unit to which revenue sharing (or value appropriation) is directly relevant.

As with many platform mediated networks (Eisenmann, 2006b; Gawer and Cusumano, 2008), the MPMN has one and only one platform provider, but a number of app developers. For instance, in the iOS mediated network Apple is the only platform provider, whereas there are more than 370 thousand active app developers as of February 2015.<sup>6</sup> In order to create a positive value the MPMN must have the platform and at least one app but not necessarily multiple apps; apps alone, regardless of number cannot create a positive value without the platform. In many cases an app can be substituted, not necessarily perfectly, by other apps.<sup>7</sup> Many text-messaging apps available in the mobile ecosystem are similar in their functionality. There is consequentially an ‘inherent’ asymmetry in bargaining power between the platform provider and app developers – the platform provider possesses superior bargaining power over app developers (Foros et al., 2013) – which likely leads to an asymmetric value appropriation in favor of the platform provider. The bargaining literature shows that the value each participant in an ecosystem appropriates is determined by its bargaining power (Maschler, 1963,

1976; Rapoport and Kahan, 1984; MacDonald and Ryall, 2004).

A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant body of literature (van den Nouweland et al., 1996; Lippman and Rumelt, 2003; MacDonald and Ryall, 2004; Brandenburger and Stuart, 2007; Ryall and Sorenson, 2007; Shiller and Waldfogel, 2012) examines and theoretically predicts the value appropriation among participants within the various forms of ecosystem using bargaining models such as Core, Nash bargaining solution, Nucleolus, and Shapley value. However, these bargaining models do not adequately capture the asymmetry in bargaining power between the platform provider and app developers within the MPMN in addition to the bargaining dynamics between them.<sup>8</sup> Furthermore, we are unaware of a study that provides empirical data consistent with these model predictions. These bargaining models often either do not predict any outcome or predict multiple outcomes, making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to empirically verify their predictions. We enrich the literature by proposing a new bargaining model that predicts the value appropriation between the platform provider and app developers within the MPMN. Our model further provides supporting results using data collected from the network operator’s early mobile ecosystem as well as Apple’s iOS and Google’s Android mediated networks. One salient contribution of our paper is that our analytical results <sup>fi</sup>t the empirical data of various platform leadership con<sup>fi</sup>gurations along the evolution of the recent mobile ecosystem.

Our model is similar to the Apex game (Horowitz and Rapoport, 1974; Hart and Kurz, 1984; Montero, 2002; Fukuda and Muto, 2004), as well as its variations such as the experimental game designed by Murnighan and Roth (1977) and the 2:1 exchange and assignment games introduced by Lippman and Rumelt (2003). We refer to our model as the modified apex game (MAG). The Apex game models a network with one apex player and multiple minor players. In the Apex game the network creates a positive value when it has either: (i) an apex player and at least one minor player, or (ii) all minor players. Similar to the Apex game, the MAG models the value appropriation between the platform provider (equivalent to the apex player in the Apex game) and app developers (equivalent to minor players in the Apex game) within the MPMN. However, unlike in the Apex game, in the MAG, the MPMN that includes only app developers but not the platform provider does not create a positive value. The MAG clearly represents the MPMN’s characteristics better.

An important missing feature in the MAG is that it does not consider competition among multiple platforms. Although this assumption allows us to focus on the bargaining interaction between the platform provider and app developers when there is only a single mobile platform, it risks missing an important element that has the potential for a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the result. In this regard the MAG may be thought of as a description of conditions at the beginning or genesis of a particular mobile ecosystem when the inter-platform competitive force is either non-present or less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

Our model’s theoretical analysis predicts that in a stable equilibrium the platform provider receives 75% of the total value created by the MPMN and app developers collectively receive the remaining 25%. This prediction is consistent with our empirical observations.<sup>9</sup> In contrast, the well-known Shapley value bargaining model predicts that no more than two-thirds of the total value goes to the platform provider (Murnighan and Roth, 1977; Roth, 1988; Eichberger, 1993). The Shapley value allocates each participant’s expected marginal contribution respectively, yet it is not necessarily a stable equilibrium. Our analysis suggests that in the stable equilibrium the platform provider would likely receive an economic rent that exceeds its expected marginal contribution.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. We review the related literature in the next section and describe the MAG in the subsequent section. In the latter section we <sup>fi</sup>rst derive the equilibrium in the 3-entity MAG and then generalize it to the n-entity MAG. We empirically illustrate our theoretical results using data collected from the mobile ecosystem in the penultimate section. Finally, we conclude with the summary and discussion in the last section.

## Literature review

The mobile ecosystem is highly dynamic and complex due to the rapid changes in mobile technologies (Basole, 2009). Within its relatively short history numerous new businesses have entered the ecosystem, and some of them have substantially altered the relationships between businesses and the ecosystem’s structure. The relationships between businesses within the mobile ecosystem and how these relationships have changed over time are well visualized and analyzed in Basole (2009), and Basole and Karla (2011). During this evolution the platform leadership has evolved from telcos at the network layer to Google and Apple at the device layer as mentioned in the introduction. We focus on the relationship between the platform provider and app developers that support the platform, forming the MPMN.

The relationship between the platform provider and app developers (or service providers in a general platform context) has been explored in an extensive body of literature on platform economics. This literature broadly addresses three major research topics – the level of openness in the platform, the network effects in platform mediated networks, and platform leadership. The level of openness in the platform has been an important research topic since the value of a platform relies on its services. An open platform refers to one where there is no restriction on participation in and use of the platform (Parker and Van Alstyne, 2013). West (2003) explores the tradeoff between open and proprietary platform strategies in three abbreviated case studies. Nielsen (2004) explores the standardization approaches for mobile content provider access platform in Norway, and De Reuver et al. (2011) explore the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of mobile service platforms. Lee (2013) investigates the impact of vertical integration and exclusivity on industry structure and social welfare in the United States video game industry, while Boudreau (2010) and Parker and Van Alstyne (2013) show the relationship between platform openness and innovation.

A platform mediated network is often de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a twosided network. The value of a platform (or utility from a platform) in a two-sided network increases as more people either join the group on the same side (direct or same-side network effects) or on the opposing side (indirect or cross-side network effects) (Eisenmann, 2006a). Nikou et al. (2014) show that the number of available apps are the most important factor affecting consumers’ mobile platform choice decisions, while Hann et al. (2013) empirically show the relationship between the diffusion of multi-generation platforms and (direct or indirect) network effects using data collected from the mobile ecosystem. In a two-sided network the platform provider often subsidizes one side and charges a premium to the other (Eisenmann et al., 2006). Rochet and Tirole (2003), Parker and Van Alstyne (2005), Eisenmann et al. (2006), and Evans and Schmalensee (2007) analyze such a platform provider’s pricing strategies.

Eisenmann (2006b) and Gawer and Cusumano (2008) argue that many mature industries are dominated by a handful of large platforms due to platform characteristics such as network effects and multi-homing costs, and often a single platform emerges as the winner. Many studies (Bresnahan and Greenstein, 1999; Gawer and Cusumano, 2008; Tee and Gawer, 2009; Zhu and Iansiti, 2012) have accordingly discussed strategies on how to become the winning platform. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant body of literature speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focuses on mana ging the relationship between the platform provider and app developers (or service providers). Cusumano and Gawer (2002) and Yof<sup>fi</sup>e and Kwak (2006) highlight the importance of managing a good relationship between the platform provider and service providers; Iyer et al. (2006) and Iansiti and Richards (2006) discuss the role of each participant within an ecosystem. Nielsen and Aanestad (2006) explore the relation ship between control and innovation using the case study of two Norwegian telecommunications operators; Jacobides et al. (2006) theorize the tradeoff between complementarity and mobility in the value creation and appropriation between <sup>fi</sup>rms within a co-specialization context. Perrons (2009) emphasizes the balance between trust and power in platform leadership strategy. Cennamo and Santalo (2013) empirically show how platform provider’s strategy that squeezes service providers can be detrimental using data collected from the US video game industry. Ghazawneh and Henfridsson (2013) investigate how resourcing and securing strategies interact with service providers using a boundary resources model in the case of Apple’s iOS service system, and Eaton et al. (2015) explore the evolution of boundary resources extending Ghazawneh and Henfridsson (2013). We complement these studies by proposing a new bargaining model in order to investigate how value is appropriated between the platform provider and app developers within the MPMN, as well as provide theoretical support explaining the tension regarding revenue sharing. Our model’s validity is then illustrated empirically across three representative mobile ecosystems: a network operator, and the Apple and Google ecosystems respectively.

## Model description

## Model context and boundaries

We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the context and boundaries of the model presented in this section. We model a MPMN that has n⩾3 entities. One of these entities is the platform provider and the other n−1 entities are app developers.

## Platform provider

Like many platform mediated networks (Eisenmann, 2006b; Gawer and Cusumano, 2008), the MPMN has a unique platform where the platform provider is the only one within the MPMN. We follow the literature on platform economics (Gawer and Cusumano, 2002; Parker and Van Alstyne, 2005; Eisenmann et al., 2006; Gawer and Henderson, 2007; Cusumano, 2010; Eisenmann et al., 2011) in assuming that a platform without apps provides no value to a consumer. For instance, iPhone without apps is merely a fancy box (Cusumano, 2010).<sup>10</sup>

## App developers

App developers provide apps that support (or add value to) the platform within the MPMN. Apps include mobile applications as well as various mobile content. We assume that apps can be delivered to a consumer only through the platform. For instance, there is no perfect way to run iPhone apps on other devices other than iPhones (Egan, 2014). App developers therefore cannot create value without joining the MPMN. In many cases an app can be substituted by other apps but not necessarily perfectly; however, we assume that apps are perfect substitutes for the following technical reasons. First, if apps are perfect substitutes then the marginal contribution of any subset of app developers to the MPMN is zero. If apps are not perfect substitutes then in order to derive the equilibrium we must make additional assumptions regarding app developers and de<sup>fi</sup>ne the marginal contribution for all possible subsets of app developers to the MPMN. This would complicate the problem enormously. Second, if apps are perfect substitutes, then all app developers receive an equal share, allowing us to focus on value appropriation between the platform provider and app developers and forgo value appropriation among app developers.

## Value appropriation

The value of a platform without apps is zero, and apps can create value only through the platform. That is, the platform provider and app developers create value only by forming the MPMN. Following the literature (Ellegaard et al., 2009), we refer to the value of the MPMN as the pro<sup>fi</sup>t that the platform provider and app developers co-create; the value appropriation is therefore the net result of revenue sharing. In some cases, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for that value created from information goods (e.g., apps), the value appropriation is fairly identical to revenue sharing since the marginal costs of most information goods are negligible.

## Bargaining

The platform provider and app developers have clear incentive to form the MPMN and co-create value since they can create value only by forming the MPMN. Following the bargaining literature (Hart and Kurz, 1984; Montero, 2002), we assume that the transaction cost is negligible such that the platform provider and app developers can freely talk to each other and form the MPMN. Each can also freely negotiate with each other for its share; however, there is a disagreement cost. If negotiation fails and either the platform provider or all app developers leave the MPMN there would be an associated cost since the MPMN no longer creates value.

Now consider (b). If $( 1 { - } \alpha ) V = ( 1 ) /$ (4)V, then (b) is stable. Neither the platform provider nor Entity 2 can receive a higher value by leaving one bargaining unit and joining another. Analogously, (c) is stable if (1−α) $V = ( 1 ) / ( 4 ) V . \bar { \bigstar }$

The intuition for Proposition 1 is as follows. If app developers negotiate collectively with the platform provider, then each app developer receives one fourth of the value created by the MPMN. However, the platform provider can pro<sup>fi</sup>tably induce one of the app developers to leave its bargaining unit of only app developers by offering a small amount greater than one fourth of the total value. Note that this leaves the remaining app developer with a zero share, and hence, no app developer has incentive to claim more than one fourth of the total value.

We now apply the traditional bargaining models – Core, Nash bargaining solution (NBS), Nucleolus, and Shapley value – used in prior studies to our problem and compare the outcomes of those models with the equilibrium predicted by the MAG. We assume that V = 100 and other aspects remain the same as in the 3-entity MAG. Core predicts that each entity receives more than its stand-alone value $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \nu ( \{ i \} ) )$ but not more than its marginal contribution to the MPMN (i.e., v $( M ) - \nu ( M \backslash \{ i \} ) )$ (Eichberger, 1993). The Nash bargaining solution argues that each app developer receives its marginal contribution to the MPMN and the platform provider receives the remaining value (Compte and Jehiel, 2010). In Nucleolus (Schmeidler, 1969) the app developers collectively receive the marginal contribution of an app developer and the platform provider receives the remaining value. The Shapley value allocates each entity’s expected marginal contribution to the MPMN (Roth, 1988; Eichberger, 1993). ## Empirical illustration

## Value appropriation in the early mobile ecosystem

We collected data from a network operator in Korea (hereafter, the platform provider) on a monthly basis from January 2001 to November 2007. Like the mobile ecosystem in many other countries during this pre-smartphone period, the mobile ecosystem was built based on the network infrastructure including the gateway to the network operator’s mobile services. Apps include various mobile services such as ringtones, e-books, mobile games, mobile banking and stock trading applications, mobile instant messengers, mobile video on demand, and live TV.

## Value appropriation in the recent mobile ecosystem

## Value appropriation in the iOS mediated network

The mobile ecosystem has been substantially changed since Apple introduced the iPhone and the App store in 2008. App developers now deliver their apps to consumers through the mobile operating system’s platform instead of through the network operator, that is, the platform identity of the iOS mediated network now reisdes in the logical capability and physical machinery layers of the device layer. Consequently, the relationship between the platform provider and app developers along with the value to be appropriated have changed.

Apple and app developers co-create the value of the iOS mediated network, including both the iOS device (e.g., iPhone) and apps that support the iOS device. The value of the iOS device without apps is little if not zero, and only those people who have the iOS device purchase iOS apps. We therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ne the value of the iOS mediated network = the value created from the iOS device $( V _ { D e \nu i c e } )$ +the value created from apps $( V _ { A p p s } )$ , arguing that the value appropriation of Apple Π<sub>Apple</sub> is computed as:

$$
\Pi_ {A p p l e} = \frac {V _ {D e v i c e} + 0 . 3 V _ {A p p s .}}{V _ {D e v i c e} + V _ {A p p s}}
$$

Apple takes the entire value created from the iOS device and shares the value created from apps with app developers using a 30–70 revenue split model. Apple’s 30–70 revenue split model has been considered as a revenue-sharing model that favors app developers. However, app developers have occasionally claimed that Apple should drop its share (Boudreau, 2008); this tension between Apple and app developers has been expressed in various ways since Apple announced that it would take the same 30% cut of the revenue generated from in-app subscriptions.

Unfortunately Apple’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t breakdown is not publically available; however, it is estimated that Apple’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t from iPhone handsets was \$8.3 billion USD (Blodget, 2012) and the app revenue was \$4.2 billion USD (Gartner, 2010) in 2009. We would like to note that 2009 is a good year to analyze the value appropriation of Apple in the iOS mediated network because it was the year after the App store launch; it is therefore plausible to assume that the iOS mediated network was fairly stabilized. This period is before Apple introduced another iOS device, the iPad, which complicated the iOS mediated network. We estimate that in 2009 Apple captured approximately 76% of the total value created by the iOS mediated ecosystem, consistent with our theoretical predictions as well as empirical observations presented in the section ‘Value appropriation in the early mobile ecosystem’.

There is a great deal of anecdotal evidence showing that app developers have searched for a way to bypass Apple’s 30% cut and keep the entire revenue from app sales (CBS News, 2011; Dredge, 2012). If Apple agreed on app developers’ contentions that Apple’s contribution in selling a subscription is different from that in selling an app and decided to charge no fee for inapp subscriptions, then the ‘freemium’ model could have been an option for app developers to avoid Apple’s 30% cut. However, Apple’s superior bargaining power as the platform provider was able to effectively suppress app developers’ attempts to capture more than 25%percent of the value created by the iOS mediated network. In fact, following the initial con<sup>fl</sup>ict Microsoft <sup>fi</sup>nally gave up <sup>fi</sup>ghting over subscription sharing and it now shares 30% of Of<sup>fi</sup>ce 365 subscriptions (purchased within the app) with Apple (Fried, 2014).

\Pi_ {G o o g l e} = \frac {\beta V _ {D e v i c e} + 0 . 0 5 V _ {A p p s}}{V _ {D e v i c e} + V _ {A p p s}},
$$

where $\beta V _ { D e \nu i c e }$ is Google’s share of the value created from Android devices. It is estimated that the $\beta V _ { D e \nu i c e }$ between 2008 and 2011 was \$543 million USD (Arthur, 2012; Duncan, 2012). $\left( 1 - \beta \right) V _ { D e \nu i c e }$ is the value created from Android devices <sup>ð Þ</sup>allocated to device manufacturers which is not publicly available. However, we assume that it is negligible (i.e., β≅1) due to the intense price competition within the Android ecosystem. For example, the average price of Android devices is only approximately 35% that of iOS devices (Chowdhry, 2015) and the margin from the Android smartphone handset is much smaller than that of iOS ecosystem. Finally, we estimate that Android’s app revenue between 2008 and 2011 was \$353 million USD.<sup>16</sup> We therefore have $\Pi _ { G o o g l e } = 0 . 6 2 5 7$ that is, between 2008 and 2011 Google captured approximately 63% of the total value created by the Android mediated network.

This result shows that Google captured a lower percentage of value created by the Android mediated network than what our theoretical model predicts. However, we would like to note that if Google took 30% of value created from apps as Apple does, then the $\Pi _ { G o o g l e }$ would have been 0.7242, which is close to our theoretical prediction. We can therefore conjecture that Google has an incentive to – and more importantly can – successfully negotiate with app developers and/or network operators and increase its share from app revenue. In fact, it was reported that Google negotiated with several network operators in order to increase its share from app revenue (Smith, 2013; Yarow, 2013). Further, it was also reported that Google has increased the number of Google apps that must be pre-installed on Android devices for several device manufacturers (Efrati, 2014). These efforts will lead to a larger share of value created from Android devices for Google in the future.

## Summary and discussion

Throughout the recent evolution of the mobile ecosystem we have witnessed a transition of platform leadership from network operators at the network layer to iOS and Android at the device layer. Platform leaders exert signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effort into identifying, understanding, and possessing key platform design capabilities in order to attract <sup>fi</sup>rms outside the platform and tap their higher generative potential. We identify one such element, value appropriation between the platform provider and app developers, showing that there is a relatively stable mechanism and result underlying their value appropriation across differing platform leadership types.

We theoretically analyze the value appropriation between the platform provider and app developers in the MPMN by proposing a bargaining model, the MAG. The analysis of the MAG shows that in the stable equilibrium the platform provider receives 75% of the total value created by the MPMN, and app developers collectively receive the remaining 25%. This result is supported by our empirical illustrations using data collected from the mobile ecosystem in Korea as well as the iOS and Android mediated networks. On the other hand, the well-known Shapley value bargaining model predicts no more than two-thirds of the total value allocated to the platform provider. The Shapley value allocates to each parti cipant its expected marginal contribution, yet it is not necessarily a stable equilibrium. Our result therefore suggests that in the stable equilibrium the platform provider is likely to receive an economic rent that exceeds its expected marginal contribution.

Our <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that the economic rent that the platform provider is able to receive due to the superior bargaining power it possesses within the mobile ecosystem might be the reason that app developers perceive the equilibrium revenue sharing as being unfair. Further, this economic rent might explain why there has been strong competition on mobile platforms. Mobile operators have been collectively attempting to launch another gateway to mobile services in order to compete with Apple and Google, although such a gateway has not been successful yet (Duryee, 2010; Harvey, 2012). Our <sup>fi</sup>nding has important implications from the policy perspective as well. Social planners may need to carefully monitor the mobile ecosystem because there is always the likelihood for dispute concerning revenue sharing between the platform provider and app developers due to the natural tendency toward asymmetric value appropriation. Anecdotal evidence shows that a dispute regarding revenue sharing can cause the discontinuity of services as well as substantial economic losses (Klowden et al., 2008; Stelter, 2008; Sherter, 2013).

The MAG structure in which there is a single mobile platform provider and multiple app developers suggest the possibility of a model extension in order to explain ecosystem and platform contexts other than mobile services. On the other hand, the assumptions and structure behind the MAG strongly represent the asymmetry in bargaining power between the platform provider and app developers, making it tailored to the mobile ecosystem context. In general, a mobile platform is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to replicate by other <sup>fi</sup>rms compared to other general platforms for reasons such as the huge initial investment cost, advanced technologies, and related patents. Either a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed or more general set of assumptions on asymmetry in bargaining power is therefore necessary in order to apply the MAG to other ecosystem contexts.

There is a high degree of consistency between our theoretical prediction and empirical observations. Despite this consistency our theoretical model has a number of limitations that collectively suggest directions for further research. First, we assumed in our analysis that apps are perfect substitutes. Although apps are close to perfect substitutes in many cases, perfect substitution is a strong assumption. However, we note that this assumption allowed us to focus on value appropriation between the platform provider and app developers, forgoing value appropriation among app developers. Moreover, even when apps are not perfect substitutes all of our results hold as long as Assumptions 1 and 2 are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. We leave the analysis of the case where apps are not perfect substitutes for future study.

Second, the competition among multiple platforms is important yet missing in our analysis. The incorporation of competition between platforms with differing con<sup>fi</sup>gurations such as the one between the iOS and Android mediated networks might shed light onto the relationship between platform con<sup>fi</sup>guration and value creation as well as appropriation, yielding a richer discussion on strategic implications in platform provision. Such a study could possibly lead to insights into the overall evolution of mobile ecosystems and platforms we are witnessing.

Third, an extension of the MAG incorporating factors important for relationship management in the ecosystem and platform literatures (such as level of openness and network effects) will certainly identify re<sup>fi</sup>ned platform strategies.

Finally, our empirical study is limited by a lack of data and detailed statistical analysis. For example, our discussion of value appropriation in recent mobile ecosystems (iOS and Android mediated networks) is characterized by a number of assumptions and serves only as an illustration. Another important missing element is the aforementioned platform competition. An extension of our MAG model that encompasses platform competition as well as more detailed value performance data would yield a more realistic insight into mobile ecosystem evolution and platform strategies.
