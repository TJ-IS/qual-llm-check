# THE SUPPLY AND DEMAND OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS DOCTORATES: PAST, PRESENT, AND FUTURE<sup>1</sup>

By: Lee A. Freeman Department of Management Studies School of Management University of Michigan, Dearborn Dearborn, MI 48128 U.S.A. lefreema@alumni.indiana.edu

Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa Center for Business, Technology & Law University of Texas at Austin Austin, TX 78712 U.S.A. sjarvenpaa@mail.utexas.edu

Bradley C. Wheeler Department of Accounting and Information Systems Kelley School of Business Indiana University Bloomington, IN 47405 U.S.A. bwheeler@indiana.edu

## Abstract

This paper reports on a survey of North American IS programs and secondary data assessing the supply and demand of Information Systems (IS) doctorates. The data document a large and growing lack of supply to meet current and future demand. Demographic factorsóincluding the number of university students, their selection of majors, and retirements among IS facultyófavor a probable scenario for continuing strong demand for IS faculty in the longer term. We argue that the severe imbalance will continue if the current state of the economy and businessesí need for technically-savvy managers continues. Implications and recommendations are presented for ensuring the long-term health of the IS discipline in addressing this imbalance.

Keywords: Doctoral student supply, doctoral student demand, survey, North American universities, IS faculty, IS programs, forecasts and implications

ISRL Categories: AI0104, BA0101, BA0202, ID05

## Issues at Stake

As the network era of computing claims center stage in modern economies, industry demand for skills in designing and implementing information systems is extremely high. Many companies are vigilantly recruiting for these skills in both university undergraduate and graduate programs, and IS course enrollments are rising. While some of this demand can and is being serviced by nontenure-track lecturers, accrediting agencies, such as the American Association for Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB), mandate minimum ratios of students-to-doctorally-qualified faculty. Shortages strain existing programs, inhibit the development of new IS programs, and adversely affect research productivity.

Since universities use a variety of terms to describe professorial roles and degree programs, we offer the following definitions for the terms used in this paper. University refers to a degreegranting school, college, institute, or university. IS faculty is defined as a full-time, tenure-track faculty position whose primary responsibilities are to conduct research and/or to teach courses in IS. An IS doctorate refers to a person with a Ph.D. or DBA that has been or will be awarded for a dissertation that is primarily focused on IS. IS program refers to the organizational grouping of IS faculty within a university that delivers IS courses.

At face value, there appears to be an extreme and growing shortage of IS faculty. The 1998-99 Association for Information Systems and International Conference on Information Systems (AIS/ ICIS) placement system had 247 registered tenure-track IS faculty positions (including 11 endowed chairs or professorships) and 105 applicants who have or will have doctoral degrees in IS. The 1999-2000 data are even more compelling. At ICIS 1999 (Charlotte, NC), there were 395 North American positions listed and 145 applicants, with only 93 of these being new North American IS doctorates. The imbalance between supply and demand has widened in each of the last four annual hiring cycles. Thus, many universities face intense competition in terms of hiring IS faculty and particularly new doctorates.

The shortage occurs at a time when IS faculty members report high work-related stress. They are under increasing teaching and administrative loads at the same time that pressure to publish is increasing in both teaching and research-oriented universities (Whitman et al. 1999). For an individual faculty member, the rising demand for IS teaching is likely to come at the expense of productive, top-tier research. In some universitiesó particularly those regarding themselves as highly research intensiveógrowing teaching and administrative responsibilities over time may put individual IS faculty members as well as the IS faculty group at risk. If left unaddressed, the shortage of IS faculty may lead to declining rates of promotions and a decline in research stature of the IS group in some universities.

An imbalance in the supply and demand of IS doctorates is an important issue for the IS discipline as a whole. The adequate supply of high quality IS doctorates is essential to the continuing viability of the discipline, and it is unlikely that any single university can unilaterally affect the supply or demand over the long term. Reliable data are needed to understand current trends, interpret their implications, and provide evidence to support proactive adjustments in the size of IS doctoral programs.

We argue that three issues are especially relevant to those universities, faculty, and students affected by changes in the supply and demand of IS doctorates: (1) Is there, in fact, an imbalance in supply and demand? (2) If so, what is the temporal nature of the imbalance (i.e., short term and self-correcting or structural)? (3) What are the implications for IS faculty, administrators, IS students, and universities? Other issues related to the stature of IS programs at top universities are beyond the scope of this article.

We address these questions by drawing on data from a 1998 survey of North American universities and secondary data sources regarding the supply and demand of IS doctorates (see Appendix A for the survey and Appendix B for a list of universities participating in the survey). Other academic disciplines, such as Computer Science (Computing Research Association) and Accounting, publish annual or biennial reports on the supply and demand of doctorates in their field. The Jarvenpaa et al. (1991) study of 51 U.S. universities with doctoral programs in IS is the most recent report for our discipline. It should be noted that our numbers and estimates do not take into account graduates from management science, computer science, and other related disciplines that may influence the total supply of candidates for IS faculty positions. Similarly, the study does not address the supply of adjunct faculty who do not hold doctorates, nor does it address supply and demand outside North America.

## Conceptual Framework

Imbalances between the supply and demand for IS doctoratesóperceived or realócan occur through changes in supply, changes in demand, or both. Their temporal nature may be one-year anomalies that self-correct in the short term or are more structurally enduring for the longer term. The source of the imbalance and its temporal nature pose different implications for the university stakeholders that produce and hire IS doctorates. These stakeholders may choose different coping tactics depending upon the nature of the imbalance.

The supply of IS doctorates consists of existing faculty plus new graduates. The left side of Figure 1 lists the major factors that may affect the supply of IS doctorates in a given academic year and the right side lists the factors that may affect demand. Some of these factors can be logically decomposed further. For example, a strong economy that provides lucrative career alternatives can diminish the number of applicants to IS doctoral programs. Many secondary factors such as state or university budgets, historical success in placing graduates at peer universities, shifting degree priorities within business schools, and the perceived legitimacy of the IS field can drive the number of IS doctoral programs and number of positions. Similarly, the number of retirements or other exits from the career affect the number of current IS faculty. The proportion of university students who choose IS courses and the scope of IS research and teaching efforts within universities affect demand for IS faculty.

## Data Sources

Our analysis addresses the three questions posed above by drawing on multiple data sources for evidence. The primary data source for answering these questions is a survey conducted during the winter and spring of 1998. Secondary data sources include the AACSB, AIS/ICIS placement data, ISWorld Listserv announcements, and the U.S. Department of Education.

## Survey Methodology

Surveys were mailed to the Ph.D. advisor or department chair of 472 universities within the U.S. and Canada. These universities were the commerce-oriented schools with at least one IS faculty member listed in the ISWorldNet Faculty Directory or an IS program as of 1997. Due to the nature of the survey questions (see Appendix A), universities without current IS programs or even one IS faculty member were not included in the sample. However, of the top 60 business schools recognized in the 1998 Business Week rankings,

55 universities (90%) received surveys and 29 of these responded (53% response rate).

Respondents were asked to complete the written survey with February 1, 1998, as the reference date for information regarding their IS program and/or doctoral program. Six weeks after the mailing, a mass e-mail message was sent via ISWorldís Listserv to prompt additional responses. The authors used e-mail and telephone to followup with non-respondents. The 119 returned surveys represent a 25% response rate (Table 2).

The responses from 54 doctoral granting (DG) universities compare favorably to the responses from the 51 DG universities reported in Jarvenpaa et al. (1991). All but four universities that appeared in the Jarvenpaa et al. (1991) study responded to the current study. One of the four missing universities wrote to us that ìsince 1993 or so there has been no Ph.D. concentration in IS at the business school here.î The 65 non-doctoral granting (NDG) universities represent 16.8% of all universities without doctoral programs. ## Secondary Sources

Secondary data regarding supply and demand among the population of universities were also gathered from the ISWorldNet Faculty Directory, AACSB, the AIS/ICIS Placement Services, and the U.S. Department of Education. While not representative of all of North America, the U.S. Department of Education publishes data regarding high school graduates, undergraduate enrollments, and total university enrollments. These data include predictions to the year 2007 and provide some projections regarding the size of future university enrollment. Data from these sources were used to corroborate the survey results and to permit additional forecasting. The next section integrates the data sources to present a composite view of the existing supply and demand of IS doctorates. It is followed by an assessment of future trends in the supply and demand of IS doctorates.

## Existing Supply and Demand

One indicator of the existing supply of IS faculty is the ISWorldNet Faculty Directory as of early 1998.

It listed 1,662 tenure-track IS faculty: 469 assistant professors, 619 associate professors, and 574 full professors. This does not capture non-tenure-track faculty who may be serving as lecturers, adjuncts, or part-time faculty. While new doctorates add to the supply of IS faculty, retirements or other exits from the teaching and research faculty reduce it. Nine, 12, and 15 retirements were reported for 1995-96, 1996-97, and 1997-98, respectively. Using the 1997-98 number of 15, this would represent an annual retirement rate of less than 1%, thus having minimal impact on the current supply.

## Survey Results

Table 3 contains the survey results for the number of hires, departures, and IS doctorates awarded. Hires include all IS faculty that were hired into the university at all ranks including those moving from another university. Departures include all faculty leaving for any reason including retirements, exiting the profession, or going to another university.

The survey data in Table 3 indicate a four-year increase in demand (hires) among DG universities and a three-year increase among NDG universities. Demand for IS doctorates was up strongly in 1997-98 with a total of 133 hires, representing a four-fold increase from 1994-95 and double the 1990-91 number of hires. Departures have shown much less variability with an average of 36 per year.

Doctoral production is down from a decade ago when the Jarvenpaa et al. survey reported 120 graduates in 1988-89 and projected 140 graduates in 1989-90. The number of admissions to doctoral programs declined from 139 in 1990 to 104 in 1995. The decline occurred through downsizing of the Ph.D. programs rather than discontinuation of programs. In fact, there has been a slight increase in the total number of doctoral programs. In 1989, 61 universities in the U.S. offered a Ph.D. or Doctor of Business Administration (DBA) concentration in IS. In 1998, we estimate that there are 86 universities in North America with active IS doctoral programs, with 80 of these located in the U.S. However, the size of the programs has decreased. In 1989-90, the annual average number of graduates per program was 3.4 (Jarvenpaa et al. 1991). In 1998, it was down to 2.2 (33 programs granting 72 degrees).

The downsizing has been most severe in the largest doctoral programs. These are the universities that graduate more than the average number of doctoral students annually (three or more per year), and we will refer to these as the ìover-averageî group. In 1989-90, IS doctorates from the 13 over-average universities (25.5% of the 51 in the survey) accounted for 39% of the IS doctorates. By 1997-98, only seven universities were producing three or more IS doctorates per year. These seven accounted for 40% of IS doctorates, thus indicating the fieldís growing dependence on a small set of universities.

In summary, the survey data show that IS doctorate production has been falling and that the decrease has been the most severe in the topproducing universities. The most recent years, however, do show a small upward trend in the number of admissions to doctoral programs.

## AACSB Data

AACSB conducts an annual survey of its member universities and reports the results via its Newsline publication. AACSB data show that the number of doctoral graduates from business schools (all disciplines) has steadily declined from 1,204 in 1993 to 1,006 in 1998. Similarly, the number of IS doctorates granted has declined by 43% during the same period. Figure 2 graphs six years of Newsline data that depict the number of IS doctorates granted, open IS faculty positions, and planned growth of IS faculty positions for the next academic year. Planned growth is a forecast for the number of IS faculty that will be hired in the next year.

The AACSB number of IS doctorates granted compares closely with the survey data as both indicate a sharp decline in the most recent three years. The AACSB data also reveal an interesting and persistent underestimation of planned growth in the number of IS faculty. For example, the 1993-94 survey asked about planned growth for 1994-95 with a prediction of 51. In Figure 2, the prediction of 51 has been shifted to the actua year (1994-95) for comparison to the 134 open positions that were actually recruited for in that year. As the graph shows, the planned growth number has consistently underestimated the number of IS positions that were actually open in the next year. On average, the underestimate has been 73 positions each year.

## Placement Services and ISWorld Postings

Our data and the AACSB survey provide selfreports from universities regarding the supply and demand for IS doctorates. Other data sources can provide direct measurement of activities related to supply and demand. One direct measurement source is the annual placement service that is operated jointly by the Association for Information Systems and the International Conference on Information Systems. Table 4 reports on data gathered from this service. These data show an increasing number of universities and positions listed since 1995-96. The number of applicants listed in the service shows greater variation than the survey as these are not all new IS doctorates. Some applicants, even those holding the rank of associate or full professor, decided to test the market by listing their credentials for the recruiting universities. For the 1999- 2000 set of applicants, 93 of the 145 applicants did not yet hold an IS doctorate, thus leading to an inference of 93 anticipated new North American doctorates (nine additional applicants were from non-North American universities). We also mention that the 1995-96 and 1996-97 data are from the early years of AIS when the role of its placement system was still emerging and not yet embraced by all universities. By 1999, it appears that the 1996 merger of the AIS and ICIS placement systems had positioned it to attract a very high percentage of all positions and applicants.

Another direct measurement of the demand for IS doctorates is the ISWorld Listserv, which is commonly used to announce IS faculty positions. Messages on the listserv were analyzed between June 1, 1997, and May 31, 1998. Table 5 lists the announcements of North American positions by faculty rank, including postings for chaired or named professorships, posted on ISWorld.

## Summary

The data generally show a growing imbalance in the supply and demand of IS doctorates. There has generally been a gradual decline in the number of new IS doctorates since 1993 while the number of tenure-track faculty positions has grown dramatically. The four measurements of demand for the 1997-98 academic yearó133 from the survey, 191 from the AACSB, 231 from the placement service, and 152 from ISWorld announcementsóprovide a consistent picture of strong recent demand for IS doctorates. The trends presented in Figure 2 are reasonably consistent with other data sources and graphically depict this growing imbalance.

Viewing the data in a different way, Figure 3 represents the imbalance between supply and demand as the ratio of open faculty positions to the number of doctoral students seeking positions. Because the availability of AACSB data substantially lags data from the AIS/ICIS placement service, and the joint placement service was not operating prior to 1995, both sources have been combined to create a more complete perspective. As the line is always above the value of 1, this indicates a consistent pattern of more open positions than new doctorates.

Therefore, we conclude that a chorus of data sources supports the existence of a growing imbalance in the supply and demand of IS doctorates.

## Future Trends

The second question addresses the temporal nature of the imbalance. Will it continue? This section draws on survey and secondary data sources to forecast supply and demand in the near and long terms. Near term refers to the next three to five years. Long term refers to beyond five years. Like the preceding section, the analysis examines changes in the existing supply of IS faculty, the production of new doctorates, and the demand for IS faculty.

## Expected Supply and Demand

We can reasonably expect that some IS faculty will choose to exit their faculty teaching and research roles to administrative assignments, other careers, or retirements. Historically, administration and career changes have been a very small number. Jarvenpaa et al. predicted that because of the youth of the discipline, the next 10 t0 15 years (1990-2005) would be characterized by faculty retirement rates of between 1T and 2% and after that at the rate of 3%.

## Near Term

Forecasting the near-term supply of doctorates is relatively straightforward. The best prediction of near-term supply stems from the current number of students enrolled in doctoral programs adjusted for students who will not graduate. Jarvenpaa et al. estimated that 31% of first year students do not graduate whereas our survey data show that on average 35% of doctoral students do not graduate. While most respondents reported degree programs designed for four years, the survey found that the average student is taking 4.6 years to complete an IS doctorate. This is up slightly from the 4.2 years in the 1991 study.

The survey data on current enrollments in IS doctoral programs are reported in Table 6 along with an estimated number of graduates for five years. Not all of these graduates will enter the supply of North American IS faculty, however, as some will decide to go into industry and others will take positions in non-North American universities. These data show relatively little anticipated growth in IS doctorates. The survey also asked respondents to predict their hiring plans for the next three years and these are also incorporated in Table 6. The anticipated near-term demand for IS doctorates remains considerably higher than the historical annual hiring between 1990 and 1997 for both DG and NDG universities (Table 3) and is approximately one-third of the number of actual positions listed in the 1999-2000 AIS/ICIS placement service.

## Long Term

Moving beyond forecasting for the next three to five years provides even more problems for predicting the supply and demand of IS doctorates. While the near-term supply is based on current enrollments, the medium- and long-term supplies are based on predicted doctoral student enrollments. In the Jarvenpaa et al. study, 45% of the doctoral granting universities expected growth in their doctoral programs. In the current survey, 78% of doctoral granting universities expect their admission rate to be unchanged in the future with only 15% expecting it to increase. Thus, the medium-term supply of IS doctorates appears relatively fixed as the admission rates will remain unchanged for the most part. Noteworthy is the fact that five of the responding NDG universities indicate that they are either planning to develop or thinking about developing a doctoral program. These universities will increase the number of doctorates produced, although it will be quite some time before this takes effect.

## Other Factors

While faculty churn among universities does not affect the aggregate supply or demand, it usually does leave an opening at the former university.

Many survey respondents are concerned about faculty retention. Of the respondents, 49% believe the current risk of faculty flight (including leaving to work at a different university) to be moderate, another 15% believe the risk to be high, and 36% believe the risk to be low. When these numbers are combined with the fact that 50% fee this risk has increased over the last three years and the other 50% feel this risk has remained constant, there seems to be a sense of uneasiness over the retention of current IS faculty. When asked about the next three years, 61% feel the risk will remain as is, 3% feel it will lessen, but 36% feel the risk of faculty flight will increase. The DG universities rated the current risk to be slightly higher than the overall survey average.

In summary, the demand for IS faculty appears to be strong in the near and medium term. The long term cannot be predicted with great precision, but swelling university enrollments, a growing number of IS faculty retirements, and favorable career options for IS majors pose very credible scenarios for continuing strong demand for IS doctorates. Of course, changes in the economy, priorities of universities and businesses, substitutes for IS courses, and student course choices as well as developments in technology could greatly influence these numbers. The previous peak of IS faculty demand occurred at a time of widespread personal computer deployment by businesses. The current peak is coinciding with the rapid growth of electronic commerce or e-businessóthe automation of transactions, communication, and interaction using computer and communications technologies for commercial purposes.

## Limitations

The reader should bear in mind several genera limitations of the data, sources, and procedures reported here. First, the data only address supply and demand in the United States and Canada, and both supply and demand for IS doctorates exist outside of this geographic scope. Some of the data sources are U.S. only. Second, our reporting of multiple data sources has demonstrated the difficulty in precise and consistent measurement of the phenomena. The response rates for the survey (Table 2) are a limiting factor on the reliability of the projections. A number of smaller universities did not respond. These universities would, however, have a lower relative effect on the supply and the demand of IS doctorates. Additionally, any individual university that was not given the opportunity to complete a survey because of possible selection biasólack of a current IS program or any registered IS facultyó would likely only have a very small effect on the overall demand results if that institution were to implement an IS program in the near future.

Third, certain caveats are always appropriate with self-reported data in surveys. Both the survey and some of the secondary sources rely on selfreported data by a single individual within each university (the Ph.D. Advisor, the Department Chair, the Program Coordinator, or possibly a non-administrative professor). The responses, therefore, are the opinions and knowledge of that particular individual and may not reflect the opinions and knowledge of other individuals within the same department. Finally, the surveyís focal academic year of 1997-98 already dates some of the data presented here.

## Discussion

The survey and other data reported here do not stand alone, but rather are one historical thread in the development of IS as a discipline. As a young academic discipline, the field of IS experienced a severe shortage of IS faculty for much of its first 20 years. But, by 1990-91, there were signs of change, such as increases in doctoral programs and production, which triggered the original survey by Jarvenpaa et al. That study reported that the supply of IS doctorates was growing faster than demand. In 1989-90, the IS programs in the U.S. were expected to graduate almost twice as many doctorates as they did in 1986-87. North American IS doctoral production was growing over 20% annually in the late 1980s. The growth was expected to continue because of several newly launched doctoral programs and aggressive growth plans in nearly half of the existing programs. There were also several additional doctoral programs under development.

At the same time, the number of new IS faculty positions was diminishing, particularly in the doctoral granting universities. In 1990-91, the unfilled and new positions in DG universities were estimated to consume over half of the new IS doctorates. But by 1992-93, the estimate was less than half of that (25%). An increasing percentage of new IS doctorates would find positions in NDG programs.

The imbalance between supply and demand not only narrowed, it all but disappeared over the three-year period from 1992 to 1995. In 1992-93, the number of new IS faculty positions was half of what it had been only three years previously. The weaker IS faculty demand was followed by reductions in doctoral production. There were sharp drops of doctoral production particularly in the universities that had previously been producing at the above average rate. In the mid-1990s, many business schools also experienced a greater shift in emphasis toward MBA programs at the resource expense of doctoral programs.

The demand for IS faculty started to increase again in 1995-96, which coincides with the broader commercialization of the Internet and the explosive growth of the network era of computing. Universities reported that the number of hires increased 150% from 1996-97 to 1997-98 (Table 3). The rate of hiring again surpassed the rates of the late 1980s. In 1988-89, of the 51 doctoral granting programs surveyed in Jarvenpaa et al., 27 reported hiring 44 faculty. In 1997-98, of the 54 doctoral granting programs, 36 reported hiring 72 faculty (Table 3); in 1998-99, 40 DG programs estimated that they would hire another 72 faculty (survey data). Only a slight increase in hiring was expected in 2000-01. Hence, the demand for IS faculty has been increasing at a time when there were few signs of substantive increases in IS doctoral production.

The trends reported here are very similar to those in the Taulbee Survey for doctorates in Computer Science (CS) and Computer Engineering (Computing Research Association). The Taulbee study, conducted annually for the past 28 years, shows that CS doctoral production peeked at 1,113 in 1992 and declined to 893 in 1996-97. Production of new CS doctorates has risen slightly in the past two years with a forecasted number of 959 for 1999. The Taulbee Survey has not historically captured data regarding the demand for CS doctorates, although it began doing so in its 1998 survey. It found that 156 tenure-track CS positions went unfilledóeven after graduating 933 doctorates in 1998. The data corroborate the widespread demand for faculty in technologyrelated disciplines and also suggest that neither discipline has any excess graduates to provide substitutes for the needs of the other.

## Implications

We raised three issues for universities, faculty, and students regarding the supply and demand of IS doctorates: (1) Is there an imbalance in supply and demand? (2) If so, what is the temporal nature of the imbalance? (3) What are the implications for IS faculty, administrators, IS students, and universities? Our analysis indicates the following:

## An Imbalance Does Exist

First, we believe that the survey and secondary data unequivocally document a current imbalance in the supply and demand of IS doctorates. While informal discussions and anecdotal evidence have made this the current conventional wisdom, the data reported here document the existence and likely continuation of an imbalance. The imbalance is further exacerbated when it is considered in an international context. For example, Asia-Pacific universities have relied on North American doctorates to fill some of their faculty positions. The survey did not capture demand (or supply) from those sources.

## The Imbalance Is Structural In Nature

Second, we believe that the temporal nature of the imbalance is structural, persistent, and not a short-term anomaly. It is almost impossible to substantively affect the production of IS doctorates for the next five years, and most universities do not expect there to be any change in the size of their doctoral programs in the foreseeable future. Therefore, any easing of the imbalance will have to come from a reduction in demand or use of substitutes.

We contend that the imbalance will persist for the near term and possibly for the long term. Three structural reasons support this view. First, many schools with IS doctoral programs have reduced the number of IS doctoral students and therefore limited production. Many are reluctant to admit more students due to difficulties in attracting quality student candidates, funding, demands on faculty time, and fears of overproducing should another economic downturn reduce demand. Second, the rising number of IS faculty retirements (Figure 4) and rising university enrollments (Figure 5) will exacerbate the imbalance after 2000 whereas these factors were of lesser concern in the 1990s. Finally, we believe that the economic transition to the network era of computing and an interconnected global economy wil continue in the medium term, and this will fuel demand for technology and business-related careers. This will increase the popularity of IS courses and, consequently, the need for IS faculty.

While we hold that the imbalance is structural in nature and likely to persist, the current severity of the imbalance (395 positions competing for 93 new doctorates) is unlikely to persist. There is some evidence to suggest that a portion of this demand is carry-over from positions unfilled in prior years (e.g., renewed searches for chaired professorships and unfilled positions reposted).

Whereas predicting the future is an imprecise exercise, others might argue that the severe IS shortage will be more temporary than is argued here. It is also reasonable to suggest that the current sustained economic boom, the Year 2000 computer scare, and the rapid growth of electronic commerce might have artificially inflated the IS faculty demand more than we have suggested here. In addition, a cycle of supply and demand may exist in IS doctoral production. In 1986, a situation existed that parallels the present one; by 1993, the imbalance had disappeared. Perhaps in another five to seven years, this imbalance will disappear as well because of the above economic reasons or others.

## Alternate Views<sup>3</sup>

Some readers of this paper may concur that an imbalance exists and will even persist in the supply and demand of IS doctorates, but they may feel it is not an issue of importance for schools of business where the majority of IS programs reside. The nature of these arguments may include:

1. The need for training and expertise in IS by business managers will decline in the future due to a more technically-savvy younger generation and advances in technology that make systems more user friendly.

2. IS is not a core management field and so courses and faculty with this specialty are not needed.

3. Much of what is taught in IS courses can be taught by faculty in other disciplines (e.g., Computer Science, Engineering, Strategy, General Management) and so the need for IS faculty is not that strong.

Clearly, each university can choose its own path for addressing rising student demand for courses related to the development and application of information systems. No single answer will fit for every university. We dissent, however, and suggest that these arguments assume away much of the real complexity of developing timely information systems that add business value. While increasing user friendliness is likely to develop with advances in hardware and software, the increased interaction of inter-organizational systems and business strategy will require a more sophisticated understanding of IS rather than less. Mastering personal productivity software or using the Internet is not a substitute for the skills required to design, implement, and effectively manage sophisticated information systems. Emerging technologies will always ensure a need for new expertise and training. Similarly, we believe that the rise of e-business and electronic commerce is quickly making a sophisticated understanding of IS a core management field for the 21<sup>st</sup> century.

Substitutes are always possible in universities. Some Financial Accounting faculty could readily cover Finance courses or some Marketing faculty could teach an Entrepreneurship course. Managing IS and effectively aligning it with business strategy, however, requires a sophisticated understanding of the technology itself that is often beyond the existing competencies and interests of many faculty in other disciplines. While courses in Management are often rich in business principles, they lack hands-on exposure to building systems that solve business problems. Hardware and software courses from Computer Science and Electrical Engineering will usually lack linkages to underlying business principles or may be too technical for business students who have not taken sufficient foundation courses. Thus, we agree that some IS topics can be covered by faculty from other disciplines, but just as in the examples above, this strategy is not optimal for learning the deeper knowledge in the IS discipline.

## Implications for Stakeholders

If our views prove correct, there are important implications of an imbalance in the supply and demand of IS doctorates for faculty, administrators, students, and universities as stakeholders in higher education. Table 7 lists these implications by stakeholder group.

One clear implication of this growing imbalance for IS has been an increased reliance on the use of non-tenure-track, non-Ph.D. lecturers to fill teaching needs. During the past two years, 63% of the survey respondents indicated that their use of lecturers to teach IS courses has increased while 32% said that lecturer use has remained constant. Looking ahead, 39% believe lecturer use will increase and 48% expect it to remain constant. Although use of lecturers in the short term can alleviate the severe strain on the demand for IS courses, the long-term implications are unclear in terms of IS research productivity and the stature of the IS programs within the universities. When the number of IS research faculty is small in a university, the new IS faculty might be particularly endangered from a lack of role models who understand the research traditions and publication outlets of the IS field.

## Guiding Principles for Action

Stakeholders may engage a range of possible actions to affect the imbalance. We argue that three guiding principles should steer any actions on the part of IS faculty and administrators to address the supply and demand imbalance. These principles focus efforts on long-term resolution of the supply and demand imbalance rather than short-term tactics:

1. The field, and ultimately all of its stakeholders, is best served by producing well trained IS doctorates who are capable of long-term success in the teaching and research missions of universities (quality imperative).

2. At present, the IS field does not have the capacity in faculty time or resources to increase the supply of well-trained IS doctorates in the short run through adding new programs or growing existing programs (capacity constraint).

3. Any efforts to quickly increase the short-term supply of IS doctorates are likely to do so at the expense of quality (because of the present capacity constraint). Therefore, evolutionary approaches to growing IS faculty and doctoral programs will be more successful in the long run than revolutionary approaches (timing constraint).

## Recommendations to IS Faculty and Administrators

Within the framework of these three principles, we propose six recommendations to IS faculty and administrators.

1. Refrain from increasing production without expanding resources: While the large number of open positions is enticing, we believe that efforts to begin new doctoral programs or to increase the size of existing programs in the absence of sufficient resources (e.g., doctoral student stipend support, time for research, and faculty time for mentoring) would be misguided. Any focus on growth in production must ensure availability of the resources necessary to yield well-trained and high quality IS doctorates. An interesting corollary of this recommendation is that some existing programs report having trouble attracting sufficient qualified students for open (and funded) doctoral student positions.

2. Innovate in value-adding doctoral education: The IS discipline is well positioned to lead with innovative uses of IT for interinstitutional doctoral seminars. New doctora consortium courses led by the University of Texas, Indiana University, Harvard University, and others have demonstrated the technica feasibility of these types of courses. They can provide an opportunity for doctora students to interact with other leading researchers in cameo roles who can address multiple doctoral seminars via voice, video, and data technologies. Through these interinstitutional courses, doctoral students also gain early networking opportunities across universities and with faculty beyond their own programs. These types of courses pose substantial challenges to existing university reward systems for faculty and to the logistics of course scheduling. We advocate that IS faculty should take the lead within their business schools to experiment with interinstitutional doctoral course-sharing and credit arrangements among universities. While some faculty may fear that such courses reduce their opportunities to teach desirable doctoral seminars, quite the opposite can be true. The courses can provide the requisite numbers of doctoral students for smaller IS programs. Furthermore, these inter-institutional arrangements can help bolster the quality of IS doctorates by providing additional doctoral course offerings that might not be economically feasible at a single university. Finally, these inter-institutional arrangements can allow universities to offer courses for which they have no expertise (by tapping the expertise of other universities). We view them as a supporting opportunity for doctoral programsí coursework, as dissertation and collaborative research will likely remain a ìcraftî process requiring frequent supervision and guidance with a local faculty mentor.

3. Create continuing education opportunities for IS faculty: The technologies that enable information systems are changing at a relentless pace. Most faculty face the time dilemma of either letting their technical skills atrophy or spending countless hours learning on their own. Neither of these is productive for the continued quality of IS teaching and research. We challenge faculty and administrators to institute programs in their departments for annual, continuing education for technical skills (e.g., languages such as Java, software such as SAP, Oracle, or Windows NT Server, or technical concepts such as electronic commerce website management). While many IS courses appropriately focus on the managerial issues of technology, current technical skill-sets are essential to maintaining the credibility of the IS faculty with their students. The administrators must acknowledge the continuing education requirement in IS faculty workloads; maintaining technical skills should not come at the expense of research productivity.

4. Increase home-country sponsorship of IS doctorates: Non-North American universities that face difficulties in recruiting IS doctorates may need to develop their own local IS doctoral programs. Alternatively, they may sponsor citizens to attend foreign doctoral programs with commitments to return as IS faculty. As global labor markets allow greater mobility, these universities may be pressed to offer attractive remuneration packages to attract and retain top talent.

5. Support AIS and its efforts to increase the visibility and resources of the IS field. Evolutionary growth in doctoral production will require additional institutional resources, and this in turn requires greater marketing and lobbying efforts. The IS field is fortunate to have a professional organization, the Association for Information Systems (AIS), whose purpose is to serve academicians around the world specializing in IS. One of the objectives of AIS, which was founded in 1994, is to ìprovide a focal point for contact and relations with bodies in government, the private sector, and education that influence and/or control research and education in information systemsî (http://www.aisnet.org). In the U.S., AIS is recognized by AACSB as the voice for IS faculty (Davis, 1999). Such recognition allows AIS to have influence on new and existing programs within business schools. AIS is pursuing similar recognition with other accreditation agencies and legislative/ regulatory bodies globally. Such recognition is critical for lobbying for additional resources from governments and businesses for ISspecific education and research efforts. AIS can also be instrumental in setting standards for quality (as addressed in recommendation #1) for doctoral programs. Such efforts may improve the image and reputation of IS Ph.D. programs.

6. Seek cooperative relations with other disciplines to expand on the resource base or identify new efficiencies. As the knowledge and understanding of the IS field increases, so might the attacks by other related fields in academia that are competing for similar scarce resources. Such zero-sum conflicts over resources can be somewhat minimized by collaborative action, for example the use of joint courses and combined degree programs. For continuing growth of the IS field, IS faculty must increase the density of their ties and interaction with other disciplines. Interdisciplinary activities can help expand the IS resource base and create a positive image of cooperative activity and boundary-spanning in the university. For example, IS faculty can work with marketing and economics faculty in the area of electronic commerce. IS faculty can also work with social psychology and communications faculty in the area of computer-mediated communication.

## Implications for Non-IS Faculty

While this article documents in detail the current and future situation regarding the supply and demand of IS doctorates and the implications for current IS faculty and administrators, non-IS faculty may also benefit from the data and analysis conveyed here. First, for outsiders looking in on this phenomenon affecting colleagues in IS programs, this article provides one view of the market conditions for productive IS faculty. Second, allocating additional faculty lines for hiring will not guarantee success. Other disciplines such as Finance have long faced severe shortages and difficulties in attracting good faculty. Thus, the likely success of short-term hiring plans for IS faculty may be quite low. Third, if students do continue to favor courses that are best taught by someone with formal training in IS and hiring is unsuccessful, the IS faculty will face rising demands for teaching more and larger classes, developing additional programs, and mentoring more majors. Fourth, rising demands for IS teaching skills will seriously erode time for productive, top-tier IS research unless faculty capacity for research is protected. Fifth, other disciplines may benefit from conducting similar studies within their own field, as do Accounting and Computer Science. Finally, this article provides guidance for longer-term structural solutions, not short-term fixes. Joint curriculum evolution with other disciplines and effective resource allocations in universities toward long-term solutions are unlikely to succeed within IS programs alone without cooperation and engagement from other faculty disciplines.

## Conclusion

This article has documented the existence and nature of an imbalance in the supply and demand of IS doctorates. Imbalances are not new to the discipline, but the discipline has shown little historical ability to proactively adjust to these imbalances. We hope the data and interpretations provided here (e.g., Figure 3) will give some evidence for stakeholder decisions and action. Far too much time has elapsed between the 1991 study and this one to provide timely data for decision making. We hope that IS, like other disciplines, will conduct this study on a biennial basis. Furthermore, since the issues presented here are not limited to North America, we encourage other regions of the world to produce similar reports regarding the supply and demand of IS doctorates. These studies could provide an important step toward a global measurement of supply and demand conducted under the auspices of AIS. Future studies might also consider how geographic dependencies (e.g., Asia hiring European IS doctorates) and substitutes from other disciplines affect supply and demand of IS doctorates in order to produce a more complete conceptual model.
