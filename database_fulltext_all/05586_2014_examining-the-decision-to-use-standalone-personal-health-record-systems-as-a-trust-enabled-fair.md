# Examining the decision to use standalone personal health record systems as a trust-enabled fair social contract

## a b s t r a c t

Despite the growing research interest in the digitization of healthcare, current understanding of barriers to using health IT is mostly centered on providers. There is a lack of understanding of how to get patients involved in managing their own digital health information using standalone Personal Health Record Systems (PHR). To <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap, this study proposes a trust-enabled fair social contract model to theorize and empirically test how individuals' intention to use standalone PHR is driven by a trust-enabled privacy calculus, buttressed by the level of perceived privacy control over their own health information and trust. The perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using a standalone PHR, perceived privacy control and trust were found to be the major factors determining intention to adopt the PHR, overriding the effect of potential privacy risks of PHR. In addition, the results of the study suggest that the effect of perceived privacy control varies based on one's prior experience of falling victim to privacy invasions.

## 1. Introduction

Traditional paper-based documentation of medical records is errorprone and inef<sup>fi</sup>cient. Medical errors cause between 44,000 and 98,000 deaths each year, of which over 50% are avoidable [31]. Also, patients often receive unnecessary duplicate tests since paper-based patient data are not easily transferred among different healthcare providers. Health IT has been advocated as a means for improving ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, quality and safety of healthcare, and eventually curbing the hiking healthcare cost [28]. Health IT could potentially prevent thousands of errors, and save about \$80 billion each year in the United States if it is widely adopted [29]. The healthcare industry is under the pressure to go through a digital transformation. The recent economic recovery package of the Obama administration will pay physicians \$44,000 to \$64,000 for adopting and effectively using EHRs from 2011 to 2015 [52]. Considerable research has been devoted to examining the impact of electronic health record systems (EHRs) and how to motivate healthcare providers such as physicians and hospitals to use EHRs [10,57]. However, the role of healthcare consumers in the wide deployment of health IT is largely overlooked. Besides hospitals and physicians, the participation of healthcare consumers in the digital transformation of healthcare industry is critical to its acceptance and success. Although, under HITEC and HIPAA acts, patients have indirect access to their medical records that are stored in various clinical databases such EMR systems through their care provider, the availability and convenient access to a complete portfolio of patient records for their own use remain limited. Such information exists in different formats (images, pdf, report, etc.) and is held in various clinic-owned databases such as (proprietary or customized) EMR systems, pharmaceutical health information systems (PHIS), radiology information systems (RIS), etc. In addition, personal health information may also be scattered in various hospitals, if the patient received care at multiple clinics. Generally, these clinics and hospital-owned databases don't have mechanism in place that allows for easy exchange of information among them, making it even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for patients to access, manage and track their own health. This lack of an easy access to personal health records poses an even bigger challenge for patients suffering from chronic ailments that last over long time periods. Chronic diseases account for 75% of the nation's health care dollars [6]. The care of chronic diseases requires ongoing monitoring of patients' condition and communication between patients and their healthcare providers, making convenient and continuous access to medical records a critical need. All these highlight the importance of adopting PHR systems where patients take ownership of managing their personal records. This also re<sup>fl</sup>ects a paradigm shift in how patient information is being maintained, i.e. patient information not simply existing in care provider managed databases but also in patient managed datasets such as PHR systems [35].

Two types of personal health records systems (PHR) have been implemented to provide patients access to their personal health records and enable them to actively manage their own health information [52]. One is the integrated PHR, which is an extension of physicians' EHRs or a portal to data stored in EHRs. Another type is the standalone PHR systems such as Microsoft HealthVault, that are developed by online commercial companies. A standalone PHR is web-based, empowering patients with control of their own personal health data. Patients can gather, store and manage their health records using a standalone PHR and easily share the data with any healthcare provider. Online PHR is particularly valuable in case of emergencies when the hospital can be informed about a patient's current and past medication history expeditiously.

PHR is an emerging health IT. In a recent literature survey paper, Goldzweig et al. [28] emphasized the need for research into patientfocused IT applications. Currently, there is little theory-based scholarly research on PHR. Ozdemir et al. [44] examined the switching cost of patients' using PHR. Whetstone and Goldsmith [56] applied the TAM model [17] to investigate factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence intention to use PHR. However, the acceptance of PHR by patients is still a largely untapped research area. To <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gap, this study focuses on standalone PHR as it requires more active involvement from patients than an integrated PHR. We extend a privacy calculus model to examine factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence patients' willingness to use standalone PHR.

Consumers face serious threats to the privacy of their health information when such information is captured and stored digitally. In 2009, <sup>fi</sup>ve computers and a <sup>fl</sup>ash drive containing medical records of about 10,000 individuals were stolen in Detroit [25]. A standalone PHR as a Web-based service may be hacked, exposing patients' health information to unauthorized access. To use PHR, one of the major barriers consumers have to overcome is their concern over information privacy. Patients may refuse to have their health records digitized due to privacy concerns [2]. A national survey conducted by the California Healthcare Foundation found that 67% of people are concerned about the privacy of their personal medical records [5]. Therefore, information privacy should play a key role in consumers decision to use standalone PHR.

Consumers' privacy-related decisions have been widely considered to arise from a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analyses or a “privacy calculus” [16,19]. In this study, we adopted a social contract perspective to understand the privacy calculus in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individuals' decision to use standalone PHR. The privacy calculus is posited to be embedded in a trust-enabled social contract between the PHR vendor and the consumer re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the level of perceived control the consumer has over the privacy of their health data. In particular, our research questions are: 1) what are the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in the privacy calculus that factor into individuals' decision to use standalone PHR? 2) how does perceived privacy control act as a fairness lever adjusting the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoff analysis? and 3) how do preexisting trust beliefs in online vendors in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals' adoption decision?

## 2. Theoretical foundation

Prior studies have suggested that the effect of information privacy is malleable with situational stimuli [2,37]. Unable to achieve absolute information privacy, consumers often make a situational tradeoff when deciding whether to disclose their information to receive certain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. For example, online shoppers would have to disclose some personal information to complete ecommerce transactions. Similarly for standalone PHR, people would need to agree to build their medical pro<sup>fi</sup>les online and share them with healthcare providers to receive necessary medical care. It is important to examine individuals' intention to use standalone PHR in an exchange context as a privacy calculus involving assessments of competing exchange bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy risks. Individuals need to weigh the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of PHR against risks of storing and managing their health information over the Internet. They would be more likely to use standalone PHR if the privacy risks could be overridden by the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of PHR. We further integrate this calculus perspective with that of a social contract to better understand the privacy cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoffs involving highly sensitive personal health data and the adoption of embryonic health IT. The perspective of a social contract allows us to have a <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained examination of the privacy calculus speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the exchange of highly sensitive personal health information and incorporate the role of trust and fairness into the privacy calculus framework. Thus, we propose that the privacy calculus associated with health data disclosure is embedded in a social contract with trust as the central bond and privacy control as a lever signaling the procedural fairness of health information exchange.

## 2.1. Information privacy and calculus perspective

Information privacy refers to the ability of individuals to control when, how, and to what extent their personal information is exchanged with and used by others [16,50,55]. The issue of information privacy arises when information is exchanged to enable the primary transaction involving acquisition of products or services. During the information exchange, consumers' decision of whether to share private information or not involves a privacy calculus, where privacy risks are weighed against exchange bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [16]. The concept of a privacy calculus has received consistent empirical support in prior research [19,37,60]. However, speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of privacy calculus and their effect on privacy decisions may vary depending on the context of technologies. In this study, we further tailor the privacy calculus to the context of adoption standalone PHR. In particular, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and empirically tested several bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of PHR technology to <sup>fi</sup>nd speci<sup>fi</sup>c bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that factor into individuals' decision to use standalone PHR. In addition, people are particularly sensitive about the privacy of their health records [2]. Personal health records in digital form may aggravate people's privacy concern over the potential misuse of their health records. Thus, the decisions to adopt standalone PHR would involve a highly salient privacy calculus in which consumers actively assess the competing privacy risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 2.2. Information exchange and social contract

Besides the calculative assessment of competing risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, personal information exchange is also affected by the fairness of the social contract, when the exchange involves unknown consequences [16,36,37,41]. The underlying assumption of a social contract is bounded moral rationality, i.e. “individual moral agents lack the information, time, and emotional strength to make perfect judgments” [20, p. 18]. The disclosure of personal health information to companies providing standalone PHR is highly susceptible to such bounded moral rationality. Individuals often do not have complete information for judging the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of using PHR. For example, the vendor may disclose the health information to a third party without the awareness of patients or use patients' information to conduct marketing activities not authorized by patients. Thus, the uncertain nature of personal health information exchange is consistent with the assumptions of the social contract, demanding the existence of an implicit social contract to govern information exchange.

A social contract consists of shared norms or understanding about the rights and responsibilities between two parties in an exchange relationship [21]. The underlying norms in a social contract are context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, i.e. varying with the situation of the exchange. For information exchange, some basic norms identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior studies are organizations' social obligations to respect consumer information privacy, [30] and the understanding of risks and returns in the exchange by the two parties entering into the social contract [9]. Therefore, the social contract governing information exchange involves a privacyrelated cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis or has the privacy calculus built into it. Alternatively, we can view the privacy calculus as being embedded in a social contract.

The rights and responsibilities in a social contract are neither de<sup>fi</sup>ned explicitly in advance nor enforced through laws. Instead, a social contract is implicit, which is formulated and executed in the context of a cooperative relationship built on trust [30]. Trust is an essential element of a social contract. Without trust, shared norms or the social contract may not form between two parties as consumers are less likely to believe that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will respect the rights and responsibilities in a social contract and more likely to think that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will conduct opportunistic behaviors. All these increase the uncertainties of information exchange, causing consumers to further adjust their assessments of risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts or even avoid the exchange. In the context of adopting standalone PHR, the role of trust in the social contract becomes particularly salient as personal health data, such as medications, illnesses, and history of medical conditions, have high sensitivity. When consumers do not trust the PHR vendors, they may not even engage in the privacy calculus.

Recent studies in information privacy have also integrated justice theory into the social contract concept [16,36,37]. In particular, the concept of exchange fairness derived from justice theory, is considered a key norm in the social contract governing information exchange [37]. The social contract regulating information exchange should also be a fair social contract. Lack of fairness will break the social contract. For example, the social contract may be considered violated when consumers are not granted control over whom their personal health information is shared with. In addition, exchange fairness acts as an important lever adjusting the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis or privacy calculus [16,36,37]. People were found to be less concerned about the risks to their information privacy when fair information practices (FIPs) were implemented [14]. FIPs are “procedures that provide individuals with control over the disclosure and subsequent use of their personal information and govern the interpersonal treatment that consumers receive” [16, p. 330]. The perceived level of control over health information, i.e. privacy control, serves as an important basis for Internet users to form their perceptions of procedural fairness [49]. Privacy control may be particularly salient for the adoption of standalone PHR as it empowers consumers with increased level of control of their personal health information. Therefore, in this study, we use perceived privacy control as a proxy for perceived fairness of information privacy procedures to understand the effect of privacy control on individuals' privacyrelated cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoff analysis when they decide whether to adopt standalone PHR.

From the above, we posit that privacy-related behaviors are driven by an assessment of competing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy risks, i.e. a privacy calculus. Due to uncertainties in disclosing personal health information, the privacy calculus involved in health IT adoption is embedded in a trust-enabled fair social contract. Trust and perceived privacy controls are two vital elements in this trust-enabled fair social contract, with both in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoff analysis.

## 3. Research mode

The research model (Fig. 1) shows how individuals' intention to use standalone PHR is driven by a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoff analysis embedded in a trust-enabled fair social contract. The model suggests that individuals' intention to use standalone PHR could be increased in three ways: 1) enhancing consumers' trust beliefs in vendors providing standalone PHR; 2) providing suf<sup>fi</sup>cient bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as easier access to test results and increased quality of health care; and 3) increasing perceived privacy control over health information.

## 3.1. PHR benefit

The social contract governing the information exchange entails an assessment of risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. As stated earlier, consumers will participate in the social contract, i.e. involved in disclosing personal information, as long as the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts exceed the risks [15]. Privacy-related risks have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the major reason why many people hide their medical information [5]. Moreover, digital health information tends to have higher privacy risks than paperbased records [2]. A higher level privacy risk would lead consumers to expect substantial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from PHR if they are to overcome their fear of privacy risks and consider using standalone PHR. Some of the most important bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for patients and other consumers are increased access to credible health related data, improved communication and increased connectedness between patients and caregivers [51]. Perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of standalone PHR should increase patients intention to relinquish some privacy in return for the utility from PHR. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1. Perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t has a positive impact on patients' intention to use standalone PHR.

## 3.3. Trust belief

Trust belief, consisting of a set of speci<sup>fi</sup>c beliefs about integrity, benevolence, and competence, refers to one's perception about the trustworthiness of a person or institution [26]. Trust plays a critical role in the formulation and execution of the social contract involving uncertainties [30]. With respect to the adoption of standalone PHR, consumers not only face uncertainties from potential opportunistic behaviors of PHR vendors but also uncertainties in the capability, reliability and security of the technology, as PHR is still in its early adoption stage. Thus, consumers often do not have complete information to judge the risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, causing them to rely on the trustworthiness of vendors to dynamically adjust their expected level of privacy risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using the technology.

Various studies have found that trust beliefs play an important role in mitigating perceived risks in transactions involving uncertainties [33,41]. Following the concept of social contract, when consumers trust a <sup>fi</sup>rm, they tend to believe that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will share their values about information privacy [30] and is less likely to engage in opportunistic behaviors, i.e. violating the social obligations of respecting consumer information privacy. Therefore, consumers who trust vendors providing standalone PHR are more likely to anticipate low privacy risks.

H3. Trust belief has a negative impact on individuals' perceived privacy risks.

A social contract also includes norms about expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Consumers who believe in the integrity, benevolence and competence of vendors providing standalone PHR would be more likely to think that those vendors share their understanding about bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and are capable of achieving the expected level of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts enclosed in the social contract. Conversely, if vendors of standalone PHR cannot be trusted, consumers will feel more uncertain in receiving the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and lower their expectation about bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. To the extent that it is a subjective guarantee for enforcing an explicit social contract, trust helps to assure customers that they will receive the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from vendors of standalone PHR. Trust may also exert a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence over individuals' intention to use the technology. The initial adoption stage of standalone PHR compounded with high sensitive nature of personal health information could greatly increase the uncertainties in estimating costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. As a result, consumers may primarily rely on the trustworthiness of PHR vendors as information to participate in the social contract and make their privacy-related decisions. Therefore, in a context marked by very high uncertainty, consumers are likely to use trust as the direct route to make their adoption decisions. Individuals with no prior trust in vendors of PHR may not even engage in the social contract and may choose to avoid using the technology directly. Thus,

## 3.4. Perceived privacy control

As discussed earlier, the decision to use standalone PHR demands a fair social contract, with exchange fairness as one of the grounding norms. The cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis is subject to the adjustment by the exchange fairness [16,36,37]. High fairness would cause consumers to focus on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the information change while low fairness would alert consumers about potential risks involved in entering the social contract [16]. In the context of consumer privacy, a central fairness element is one's ability to control the disclosure and subsequent use of his or her personal information. Due to the increased uncertainty of online transactions, Internet users are particularly concerned about their ability to control their personal information [41].

Perceived privacy control is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the perceived level of control over the disclosure and subsequent use of one's personal information [59]. Patients may rely on the perceived control over their health information disclosure and usage as a signal to assess the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and potential privacy risks they may gain from using standalone PHR. A high level of perceived control over information practices could assure consumers that the organization is less likely to behave opportunistically, causing them to form more favorable judgments about the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of PHR. For example, patients would perceive PHR to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial if they have control over sharing their health information with physicians and their family members. On the other hand, they would perceive a high level of privacy risk when they feel they have little control over the collection and subsequent usage of their health information stored in standalone PHR. Therefore, we have:

H6. Perceived control has a positive impact on individuals' perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

H7. Perceived control has a negative impact on individuals' perceived privacy risk.

## 3.5. Individual differences

Besides situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c stimuli in the environment, individual differences may also play a role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individuals' beliefs and subsequent use of information technologies [53]. In this study, we are interested in how patients' general privacy concern and their prior privacy invasion experience in<sup>fl</sup>uence their privacyrelated cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t tradeoff analysis. General privacy concern is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual's general tendency to worry about information privacy [41]. It is a type of individual difference, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting one's disposition to value privacy. Those with strong concerns for their information privacy are likely to perceive more privacy risks from information disclosure. Thus, we have:

H8. General privacy concern increases individuals' perceived privacy risk.

Previous privacy invasion experience re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual's direct experience of being a victim of privacy invasion. Such experiences have been posited to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their future context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c privacy beliefs and technology acceptance [60]. For the adoption of standalone PHR, the effect of previous privacy invasion experiences may be particularly important due to the high level of uncertainty and the highly sensitive nature of personal health data. Individuals with frequent experiences of privacy invasions may use trust as the primary basis to assess privacy risks, and give less or little consideration to perceived privacy control. The effect of perceived privacy control could even become even super<sup>fl</sup>uous for such individuals. Conversely, those with little or no prior privacy invasion experience are more likely to enter the fairness-based social contract, relying on privacy control or exchange fairness to assess the level of privacy risks in using standalone PHR. Thus, we have

H9. Previous privacy invasion experience moderates the relationship between perceived privacy control and perceived privacy risk.

H9a. For those with infrequent privacy invasion experience, perceived privacy control has a negative impact on perceived privacy risk.

H9b. For those with frequent privacy invasion experience, perceived privacy control has no impact on perceived privacy risk.

## 3.6. Covariates

The research model also incorporates three additional individual differences that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence an individual's intention to use standalone PHR. They are age, gender, and poor health condition. Prior studies have found that older consumers and female consumers are more concerned about their information privacy [8,43]. Those in poor health conditions are more motivated to use electronic health records (EHRs) to improve the management of their health [2]. An individual's health condition determines the degree of personal relevance of PHR to an individual. Those in poor health condition are likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from using PHR than those with very good health and, therefore, may be more likely to adopt standalone PHR.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Study design and procedure

The research model was tested using an online survey. Students at a major northern U.S. university were recruited as subjects for this study. The recruiting message was distributed to about 265 students enrolling in general business core courses. Extra credit worth 1 to 2% of their total grade was used as an incentive for their participation. A total of 192 usable responses (91 males and 101 females) were received and used in our data analysis. The age of the subjects ranges from 19 to 51 with an average of 25. Ninety two percent of them have used the Internet for 4 or more years. Prior studies suggest that the younger generation (that includes students) is the primary population using the Internet for health services [4,32]. The latest national survey by Pew Research Center [24] has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed certain demographic groups as more likely to use online health information, including those adults between the ages of 18 and 49 and those with at least some college education. All these suggest that the demographics of our student sample align well the dominant population using online health information. A recent study also suggests that young people (primarily students) show stronger interest in the functions of a standalone PHR than older people [54]. Moreover, although the younger population is generally believed to be healthy, they still suffer from various health issues [4]. We further analyzed the health conditions of the student subjects used in this study. Eighteen percent of them reported major pains and discomfort for an extended period of time with moderate to high frequency. Twenty six percent of them had moderate to very severe chronic conditions. All these suggest that the use of student subjects is appropriate for examining the adoption of Web-based standalone PHR.

At the beginning of the online survey, subjects were asked to read an informed consent form. If the subject agreed to participate, s/he was then presented with a page describing and de<sup>fi</sup>ning key terms mentioned in the survey such as personal health record, personal health record systems (PHR), standalone PHR and Integrated PHR. In particular, we provided detailed description about standalone PHR including examples of current commercial products (i.e. Microsoft HealthVault), major utility and the typical information stored in PHR. The subjects were then required to answer the questionnaire.

## 4.2. Variable measurement

Most of the variables were measured using existing published scales with slight rewording to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the context of our study. Perceived privacy control was adapted from Xu [58]. Trust belief was measured using scales developed by Gefen et al. [26]. Perceived privacy risk was adapted from the instrument by Malhotra et al. [41]. Three items developed by Malhotra et al. [41] were used for measuring general information privacy concern. Past privacy invasion experience was measured using the scale developed by Smith et al. [48], which consists of a single question gauging how frequently subjects have personally fallen victims to privacy invasion. Intention to use standalone PHR was measured by scales after Malhotra et al. [41] and MacKenzie and Spreng [40]. Personal health condition was measured using the scale by Bansal et al. [4]. In addition, four questions developed in this study together with one question from Whetstone and Goldsmith [56] were used to measure perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of standalone PHR. The perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t scale was implemented as a formative instrument as an increase in one type of bene<sup>fi</sup>t such as better access may not associate with the increase in other types of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as better communication with physicians. The other scales were operationalized as re<sup>fl</sup>ective ones. All these constructs are measured on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale. The detailed measures for each construct are provided in Appendix A.

## 5. Data analysis

Partial least squares (PLS) analysis, a component-based structural equation modeling (SEM) technique, was applied to test the measurement model and research hypotheses. PLS is more amenable for analyzing formative constructs such as perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t construct in our study than covariance-based SEM techniques such as LISREL. Moreover, PLS does not assume a multivariate normal distribution and interval scales [12], which works well for controlling binary variables such as gender. The minimum sample size required by PLS is ten times the largest number of formative indicators or ten times the largest number of paths leading to an endogenous re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct [11]. For our research model, the maximum number of formative indicators is <sup>fi</sup>ve and the maximum number of paths entering an endogenous re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct is six, including the control variables. Therefore, a sample size of 192 is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for us to analyze the research model using PLS technique.

## 5.1. Measurement model

We applied different procedures to test the measurement quality of re<sup>fl</sup>ective and formative constructs as they require different validity and reliability tests [18]. For example, composite reliability measures are not applicable to formative scales as items of a formative construct are not expected to be highly correlated. We followed the suggestions by Chin [11] and Diamantopoulos et al. [18] to test the measurement quality of formative constructs. We <sup>fi</sup>rst checked the correlations among items of a formative construct, which should not be highly correlated (above 0.8) [45]. The absolute correlations among <sup>fi</sup>ve items of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t were found to be moderate, varying from 0.46 to 0.64. The variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) was then computed to test the level of multicollinearity among items of a formative scale as excess multicollinearity could make the instrument less stable and raise serious concerns about the validity of the formative instrument [34]. We found that the VIF values vary from 1.8 to 2.2 for the <sup>fi</sup>ve items gauging perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which are far below the cutoff level of 10.0. Therefore, multicollinearity was not a concern for the formative perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t scale.

The measurement quality of re<sup>fl</sup>ective latent constructs was evaluated based on their convergent validity, reliability, and discriminant validity. Convergent validity re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which indicators measuring the same construct overlap. Convergent validity is suggested if factor loadings are 0.60 or higher [3] and each item loads signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly on its latent construct [27]. We found that all items except two items have loadings equal to 0.6 or higher and load signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly on their respective latent constructs. Therefore, we removed the two items with loading below 0.6 from the following data analysis and reran the measurement model. The resulting scales show adequate convergent validity (Table 1). We then assessed scale reliability using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) measures. All re<sup>fl</sup>ective scales in this study were found to have CR above 0.7 and AVE above 0.5, exceeding the cutoff criteria suggested by Bagozzi and Yi [3].

Discriminant validity re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which indicators tapping different constructs are distinct from each other. To test discriminant validity, we examined the loading and cross-loading matrix (Table 1) and the correlation matrix (Table 2). Discriminant validity is established when all measurement items of those re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs load more strongly on their respective underlying construct than on other constructs. In addition, the square root of AVE of each re<sup>fl</sup>ective latent construct should be higher than the correlations between that construct and any other constructs [23]. As shown in Tables 1 and 2, both criteria are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed by those re<sup>fl</sup>ective latent constructs in our model.

As with all cross-sectional studies, common method variance (CMV) may bias the results of our study. To test the degree of CMV, we followed the suggestions by Podsakoff et al. [46] and performed both Harmon's single factor test and partial correlation procedure. In Harmon's single-factor test [46], all measurement items of those latent constructs were loaded into a principal component factor analysis. The unrotated factor solution consisted of seven factors with the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounting for 28.5% of the variance. Therefore, no single factor could explain the majority of the variance, suggesting that the data set does not have substantial amount of CMV. The partial correlation procedure was implemented using marker-variable technique suggested by Lindell and Whitney [38], which not only provides an estimate of the magnitude of CMV but also assesses its impact on correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients among those latent constructs. The second smallest positive correlation among the manifest variables was selected as a more conservative estimate of CMV (i.e. $\Gamma _ { \mathrm { m } } ) ,$ , which was found to be 0.01. CMV-adjusted correlations among latent constructs were then computed by partialing out $\Gamma _ { \mathrm { { m } } }$ from the uncorrected correlations. The CMV-adjusted correlations were only slightly lower than the unadjusted correlations. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels for all correlations except the one between privacy concern and behavioral intention remain the same. The p-value for the correlation between privacy concern and behavioral intention only increases slightly from 0.05 to 0.06. This suggests that CMV is not an issue of concern for our data set.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key findings and limitations

In this study, we examined individuals' intention to use standalone PHR in an exchange context involving assessments of competing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy risks, i.e. privacy calculus. To address our research context marked by highly sensitive personal information and embryonic health IT, we proposed and empirically tested a trust-enabled social contract model to examine individuals' dynamic decision process involved in adopting standalone PHR. The results of our data analysis established the saliency of the privacy calculus in this decision process. Individuals will tend to adopt standalone PHR when their perceived privacy risks could be overridden by perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and trust. Among those competing factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing adoption decisions, perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t is the most prominent determinant based on the magnitude of path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. In particular, based on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of different types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, we found that better access to health information, better quality of healthcare and more effective management of one's own health are the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that factor into an individual's adoption decision.

In addition to the privacy calculus, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the essential role of trust belief in the social contract governing the exchange process. Pre-existing trust toward vendors providing standalone PHR (trust belief) is found to directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavioral intention as well as indirectly through adjusting perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t and privacy risk beliefs.

Therefore, our study suggests that pre-existing trust could be extended to new IT products or services offered by those companies. Such preexisting trust is not only critical for the initiation of a social contract but also for the dynamic adjustment of the privacy calculus at a later stage. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is different from the study by Luo et al. [39] examining the acceptance of mobile banking services. In their study, preexisting trust beliefs were found to have no effect on people's intention to use mobile banking service. There are two possible reasons accounting for the different effects of pre-existing trust in the acceptance of embryonic IT artifacts offered by existing vendors. In their study, vendors are banks which are not leaders in IT while we use Microsoft as exemplar vendors for providing standalone PHR. The pre-existing trust may be particularly effective in alleviating uncertainties inherent in embryonic IT artifacts if the new IT artifacts are offered by IT leaders such as Microsoft. Another reason is due to the difference in the underlying IT platforms of these two types of embryonic IT artifacts. Standalone PHR is Web-based with no requirements of open air transmission while mobile banking is deployed over wireless Internet platform which could not be fully safeguarded by banks.

The results of our study also suggest that perceived privacy control is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant fairness-based lever, which further adjusts individuals understanding of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy risks of adopting standalone PHR. A higher level of perceived privacy control was found to increase individuals' perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. In addition, interestingly, we found that the effect of perceived privacy control on perceived privacy risk is conditioned upon individuals' previous privacy invasion experience. Perceived privacy control signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces perceived privacy risks only for people with infrequent previous privacy invasion (b2.4 on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale). But, for those who frequently fall victims to privacy invasions, perceived privacy control is not factored into individuals' assessment of potential privacy risks. Instead, individuals' pre-existing trust, general privacy concern and direct privacy invasion experience may play a more important role in determining their perceived privacy risk.

Despite the support for our research model, this study has some potential limitations. First, although the student subjects are a natural part of the population for accepting standalone PHR, future studies could be conducted to test our model using a more general population or using speci<sup>fi</sup>c groups of people such as senior citizens, those with chronic conditions or those with stronger need for emergency care. The intention to use standalone PHR may vary with patients with different types of diseases. Second, our study focused on the role of privacy-related cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis in driving individuals' adoption decisions. Future studies could examine the effect of other types of risks such as performance risks and <sup>fi</sup>nancial risks. Third, we used Microsoft as exemplar vendors for standalone PHR. They are leaders in IT industry. Additional research is also needed to test the validity of our model on smaller IT companies or vendors of health IT in other industries such as WebMD. Fourth, this study only examined privacy control as the proxy for fairness levers adjusting the privacy levers. Future studies are also needed to directly measure fairness perceptions and test their impact on privacy calculus.

## 6.2. Implications for research

The results of the study have several important implications for research on information privacy and the adoption of embryonic IT artifacts. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the premise that patients' decisions to adopt standalone PHR is driven by a privacy calculus involving the assessment of competing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the technology and privacy risks. Although privacy risk is not a major factor considered in prevailing technology adoption studies [17], it is a highly salient factor for adopting standalone PHR and needs to be incorporated into future studies examining the adoption of other technologies involving the exchange of highly sensitive personal information.

Second, the results of the study suggest that the concept of social contract is a useful lens to examine privacy calculus in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. In this study, it allows us to expand our understanding of privacy calculus in a context marked by highly sensitive personal health information and embryonic IT artifacts such as the adoption of standalone PHR. Relying on the social contract lens, our model not only accounts for privacy calculus but also recognizes the role of trust belief and exchange fairness. Therefore, the concept of social contract could potentially advance our understanding of complex privacy decisions in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context involving high uncertainties. So far, to the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have empirically tested the privacy calculus as embedded in a trustedenabled fair social contract. Future studies could further apply our model to other privacy-related decisions involving high uncertainties.

Third, the results of our study highlight the central role of trust in a social contract governing the disclosure of highly sensitive personal information. In particular, we found that trust has both direct and indirect effects on individuals' adoption intention. The direct effect emphasizes the importance of trust in the formation and execution of a social contract. Without a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient pre-existing trust in the PHR vendors, the social contract may not even form and consumers may just choose to stay away from the technology due to highly sensitive nature of personal health data. The indirect effect of trust through the privacy calculus suggests that trust also helps individuals form and adjust their understandings of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of the exchange. Thus, the role of trust should not be ignored in the examination of the privacy calculus involving highly sensitive personal information. Otherwise, the results may be invalid or contradictory as the social contract underlying the privacy calculus may not even take hold.

Fourth, our results also shed some important insights on when pre-existing trust may be extended to emerging IT products or services. From the comparison with the study by Luo et al. [39] discussed in the prior subsection, the role of pre-existing trust seems to depend on the technical capability of the vendors in providing IT products or services. Pre-existing trust may not be extendable to new IT artifacts if the vendors are not perceived to be strong in providing IT products. Future studies are needed to further explore the effect of pre-existing trust in different contexts with different emerging IT artifacts.

Fifth, our results support the argument that the social contract governing health information exchange is a fair social contract where the privacy calculus is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by exchange fairness. Perceived privacy control acts as a fairness lever adjusting individuals' assessment of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy risks in using standalone PHR. Prior studies have also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed other fairness levers adjusting the privacy calculus in a variety of other research contexts such as the presence of a privacy policy and perceived relevance of information collected [36] in a general ecommerce context, and monetary compensation and government regulation [60] in the context of location-based services. These drivers may play a different role in the context of health information exchange. For example, monetary compensation may not be effective in enhancing patients' perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using health IT as “information exchange in conventional marketplace is governed by a social contract in which personal information cannot be clearly priced” [37, p. 62]. Therefore, future studies are needed to test the effect of these fairness drivers or identify new fairness drivers in the context of health information exchange.

Overall, this research contributes to the literature on information privacy and the adoption of embryonic health IT by proposing a trust-enabled fair social contract model. The model integrates the privacy calculus and social contract perspectives to account for the effect of trust and adjustment of perceived privacy control. The model expands our understanding of complex privacy decisions in a context marked by high information sensitivity and embryonic technologies.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study also have important practical implications for vendors providing embryonic health IT to patients. First, our study suggests that the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t of standalone PHR is the major factor that motivates individuals to adopt standalone PHR. Vendors could increase patients' adoption intention through educating them about the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of standalone PHR. For example, vendors may rely on media reports, current customers' testimonies on their Web site or work with physicians to increase patients' awareness of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of standalone PHR.

Second, by modeling perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as a formative construct, this research identi<sup>fi</sup>ed speci<sup>fi</sup>c bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that factor into patients' adoption decisions, including better access to health information, better healthcare quality and effective health management. Therefore, vendors would need to emphasize these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in their communication with patients. For example, vendors could provide video tutorials on how easily they could access their personal health information and how they could use their digital health records to monitor their health or receive medical recommendations.

Third, perceived privacy risk is a salient barrier to the adoption of embryonic health IT in a context marked by high information sensitivity. To mitigate the effect of perceived privacy risk, vendors of standalone PHR could take several approaches. Vendors should actively build and sustain their trustworthy reputation. The perceived integrity, benevolence and competence of a vendor in offering existing products or services can in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' desire to take privacy risks to try new products or services offered by the same vendor. In addition, vendors could alleviate the effect of perceived risk by providing patients with a high level of control over the privacy of their information. They should educate customers about privacy controls available to them, how they could control who to share their health information with and what information to share. However, vendors should be aware of the limitation of increasing perceived privacy control, which is not effective for individuals who have been invaded frequently in the past. The intriguing <sup>fi</sup>nding on the moderating effect of previous privacy invasion experience suggests that vendors should consider consumers' individual differences when implementing the above two approaches to alleviate consumers' privacy risks. Vendors shouldn't solely reply on providing high level of privacy control. Both building trustworthy reputation and providing high level of privacy control are necessary since the targeted customers have different experiences with privacy invasion.

## 7. Conclusion

Despite the rising research interest in emerging health IT, there is little theory-based empirical research in examining patients' adoption decisions. Also, there is a lack of consideration of the adoption context, i.e. high information sensitivity and high uncertainty from embryonic technologies. To <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gap, this study integrated privacy calculus and social contract perspectives and proposed a trust-enabled fair social contract model to understand patients' adoption decision.

The model not only accounts for the privacy-related cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis but also the critical role of trust and exchange fairness in our research context. Individuals' decision to use standalone PHR is found to be driven by a privacy calculus embedded in a trust-enabled fair social contract. Trust belief is not only an important prerequisite for patients to consider the initial adoption but also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the execution of the social contract through in<sup>fl</sup>uencing patients' perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and privacy risks. Perceived privacy control acts as a fairness lever that enhances the positive impact of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and mitigates the negative impact of perceived privacy risks. In addition, the study found that the effect of perceived privacy control is conditioned upon an individual's prior privacy invasion experience.
