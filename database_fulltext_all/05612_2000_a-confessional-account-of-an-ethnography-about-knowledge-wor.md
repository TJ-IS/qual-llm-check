# A CONFESSIONAL ACCOUNT OF AN ETHNOGRAPHY ABOUT KNOWLEDGE WORK<sup>1</sup>

By: Ulrike Schultze Cox School of Business Southern Methodist University P.O. Box 750333 Dallas, Texas 75275-0333 U.S.A. uschultz@mail.cox.smu.edu

## Abstract

Information systems research has traditionally focused on information as an object that serves as input to decision making. Such a perspective attends mainly to the use of information. Increasingly, however, organizations are concerned about the production of information. This paper focuses on the work of producing informational objects, an activity central to knowledge work. Based on data collected during an eight-month ethnographic study of three groups of knowledge workers—computer system administrators, competitive intelligence analysts, and librarians—I explore the informing practices they relied upon. These are identified as ex-pressing, monitoring, and translating. Common to these informing practices is the knowledge workers’ endeavor to balance subjectivity and objectivity, where subjectivity is a necessary part of doing value adding work and objectivity promises workers authority and a sense of security.

Recognizing that researchers are knowledge workers too, I draw on my own experiences as an ethnographic researcher to identify parallels between my informing practices and those of the knowledge workers I studied. These parallels are intended to challenge the taken-for-granted assumptions underlying scientific practice. I adopt a confessional genre of representation for this purpose.

Keywords: Social science, ethnography, evaluation criteria, practice, confessional genre of representation, objectivity, subjectivity, reflexivity, information, knowledge creation

## Introduction

The data-processing view of information, in which information is regarded as an input to decision making and the decision maker is considered a passive recipient of this information, is being challenged in the information systems literature. Scholars including Lyytinen and Ngwenyama (1992), Boland and Tenkasi (1995), and Ngwenyama and Lee (1997) have criticized this view for being inadequate in the context of communication technologies such as e-mail and groupware, which require both the sender and the receiver of a message to play an active role in the information exchange. Furthermore, the data processing view is criticized for not considering the need to create new information and knowledge, both of which are increasingly important resources in the information age and the post-industrial era (e.g., Bell 1976; Davenport and Prusak 1998; Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995). Nonaka (1994, p. 14) expresses well the need for information creation: “Any organization that dynamically deals with a changing environment ought not only to process information efficiently but also create information and knowledge.”

The knowledge management literature has focused research attention on the creation of information (e.g., Nonaka 1994), but it has not paid much attention to a detailed exploration of knowledge work, i.e., the work of producing and reproducing information and knowledge. Instead, many of the knowledge management theories focus on the classification of knowledge into different types (e.g., Hedlund 1994; Spender 1996). Despite these efforts, a robust definition of knowledge remains elusive and, due to its metaphysical nature, it is very difficult to study knowledge itself. This is why Blackler et al. (1993, p. 1017) recommend that research on knowledge work be focused on what people do, i.e., their work practices, rather than what they know. This suggests that research on the work practices of knowledge workers is required.

Practice-oriented research investigates what people “actually” do rather than what they say they do or what they ought to be doing (Pickering 1992). It focuses on the socially situated, meaningful and intentional actions that follow certain general principles of procedure (Turner 1994). A practice orientation also takes into account not only the objectified social structures within which actions occur, but also the construction of these structures (Bourdieu 1977). An understanding of the work practices is assigned increasing importance because the field of information systems has traditionally been plagued by high implementation failure rates.<sup>2</sup> These high failure rates are frequently attributed to developers’ poor understanding of work practices (Grudin 1994; Suchman 1995). In order to develop a better understanding of knowledge work and the role of knowledge management technologies in it, research on the work of producing information in practice is necessary.

## Research Objectives

The aim of this research is to explore knowledge work in situ in order to develop a grounded understanding of what producing information entails and what knowledge work is like. Many have called for the study of work practices in their mundane, day-to-day detail, especially using methods, such as ethnography, that are sensitive to the social conditions of work (e.g., Barley 1988; Blomberg et al. 1993; Brown and Duguid 1991). Heeding these calls, this research relies on data collected during an eight-month ethnographic study in a U.S.-based, Fortune 500 manufacturing firm. It investigates the informing practices of three groups of knowledge workers: computer systems administrators, competitive intelligence analysts, and librarians. Both the rich descriptions of these professionals’ work lives and the theoretical insights generated by this research have implications for determining the appropriateness of knowledge management technologies.

Recognizing that scientists are knowledge workers too (Bell 1976; Ruhleder 1994), I seek to leverage both my own (the ethnographer’s) and the readers’ experiences with information and knowledge creation. This is accomplished through a representational genre known as confessional (Van Maanen 1988) or vulnerable writing (Behar 1996). Confessional writing requires the ethnographer to give a self-revealing and selfreflexive account of the research process. The ethnographer designs such an autobiographical exposition to draw readers into the text so that the assumptions and practices of the “foreign culture” serve as a mirror in which the reader’s own assumptions and practices are reflected. The confessional ethnographer intends this mirroring to challenge readers to examine their own informing practices.

Another objective of this paper is to establish criteria for evaluating ethnographic research in general and the confessional genre of representation in particular. For this reason, I outline these criteria and I assess this research according to them. Furthermore, by adopting a novel genre of ethnographic writing, I seek to encourage other IS researchers to experiment with their styles of representation, as these may indeed illuminate theoretical issues in novel ways (Marcus and Fischer 1986).

This paper proceeds as follows. First, I review the literature on knowledge work to establish the production and reproduction of information and knowledge as a central activity in knowledge work. As part of this discussion, I draw distinctions between information and knowledge. Then, I discuss the characteristics of ethnographic research and confessional writing. This is followed by a description of the research setting and the method used. In the discussion of the research results, I compare the predominant work practices of each of the three groups of knowledge workers with my own informing practices. I then follow this with a discussion section, which will highlight the knowledge workers’ effort to balance subjectivity and objectivity. Before drawing out the implications of these results, I assess my work with respect to the evaluation criteria for ethnographic research and confessional writing.

## Knowledge Work

Knowledge workers form a special class of whitecollar worker. This class includes professionals,<sup>3</sup> consultants, technicians,<sup>4</sup> scientists, intellectuals, and managers (Bell 1976; Quinn 1992; Ruhleder 1994; Wuthnow and Shrum 1983). Knowledge work is described as having the following characteristics:

it produces and reproduces information and knowledge (Machlup 1962; Stehr 1994);

unlike physical blue-collar work, knowledge work is cerebral (Davis et al. 1991; Davis and Nauman 1997), and involves the manipulation of abstractions and symbols that both represent the world and are objects in the world (Fuller 1992);

unlike service work, which is frequently scripted (Leidner 1993), knowledge work defies routinization and requires the use of creativity in order to produce idiosyncratic, esoteric knowledge (Drucker 1993; Ledford 1995; Sviokla 1996); and

it requires a formal education, i.e., abstract, technical and theoretical knowledge (Frenkel et al. 1995; Starbuck 1992).

Defining knowledge work in more absolute terms is difficult because the concept of knowledge is ambiguous.<sup>5</sup> Definitions of knowledge frequently draw upon distinctions between information and knowledge. For instance, for Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995, p. 58) the difference is one of flows and stocks (also Choudhury and Sampler 1997; Starbuck 1992). Information is a flow of meaning and significance that changes a stock of knowledge. This change can occur either by adding to the stock of knowledge or by restructuring it in some way.

The notion of knowledge as stock, i.e., a fairly stable accumulation of a substance, implies that knowledge can be created only once (e.g., Stehr

1992). This is because knowledge, once produced, is never used up (even though its value typically dissipates over time). As a public good, it can be shared or rented but never completely owned or consumed (Spender 1996). There is also fairly widespread agreement in the management and information systems literatures that knowledge is closely related to action. For instance, Stehr (1994) defines knowledge as the capacity to act; Fuller (1992) sees knowledge as anything an agent needs to determine his/her market strategy; Davenport and his colleagues (Davenport and Prusak 1998; DeLong et al. 1996) view knowledge as the combination of human context and information that makes information actionable; and Drucker (1993, p. 210) maintains that knowledge “is always embodied in a person; carried by a person; taught and passed on by a person; used or misused by a person.”

In summary then (please also refer to Appendix A), information and knowledge are in a dialectic, mutually constitutive relationship. Wikstrom and Norman (1994), for instance, define information as objective facts and the lowest form of knowledge. Information is a flow that is not only derived from the stock of knowledge but that also has the capacity to change it. A person can perceive knowledge only once it is instantiated in either human action or information. It is for this reason that this research focuses on the work practices, i.e., actions, of knowledge workers as they produce information. This focus is captured in a concept that I here label informing practices.

Since this research seeks to explore the work practices of knowledge workers, we need to turn our attention to the activities that define their work. Machlup (1962), one of the first to use the knowledge work label, defined knowledge work as the production and transmission of knowledge.<sup>6</sup> Stehr (1994) develops this definition further by proposing that knowledge work is the production and reproduction of information and knowledge. He notes that the concepts of information transmission or transfer assume that the meaning of information is so transparent and immutable that it requires no interpretation prior to the act of passing it on. Arguing that the transfer of information is generally a transformative activity in the sense that the information is acquired, manipulated, and organized before it is communicated, Stehr regards information transfer as a productive activity in which a worker’s knowledge is applied and the information is changed in some way. In this research, I adopt Stehr’s definition of knowledge work as the production and reproduction of information.

In the knowledge management literature, much attention is paid to the challenge of discovering an individual’s tacit knowledge and then converting it into an informational object that can be communicated to others and stored in organizational routines and databases (e.g., Hedlund 1994). Tacit knowledge, i.e., the kind of knowledge that people possess but that they are unable to articulate, is a form of subjective know-how that allows people to act. Examples of actions that rely heavily on tacit knowledge include kneading dough (Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995), riding a bike (Cook and Brown 1999) or hitting a baseball (Davenport and Prusak 1998, p. 71). Even though these examples all entail physical actions that make tacit knowledge appear particularly applicable to craft work, Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995, p. 8) maintain that mental models, on which knowledge workers depend, also qualify as tacit knowledge.

It is noteworthy that knowledge management’s celebration of the individual’s idiosyncratic, subjective, and tacit knowledge in the creation of information and knowledge stands in stark contrast to science’s view of knowledge production. In scientific inquiry, objectivity is revered and regarded as a sign of excellence (Churchman 1971, p. 149). Objective and universal knowledge is preferred over the subjective and the situated. Hard quantification is preferred over soft qualification (e.g., Burrell and Morgan 1979; Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991). Objectivity is associated with representation that corresponds to an external reality and freedom from the distorting perceptua bias of the individual thinking agent (Shapiro 1997). Subjectivity, on the other hand, signifies the emotional, personal, individual, and idiosyncratic side of human perception and knowledge. It refers to social phenomena which originate and exist within the observer’s mind, and which are impossible to check directly.

Even though the role of tacit knowledge and subjective insight in science has been recognized (e.g., Polanyi 1966), the scientific agenda has been to minimize their effect on knowledge production (Deetz 1996). This agenda is rooted in the subject-object dualism, which posits that reality is made up of objects that stand in fundamental opposition to subjects, i.e., individuals with minds and feelings. This scientistic agenda has been successfully challenged, however, by social constructionism (Berger and Luckman 1966) and work on the sociology of science and knowledge (e.g., Kuhn 1970). Social constructionism posits that neither a position of absolute subjectivity nor of absolute objectivity is possible. Instead, both become relative positions in the intersubjective social consciousness. Thus the scientistic subject-object dualism is replaced by a subject-object duality, i.e., a view of subjects and objects as inextricably intertwined and mutually constitutive.

Research in the sociology of science concerns itself with the social processes and conventions that scientific communities establish as they convert accidents and the subjective insights of the lone knowledge worker into objective facts and universal “truths” (Latour 1987). Seen from the perspective of the sociology of knowledge, the difficulty in information and knowledge creation lies in convincing others of the reliability and validity of a knowledge worker’s knowledge claims. This suggests that the articulation of tacit knowledge may not only be made difficult by the individual’s inability to articulate it, but also by the constraints that others in the social community impose on the articulation. These constraints include the format that knowledge needs to take in order to be acceptable to others, the language or symbol system within which it must operate, and the evidence that knowledge workers must provide to support their new knowledge claims. Subjective insights and tacit knowledge must thus be articulated in a way that is consistent with a community's expectations of what constitutes informational objects.

In summary, knowledge work is the production and reproduction of informational objects. It is human work that requires a feeling and thinking agent who brings his/her subjectivity (in its relative, intersubjective sense) and tacit knowledge to bear on the informational object that is the product of knowledge work. For these informational objects to be useful vehicles of transferring knowledge that facilitate control at a distance, their recipients need to be convinced of their reliability and validity, i.e., their objectivity (again in its relative, intersubjective sense).

Ethnographic research and confessional writing are particularly well suited to the study and representation of knowledge work because they make the role of the researcher as knowledge worker explicit. In the next section, ethnographic research as knowledge work is discussed. Furthermore, the representational genre of confessional writing is introduced.

## Ethnography and Confessional Writing

Ethnography is an anthropological research method that relies on first-hand observations made by a researcher immersed over an extended period of time in a culture, with which he/she is unfamiliar (Agar 1986; Atkinson and Hammersley 1994; Hammersley 1992). The ethnographic method requires the researcher to closely observe, record, and engage in the daily life of another culture, and then write about it in descriptive detail (Marcus and Fischer 1986, p. 18). Confronted with situations and ways of life foreign to them, ethnographers assume a sensemaking and learning role rather than a scientistic hypothesistesting one (Kirk and Miller 1986). Adopting a flexible and frequently unstructured research design, they attempt to suspend their own frames of reference and allow themselves to be led by the social setting to new and unexpected insights. Agar (1986, p. 23) describes the research process as a movement from breakdowns of established understandings and taken-for-granted assumptions, through a resolution of such breakdowns, to the construction of a coherent narrative.

Ethnographers’ reliance on challenges to their own assumptions for the generation of new insights implies that the researcher acts as his/her own research instrument. Ethnographic research is thus highly dependent on the individual’s unique knowledge and experience, and his/her actions as a thinking agent who brings his/her subjectivity to bear on the construction of information and knowledge. Nevertheless, like all scientific research, ethnographies are expected to meet standards of objectivity,<sup>7</sup> albeit an epistemologica rather than an ontological objectivity (Shapiro 1997). Ethnographers as knowledge workers are thus confronted with potentially conflicting demands. In their role as instrument they rely on their personal experience and subjective engagement with phenomena in the field to generate insights, whereas in their role as scientist they need to convince the scientific community of the trans-situational (Preston 1986) and reliable nature of these very phenomena. This suggests that ethnographers need to find ways of balancing subjectivity and objectivity.<sup>8</sup>

A confessional (Van Maanen 1988) or vulnerable (Behar 1996) account of ethnographic research highlights the ethnographer’s experience of doing fieldwork by giving a self-reflexive and selfrevealing account of the research process. It presents the ethnographer’s role as a research instrument and exposes the ethnographer rendering his/her actions, failings, motivations, and assumptions open to public scrutiny and critique. By revealing themselves in their confessional writings, ethnographers put themselves on a par with their “subjects” who typically feel exposed and criticized by ethnographic texts (Miles 1979; Whyte 1996). The criticism that “confessional ethnographers” level against themselves also reflects on their community. By juxtaposing their assumptions and practices with those of the “foreign” culture, ethnographers prompt their readers to do the same. Thus cultural critique (Marcus and Fischer 1986), i.e., using the research to reflect on the world of the researcher and reader, is achieved.

There are different genres of confessional writing. The style chosen for the research presented here is based on Behar’s (1996) vulnerable writing. It represents a confessional genre in which the ethnographer’s experience of doing the research is thoroughly interlaced with the “actual” ethnographic material (for examples, see Wolf 1992; Behar 1993). Interlacing self-reflexive and autobiographical material with actual ethnographic material is particularly effective for achieving cultural critique because readers are constantly challenged to examine their own practices and assumptions while they are learning about the practices and assumptions of others.

In the following section, which describes the research method, the self-revealing and selfreflexive nature of the confessional genre of representation chosen for this research will become evident.

## Research Setting and Method

## Selecting the Field Site

Arguing that knowledge workers looking for a technology to help them improve their information creating processes represented an optimal field site, I identified a technology to lead me to a situation in which I could study people engaged in creating information and knowledge. The technology I chose as a guide was KnowMor,<sup>9</sup> a knowledge management technology that resided on a Lotus Notes platform. My prior experience with research on Lotus Notes influenced this decision. KnowMor was built on the premise that news only constituted information when a group of people agreed that it had relevance to their organization. Its design therefore embodied an informing process of “alert—assess—escalate,” also referred to as the “gatekeeper model.” A “gatekeeper” designated a person who either had an interest in a topic area or was deemed an expert in a subject matter. His/her task was to continuously scan the environment so as to be “alerted” to relevant news, to “assess” it, and to “escalate” it, i.e., pass onto others, if it had particular importance to the organization.

U.S. Company, a large Fortune 500 manufacturing firm<sup>10</sup> headquartered in a midwestern state of the United States, was about to embark on a KnowMor implementation project. Their plan was to give the technology to researchers, including scientists and engineers at U.S. Company’s research and development facility, as well as marketers, competitive intelligence analysts, and senior managers located at the corporate headquarters. For a variety of reasons, including the company’s location within driving distance from my home, the early stage of their KnowMor implementation and the diverse composition of the intended user community, I chose U.S. Company as my field site. In 1995, the company recorded sales of \$3.6 billion and employed 17,000 people worldwide.

Negotiating access was easier than I had anticipated. Within a week of contacting Jerry Hunt,<sup>11</sup> the competitive intelligence analyst who was championing the KnowMor project, I was given permission to conduct my research. I believe that the promise of visibility, legitimacy, and prestige that the research activities of a Ph.D. student lent the KnowMor project made gaining access fairly easy. Jerry was one of my key informants. Since I had never worked in an American corporation, he taught me a lot about U.S. Company and its industry.

Employing a technology as a guide to a venue in which to study information creation work can be effective in that the introduction of a new information system is typically associated with a certain amount of disruption and change. During times of change, people are forced to engage in more sensemaking of their past, their present, and their future than they do otherwise. Situations of organizational and technical change thus offer themselves as windows of opportunity for studying social phenomena (Zuboff 1988).

These three groups were well suited to studying the practices of informing because the production and reproduction of information was central to their responsibilities. The competitive intelligence analysts represented the archetypal gatekeepers as they scanned the business environment, assessed the credibility of sources and the significance of events, and alerted decision makers in the business units to important developments both inside and outside the organization. The librarians’ mission was to respond to their customers’ information requests. They translated their customers’ questions into on-line search commands and retrieved the information their customers needed. The system administrators were responsible for maintaining a stable Lotus Notes environment. This required them to not only configure the hardware and software, but also generate information primarily in the form of documentation that would assure the systems’ smooth operations on an ongoing basis.

However, relying on a technology as a guide can also be risky due to the high implementation failure rates common among technology projects. In the case of this research, KnowMor was not implemented successfully in U.S. Company. Following the technology was nevertheless effective, albeit in ways that surprised me. Whereas I counted on KnowMor to lead me to study the informing practices of the scientists, engineers, and managers who were its intended users in U.S. Company, the knowledge workers it led me to instead were the people promoting and implementing KnowMor. These were three system administrators, three librarians, and four competitive intelligence (CI) analysts (refer to the organization chart in Appendix B). Only the CI analysts were among the intended users of KnowMor.

Whereas I was unfamiliar with the work of the CI analysts and the librarians, my academic background in information systems gave me some knowledge of computer systems work. I had also conducted some prior research on Lotus Notes. I was thus familiar with the applications on which they worked.

## Data Collection Method and My Position in the Field

I was in the field over an eight-month period, from October 1995 to mid-May 1996. Similar to the research method Preston (1986) employed in his research on the use of information technology in informing behavior, I was in U.S. Company Monday through Thursday. My stay amounted to a total of 111 days. Throughout my fieldwork, a typical week started early on a Monday morning and ended late on a Thursday evening. I drove either to HQ-City (a two hour car ride) where U.S. Company’s head office was located, or to its research and development facility at R&D-Site (a three hour car ride). During the week, I stayed in a motel if I was in HQ-City or with Norma, the leader of the U.S. Company library, if I was at R&D-Site. Norma thus became my other key informant.

The degree of my involvement with the three groups of knowledge workers varied. According to Adler and Adler’s (1994) classification of membership roles, my role among the librarians and system administrators resembled that of the active-member-researcher in that I took on work responsibilities that constituted the core of the group’s identity. This gave me both an identity as a group member and as an outside researcher. With the competitive intelligence analysts, my role resembled that of a peripheral-member-researcher in that I interacted closely enough with group members to establish a perspective similar to that of an insider. However, I did not take on any work responsibilities.

Gender and the nature of the work the groups were doing were key determinants of my membership role. Being a woman meant that I established an easier rapport and a closer relationship with the women in the field than I did with the men. Among the three librarians, all of whom were women, and the three system administrators, one of whom was a woman, I was more quickly accepted as an insider than I was by the four competitive intelligence analysts, all of whom were men. I was particularly aware of gender roles when it came to contact outside the work setting. For instance, as a single, 30 year old woman, I could uninhibitedly ask other women to go to lunch; however, asking the men, most of whom were also older than me, was not as comfortable and seemed to require more of a justification.

Besides gender, the nature of the groups’ work influenced both my relationship with them and the kind of data I could collect. That the work of the librarians and the system administrators revolved around computers had the following implications for my research:

Due to the solitary nature of computer work, it was easier for me to ask for permission to observe a librarian’s or a system administrator’s activities. The competitive intelligence analysts, in the meantime, frequently attended meetings with their customers. Gaining access to these meetings was more difficult, because consent for my presence needed to be negotiated among a larger number of people. Nevertheless, I did attend the CI analysts’ monthly staff meetings and observed some of their interactions with their customers.

The librarians’ and system administrators interaction with the technology made some of their cognitive processes visible and observable, thus offering me a starting point for asking questions. With the CI analysts, I relied mainly on their own descriptions of their work.

Given my knowledge of information technology, I had more opportunities at trying my hand at the work of the librarians and the system administrators. This made me usefu to them. My first-hand experience with their work was also an invaluable source of insight that I could not replicate in the CI analysts realm of work.

The work spaces of the librarians and the system administrators were located close to one another and the workers could generally be found at their desks. This made ongoing observations and informal conversation relatively easy. The CI analysts, on the other hand, were frequently in meetings or out of town, which required the scheduling of more formal interviews with them. Their offices were also spread out throughout the building, making it more difficult to engage in casua interactions in the corridor.

Because I was interacting almost daily with the three groups, I decided not to conduct formal tape-recorded interviews. Initially I had taped some semi-structured interviews with the intended users of KnowMor and found that the interviewees were less than candid in their responses to questions about their relationships with other departments, for instance. I further believed that such a formal and intrusive approach to data collection would disrupt the continuity and trust that was developing between the participants and me. Given that I was interested in the informing practices of these groups of knowledge workers and the social conditions surrounding their work, I opted for data collection techniques that would not jeopardize my ability to observe in-themoment reactions and behaviors.<sup>12</sup> I therefore relied mainly on unstructured interviews and informal conversations with participants as a data collection strategy. Even though my decision not to tape-record conversations decreased my ability to let the people I was studying “speak for themselves” in my textual reconstruction of the field, I considered it more important to build a level of trust with participants, which would allow me to repeatedly observe events in contextualized detail over an extended period of time, than to capture exact words spoken once. I nevertheless did tape-record interviews with people that I had the opportunity of interacting with only once. These included the CIO, several full-time members of the IS department, and various members of committees looking into knowledge management technologies. All of these interviews were semistructured. In total, I conducted 35 interviews, 20 of which were taped.

I recorded my fieldnotes into a notebook computer, relying on the conventions proposed by Silverman (1993, p. 147): double quotes for verbatim speech, single quotes for paraphrased speech, and round brackets for contextual data, personal comments and immediate interpretations of events (also Kirk and Miller 1986). During conversations and meetings, I took hand-written notes, which I then used as the basis of more elaborate narrative descriptions in my typed fieldnotes. I also recorded fieldnotes into a tape recorder while I was driving between the field sites and my home. Even though I tried to write fieldnotes as soon after an event as possible, I wrote most of them at night in the privacy of my motel room. I also wrote fieldnotes during the day sitting in my office in U.S. Company, always cognizant, however, of the trade-off between writing more detailed descriptions of events and exposing myself to interactions and experiences, with people in the field. I tended to err on the side of seeking out experiences and encounters with people, even at the risk of forfeiting detail in my fieldnotes. I reasoned that it was impossible to capture the complete richness of events in words at any rate and that I would have to rely on my headnotes, i.e., events, experiences and interpretations that are committed to the researcher’s memory rather than to writing (Ottenberg 1990). Unlike fieldnotes, which are not edited once they are written down, headnotes are subject to constant revision.

I collected a variety of documents including literature about KnowMor and its competing products, press releases concerning U.S. Company, bulletin board messages communicating internal company events, reports the CI analysts generated for their customers, results from the librarians’ searches, documentation written by the systems administrators, PowerPoint presentations made to advance the KnowMor project inside U.S. Company, and proceedings of conferences attended by people working on the KnowMor project. Many of these documents were in electronic form.

Throughout my fieldwork, I was engaged in data analysis. To summarize the data and clarify my thinking, I experimented with concepts and themes by drawing tables and diagrams. I also shared my experiences and my initial interpretations of events with people outside the field. For the first third of my fieldwork, I wrote weekly reports for my dissertation committee. For the remaining two-thirds, I met every Sunday for at least two hours with my dissertation advisor to make sense of events that had occurred in the interim, to identify themes and gaps, and to strategize how to begin filling those gaps. A more methodical data analysis began after I had left the field. This will be described in more detail later in this paper.

My decision to leave the field after eight months was influenced by numerous factors. These included my sense that it was time to analyze the data as a whole, my desire to complete my dissertation by the following summer, and my need to fulfil my responsibilities as a teaching assistant. Furthermore, there were numerous changes in the field. The KnowMor project was entering a new iteration as a new technology taskforce took ownership of it. The system administrators’ contract had been reduced from two people to 1.5. This meant that the team membership was about to change. In the library, personnel changes were also imminent. Dana was looking for a new job and she left U.S. Company shortly after I ended my stay in the field. Thus, the timing of my departure coincided with events of closure that occurred “naturally” in the field.

After defending my dissertation in May 1997 and before filing it in July 1997, I sent copies of each of my three “data chapters” to the participants, asking them to comment on the representativeness of my descriptions and interpretations. I then met with all but two of the 10 participants. The two system administrators I did not meet nevertheless e-mailed me their comments. The CI analysts had few comments and felt that I had aptly described and interpreted their work practices. Two of the librarians and one of the system administrators felt criticized and maintained that I had portrayed them as unprofessional. I worked with them to understand their concerns and I rephrased sentences so that they were less offensive to them without undermining the integrity of the research. In contrast, both Jerry and Norma, my key informants, found my analysis fair and illuminating.

## Results<sup>13</sup>

## Ex-pressing: Reconstructing the Experiencing Self into an Object

## System Administrators: Documenting Their Thoughts and Actions

The three system administrators, Ilana, Dan, and Jon, were employed by ConsultCo, a technology consulting company that had about a dozen contractors working in U.S. Company. They were contractors who had been hired as “third-level support”<sup>14</sup> for U.S. Company’s Notes environment. Each of them worked between 16 and 40 hours a week in U.S. Company. The rest of the time they worked at other client sites. They all lived between two or three hours’ drive from the U.S. Company headquarters. This meant that they typically lived in hotels during the week. Even though they groused about their life on the road and felt that they would not want to do contract work for the rest of their careers, they also enjoyed the variety of organizations, technologies, and problems to which they were exposed.

Even though the system administrators referred to themselves as “consultants,” the people in U.S. Company, e.g., the librarians, the CI analysts, and full-time members of the IS department, called them “contractors” and labeled their work “commodity.” Throughout U.S. Company, the term “commodity work” was used to describe activities that were not highly valued by the firm in that they were not part of its core competence and had no apparent strategic value or implications for competitiveness. Commodity work could thus be outsourced to workers whose skills could easily be purchased on an as-needed basis. Commodity workers were highly interchangeable and could readily be plugged in and out of work processes such as system administration. In contrast, “value adding work,” which signified the opposite of “commodity work,” could not be outsourced because it required specific knowledge that was of strategic importance to U.S. Company.

Their contractor status placed the system administrators in a somewhat vulnerable position. Not only could they easily be replaced, but they also lacked an advocate in U.S. Company. Furthermore, blaming and fingerpointing were pervasive in the technological environment in which every breakdown had to be explained and traced to its root cause. When a program had worked the day or even a minute before and all of a sudden stopped functioning, questions like “what happened?” and “what’s changed?” quickly turned into a who-dunnit witch-hunt to identify the person who last “touched” the system. As commodity workers, the contractors were easy targets for blame, humiliation, and termination.

In this environment, the system administrators relied on their documentation to protect themselves against attack. They used three sets of documentation, all of which were Notes databases. They recorded their daily activities in the Tasks Database. If a user called in with a Lotus Notes problem, their request was documented in the Tasks Database and assigned to one of the three contractors. All the actions, problems and decisions related to the call were then documented in the same record. The System Guide was a database that contained policies, standards and procedures tailored to the client organization. It was a documentation shell that ConsultCo had developed and sold to U.S. Company. The system administrator’s task was to populate this database. Most entries were step-by-step instructions for accomplishing certain tasks, e.g., installing KnowMor client software on a PC. The Project Database contained meeting agendas, minutes of meetings, and reports pertaining to “special projects.” A “special project” was any work that the system administrators considered outside the realm of normal administration. For instance, the conversion to TCP/IP was deemed “special” whereas registering new users was a normal part of their administrative responsibilities.

The system administrators used all three databases as a shield to protect themselves against accusation, blame, and finger pointing from their client and other consultants. My fieldnotes include this example:

Dan gave us another example of an Itold-you-so incident; the Notes databases that [the SAP consulting company] used as “a rolodex to SAP” had too many views which was going to bring down the servers; Jon and Dan recognized this and told [an SAP project manager] who basically said “I hear you but I am not going to do anything about it”; eventually the servers were brought down by the application and then something was finally done about it; Dan proudly declared that “we were totally covered” because they had all the documentation that went on between them and [the SAP project manager] so that [the SAP project manager] stood there as the fool for not having listened to them in the first place. (Fieldnotes, 01 May 1996)

For the documentation to serve as a dependable shield, however, it had to meet certain standards of complete and accurate reporting. The system administrators believed that they adhered to higher documentation standards than other contractors working in U.S. Company.

At about 6:30 pm the mail people were still at their desks and the Microsoft consultants came by on their way out. They got pulled into a conversation about the [electronic] mail fiasco of today. [The Microsoft consultants] said that they did not understand what had changed. Yesterday the system had worked just fine, so who did what. The one [electronic mail specialist] said that he had asked the same question and had gotten the answer “nothing”. The Microsoft consultants sneered and said “of course” in a sarcastic tone. Dan leaned over and said to me in a hushed tone “these guys are not following procedure. With us, you can look up the documentation and see exactly what has changed”. (Fieldnotes, 18 April 1996)

When documenting events in the Tasks Database, their main documentation database which also served as a source for entries made into the System Guide, the system administrators were careful to delineate the role they and others had played in completing a task. As can be seen in the entries presented in Figure 1, they strove to reconstruct the work scene in as accurate and complete a way as possible, relying on rhetorica devices such as writing in a passive voice and writing about themselves as subjects in their own texts. They also used strikeouts and added text that qualified or corrected earlier text rather than editing or deleting it. All of these strategies helped create an impression of reliable, objective reporting.

Developing such accounts was hard work and both Ilana and Dan resented it. They were much happier engaging hands-on with the technology than writing about the technology and themselves in interaction with it. During their interactions with the hardware and software, they relied on their tactile and tacit knowledge to solve problems, typically circling in cycles of trial and error until they reached a solution. Documenting required them to enter a self-reflexive state and to articulate both their taken-for-granted tactile knowledge of the technology and the things they had learned while they were working with it. It required them to reconstruct the path they had taken through the technology.

– observing Ilana write stuff into the Tasks Database was really an eyeopener; it occurred to me that these ConsultCo people are not only doing things, i.e. programming or reconfiguring fileservers and programs, but also documenting what they are doing or have done; so they are constantly in a reflexive mode; for instance, Ilana created a Notes ID for a user, she then went and made an entry into the Tasks Database saying that she had created an ID for him and that she had sent it to [a contractor in R&D-Site]

Documenting their actions in this way required the Notes administrators to be in multiple places at the same time. They not only needed to be in their chair touching the machine, but also above the chair, observing themselves at work, extracting an explanation out of what they had done, and articulating in words what they had learned during their excursions into the technology. Acting as their own “back seat driver,” they were continuously moving between the realm of doing and the realm of documenting. A physical manifestation of being in multiple places at the same time was their flicking among doing-windows and documenting-windows on their computer screens, and their working with two computers simultaneously.

I label this informing practice ex-pressing. It involves extracting knowledge and experiences out of the individual workers’ mind (and body), pressing these into words, and then pressing these words into a coherent linear text. In expressing themselves, the knowledge workers also press themselves out of the event, i.e., they have to distance themselves from it and suspend their subjectivity in order to become a seemingly objective observer. This is accomplished by splitting the self into an experiencing and a writing self such that the writing self is hovering above the experiencing self, observing it like an actor in a scene. The practice of ex-pressing builds on the assumption that knowledge can be disembodied and captured into informational objects such that knowledge can exist independently of the individual. Ex-pressing thus creates the conditions under which computer system administration can proceed as a smooth, continuous process even though discrete knowledge workers are swapped in and out of it.

In summary, the system administrators created informational objects that afforded them protection against fingerpointing, blame, and attacks on their competence. They relied on the informing practice of ex-pressing to recreate their thoughts and actions in a set of documentation that existed independently of them. Ex-pressing required the system administrators to separate their experiencing from their writing selves and thereby suspend their subjectivity to create informational objects from the vantage point of a seemingly objective observer.

## Parallels to My Practice of Informing: Ex-pressing Myself in My Fieldnotes

Before entering the field, I devised a set of documentation templates to guide my fieldnote writing. I developed four templates: one for meetings, one to describe organizational initiatives,<sup>15</sup> one for individual people, and one to describe the events on a given day. Their purpose was to guide my thought processes through the different aspects of the fieldwork, to ensure that relevant details were not omitted, and to classify my notes with respect to such dimensions as the source of the information and the origin of an idea. I also expected my fieldnote templates to enhance the reliability of my research in that they ensured my compliance with the conventions of ethnographic research.<sup>16</sup>. Of the four templates I designed, I used the daily events or “DAY” template the most (see Figure 2).

Even thought I acknowledged to myself that no observation or description could be free of interpretation and a subjective point of view, it was nevertheless important to me to separate, to the best of my ability, my personal reflections, speculations, and situated interpretations from the events ‘as they happened.’ I believed that this would allow me, once I had completed my fieldwork, to reinterpret my notes with the benefit of hindsight, a more holistic understanding, and a specific theory. I thus endeavored to record incidents as objectively as possible. This meant that I carefully described events from the perspective of a distant observer, presenting them in their rich detail and including myself as a subject in the scene. I made my impressions, reactions, and interpretations as explicit as possible, pretending that they would be used by someone who was unfamiliar with my field site. In this way, I hoped to present incidents independently of my self, i.e., my assumptions, knowledge, emotions, and experiences. I also wanted to minimize my reliance on my headnotes, i.e., the events as I stored them in memory.

I experienced fieldwork as a constant writing (also Clifford 1986). While I was physically in a place as an actor on the scene, I was simultaneously suspended above the setting, observing others and myself from a distance and putting into words what was going on. On converting these headnotes into fieldnotes, I again found myself in multiple places, i.e., physically in front of my computer with my handwritten notes, and mentally replaying the scenes of the day. Like the system administrators, I operated in a self-reflexive mode, splitting myself into an experiencing and a writing self. The informing practice of ex-pressing was hard work, both physically and mentally.

writing notes until 12:15 tonight...and I did not get much sleep last night because I wrote the dissertation statement for [a Fellowship application] and faxed it to school at 1 a.m.; I know I should be writing some of the things down that Norma talked about tonight, but I am tired and my back is killing me, my shoulder are tense and my wrists are bright red (Personal Notes, 8 April 1996)

On some occasions, I found it virtually impossible to write fieldnotes and it took all the discipline I could conjure up to convert the mix of images, thoughts, and feelings into words and categorized, linear text. Discipline was not all that was required, however; I needed enough distance between my experiencing and my writing self.

– today [Thursday] I managed to write about the Tuesday meeting; I don’t know what my block is sometimes about these fieldnotes; it is almost as if I am so much in the moment at the time when things happen, that it takes me a while to pull myself out of it and be able to make it explicit....I have to dissociate my self from the events

– maybe that is what is going on, but maybe it is that initially there is so much that I remember that writing it up is overwhelming and when things have just happened and when I’ve had time to think it all over, more of a story emerges and that is easier to write down...but that story emerges too when I am writing up straight after an event (About my Research-Process section, 01 Feb 1996)

## Monitoring: See(k)ing Without Being Seen

## Competitive Intelligence Analysts: Maintaining an Identity as Internal Consultants

The four competitive intelligence (CI) analysts— Jerry, Ned, Dave, and Masato—were part of U.S. Company’s 11 member Business Research department. The other members were market researchers and administrative assistants. The mission statement of Business Research read:

To lead the process for acquiring and managing knowledge about end users, customers, competitors, market dynamics and future environments, and to have this knowledge reflected in business decisions.

The department had adopted a strategy of colocating its CI analysts and market researchers with the business divisions they served. One of the ways in which the CI analysts’ work distinguished itself from the market researchers’ lay in the kind of information gathering that the two groups relied upon. Whereas the market researchers were generally working on projects with a specified purpose, scope, and deadline, the CI analysts were continuously scanning the business environment for significant events to which they then alerted their customers. The CI analysts thus referred to themselves as “strategic advisors” whose responsibility it was to interact personally, and on a daily basis, with “decision makers,” e.g., the division presidents. Even though the CI analysts did compile information in response to questions from their customers, e.g., “How will competitor A react if we raise prices in Region Y?,” their primary objective was to preempt such questions by continuously providing their customers with information about the state of U.S. Company vis-a-vis its competitors. They regarded themselves as “value adding” workers because of their vast experience in the industry and their complex understanding of it, which made it possible for them to identify relevant information and to assess its importance to their customers, i.e., the decision makers in U.S. Company.

The CI analysts referred to themselves as “internal consultants,” a term that highlighted their dualistic and somewhat conflicting role as both insiders and outsiders. As insiders and accepted members of the organizational community, they were expected to take on an identity and build long-term relationships of trust with others in the organization. This implied that they needed to take a stand on corporate issues and take their place in the company’s socio-political network. As consultants, however, they were expected to take an outsider’s impartial perspective, suspend their personal affiliations, and generate objective recommendations.

However, unlike external consultants, these internal consultants stayed in U.S. Company after they had generated a set of recommendations. This presented the following problem. The information the CI analysts produced frequently had direct consequences for organizational members.

For instance, the quarterly analysis of how U.S. Company fared compared to its competitors could have positive or negative impacts on the managers and vice presidents responsible for the numbers. As members of the organizational community, the CI analysts needed to take responsibility for the information they had generated and its consequences for their customers, friends, and enemies within U.S. Company. Walking away from the organization, like external consultants could, was a luxury that the CI analysts did not have. The need to deal with the “aftermath” of their own recommendations made it more challenging to stay objective.

– Jerry got talking about consultants; he does not like them, starting with [the CEO’s consultant] who he says can say anything he likes and get away with it because he is a consultant; he also told me the story of the boss he used to work for before [his current boss]; everybody hated his previous boss because he would come in "stir the pot", cause confusion, upset people, add to their workload, and then leave; he was not there to deal with the aftermath

– Jerry also acts as an internal consultant, but unlike his previous boss, he does not leave; he is still there the next day; that makes the whole task of doing objective analysis of the company's performance a lot more difficult (Fieldnotes, 19 October 1995)

Staying objective and assuring others in U.S. Company that they had “no axe to grind” was particularly important to these “internal consultants” because they wanted their customers to use the competitive intelligence they produced. Since they forfeited the authority and respect that was typically afforded to external consultants for their apparent objectivity, the CI analysts had to work at building a reputation of objective, accurate, and reliable reporting. They thus took on a seemingly distant and disinterested stance to present themselves as objective.

– Jerry [Hunt] puts out a quarterly analysis—a 2 page report with graphs— comparing U.S. Co’s performance with that of its competitors

– at the beginning of this year, Jerry had put out an analysis in which he pointed out how U.S. Company’s main competitor, was outperforming them; he however “gave the division presidents an out” by saying that “had it not been for..., U.S. Company would have outperformed its competitors”

– that analysis resulted in an e-mail message from [the CEO] which...said “I find Hunt's analysis cavaleer”

– Jerry scorned that [the CEO] had misspelt cavalier

– when I asked him what “cavalier” meant, he said that he interpreted it as meaning that [the CEO] did not think he had been hard enough on the division presidents; that Jerry was not taking the gravity of the situation seriously enough – in his 2nd quarter analysis he had been a lot harder on the division presidents, so he got a lot of angry responses from them, saying things like “but we have [this new product] in our numbers, and that makes us look bad”, to which Jerry responds that [the competitor] also has all its new product developments in its numbers, and therefore it is a matter of “comparing apples with apples”; he displays the numbers and gives his interpretation, and the division presidents are welcome to add their excuses if they want; it is not his problem (Fieldnotes, 19 October 1995)

To assure themselves of the objectivity and accuracy of their recommendations and analyses, the CI analysts needed reliable information as input. They thus sought ways of limiting their influence on the information they collected. They were particularly wary of straightforwardly asking questions, because questions anticipate answers and therefore influence the response. Questions also give information away in that they say something about the questioner, e.g., his/her motivation for seeking information and his/her expected use thereof. This information, in turn, influences the response. Furthermore, when asking questions the CI analysts had to anticipate that informants would respond with questions of their own. For instance, when people in U.S.

Company asked the CI analysts for information, their typical response was “why do you want to know?” This was because the CI analysts believed that the dissemination of information should be carefully restricted and that competitive intelligence meant “having the wherewithal to protect what you know.” At the same time, they wanted to avoid being asked “why do you want to know?” when they were searching for information.

One of the informing practices they relied upon to obtain reliable information was monitoring. Monitoring represented an unobtrusive mechanism for gathering a wide variety of “just-in-case” information on a continuous basis. Using clipping or on-line news delivery services to collect information about competitors and the market in general are examples of the informing practice of monitoring. Monitoring was not only useful because the CI analysts were primarily engaged in non-focused scanning, but also because it minimized the risk of the CI analysts influencing the information being received. It also meant that they minimized the risk of inadvertently giving information away about U.S. Company’s strategic direction.

In summary, their status as “internal consultants” made it particularly important for the CI analysts to be perceived as impartial and objective by the constituents in U.S. Company. Objectivity promised them the authority and respect that external consultants commanded naturally. Furthermore, an objective position also shielded them against challenges to their competence and their ability to add value. The informing practice of monitoring was one way in which the CI analysts signaled an objective and impartial stance. Furthermore, the surreptitious and ongoing gathering of “just-incase” information gave them the assurance that the information they relied upon for their analyses and recommendations was unadulterated by their interest in it.

## Parallels to My Practices of Informing: Observing Rather than Participating

Non-interventionist observation has traditionally been the hallmark of the ethnographic method (Adler and Adler 1994). Researchers relying on observations alone do not ask questions, and they avoid creating any kind of provocation in the field. However, despite their attempts to stay invisible, observers intrude and therefore cannot claim that the data they collected is objective, i.e., independent of them. Despite knowing that my presence alone constituted an intervention, I nevertheless sought ways to minimize my contamination of the data. I perceived my inadvertent introduction of “foreign” concepts as an intervention and a mistake.

I pointed out very early on in the meeting that I was trying to set up interviews with the people in the pilot and that they did not seem to know about the fact that they were getting KnowMor; so I pointed out that there seemed to be this “void” between the IS people who were trying to get KnowMor installed and the users and us...using the word "void" was a mistake, because Norma kept using it from then on, as did Jerry (aside: I also realize that the distinction between “individual productivity tools” and “groupware” [which I drew in a conversation with Jerry] is becoming more and more real) (Meeting between Norma and Jerry in R&D-Site, 19 October 1995)

Whereas I was concerned about contaminating the data by introducing foreign concepts in the early stages of my fieldwork, I was in less danger of this mode of intervention as I learned to express myself using emic terms, i.e., those indigenous to the social setting. However, as I took on more of an insider identity, my body language proved to be yet another mode of intervention.

at some stage during the conversation Jerry said to me “you never believe me” and I asked where he had come up with that idea; he said that I look at him in such a way...I said I would try to stop that, and I really must try

(But he is onto something; I take him seriously but I do qualify things he says because he exaggerates such a lot and he is such a loose canon; I also don't have faith in his promises...; he comes through on some things, but conveniently forgets others) (Fieldnotes, 25 January 1996)

The tension between observation and participation further presented itself in my need to juggle emergence and design. On the one hand, I wanted to push my way into situations that would allow me to study information and knowledge creation work, while on the other, I did not want to disturb the internal logics operating in the socia setting. A large part of my inquiry needed to be geared toward understanding these very logics. I therefore had to allow things to happen. Debating the merits of pushing my own agenda and of waiting for events to unfold ‘naturally’ was nerve wracking, especially because the completion of my Ph.D. degree depended on it.

– I am very preoccupied with the idea that this whole project might go bellyup...when am I going to get off this ship? When am I going to say there is no more hope?

– I am killing time...what am I doing here? There must be better things I can do with my life? (Personal Notes, 16 January 1996)

These moments of anxiety and severe frustration express the tension I felt in finding a balance between subjectivity and objectivity. My struggle to find my place between active participation and distant observation, interaction and silence, and design and emergence, was also reflected in my search for an identity in the field. I wrestled with maintaining my initial position as an outsider and learner while becoming an active participant whose prolonged presence in the field was at best welcomed and at worst tolerated by the field’s members.

[they] are expecting that I will interview people to find out what information they want from KnowMor...I need to handle the balance between becoming a member on the implementation team and an observer carefully...seeing we are dealing with technology, this is not a culture that is completely foreign to me and in order for them to be open with me and feel comfortable around me, I cannot act dumb...I need to be true to the “competent self” that I am both on the inside and the outside. (About my Research-Process section, 2 October 1995)

The longer I stayed in the field, the more I needed to take on an identity as a “competent self” and a legitimate member in the field. I needed to engage in informational exchanges by letting people know where I stood on issues and what I thought and felt. I needed to answer questions in order to be allowed to ask them. I needed to make myself visible and vulnerable if I was expecting others to make themselves visible and vulnerable by trusting me and telling me what they thought.

Becoming an insider and having an identity brought with it expectations of consistency and integrity. Since I was mediating between three groups that sometimes spoke negatively of each other, having an identity implied that I needed to take sides.

when Jerry was telling me about his understanding of where libraries were going, i.e. away from this traditional model of searching, I started defending the women in R&D-Site; somehow I could not just sit there and listen and not say anything; I felt like an accomplice and therefore challenged Jerry to tell me more about his understanding of this new kind of library; he did not really say that much except that he did not see the need for a lot of books, seeing a lot of this information was online

(Jerry was trying to see whether I would approve and apparently I had an expression on my face that indicated to him that I did not agree with him; he laughed about my facial expression and when I said good-bye to him at about 6:30 he said “get out of here; you have given me enough of a hard time today” and then said that he was only joking) (Fieldnotes, 14 May 1996)

Much like the CI analysts, I engaged in the practice of monitoring in an effort to limit my engagement with the field and minimize data contamination. Monitoring implied observing more than participating, waiting for events to present themselves ‘naturally’ rather than pushing my own research agenda, and acting as a note-taking instrument rather than becoming a ‘real person’ who had her own mind, opinions, and emotions.

Unlike the CI analysts who focused their monitoring activities on the external environment, i.e., their competitors and the market, I applied monitoring inside the field, i.e., the very place of which I wanted to become a part. For me, the viability of monitoring as an objectivity-enhancing practice of informing decreased as I took on more of an identity in the field. I needed to participate in open exchanges of information, make my thoughts and opinions known, and take a stand— and a side—on issues. I nevertheless endeavored to minimize my intervention in the field and I was acutely aware of my efforts to balance subjectivity and objectivity.

Translating: Developing Coherence by Tacking Back and Forth Across Contexts

## Librarians: Searching as a Series of Translations

Even though the library was located at R&D-Site, about half of the requests for library services came from business customers in HQ-City. The Business Research department was one of the library’s most frequent customers. The library employed seven women, of whom only three— Norma, the leader of the library, Mandy, and Dana—were engaged in information “sourcing” and “searching,” the library’s core functions. Their overall objective was to increase the availability of information for the members of U.S. Company. Their mission statement read:

Knowledge Resource Services<sup>17</sup> develops, implements and manages the strategy to maximize and expand the access, transfer and utilization of knowledge assets for U.S. Company personnel worldwide.

The librarians considered themselves “information specialists” whose core competence lay in “content” rather than “technology.” This meant that the librarians were concerned with retrieving—in the most cost-effective manner—the information their customers wanted, both on an ad-hoc and a recurring basis, irrespective of the technological means of storing, finding, and distributing it. The library provided over 600 information services and products including news services such as electronic news feeds from Reuters, CD ROMs, and on-line databases such as Lexis Nexis.

A “search” was prompted by a customer’s question or request for information. It put the librarians into an agency situation in which they acted as intermediaries who shuttled between the internal organizational realm, from which their customer’s question had originated, and the external informational realm that contained answers. Neither realm was completely knowable to the librarians as they had neither their customers understanding of the organizational realm nor a full comprehension of the information realm, which was constantly changing as new information became available. “Searching” thus involved the development of a coherent question-answer combination. To achieve this, the librarians tacked back and forth between the organizational realm of questions and the informational realm of answers. They also toggled between inductive and deductive information processing.

Once a customer recognized a need for information, the librarians began interpreting the customer’s request. They relied on their knowledge of the customer, the company and the subject matter to read meaning into the words their customers used to describe what they were looking for. Frequently, what their customers said they wanted was not what they meant.

Mandy gave me an example of a customer who had asked her whether the BCC report on superconductivity had been updated yet; she checked and came back with the answer “no”; a few days later he came back to the library and said that he could not believe that there had been no reports on superconductivity in the last month; Mandy said “let’s check” and found 193; he said that she had told him there had not been any and she corrected him saying that there were none by BCC; she said that she felt bad about “being mean” to people (Fieldnotes, 10 April 1996)

Interpreting the customer’s question was an exercise of induction in which the librarians relied on their personal experience and knowledge to develop an image of the kind of information for which the customer was looking. They did this by quizzing their customers about the context in which the need for information had arisen and by asking such questions as “what do you want to know?” and ‘what does the ideal article look like?’ However, they did not ask their customers to justify their requests. Unlike the CI analysts, the librarians did not ask “why do you want to know?” They associated this line of questioning with attempts to deny an information request, an orientation that ran counter to the library’s mission of “giving information away.”

After this initial interpretation of the customer’s question, the request was converted into a program-like search strategy. This proceeded in a deductive manner as the librarians applied an established procedure for developing an optimal search strategy. This procedure started with an identification of the most cost-effective information service. Cost-effectiveness was evaluated in terms of such factors as the comprehensiveness with which a service covered a topic area, the appropriateness of its information content and formatting,<sup>18</sup> the cost of the information, the connect fees the service charged, and the amount of time it would take for the librarian to find and retrieve the information.

Once the service had been identified, a series of search commands was developed. Since there was little standardization across the different services, the librarians had to be familiar with the technical intricacies of many different systems. These technical intricacies included the syntax of the command language, the service’s pricing strategy, and the logic underlying the way it structured information. Because on-line information services charged for connect time, the librarians developed a sequence of search commands off-line. While off-line, they also verified technical details in their reference materials.

The objective of following an established procedure for developing a search strategy was not only to ensure cost-effectiveness, but also to give the librarians the assurance that they had considered the universe of information in the search. Given that the universe of information was not knowable, the development of a search strategy that followed an established procedure gave the librarians some degree of certainty that their search results were complete and reliable. The assurance that they had, within reason, completed an exhaustive search was particularly important when their searches came up empty and their customers refused to believe that the information they wanted, e.g., a survey on a niche market, did not exist. If this happened, some customers wanted to know the specific details about the librarians search technique.

– when Mandy was writing the message for Masato she made quite sure she spelled fibers in both the English, i.e. “fibres”, and American way, i.e. “fibers”; she said that Masato [would otherwise] come back and say “you searched it wrong”;

(I remember that he wanted all "fiber" keywords that I entered into KnowMor for him, to be in a truncated form, i.e. fib\*) – Mandy anticipated that Masato would call with questions; she said “he always does” (Fieldnotes, 6 May 1996)

Other customers requested that back-up searches be completed by independent library services if they were not satisfied with the information that the librarians had delivered.

– Dana [came into Mandy's office and] said that [the external library service] had better find something for [a customer who had asked her to order a back-up search] because if they don’t that would mean that she had wasted \$300 on this request; Dana seemed defensive when she told Mandy about the incident, saying that the search was for Vera and “you don’t ever do anything half-assed for Vera” [Vera was one of the library’s most difficult customers]

(all of this seemed to indicate that Dana was slightly put out by the implication that she had not searched well enough to find the right information) (Fieldnotes, 10 April 1996)

As it turned out, the external library service did find additional information, but it was an article that Dana had found too. However, Dana had discarded the article for not being “relevant” to the customer’s needs. The librarians’ assessment of a search results’ relevance occurred during a later phase of the search, a phase that focused on the interplay between the “hits” that a search had generated and the librarian’s interpretation of the customer’s question. This assessment of a hit’s relevance required the librarians to use their own subjective understanding of what constituted an acceptable number of “records” that the customer was willing and able to look at, and whether the hits contained information that their customer needed. This was an inductive process during which the librarians needed to tack back and forth between the external realm of information sources and the internal realm of their customer and his/her question.

– [to complete a search on patents assigned to company Z, Dana] opened Lexis Nexis which is the main supplier of U.S. patents and with whom they have a flat fee arrangement; she dialed up via modem

– she chose the “Lexis portion” which contains legal information

– first she entered the customer’s name for record keeping, then she typed in “compny” for a search on Z; she got 309 hits; so she focused the search by typing in “.fo” and then entered “vacuum and evacuat!”; that gave her 29 hits; she said “too many”

– so she changed the search command again (I did not catch how exactly) and got 18 hits; she started looking through those and was satisfied that they were “relevant”, i.e. what the customer was looking for;[...]; she scrolled through the patents and thought that most of them were relevant even though some were not “super relevant” (Fieldnotes, 23 April 1996)

As the excerpt above shows, the librarians assessment of a search result’s relevance was iterative. Search commands were refined in response to the results they had produced. The librarians referred to this as “dynamic programming,” i.e., modifying or “tweaking” the initia search strategy based on their reading of the results. They saw this as a “creative” process. The back and forth inherent in “dynamic programming” illustrates the dialectic relationship between the question and the answer, between induction and deduction, and between the organizationa and information realms that the librarians straddled.

Despite the need for their subjective judgement during this dynamic and creative search process, the librarians were also uncomfortable with exercising their own judgement, i.e., bringing their subjectivity to bear, in determining the relevance of information.

– [Dana] then went through the entire file; putting new patents either on new pages, or adding a couple of blank lines before a new patent would start; the ones she did not consider relevant any more, she deleted; there was [a patent] that [the inventor] H had invented, but it had nothing to do with the search, so she decided to delete it saying that she preferred to err on the side of being too focused; taking out the records she said “that’s me knowing what the customer wants”

– she seemed uncomfortable with me seeing her exercise her judgement when it came to deleting records; she also made all sorts of qualifying remarks about doing such a “sloppy search” on Lexis Nexis; she said that with a flat fee you could afford to go back and forward without worrying about it – she said that she might be too focused in the output she gives to customers (Fieldnotes, 23 April 1996)

Given the imminent threat of the library’s outsourcing, the librarians’ discomfort with subjectivity was problematic. To present themselves as more “value adding” workers, they were thus planning to do more “analysis” of the search results, i.e., summarize and interpret “hits” to construct answers to their customer’s questions. These analyses would require the librarians to add more of themselves, i.e., their personal experience, knowledge, and subjective insights about their customers and U.S. Company, to the information they produced.

I label the librarian’s practice of informing translating. It entailed a tacking back and forth across multiple contexts, as well as a toggling between induction and deduction, i.e., a reliance on subjective, situated knowledge and objectified, procedural knowledge respectively. Translating thus represents an ongoing dynamic balancing between subjectivity and objectivity.

In summary, as “searchers,” the librarians were acting as translators or agents that shuttled between the external realm of information and potential answers, and the internal organizational realm of questions and information requests. They interpreted their customers’ questions, assessed the relevance of information that their search strategies produced, transferred information from one realm to the other, and transformed this information so as to render it recognizable in each realm. Induction and deduction played an important part in this translation process. Deductively applying an established procedure to develop a search strategy balanced the inductive phases of their translation work with some degree of objectivity. This gave them the assurance that they had completed a comprehensive search. This assurance was particularly important in the context of customers questioning their competence and judgement.

## Parallels to My Practices of Informing: Data Analysis as Induction and Deduction

I agree with Miles (1979) that “the central difficulty in the use of qualitative data is that the methods of analysis are not well formulated” (p. 590). First, it is difficult to isolate when data analysis takes place in ethnographic work because it is happening all the time as the ethnographer moves from the periphery toward legitimate membership in the field (Lave and Wenger 1991). Second, when it comes to analyzing the material collected during the fieldwork, the primary activity involves reading the fieldnotes over and over again in order to categorize events and to inductively construct themes. This typically implies manipulating the fieldnotes by cutting them apart and reassembling them into coherent patterns that apparently ‘emerge’ from the data themselves. Nevertheless, the researcher cannot deny that his/her theoretica and personal biases play a central role in this ‘emergence’.

They can also not deny that their analysis is influenced by the need to present the field with integrity and simultaneously to satisfy the interests and expectations of the academic community, i.e., the ultimate audience of the research. The ethnographer acts as translator, ferrying information between the field and the academic community and thereby developing coherence between the data and theory. Analysis thus entails a juggling of induction, i.e., interpreting the data using situated and subjective knowledge, and deduction, i.e., applying objectified methods, frameworks, and theories to the data.

While I was in the field, I intuitively used a method of constant comparison to analyze and make sense of events (Czarniawska-Joerges 1992; Strauss and Corbin 1990). It was through contrasting the groups’ activities, work environments, strategies, questions, and concerns that I began to see similarities and differences among them.

– one difference between the three groups:

\* the CI people see it as their role to inform people when important events happen, i.e., not supply information on demand, but Just in Time information... information has a limited shelf life so you need to serve it while it is fresh, hot...in the right format, at the right time, etc.

\* the library is in the role of delivering routine information (i.e., periodic, automatic, weekly, monthly) as well as information on demand

\* the [system administrators] only want 2nd and 3rd level support calls from customers; they deal with trouble, i.e. when things go wrong, and don’t get much positive feedback; they have very little contact with their customers and do not really know their customers’ job; I don’t think they know much about the organization (Academic Thoughts, 29 April 1996)

In order to continue with this logic of comparison after I had left the field, I read and reread my fieldnotes with an eye to identifying themes that captured the main distinctions among the groups. The initial themes that ‘emerged’ from the data were worker identity (how workers portrayed themselves and how others perceived them), information (what metaphor the groups used to described information, what kind they used and produced, and how they interacted with it) and technology (what technology each of the groups used and how central it was to their work).<sup>19</sup> With a 3x3 matrix as my framework (the three groups and the themes of identity, information, and technology as axes), I began the cut-and-paste job of categorizing my fieldnotes. As I pasted statements from my fieldnotes into each cell in the matrix, sub-themes developed. Both incidents that confirmed and contradicted a sub-theme were included.

Then I reread the categorized fieldnotes for each group, i.e., three 90-page, 10-point-font, singlespaced documents. My analysis of each of these three documents led me to see structurational<sup>20</sup> cycles between the groups’ work with information and technology and their identity, i.e., their position within the larger organizational structure. Since I had gone into the field with a social constructionist definition of information, it was not surprising that structurational cycles for each of the groups ‘emerged’ almost magically out of the data during this inductive data analysis phase.

However, my first attempts to compose coherent narratives of the three groups’ informing practices brought to light that the combination of the three categories of worker identity, information, and technology and a generic structurational framework was not theoretically sound enough to develop consistent interpretations across the three groups. I felt that I needed an established theory that would help me look at my data deductively, i.e., from a more distant, top-down and objective vantage point. Such a theory, I argued, would give my analysis more credibility because it represented an authority that lay beyond the field and me. I also anticipated that it would be easier to convince my dissertation committee of the robustness of my work if I applied a “real” theory rather than a “home-spun” one.

Exploring Bourdieu’s Theory of Practice (Bourdieu 1977; Bourdieu and Wacquant 1992), also a structurational theory, I was surprised how closely it fit my data and my initial attempts at analyzing it. Bourdieu’s Theory of Practice also made me see my inductive interpretations in different ways.

Most importantly, Bourdieu’s theory allowed me to use more contextual data than did my initial analysis.

Much like the librarians, I relied on translating as an informing practice to identify a coherent question-answer set that was both consistent with the field and the interests and expectations of the academic IS community. For me, translating involved interpreting events through constant comparison, transferring information between the field and the academic community (made up primarily of my dissertation committee), and systematically transforming the data into different frameworks and theories. Translating required a tacking back and forth between a data-driven, inductive interpretation and a theory-driven, deductive fitting of data. Whereas induction and emergence rely heavily on the ethnographers subjective understanding of situated meanings in the data and his/her theoretical biases, deduction relies on a methodical application of objectified constructs, frameworks and theories. Whereas induction promises to generate an authentic account of localized events, deduction promises to generate a more generalizable one.

One of the ways in which my attempts to balance subjectivity and objectivity found expression was in my reliance on the 3x3 framework. Once created, this framework became an artifact that was seemingly independent of the data and of me. It thus represented somewhat of an external system that I could rely upon to ensure that all the data relating to the three groups of knowledge workers had been taken into account, and that the interpretations were both coherent and consistent across the groups. When this inductively generated framework failed to produce consistent interpretations, I reached for another system of authority, i.e., a theory documented and applied in numerous publications, to bolster the credibility of my research. By turning to a theory, I also turned more fully toward deduction, i.e., distanced, topdown analysis. However, the interdependence between the inductive and deductive analysis techniques was undeniable; my inductive interpretation and manipulation of the data made it possible for me to recognize the applicability of Bourdieu’s theory to my research.

## Discussion

Studying the work practices of three groups of knowledge workers, i.e., computer systems administrators, competitive intelligence analysts, and librarians, I identified three informing practices: ex-pressing, monitoring, and translating. A centra theme running through these informing practices was the knowledge workers’ endeavor to balance the subjective nature of the information they produced with activities that enhanced its apparent objectivity. This suggests that subjectivity and objectivity are inseparably intertwined, and that both are necessary in knowledge work. Whereas a lack of objectivity in their informing practices undermined the knowledge workers’ authority, credibility, and reputation, a lack of subjectivity, i.e., failure to bring idiosyncratic knowledge and selfhood to bear on information they produced, raised questions about the value the individua worker adds to the corporation. Their informing practices (summarized in Table 1) thus represented pragmatic solutions to their efforts to balance subjectivity and objectivity.

These informing practices also represent the conventions of intersubjectivity established by each of the groups of knowledge workers. These conventions are partly influenced by their professions. For instance, information systems professionals know the importance of documentation and competitive intelligence professionals know the importance of secrecy and highly selective information dissemination. The meaning and significance of these professional conventions is nevertheless also affected by the situated context in which these knowledge workers find themselves. For instance, the system administrators' vulnerability due to their contract status influenced their use of documentation as a protective shield.

The parallels between my informing practices and those of the three groups of knowledge workers suggest that these informing practices are transsituational and that the endeavor to balance subjectivity with objectivity is fundamental to the production and reproduction of information. The three informing practices of ex-pressing, monitoring, and translating are expected to be neither exhaustive nor unique to the group of knowledge workers in whose work they were identified. The fact that I employed all three informing practices suggests that other knowledge workers similarly employ multiple informing practices in parallel.

How, then, do knowledge workers attribute subjectivity and objectivity? How do subjectivity and objectivity manifest themselves in their dayto-day work lives? This research has highlighted that, in a commercial setting, subjectivity is regarded as a necessary component of knowledge work and that knowledge workers refer to their personal knowledge and idiosyncratic experience to make claims of being “value adding.” Objectivity, in contrast, implies that the knowledge worker is not alone in making claims about an object. Instead, by relying on conventional perspectives, theories, and practices, the knowledge worker mobilizes an entire network of actors to support his/her claim (Latour 1987). This allows the individual to speak with the authority of the network of both animate and inanimate actors such as people, machines, texts, and institutions. Furthermore, the knowledge workers can call upon the conventions as a protective mechanism in order to deflect personal attacks on their competence.

The attributions of objectivity and subjectivity assigned by the knowledge workers in this research are consistent with Western and scientific thought in which objectivity is still more highly valued than subjectivity (Deetz 1996). Nevertheless, the positive attribution of subjectivity in the knowledge management arena suggests that an idiosyncratic perspective, personal experience, and situated knowledge are valued in the information age. This is evident in the celebration of tacit knowledge in knowledge management initiatives and theories.

Such a favorable disposition toward subjectivity raises questions for the scientific knowledge production paradigm. Where, in scientific inquiry, does subjectivity “add value”? What would happen if researchers openly acknowledged where they relied on subjectivity? In what way would an open acknowledgment of subjectivity hurt the scientific enterprise? Would self-revealing texts make researchers and their work more valuable or more vulnerable? Would such texts come across as disingenuous, self-aggrandizing, and selfpromoting?

Raising these questions is intended to achieve criticality in this research. Criticality is one of the criteria by which the quality of ethnographic research can be assessed. These criteria will now be discussed.

## Evaluating This Confessional Account of Knowledge Work

Questions of how high-quality confessiona accounts might be distinguished from low-quality ones need to concern themselves with standards of ethnographic research in general and confessional genres in particular. While there has been research on the evaluation criteria of ethnography in general (e.g., Golden-Biddle and Locke 1993), similar work on confessional genres does not exist. Based on both examples of confessional styles of writing (e.g., Behar 1993; Rabinow 1977; Whyte 1996; Wolf 1992) and their critiques (e.g., Sanjek 1990), I develop evaluation criteria for confessional writing.

Golden-Biddle and Locke (1993) developed a set of evaluation criteria for ethnographic research and identified three dimensions central to writing a convincing and publishable ethnography. These were authenticity, plausibility, and criticality. At a minimum, a convincing ethnographic text must exhibit authenticity and plausibility, suggesting that high-quality ethnographies exhibit criticality as well.

The two criteria for confessional writing are that the text must reveal personal details about the ethnographer, even if this implies presenting an unflattering image of the researcher, and that the autobiographical material should ideally be interlaced with the “actual” ethnographic material. All five criteria are summarized in Table 2. Each criterion will now be discussed and its application in this research will be examined.

## Authenticity

In order to convince readers of the ethnographer’s immersion in the field, the researcher has to convey details of everyday life as lived by its members, express familiarity with the vernacular of the field, and give insight into what members think about their lives in a particular society or organization. Researchers also need to describe their path through the field so that readers can vicariously follow them around. Furthermore, the text needs to be explicit about the disciplined way in which the researcher pursued and analyzed data.

What Golden-Biddle and Locke regard as matters of authenticity, others express in terms of reliability and validity. Reliability refers to the degree of consistency with which instances are assigned to the same category by different observers or by the same observer on different occasions (Hammersley 1992, p. 67; Kirk and Miller 1986, p. 19). Even though some (e.g., Sanjek 1990, p. 394) maintain that measures of reliability are inappropriate in ethnographic work because a study can never be replicated, others (e.g., Silverman 1993 p. 146) propose that sufficiently detailed descriptions of the fieldwork allows readers to recreate the field and to formulate their own understandings and interpretations which can then be compared to the author’s. Thus reliability is established through readers’ assessment of the ethnographer’s reasonableness in interpreting events. Another way of enhancing reliability is for ethnographers to share such sensemaking work as developing interpretations and hypotheses with others outside the field during the course of the field work (Czarniawska-Joerges 1992).

Validity relates to the representativeness of the data and the truthfulness of an ethnographer’s interpretation. It expresses the extent to which an account accurately represents a social phenomenon or event (Hammersley 1992, p. 67; Kirk and Miller 1986, p. 19). To get a thorough understanding of social phenomena in their complexity and their context, ethnographers collect vast amounts of different kinds of data (e.g., documents, interviews, observation) from a variety of sources and stay in the field for an extended period of time to observe recurring patterns of behavior. Respondent validation or member checking after the fieldwork has been concluded constitutes another strategy for enhancing validity (Silverman 1993, p. 156). However, Czarniawska-Joerges qualifies the usefulness of participants feedback as a measure of validity, pointing out that people in the field might have political reasons for both supporting and denying claims made by the researcher (similarly Hammersley 1992; Miles 1979).

## Plausibility

Whereas authenticity is concerned with the conduct of field work, plausibility addresses the ethnographer’s desk work (Denzin 1997; Marcus and Fischer 1986; Van Maanen 1995), i.e., the “write up” phase. Plausibility addresses the rhetorical strategies authors rely upon to compose a text that positions their research in a way that is relevant to the concerns of the intended audience. This requires that the text be structured in a way that is consistent with the academic article genre, i.e., with specified headings and the use of citations. A plausible ethnography will also identify gaps in the literature or delineate a novel theoretical perspective to justify the research and differentiate its contribution. In order to convince readers that a study conducted in a single and maybe atypical setting, such as a funeral home (Barley 1983), is relevant to organizational research, the ethnographer has to normalize the findings by aligning them with common experiences in everyday organizational life.

In this research, I enhanced plausibility by motivating the need for practice-oriented research in the area of knowledge work. This was done by pointing to calls for such research in the literature. In Table 3, readers can find further illustrations of how plausibility was enhanced in this ethnography.

## Criticality

Criticality or cultural critique (Marcus and Fischer 1986) refers to using the research “to reflect not only on the members’ world but more importantly on the world of the researcher” (Golden-Biddle and Locke 1993, p. 614). It suggests that the purpose of ethnography is to understand ourselves and others in new and better ways. In the ethnographies that Golden-Biddle and Locke examined, they found that criticality was achieved by challenging readers to pause and think about specific situation, by provoking them to answer questions, and by guiding readers through novel ways of thinking. Marcus and Fischer maintain that cultural critique is achieved when the foreign culture is held up as a mirror to the audience’s culture so that readers learn something about themselves as they juxtapose cultures, assumptions and practices.

To achieve criticality, I wrote this research in a confessional genre, which is particularly well suited to achieving criticality in that it facilitates self-examination on the part of the author and the readers. Furthermore, the questions about the value of subjectivity in science, which I raised at the end of the discussion section, also serve to enhance the criticality of this research.

## Self-Revealing Writing

As its name suggests, a confessional account should reveal details that may not only be unflattering but that may also adversely affect the ethnographer’s reputation (Whyte 1996). And yet, confessionals need to render canonical the peculiar circumstances which ethnographers encounter in the field. Authors must show that their data are relatively uncontaminated despite the problematic and less-than-optimal research conditions that they expose in their confession.

A self-revealing text demands a personalized author (Van Maanen 1988). This not only requires the use of personal pronouns to consistently highlight that the point of view being represented is that of the field worker, but also the construction of the researcher as a reasonable yet fallible individual with whom the audience can identify. This calls for the inclusion of personal details such as age, gender, race, epistemological assumptions, and theoretical point of view.

## Interlacing Actual Ethnographic Material and Confessional Content

Even advocates of confessional writing caution against the potential excesses of this mode of representation. They warn that confessional texts can become too autobiographical and “selfserving and superficial, full of unnecessary guilt or excessive bravado” (Behar 1996, p. 14). In such instances, confessional ethnographic texts are reduced to “‘vanity ethnography,’ in which only the private muses and demons of the field worker are of concern” (Van Maanen 1988, p. 93). Autobiographical material should thus be limited to information that has relevance to the subject of the research.

To assert that one is a “white middleclass woman” or a “black gay man”...is only interesting if one is able to draw deeper connections between one’s personal experience and the subject under study. That does not require a full-length autobiography, but it does require a keen understanding of what aspects of the self are the most important filters through which one perceives the world and, more particularly, the topic being studied. (Behar 1996, p. 13)

These concerns about confessional texts becoming self-indulgent raise questions about the purpose of the confessional account, its location within the actual ethnography and the amount of autobiographical material that should be presented. There are different styles of confessional writing. Van Maanen’s (1988) confessional tale is generally found in the appendix of the actual ethnography, which is typically written as a realist tale, i.e., an account that removes the ethnographic narrator from the scene and recounts the research “findings” alone (for examples, see

Kunda 1992; Rabinow 1977; Whyte 1996). Even though the confessional tale is effective as a tool for teaching other ethnographers about the research process, its ability to achieve cultural critique is uncertain given that interest in these tales is predicated on the actual ethnography’s prior success (e.g., Whyte 1996).

Another style of confessional writing interlaces the actual ethnographic content with the confessiona material (e.g., Behar 1996). This is an effective means of achieving criticality because the ethnographer’s continuous presence in the text means that any statement about the foreign culture is also a statement about the ethnographer’s and the reader’s culture. Interlacing the self-reflexive confessional with the actual ethnographic material also minimizes the risk of the autobiographical content overpowering the actual ethnographic content, or vice versa (Behar 1996, p. 18).

In this study, by emphasizing cultural juxtaposition between the corporate knowledge workers informing practices and my own, I limited the amount of autobiographical material to details that were relevant to issues of knowledge work and the informing practices. More details and another example of how my ethnography satisfies this criterion appear in Table 3.

## Conclusions and Implications

The objectives for this research were threefold: (1) to make a contribution to the theory and practice of knowledge management by better understanding knowledge workers’ practices of informing, (2) to leverage both the reader’s and my own experience with knowledge work by presenting the fieldwork in a confessional style, and (3) to assess this research according to the criteria of a high-quality piece of ethnography and of confessional writing. These objectives were met in the following way.

This research makes a substantive contribution to our understanding of knowledge work by identifying three informing practices: ex-pressing, monitoring, and translating. Even though each of these informing practices was identified through an in-depth study of the work practices of three different groups of knowledge workers, this research shows that they are indeed transsituational because I—as an academic knowledge worker—used all three of these informing practices during my research. This research further highlights that the fundamental logic underlying each of these practices is the knowledge worker’s endeavor to balance subjectivity and objectivity (in their relative sense). This implies that informing practices are situated practical solutions to the challenge of balancing subjectivity and objectivity, where subjectivity, tacit knowledge, and the willingness to “add one’s self” is associated with being “value adding,” and objectivity is associated with authority and safety against attacks on one’s personal identity and competence.

What implications do these research insights have for practice? What do they say about the appropriateness of knowledge management technologies? This research highlights the importance of evaluating a knowledge management technology with respect to the role it plays in the knowledge worker's effort to balance subjectivity and objectivity. It suggests that a knowledge management system needs to help individuals objectify their subjective knowledge. Since any informational object or knowledge claim is subject to the socia construction process which remains largely unpredictable to the individual knowledge worker, following the conventions that represent a community’s definition of objectivity offers the individual knowledge worker protection against humiliation and attacks on his/her competence.

Templates that capture genres of communication (Orlikowski and Yates 1994; Schultze and Boland 1997) help the individual knowledge worker objectify his/her subjective insights. In this research, the overt use of templates was evident in the practice of ex-pressing, i.e., the system administrators relied on a documentation shell. A more implicit use of templates was identified in the practice of translating, i.e., the librarians manipulated the search results and formatted them such that they met their customers' expectations of what an answer to their query should look like. An opportunity for future research therefore lies in the development of templates that are matched to the informing practices of expressing, monitoring, and translating.

This research also casts doubt onto the usefulness of anonymity in knowledge management technologies. Given the positive attribution of subjectivity and tacit knowledge, it is important that individual contributors be identified. The research by Constant et al. (1994) on sharing tangible and intangible information suggests something similar. In a series of lab experiments, Constant et al. found that people are less willing to share tangible information, e.g., a computer program, with a person that previously denied them an information request, than they are to share intangible information, i.e., advice. Constant et al. conclude that intangible information is part of an individual's identity and that it is self-expressive. This implies that the sharing of intangible information results in such personal benefits as “heightened self-esteem and pride, increased selfefficacy, increased personal identification with coworkers, more respect from others and a better reputation, and reduced alienation or stronger feelings of commitment” (Constant et al. 1994, p. 406). People are thus more willing to share intangible information because this sharing results in benefits that accrue to the person sharing it. In the terminology used in the research reported in this paper, the person sharing intangible information is more likely to be viewed as “value adding” than the one sharing tangible information.

The contribution this research makes to theory lies in its demonstration of subjectivity and objectivity as a duality in knowledge work rather than a dualism. As a duality, subjectivity and objectivity are interlocked in a reciprocal relationship so that both are always necessary. This duality is the basis for the knowledge worker’s effort at balancing these two forces.

This paper also makes a methodological contribution to the field of IS. It illustrates in descriptive detail what doing ethnographic research is like and it introduces a new genre of representation, i.e., confessional writing. Through this confessional writing, cultural critique, i.e., a situation in which readers learn something about themselves and their practices by juxtaposing them with the practices of a foreign culture, is achieved.

Lastly, this paper outlines the evaluation criteria for ethnographic research, develops these criteria for confessional writing, and then assesses the research according to those criteria.
