# INVESTIGATING THE NATURE OF CHANGE IN FACTORS AFFECTING GENDER EQUITY IN THE IT SECTOR: A LONGITUDINAL STUDY OF WOMEN IN IRELAND<sup>1</sup>

Eileen Trauth College of Information Sciences & Technology, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA 16802 U.S.A. {eileen@eileentrauth.com}

Regina Connolly

DCU Business School, Dublin City University,

Dublin, IRELAND {Regina.Connolly@dcu.ie}

How and why do societal, organizational and individual factors affecting gender equity in the IT field change over time? To answer this question a longitudinal investigation of the nature of change in factors affecting the position of women in the IT profession was undertaken. It was conducted in Ireland against the backdrop of fluctuations in the nation’s socio-economic status. The individual differences theory of gender and IT was used to analyze life history interviews conducted at four points in time with a total of 63 women whose stories cover the decades from the 1970s to the 2010s. What resulted is a dynamic extension of this theory through the addition of seven themes that characterize the nature of change in factors affecting women IT professionals. The effect on women of economic changes in Ireland is shown to occur through changes in other factors: environmental (i.e., policy, infrastructural, and cultural), identity (e.g., motherhood) and individual (e.g., family). The results reveal both gradual and dramatic changes in an evolving picture of women in this sector, against the back drop of the peaks and valleys of Ireland’s economy. Both transformational and enduring images emerge from this look at Ireland over five decades.

Keywords: Culture, gender, individual differences theory of gender and IT, interpretive, Ireland, IS profession, longitudinal, social inclusion, socioeconomic, women

## Introduction

Over the past 30 years the information systems (IS) community has engaged in research and intervention<sup>2</sup> activities directed at achieving greater gender balance in the IS profession. While research attention has been given to studying factors that serve to enhance or inhibit women’s equal participation in IS, what has not been sufficiently addressed is the actual process by which such factors effect change. Consequently, what motivated this research was the desire to examine change over time in a particular context in order to shed light on how and why societal, organizational and individual factors bring about gender equity in the IT field.<sup>3</sup>

Toward that end we undertook a longitudinal study of changes in environmental and individual factors affecting women in the Irish information technology (IT) sector over five decades, from the 1970s to the 2010s. The overall story is based upon the individual stories of 63 women who, collectively, were interviewed at four different time periods: 1990, 2003, 2010 and 2012. These life history interviews were then complemented by contextual documentation, thereby linking socioeconomic changes affecting women in Ireland to their changed position in the IT sector. This work builds upon an extant gender and IS theory in order to address the research question: What is the nature of change over time in factors affecting gender equity in the IT sector?

What emerged from this research were seven core categories characterizing the nature of change in the environmental and individual factors represented in the guiding theory. Our results reveal change as an uneven process that varies across time and context, as individuals, organizations, and the wider society interact. The addition of this new change dimension constitutes a significant extension of this indigenous theory to produce a dynamic individual differences theory of gender and IT. This research represents an important step forward in understanding the gender imbalance in the IS profession by extending an indigenous gender and IS theory so that it can now account for not only the relevant factors affecting change in gender equity, but also the ways in which these factors interact to produce this change.

Our findings make both theoretical and practical contributions to the IS field. This extended theory offers future researchers a theoretical framework for examining both the factors effecting change and the mechanisms by which this change occurs. It also enables researchers working in other contexts to delve more deeply into the subtle interactions among environmental and individual influences, the transmission of these influences to women and girls, the ways in which these factors can enhance or inhibit women’s and girls’ participation in the IT field, and the alteration of these influences over time. The practical contribution is to raise awareness among policy makers, executives, IT professionals and educators about barriers encountered by women, and to suggest ways to ameliorate them. Our results point to concrete interventions that can be undertaken to redress the gender imbalance in the IS profession and provide a means of assessing their effectiveness. While Ireland is the context for this investigation of changes in the position of women and girls with respect to IT careers, the intended benefit of this work is to countries beyond its shores.

## Literature Review

## Gender and IS Research

The research on gender in the IS field is situated within two literatures. One is information systems introduction and use (e.g., Ahuja and Thatcher 2005; Bivens and Haimson 2016; Gallivan 2004; Habib and Cornford 2002; Kelan 2007; Mackrell et al. 2009; Naivinit 2009; Oreglia and Srinivasan 2016; Oudshoorn et al. 2004). While most of the gender research in this literature has focused on women, a small body looks at men and IT use (e.g., Light et al. 2008).<sup>4</sup>

The other literature, and the focus of our work, is human capital research, which examines issues about gender in the IT workforce. With the exception of a small literature on men in the IS profession (e.g., Cain and Trauth 2017; Fuller et al. 2015; Joshi et al. 2016; Kvasny et al. 2016; Riemenschneider et al. 2019) the primary focus of this work is on understanding and redressing the under-representation of women by considering girls’ educational preparation, women’s recruitment into the IS profession, the advancement and retention of women in their IS careers, and ways to achieve overall gender equity in the IS field (e.g., Ahuja 2002). This body of research originated in the early 1990s, with most of it published since 2000. One topic is career anchors and advancement (e.g., Alfrey and Windance Twine 2016; Armstrong et al. 2018; Gupta et al. 2019; Langer et al. 2020; Quesenberry and Trauth 2012; Raz and Tzruya 2018). Another focus is organizational influences, job/organization fit and workplace inequality (e.g., Kenny and Donnelly 2019; Kirton and Robertson 2018; Trauth et al. 2009; Venkatesh et al. 2017; Wijayawardena, et al. 2017). Job satisfaction and turnover is a third area of research (e.g., Joseph et al. 2015; LeRouge et al. 2013). Cultural influences gender research has been undertaken at both country and cross-country levels (e.g., Adya 2008; Frieze and Quesenberry 2020; Kvasny et al. 2008; Saifuddin et al. 2019; Trauth et al. 2003; von Hellens and Nielsen 2001; Wijayawardena et al. 2017). More recently gender and IS research has explored educational and workplace interventions to address selection of and persistence within an IS career (e.g., Adya and Kaiser 2005; Annabi and Lebovitz 2018; Blickenstaff 2005; Clayton et al. 2012; Craig 2016; Panteli 2012; Quesenberry and Trauth 2012).<sup>5</sup>

The theoretical perspectives employed in gender and IS research fall into three general categories (Trauth 2013). One is a gender essentialist perspective which assumes the existence of a gender binary and gender group-level biopsychological differences to explain the underrepresentation of women in the IS field. Wajcman (1991) critiques essentialism as the assertion of fixed and oppositional masculine and feminine natures. While much of the early gender and IS research, particularly quantitative studies of gender differences, adopted an implicit essentialist stance, more recent IS gender research has moved away from this approach. Another theoretical perspective, which also considers gender at the group level, is the social shaping of gender and gender roles (Bem 1981; Eagly et al. 2000). This perspective has been widely used in gender and IS research. However, findings from recent research that examines factors such as race and ethnicity challenge the monolithic assumptions embedded in such theorizing that all women, everywhere encounter the same gender barriers in the same ways (e.g., Kvasny et al. 2009; McGee 2018). In contrast, a newer theoretical approach is an intersectionality perspective. It rejects the gender binary and argues for considering the intersection of gender along with other characteristics such as race, ethnicity and socio-economic status (Crenshaw 1989). When applied to gender and IS research this theoretical approach allows for focusing not on “gender differences” (i.e., studying differences between women and men to better understand the gender imbalance) but on examining variation among women based on gender intersectionality.

Trauth employed this approach in developing the individual differences theory of gender and IT (IDTGIT) (Trauth 2002, 2006, 2011, 2013; Trauth and Quesenberry 2007; Trauth et al. 2004; Trauth, Quesenberry and Yeo 2008). She used a series of field studies of women in the IT workforce, carried out in several countries, to articulate and refine a theory of withingender variation in factors affecting women’s participation in the IS profession. Table 1 shows the theory’s three constructs and subconstructs.

The environmental influences construct refers to contextual factors in society, institutions and organizations which affect girls’ and women’s participation in the IS profession. Cultural, economic, infrastructural, and policy influences can facilitate or discourage interest in an IT career. Individual variation is introduced by the other two constructs of the theory. Individual identity refers to demographic characteristics such as ethnicity, age, socio-economic class, and motherhood status, which introduce variation in the effect of gender discourses and biases coming from the larger society. It also includes IT identity,<sup>6</sup> which takes into account the effects of working in different aspects of the IT field. The final construct, individual influences, incorporates the effect of significant people and experiences along with personal characteristics, on women and girls. Individual influences serve to inhibit or enhance barriers to participation in the IT field. Within gender-group variation occurs because the group-level influence of environmental factors (e.g., in a particular culture) can be either reinforced or mitigated by one’s identity and individual influences.

Since its first appearance in the IS literature (Trauth 2002), studies employing all or some constructs of this theory have explored variation among women so as to shed new light on factors affecting the gender imbalance in the IS field. In recent years this theory has been employed beyond women IS professionals to include the interplay of environmental and individual influences on information access by both men and women (e.g., Morgan et al. 2015), and social inclusion research that considers barriers encountered by underrepresented men in the IS field (e.g., Cain and Trauth 2017). Appendix A summarizes research guided by this theory.

This theory makes an important contribution to gender and IS literature through its articulation of societal, organizational and individual influences on women. It is a way to describe what observed factors are correlated with women successfully working as IT professionals. In doing so, it offers guidance to researchers regarding the types of data to collect and the ways to organize them. However, invoking Golden-Biddle and Locke’s (2007 p. 37) admonition to “construct and problematize” the relevant literature so as to reveal the theoretical gap, and the guidance of Alvesson and Sandberg (2011, p. 247) to rethink and challenge existing theoretical assumptions, we conclude that in its current state, this theory is inadequate to support the type of analyses needed to motivate actionable interventions. The major drawback of this theory is that it offers a static consideration of these factors; it does not theorize how or why these factors operate to influence women’s IT careers. Hence, there is a need to move beyond observed associations between factors and women’s participation in IT. What is needed is theoretical explanation of how and why these factors operate as they do. Our problematization challenged the theory’s assumptions, resulting in a novel research question that enabled examination of the dynamic nature of these factors over an extended period of time. This alternative assumption—that the interplay of these factors is a dynamic process responsive to actionable interventions— answered Tihanyi’s (2020, p. 329) call for research that is not just theoretically interesting, but also societally relevant.

There has been little systematic investigation of the way in which these factors exert their influence on women. Trauth and Quesenberry (2006) suggest that differences in identity factors and individual influences affect one’s interaction with environmental factors. The result is differential exposure to gender discourses (i.e., whether or not a woman receives a particular gendered message about IT careers), differential experience of gender discourses (i.e., the degree to which she might internalize a gender barrier), and differential response to them (i.e., how she might react to disempowering messages or barriers). Two other papers (Howcroft and Trauth 2008; Trauth and Howcroft 2006) argue that using a critical rather than an interpretive epistemology would lead to a better understanding of underlying forces such as power dynamics that could shed light on how and why women are underrepresented in the IS profession. Another paper describes the use of theatre to enact the ways in which gender barriers are encountered and internalized (Trauth, Keifer-Boyd, and Trauth 2016). Finally, a study of implicit theoretical perspectives underlying Australian newspaper articles about the IT gender gap suggests one way that these factors interact (Ridley and Young 2012). Given this limited body of research into the way in which the factors represented in this theory produce their effect, we saw an opportunity to overcome the static nature of this theory by using it in a longitudinal study to investigate the nature of changes in factors affecting women’s participation in the IT sector.

## Background on Ireland

Ireland is an appropriate setting to examine the evolving position of women working in IT because it has undergone a transformation from a poor, traditional and peripheral European country to a modern global economy due, in large part, to its robust IT sector (The Economist 2004). The opening up of IT careers to women occurred alongside considerable socio-economic change as the path to Ireland’s contemporary IT sector is rooted in the cultural, political, infrastructural, and economic pillars of the independent Irish State that was formed in 1922 (Breen et al. 1990; Daly 1992). In a relatively short period of time Ireland redefined its identity from that of a conservative, isolationist and rural nation to a modern, progressive, outward-looking, and fully engaged player in the world economy. This resulted from concerted efforts at industrial inward investment (Bradley and Murphy 1989; Coogan 1987; Lee 1989; O’Malley 1989). Electronics was an early industry attracted to Ireland. Over time it grew into an

IT sector that was an increasingly larger portion of the economy (CSO 2017a). Despite economic fluctuations in Ireland, a consistent trend has been the steady liberalization of women’s position in society. Because of the dramatic socio-economic changes since 1970, Ireland provides a fitting context to explore the nature of change over time in factors affecting gender equity in the IT sector. A timeline of major socio-economic events is shown in Figure 1 and discussed below.

The starting point for Ireland’s transformation was the ideal of Irish society articulated in 1943 by Eamon de Valera, the Irish Taoiseach (Prime Minister) as

… the home of a people who valued material wealth only as a basis of right living … who were satisfied with frugal comfort and devoted their leisure to things of the spirit; a land … bright with cozy homesteads, joyous with … the romping of sturdy children, the contests of athletic youth, the laughter of comely maidens; whose firesides would be forums for the wisdom of serene old age ... the home of a people living the life that God desires men should live” (Moynihan 1980, p. 466).

Prior to 1967, free vocational schools provided training for working class employment in trades, manufacturing, and agriculture; fee-paying secondary schools were reserved for children of the upper classes (Whelan and Hannan 1999). By the 1970s it was apparent that this bucolic vision of Ireland was unrealistic and the government’s policies of isolationism and protectionism were not working. Stagnation of the Irish economy led to high unemployment, mass emigration and a need for policy response (Barry and Bradley 1991; Government of Ireland 1956).

## Economic Changes

In order to boost employment and stem emigration, de Valera’s vision of Irish society was supplanted by an economic strategy of inward investment from multinational companies (NESC 1982c, 1983a). This imperative was exacerbated by Ireland’s young population, driving educational reforms and establishment of regional technical colleges (CSO 1971, 1986; Ó Buachalla 1988; Trauth 1993). Joining the European Economic Community (EEC), precursor of the European Union (EU), accelerated Ireland’s integration into the European and world economies (OECD 1994) by providing financial support, access to a large economic market, increased job creation, and foreign direct investment (RTE Archives 2019).

The nascent IT sector was first conceptualized as a multinational electronics manufacturing industry (Trauth 2000). The first multinational computer company, Digital Equipment Corporation, opened its European manufacturing facility in Galway in 1971; other technology firms followed (Fogarty 1973; NESC 1982a, 1982b, 1983b; Whelan 1980). By the 1980s Ireland’s plan of industrialization through invitation was revealing both successes and failures (Department of Industry and Commerce 1986; Fitzpatrick and Kelly 1985; Government of Ireland 1982, 1984a, 1984b; Mjøset 1992; National Planning Board 1984; NESC 1989, 1990; Trauth 1996). Considerable investment in developing an electronics industry (Department of Industry and Commerce 1989; O’Malley 1990) had not yet produced anticipated economic benefits through reduced emigration, a higher standard of living, and spillover to an indigenous IT sector (Foley and McAleese 1991; Trauth 1999). A prolonged recession with high unemployment characterized the 1980s (Hogan 2010). And whereas earlier emigrants were mostly unskilled laborers, this emigration wave constituted a national “brain drain” from a high proportion of university graduates (CSO 2004; Fitzgerald 2018).

The 1990s saw this economic development plan finally yielding results (Government of Ireland 1994; Industrial Policy Review Group 1992; NESC 1992). As computer manufacturing jobs were moving to other countries, the seeds of an Irish IT knowledge base, planted 20 years earlier, began to bear fruit (National Software Directorate 1992). This indigenous software sector slowed emigration and by mid decade the Celtic Tiger was born (Information Society Steering Committee 1996). Ireland now competed with Asian “tiger nations” for a share of the global information economy as multinationals embraced offshore outsourcing to reduce costs and increase competitiveness (Trauth 2001). Ireland’s diversified primary information sector consisted of hardware assembly, software development and information services; its secondary information sector grew as IT was infused throughout all industrial areas (Porat 1977; Trauth 2000).

Ireland’s robust IT sector in the new millennium increasingly emphasized high-end software development over lowerskilled hardware assembly (Heavin et al. 2003), thereby attracting young, mobile IT professionals (McWilliams 2005).

Irish real estate prices soared and, reflecting a post-colonial legacy of a high land ownership rate (CSO 2011; Statista 2015), land owners were some of the wealthiest people in the world! But in 2007 Ireland’s fast-growing economy slowed dramatically (Tully 2009). An economic contraction raised the country’s debt level to 32 percent of its GDP (CIA 2021; CSO 2010; Department of Finance 2018). Both emigration and the deficit grew in this recession (CSO 2017a), business volume plummeted, and some multinational companies relocated elsewhere.

During the final decade of this study Ireland weathered the storm of economic turbulence and of readjustments in the IT sector. As lower-skilled computer hardware assembly jobs moved to lower-cost regions (Barry and Van Egeraat 2008) Ireland responded to workforce opportunities resulting from the emergence of cloud computing,<sup>7</sup> mobile technology and social media. High-end electronic design, software, and research activities expanded (McHugh 2010); a skills shortage produced competition between indigenous and multinational firms for graduates (Fitzgerald et al. 2014). Assembly firms moved into higher value-added work: sales, technical support call centers, and logistics (Grimes 2006), erasing distinctions between multinational companies’ focus on hardware manufacturing and indigenous companies’ involvement with software (Bobek and Wickham 2015). A new wave of Irish startups arose alongside firms such as Google, Twitter and Yahoo, which congregated in the “Silicon Docks,” the former dockland area of Dublin (Newenham 2015).

## Societal Changes

Accompanying Ireland’s economic fluctuations were significant societal changes related to policy, culture and infrastructure. At the beginning of the time period covered in this longitudinal study, Ireland had the cultural norms and values of a traditional, Catholic, rural society, which were reinforced in public policies. The Irish Constitution afforded the institutional Catholic Church a special standing<sup>8</sup> and gave explicit recognition to a woman’s role and her duties within the home. Other public policies reinforcing this cultural view related to marriage, reproductive rights, and work. Until the passage of the Divorce Referendum in 1996, marital dissolution in Ireland was not possible. Before 1993, birth control pills were legally available only when dispensed by a pharmacist with a medical prescription. The dominant public discourse of the 1980s was around issues of sexuality and family life (Fahey and Layte 2007).

Throughout the 1920s and 1930s, Ireland adopted legislation differentiating the positions of men and women in public life (Redmond and Harford 2010), whereby a woman was economically dependent upon her husband. For example, in 1932, facing an economic downturn, the Irish government instituted a marriage bar in the civil service, which required women to quit their jobs upon marriage. This bar remained in effect for women working in state organizations until its removal in 1973 (Government of Ireland 1973). In view of the number of state-sponsored industries at the time, including the Irish airline (Aer Lingus), electricity supply board, post office (An Post), Irish sugar factories, food board (Bord Bia), national telecommunications service (Eircom), transport (CIE), turf production (Bord na Móna), industrial development authority (IDA), radio and television of Ireland (RTE), health boards, and local authorities, this bar was extensive both in reach and impact. Women were also openly discriminated against in employment, tax and welfare systems (Beale 1986). Prior to 1973, single mothers and separated women were ineligible for social welfare payments and prior to enactment of the 1974 Children’s Allowance Act, the person designated to collect the monthly child benefit payment was the father.

However, the ending period of this longitudinal study gives witness to seismic societal changes related to gender that have transformed Irish society into one of the most secular and progressive countries in Europe. In 2015, Ireland legalized same-sex marriage by popular vote (Hakim and Dalby 2015). Two years later Leo Varadkar, at age 38, became Taoiseach (Irish prime minister), signaling Ireland as a modern, diverse and inclusive country; he is the son of an Indian immigrant and became the country’s youngest and first openly gay prime minister (McDonald 2017). Further change occurred in 2018 when the Irish electorate voted (66.4 percent) to repeal its constitutional ban on abortion (Kelly 2018). The magnitude and speed of societal transformation in this country over a relatively short period of time is remarkable.

Over the course of this study’s time period, women became a significant component of Ireland’s economic growth (Fitzgerald 2018). In 1971 only 300,000 out of 1.5 million women worked outside the home, the vast majority being single women in low-income occupations (CSO 1971). By 2016 cultural change and policy changes had expanded their career opportunities. Women represented 52.2 percent of all thirdlevel graduates in Ireland (CSO 2016) and 45.1 percent of the labor force. More than 62 percent of married women aged 20-39 were employed (CSO 2016). Infrastructural and public policy changes affecting childcare services accompanied women’s movement into the paid workforce. An Expert Working Group on Childcare (1997) and a National Childcare Strategy (2000) focused on affordability for lower income parents. In 2019, the Irish government published the detailed implementation steps for “First Five,” Ireland’s 10-year strategy to assist parents in balancing working and caring duties.<sup>9</sup>

Policy and infrastructure initiatives to encourage girls’ involvement in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) careers paralleled the cultural and economic changes affecting women. The Women in Technology & Science (WITS) organization, established in 1990, works to promote Irish women in STEM.<sup>10</sup> Ireland participated in the EU project Female Empowerment in Science and Technology Academia (FESTA) (2012–2016), which examined structural changes needed for gender equality in university STEM units.<sup>11</sup> The Athena SWAN Program, which began in Ireland in 2015,<sup>12</sup> certifies academic institutions meeting gender equality criteria in order to advance the representation of women in STEM.<sup>13</sup> In 2019 Science Foundation Ireland’s Discover Programme committed €4.4 million to increase engagement with STEM, with specific measures to increase by 40 percent the number of school girls taking STEM subjects for the Leaving Certificate (i.e., secondary school degree).<sup>14</sup> Such efforts to address the gender imbalance are consistent with European-wide efforts, many of which have been driven by EU directives and funding. One example is the “30 percent movement.” Germany’s 2016 law requires that 30 percent of executive and supervisory positions be held by women. Irish organizations, not bound by law, have been motivated by the incentive to join the “30 Percent Club” (2019), which was initiated in 2015.

## Methods

Longitudinal studies in IS research are not uncommon. They include IS introduction and adoption (Angst et al. 2017; Karlsson 2013; Sun 2013; Vaast and Walsham 2009; Venkatesh et al. 2016; Viega et al. 2014); uses and impacts of particular systems (Atasoy et al. 2016; Menon and Kohli 2013); and compliance with corporate IS policies (Currie et al. 2018; D’Arcy and Lowry 2019; Steinbart et al. 2016; Vance et al. 2018). However, this longitudinal study is different in several respects. First, this study covers a much longer time period: five decades. Second, unlike the organizational or industry levels of analysis typical of IS longitudinal studies, this study was enacted at the country level of analysis. Finally, the focus is not on the IT artifact, itself, but rather on the people who develop it. The purpose was to trace over time—through the voices of interview participants—societal changes affecting women in the Irish IT sector. The particular environmental and individual factors influencing women’s participation in the IS profession has been theorized elsewhere. However, the ways in which these factors effect change have not been taken into account in previous research employing this theory. Hence, we undertook this longitudinal study in search of a better understanding of the nature of changes in societal, organizational and individual factors affecting the participation of women in the IT profession. In doing so, this study responds to calls for longitudinal studies of gender and the IS profession (Gorbacheva et al. 2019). The 50-year time period covered in this study, combined with the country-level analysis, allowed us to make unique contributions to IS literature. By tracing the nature of the changing factors over a long period of time, and by demonstrating the important role of country-level economic and societal policies, we have contributed new insights into the gender imbalance in the IS field.

## Sampling and Data Collection

A total of 63 women working in the Irish IT profession between 1990 and 2012 participated in this study. A twotiered sampling methodology was employed. Purposive sampling criteria was applied in order to ensure that participants represented a variety of demographic characteristics (including age, geographical location, educational background, socio-economic status, relationship status, family composition, and type of IT work), consistent with the guiding theory. Liaisons between participants and interviewers assisted with recruitment and provided an element of trust. These included colleagues, university and corporate personnel, and the interviewees themselves. The biographies of individual respondents and the year in which the interview was conducted appears in Appendix B, Table B1. A summary of respondent profiles is shown in Table 2.

Semi-structured, life history interviews lasting 60 to 90 minutes in length were conducted in person, via telephone or via Skype. They were audio recorded for later transcription and coding. A pseudonym assigned to each informant ensured confidentiality; these pseudonyms are invoked in the Results section of the paper. Women were asked about their journeys into the IT field, educational backgrounds, influential people, work histories, and their experiences as women working in the masculine-dominated profession. Appendix B, Table B2 shows the complete list of themes discussed in each of the interview cycles. It is important to note that each woman’s interview informed more than one decade because she commented not only on her current work situation but also on her earlier work experiences and time at school. Hence, the data used to characterize the position of women in each decade is drawn from multiple data sets. The number of participants from each interview cohort whose comments informed the interpretation of each decade is detailed in Appendix B, Table B3.

Strategic sampling criteria was applied to investigate changes in factors influencing women in several ways. First, the four different time periods of data capture were chosen to allow for sampling of different economic conditions in Ireland. Second, conducting the interviews across several decades enabled us to sample the significant changes occurring in the country’s social and cultural contexts. Third, the selection of each respondent sample took into account the women’s representativeness of changes occurring in the IT sector during that particular time period. For example, characteristic of the labor pool in 1990, less than half of the sample included women whose highest educational attainment was a university degree. All but one respondent worked in the primary IT sector, typically in computer hardware final assembly, reflecting Ireland’s IT sector at that time. However, subsequent data samples contained different respondent characteristics reflective of societal changes. For example, by 2003 the technology sector in Ireland had broadened and diversified. Consequently, that interview cohort included more women working in the software part of the IT sector, and who had university degrees. In the 2010 sample, reflective of the overall economic and cultural changes that had taken place, more of the respondents worked in the secondary IT sector, in jobs such as banking. In 2012 the informants had higher educational credentials, smaller families, and greater internationalization, consistent with these demographic changes ioccurring in Irish society (Abrivia 2016; CSO 2017b; Donnelly 2019; Engineers Ireland 2020; OECD 2018).

In order to explore the nature of change, women were asked to situate their individual career experiences within the larger context of economic, cultural, infrastructural and public policy factors that have influenced their careers, and to reflect on changes in gender relations in society and in the IT profession. To further situate these interviews within the varying socio-economic contexts of Ireland over time we also triangulated these interviews with economic development policies, mass media products, and contemporaneous research literature. Detail on sampling procedures for each data set is provided in Appendix B, Table B4.

## Data Analysis

Transcripts of all 63 interviews were coded and analyzed with selected illustrative and representative quotes appearing in this paper. Multiple rounds of coding took place in order to serve the theoretical goals of this research. The first author conducted theory-driven coding of transcripts from each interview period in a search for evidence of themes consistent with the constructs in the guiding theory. Open coding by both authors was also conducted on transcripts to explore what had changed from one time period to the next, what had not, and possible explanations. We undertook this type of coding in order to identify and refine emerging core categories about the nature of change.

At the outset of this longitudinal study theory-driven coding of the 1990 transcripts<sup>15</sup> established a baseline of environmental and individual factors, consistent with the guiding theory’s constructs, which helped to explain how women came to be working in the emerging IT sector. In addition, open coding resulted in a set of provisional categories about change. By the time of the 2003 interviews economic, social and policy changes enacted in previous decades were bearing fruit in relation to women’s participation in IT. Hence, in addition to theory-driven coding of factors influencing change, open coding resulted in additional themes about changes in the ways in which these factors were affecting women’s participation in the IT profession. These themes were then used in a retrospective coding of the 1990 transcripts and the provisional themes about change from 1990, in turn, were used to code the 2003 transcripts. Coding of the 2010 transcripts followed a similar pattern. First, theorydriven coding of influencing factors was undertaken. Then, provisional categories about change identified in the 2003 transcripts were employed to refine these themes. This was followed by a search for new change themes. These new themes that emerged from the 2010 interviews were subsequently used in retrospective coding of the 2003 and 1990 transcripts for purposes of further refinement. The same procedure was followed for the 2012 transcripts. That is, theory-driven coding for influencing factors was followed by refinement of provisional categories identified in 1990, 2003 and 2010, and the identification of any previously unidentified themes. These new themes were used, in turn, for retrospective coding of the transcripts from earlier interviews. When this was completed, we determined that theoretical saturation had been achieved.

Both forms of interpretive analysis—based on the constructs of the guiding theory as well as refinement of emerging core categories of change—were conducted through the examination of strips (Agar 1986), that is, meaningful text units consisting of a phrase, sentence or related set of sentences about a topic. A strip was interpreted through the hermeneutic process of developing meaning within context in conjunction with either the guiding theory or emerging core categories about the nature of change. To this end, the transcripts of each interview were categorized into meaningful strips then coded and recoded to bring emergent themes about change into greater focus. This involved assigning provisional labels to emerging themes about the nature of change. As the coding proceeded, strips that did not conform to the expectations embodied in existing provisional categories forced a reconsideration of an existing category and possibly the creation of a new one. (Further coding detail is provided in Appendix B, Table B5 showing theoretical coding and Table B6 showing detail about coding for emergent categories of change.)

An example of this process was the emergence of core categories that could distinguish change effected through factors exerting a “direct” influence from those exerting an “indirect” influence on women. The initial provisional category “influence” emerged in strips obtained from the 1990 interviews. An example is the cultural expectation that women should leave the workplace upon motherhood. The following strip was provisionally coded “culture: influence”: “People still want a parent at home with children if they can afford it. [Eileen]. However, as coding of this provisional category was carried out in the 2003 transcripts evidence of two different kinds of influence began to emerge. This, in turn, required returning to the strips from 1990 originally coded as “influence” and recoding them as either “direct influence” or “indirect influence.” Analysis of transcripts of subsequent interviews (i.e., 2010 and 2012) engaged in the same process, until a stable set of seven core categories emerged. Klein and Myers’ (1999) principles for evaluating interpretive field studies were applied to demonstrate the believability of the findings from this qualitative analysis (see Appendix B, Table B7).

## Results

The individual stories of the 63 women working in the Irish IT sector show that the position of women does not exist in a vacuum, that institutional and societal barriers influence women’s entrance into and persistence within an IT career. Institutional barriers include mass media messages about gender roles, an educational system that limits girls’ exposure to IT careers, and the structure of the industry in which women work. Societal barriers come from the culture, economic factors, societal infrastructure, and laws and policies. Our results demonstrate the ways in which these factors had their effect. They were experienced through influential people, organizations and women’s own personal characteristics. Societal limitations acted directly upon women through laws requiring them to quit their jobs upon marriage. Other societal influences act through significant persons in girls’ and women’s lives. Parents, teachers, guidance counselors, peers and sponsors communicated messages from the wider society about a woman’s engagement with IT careers. Hence, some of the barriers girls and women experienced appeared to come from within as they consciously or unconsciously internalized limitations from external sources about their interest, capability, opportunities and daily work, as well as expectations regarding their future. These messages and barriers were also dynamic. They were not uniform across all girls and women, and also changed over time. Young women coming of age in different time periods received different messages about working in a technical field.

Our analysis of interviews using the constructs of the guiding theory resulted in the set of key findings about the environmental, identity and individual factors affecting gender relations in the IT sector across the five eras (see Appendix C). But in the course of conducting the interviews about factors affecting women’s position, another dimension came into focus. This was a rendering of how these factors had their effect. Through our sampling and coding processes we uncovered and refined seven emergent themes about the nature of change related to the theoretical constructs/factors. Table 3 shows the factors juxtaposed against these themes, thereby presenting a new change dimension. Transformation was sometimes manifested as impact, reflecting changes occurring elsewhere in society. Change as influence was sometimes direct and other times indirect, acting through an intermediary. Some factors consistently affected women, while factors such as women’s career ambition did not initially have an effect on women but over time did so. Factors such as new laws resulted in abrupt change; factors such as culture demonstrated incremental change. Finally, the alteration in women’s position often occurred through the interaction of multiple factors, such as economic and cultural ones.

In the face of transformation that is neither uniform nor linear, the addition of this change dimension offers a means of explicating how and why environmental and individual factors differ in their effect on women in different contexts and times. As such, it represents a significant theoretical extension of the IDTGIT. By articulating themes characterizing the change process, it is now possible to reveal not only what change factors affected women over time but also how this occurred. This dynamic extension of the individual differences theory of gender and IT resulting from our research is depicted in Appendix D. Below we discuss the environmental, identity and individual factors that produced the transformation of women’s position in the IT sector over the 50-year period of this study. We employ the seven emergent themes to consider why and how these factors varied over time.

## Changes in Environmental Factors Influencing Gender Relations in the IT Sector

## Economy

We begin with the economic story. Unemployment and emigration led to a fundamental change in economic policy favoring inward investment by multinational electronics firms. This, in turn, led to the need for a qualified workforce to support Ireland’s nascent IT industry. To meet demand, women were encouraged to enter this field. Concurrently, a twoincome family was needed for the increased standard of living Irish families wanted. Over the ensuing decades and despite economic fluctuations the IT sector continued to evolve (Cassidy et al. 2009; Collins 2007; Jordan and O’Leary 2008; Lin et al. 2010). The interaction of these economic forces with other environmental factors had a profound effect on the opportunities for women’s participation in the IT field.

The other environmental factors reveal how Irish society was realigned in order to support its economic imperative. Policy, culture, and infrastructure sometimes initiated change; other times they reflected accommodations needed to keep pace with changes occurring elsewhere. Over the period of 50 years some aspects of society were altered abruptly; others were so incremental as to appear static. Throughout it all, women encountered both enablers and inhibitors to their forward momentum.

## Policy

Laws and policies played various roles in the story of women’s participation in IT. Policy incentives to attract multinational firms initiated change. The gradual dismantling of laws supporting a patriarchal society reflected transformation occurring throughout society. Gender equality legislation was directly related to the position of women, whereas economic development policies, were indirectly linked to women insofar as they created a high-tech employment sector that welcomed women. Policy changes required of Ireland’s membership in the EEC (Department of Labor 1981, 1987), such as the removal of the marriage bar, drove some of the cultural and infrastructural modifications. Greater policy focus on the position of women (Blackwell 1986; Department of the Taoiseach 1987; Employment Equality Agency 1989) led to a gradual shift in the cultural norm away from woman quitting jobs upon marriage (Rudd 1982; Wickham 1982). This gender equality legislation furthered the empowerment of women in Irish society and in the workplace, demonstrating changes resulting from the interaction among environmental factors that had an effect on individual characteristics.

Educational policies had an indirect effect on women. To feed the Celtic Tiger government schemes were needed to rapidly develop a nation of computer literate citizens. Ennis, in the West of Ireland, became an Information Age Town implementing both technical and educational infrastructures: broadband network, computers, software, Internet connections, and training (McInerney 2003; McQuillan 2002).<sup>16</sup> Educational policy also helped to erode barriers embedded in secondary school curricula when formal educational credentials became increasingly required for entry into IT jobs (Kelly et al. 2010; McInerney et al. 2009). Further changes in educational infrastructure were driven by policies that established new universities and other third level institutions.

## Culture

It is that if jobs are scarce why have two partners working? “Isn’t she married and her husband working?” If a married woman returns to work it’s even worse. It’s viewed as taking a job away from a young, single girl. I think the underlying view is women should be kept with the menial jobs because they will be leaving them anyway. [Patricia]

The culture factor evidenced mostly incremental and indirect change in gender relations. During the 1970s and 1980s, the dominant culture of traditional gender roles in a one-income family (Pelan 2005) inhibited women’s participation in IT work. Women usually worked for wages only until they got married or perhaps had children. A few chose career over marriage and family. When married women and mothers began to work outside the home, it became culturally acceptable out of economic necessity not personal fulfillment, and domestic gender roles remained unchanged. Not surprisingly, women noted being overlooked for career advancement because of the general perception that women had jobs and men had careers.

I was at a conference … recently and was the only woman in a group of 30 fairly senior attendees. One man who was there said there were not enough women represented. There are lots of women programmers [but not that many women managers]. This is a very traditional society. People are shocked that I am 34 and unmarried .... It is still frowned upon for a mother to work. [Patricia]

The need for qualified IT workers motivated change in cultural assumptions about gender roles, who could have technical careers, and how they could prepare for them. Economic considerations influenced Irish culture to shift away from traditional norms and values about women’s place in society (Stopper 2006), albeit unevenly. Some informants witnessed changing views about women and work, others noted persistent traditional norms as the natural order of things (Jackson 1986).

In my time, it was, when the girl grows up, you get married and have kids. There was no push towards a career .... [T]he boys would go to college and have a big career and women were expected to get married and raise a family. [Michelle]

But women observed a culture of equality in the new electronics industry where traditional norms about work were not as fixed as in other industries. Outside influences from the multinational companies reinforced the norm of women working in this sector (Weisinger and Trauth 2002). It was a meritocracy, at least in theory: a woman could advance insofar as this sector was based upon neither physical skills nor land and family standing.

The electronics businesses are more advanced with respect to women. The view is that if you are capable, and improve yourself, and a job is open you may get in. [Elizabeth]

Informants noted cultural changes occurring in the 1990s as women were seen as having careers not simply jobs; they no longer had to make a choice between career and family. Cultural norms about women, and women working in IT (Bradley and Valiulis 1997; Callahan and Farrell 1991; McEvoy 1998; Ward 1991), were accommodating to broader changes in society. The establishment of women’s studies curricula along with increased emphasis on gender in Irish

Studies programs (Lyons 1997/1998; O’Ceirin and O’Ceirin 1996; O’Connor 1999) resulted in a feeling that women’s position had risen along with the ascendance of the Celtic Tiger:

Because there won’t be a sense that girls do some subjects and boys do other subjects and girls do certain jobs and boys do other jobs, you know? .... And so, I think things have changed from that perspective. [Brigid].

Significant cultural changes were evident in the new century. Women had broken through the barrier of hearth and home. Gender equity had generated enough momentum to be sustained through turbulent economic times. It was considered normal for women to study technical subjects and choose IT careers. This euphoric feeling is exemplified in the first author’s interaction with two Irish students who were enrolled in an international gender course she was teaching in Austria in 2008. During their presentation on women and IT they compared working in this sector to “being a rock star.” One said her parents wanted both her and her brother to work in IT. Reflecting more cultural change, there were women professors in the classrooms, and women students interested in technical careers were not discouraged as they had been in earlier decades.

Women raised issues not about getting hired but about getting promoted. Economic considerations were reflected in their concern about advancing their careers and salaries. The interaction of several factors was in evidence (McCann 2013): a need for role models, mentors and sponsors (individual influences), gender stereotypes about technical competence (culture), and views about a mother’s commitment to work (culture). This transformation of women’s career expectations had occurred through the interaction of economic and cultural factors. The economic need for women to work in IT led to cultural acceptance of women working outside the home and the eventual family reliance upon her income. This, in turn, influenced a cultural change to women wanting to have careers. By the end of this study period, gender equity had been redefined from obtaining an IT job to advancing in an IT career. Nevertheless, the uneven and incremental nature of cultural change can be seen in the domestic sphere. Some women in every interview cohort raised issues about this topic. While culture was shown to be an influential factor throughout the five decades of the study, it shifted from being primarily an inhibiting influence to becoming an enabler of women’s participation in IT work.

The uneven pace of change resulting from contextual differences is also reflected in shifting societal norms about gender roles as they apply to balancing work and family. They changed very slowly towards greater involvement of men in the domestic sphere as evidenced in the provision of paternity leave by companies. This change instantiates interaction between cultural norms and economic considerations: pragmatic concerns of employers in the 2010s experiencing difficulties in attracting and retaining staff. Firms sought to differentiate themselves by providing support for reconciling work and family life. That economic and cultural circumstances were different in Ireland in the last century, and might be different in other national contexts demonstrates that change occurs unevenly and varies by time and context.

## Societal Infrastructure

Societal infrastructure, like culture, initially assumed a stayat-home housewife (Council for the Status of Women 1981). Limited store hours in evenings and on weekends, schools expecting only the mother for parent–teacher meetings—that occurred during the day—and scarce childcare facilities reflected a traditional society that served to indirectly inhibit women’s full participation in the IT sector. Over time and demonstrating uneven change, the educational infrastructure acted to both enable and inhibit gender equity, exerting both positive and negative influences on women’s IT careers. Since free secondary education did not become available until 1967, both men and women entering the IT workforce during the 1970s might only have had a National (i.e., primary) School or Intermediate Certificate education. Consequently, many jobs in the IT sector at that time required neither the Leaving Certificate nor a third level (i.e., post-secondary) degree. Further, education was not always embraced, parti cularly for girls, because of pressure to leave school and seek work (Gray and O’Carroll 2012). Those women with professional qualifications would have had them in teaching or nursing. Grainne cited her aunts as typical: half of them became nuns (and teachers), and the rest went to the UK to be nurses.

The migration of IT work from hardware assembly to software development and IT innovation increased employers expectations about formal education. Consequently, the educational infrastructure became an increasingly important factor of gender equity. The positive impact for women was government policy initiating new third level institutions and programs within existing universities. However, a gendersegregated secondary education infrastructure was initially a direct barrier to girls who were interested in STEM careers. Girls’ schools did not offer the prerequisite courses needed for admission to technical degree programs in third level institutions. Seen as masculine subjects, advanced courses in STEM remained within the purview of boys’ schools. Girls schools, for the most part, lacked both the expertise to advise students about technical careers and the curricula to prepare them for one. But the schools responded in divergent ways. Some girls received no help from counselors and teachers. Other single-sex schools with traditional curricula offered girls alternative means of studying STEM subjects. In these respects, the educational infrastructure lagged behind the cultural transformation.

By the 2000s, informants viewed IT as a constantly changing field requiring continuous skill updating. Yet, mixed signals remained. One the one hand, a new, more applied secondary mathematics curriculum was introduced in 2012<sup>17</sup> but due to the recession, funding for career guidance was cut.<sup>18</sup> Universities, as well, both enhanced and inhibited women’s IT careers. While economic and cultural factors encouraged women to study technical subjects in university, there remained a disconnect, at times, between the grand societal vision and the day-to-day reality, Brianna said the absence of women professors or graduate students when she was in university made her wonder if she was in the wrong place. Sometimes the message emanating from the interaction of technical stereotypes with educational infrastructure was explicitly communicated to women students, as Nuala’s compelling story indicates. After overcoming barriers in her secondary school that did not offer the mathematics prerequisite course for a computer science degree, she had to cope in university with a professor who did not accept the technical competence of women students. As she was giving her part of the final presentation for a semester-long, team computing project, the professor refused to make eye contact with Nuala and appeared not to be paying attention as she spoke. When a fellow student (a man) on her team attempted to help her present, she was accused by the professor of not doing her share of the work and almost failed the course.

Changes in the educational infrastructure illustrate the interaction of several factors. The policy of providing free secondary education resulted in an abrupt change. But it was experienced differently based on the identity characteristic of socio-economic status (SES). Whereas secondary education had already been available to Irish people of means, this new policy was felt more directly by those of lower SES. Likewise grants that became available in the 1980s for post-secondary study of IT subjects had a larger impact on individuals with lower SES. Finally, the shift from single-sex schools run by Catholic nuns to nondenominational, co-educational comprehensive secondary schools, which provided girls with greater access to STEM subjects illustrates the way in which the educational infrastructure changed in response to changes occurring elsewhere in society.

## Changes in Identity Factors Influencing Gender Relations in the IT Sector

## Personal Identity

The identity factors that shed light on variation in IT career choice and enactment over time included: age, family of origin, geography, educational background, socio-economic status (SES), marital and parental status, and IT identity. Motherhood status was the identity factor whose changes had the most profound effect on gender relations.

Initially, marital status and motherhood had a direct influence on a woman’s career as they interacted with environmental factors. Legally, the marriage bar forced married women in most industry sectors to leave jobs. Culturally, prevailing gender norms dictated that motherhood meant women should leave the workplace even where it was not legally mandated. Women had to make a clear-cut choice between marriage and children, or remaining single and having a career. Over time, the influence of motherhood on IT careers became more indirect.

So this was in 1990, what was the prevailing view about women like you, who were working full time, who had careers, and then were going to have children? [Interviewer]

I think it was becoming more normal, but it wasn’t totally normal … [Riana]

Married women, particularly mothers, remaining in the IT workplace for economic reasons engendered the cultural normalization of mothers being IT professionals. Nevertheless, domestic issues remained unchanged in women’s minds. Some informants had delayed marriage and/or child bearing, and were coming to terms with work-family decisions against the backdrop of the ticking “biological clock.” Those who either did or expected to remain in the workforce after having children acknowledged the persistence of imbalanced domestic and parental responsibilities. They said women were still expected to manage priorities about career and family, that women still bore all or primary responsibility for taking care of children.

Men’s domestic roles had not kept pace with women’s movement into the workplace, giving evidence of uneven changes in cultural norms.

That’s the answer you always get from men. “They’re not putting themselves forward.” “They’re not ambitious.” “They will not do it.” But, of course, we know that there is a complex set of reasons why that is the case. Some of it has to do with the amount of hours and commitment that is required in an industry such as ours. On a personal choice they do not want to make that decision. I mean a lot of men here—senior guys—get strung up every now and then about how much time they’re not spending with their families .... And they get a lot of grief at home about that, right. But they do not change .... That is simply not the case for women that you talk to, mothers that I talk to. It’s not about not wanting to work or not wanting to get on. Of course, they want to get on, same as anybody else, and realize their potential. But they’re very concerned and guilty about what effect that’s having on their children, and they will pull back. That’ll go on the back burner. [Deirdre]

In fact, some husbands experienced peer pressure to not participate in domestic work.

Putting it on the record, I have a brilliant husband who… if you got down to who does what, it would certainly be 50/50 in our house. But I would have to say from a lot of people that … you’d hear them talking. And you kind of think, “She’s working, she’s doing this,” you know, “How does she do all this?” And you think, that guy’s a complete tosser<sup>19</sup>.” .... Men will certainly still very comfortably fall into the traditional role, and it does amaze me how women of my own, you know, level at work and stuff are still very inclined to adopt the Irish Mother, which I would have thought changed in my generation. And I would say that my house is very untypical. [Aisling]

But by the end of the 2010s it was clear that incremental change influencing cultural norms about fatherhood was in evidence. In 2019 Guinness/Diageo Ireland established a paternity leave policy (Slattery 2019), Vodafone Ireland introduced four months of fully paid parental leave for all employees, and Hewlett Packard Enterprise introduced six months’ fully paid paternity leave. This adjusted cultural factor further enabled women’s IT careers.

The interaction of two factors—motherhood identity and economy—was revealed in an issue about maternity leave.

Informants in 1990 noted that being on maternity leave, even for six months, in a small company in the aggressive high-tech industry would make it difficult to get back into one’s career. Informants returned to this theme in 2012. In a tight economy, women said, firms might be reluctant to hire women who would be going out on maternity leave.

As noted earlier, Trauth and Quesenberry (2006) stated that identity characteristics help to explain variation in factors effect on women, which they characterized as differential exposure to, experience of, and response to enablers and inhibitors of IT careers. For example, women in this study who did not have children were not exposed to the same IT career barriers derived from cultural norms about working outside the home and the childcare infrastructure. Another identity factor directly related to varied exposure to barriers is age. The older the participant in this study the more she was exposed to inhibiting forces in the culture, and from significant people in her life.

I left secondary school in 1978. The attitude at the school was that you would go to university and get a job, but behind that, the image was “have children” .... You were expected to be high achievers, yet fellow students thought it was weird that one of our classmates wanted to go into engineering. The general attitude in my age group is that we will work but deep down, we don’t want to work until age sixty-five. My father thinks I should be at home. [Kathleen]

Geography was an influencing identity factor insofar as girls growing up in rural parts of the country were exposed to more traditional gender roles. This identity characteristic exerted an influence through interaction with culture and economy.

It was 1980’s, in the West of Ireland. You were a civil servant, teacher or worked in a bank .... There’s more opportunity in Dublin .... [T]here may be more ambition if you go to a more open-minded school, which you tended not to see in the West of Ireland. [Triona]

Similarly, SES interacted with educational infrastructure and economic factors to affect women having IT careers. Free secondary education overcame the economic barrier to obtaining a technical education. Further, Ireland’s economic development plans provided greater access to the third level educational infrastructure through IT study grants. Such government schemes offered those with limited economic resources the opportunity to get post-secondary educational preparation for IT jobs.

## IT Identity

One’s technical identity was shown to be a factor in navigating an IT career in later decades when informants represented the spectrum from primary IT sector jobs in computer hardware assembly and software development to secondary IT sector positions in other industries such as banks. The influence of technical gender stereotyping was in evidence in the assignment of gender-typed roles where women were assumed to be technically incompetent until proven otherwise. The heavily technical coding jobs tended to be all men, whereas women tended to be in “people jobs,” such as sales, where they dealt with external constituents.

Another barrier related to type of IT work emerged in the 2000s as work in the secondary IT sector proliferated. It derived from differences in gendered workplace cultures between the primary and the secondary information sectors. Whereas Janet and Marie did not encounter gender biases in their primary IT sector jobs, both Mairead and Triona did so in their secondary information sector positions in the financial industry. Triona believed that banking has retained its historical “boys’ club” culture (Morgan et al. 2004). Mairead said she and another woman experienced gendered differences in their job progression compared with the two men who entered the company with them.

The two of them are Grade 1 managers and myself and [another woman] are around the assistant manager mark with both the same rank. She sat with me and she saw the two lads down the corridor having their senior management chat, “What happened? What happened?” (shaking head). [Mairead]

## Changes in Individual Factors Influencing Gender Relations in the IT Sector

While identity factors help to explain variation in exposure to enablers and inhibitors of IT career participation, individual factors help to explain differential experience of and response to them. Significant people and experiences, and particular personal traits offer insights about why some women internalized barriers and some women were able to resist negative cultural messages. However, these individual factors were not always considered by the participants to be influencing factors.

## Personal Influences

Significant people played an increasingly important role in girls’ choices about IT careers. This reveals the incremental nature of personal influences on change, as well as the interaction between culture and educational infrastructure. The role of teachers and guidance counselors changed from reinforcing barriers to enabling resistance to them. Parents were, initially, not an influence on their daughters at all. Perhaps because many of them had no formal education beyond National School they did not play a significant role in their daughters’ decisions about the new electronics jobs that were coming to Ireland. But as time progressed parents saw IT work as a path to economic stability. Informants said parents wanted more for their children, and helped to initiate cultural and infrastructural accommodations as they actively supported their daughters’ desire to penetrate gender barriers.

This change in parental influence accompanied the turning economic tide resulting from the emergence of a recognized IT sector. Parents became more actively involved in their daughters’ IT career choices. Fathers expected both sons and daughters to get educational qualifications. Technical career advice also came from older siblings with IT jobs. Daire’s college guidance came from her father, not school counselors. He altered his opinion, viewing computing as a “safe” career once he recognized the opportunities it offered. Although Maire’s father did not attend university, he impressed upon her and her siblings that education was the only way to get a good job. Angela’s father was also an influence.

I don’t see barriers, I never felt like I shouldn’t do something because I am a woman. That’s probably a lot of influence from home where I was always encouraged to do anything, from my dad especially. [Angela].

By communicating the economic value of IT jobs to their children, parents became a conduit through which changing economic and cultural norms about career opportunities were conveyed to their daughters. Instantiating incremental change, parents came to actively encourage their daughters’ pursuit of IT careers, and worked with teachers and counselors to overcome institutional barriers to enable access to the STEM courses needed for university admission.

Participants cited school personnel as both enablers and inhibitors of their career ambitions. Informants from the 1990 interviews said that school personnel conveyed traditional gender roles. Girls’ education was directed primarily at nurturing: motherhood, teaching and nursing. While there is clear evidence of increased effort to facilitate STEM education and technical career advice as time went on, in every interview period, women spoke about educational barriers. For example, Linda, who was in secondary school in the early 2000s received conflicting messages. One was that in choosing a career she should take into account what she was good at and what she enjoyed. The other message was that girls did not have the innate ability to understand technical topics. Deanna, also educated in the early 2000s, received disempowering messages as well. She was told by a teacher that being an engineer would be antithetical to being a wife and mother.

I think of all the things we tell to a boy and we don’t tell a girl and the reverse. I read something that … when you meet somebody with a child, if it’s a girl, you are most likely to say something like “You’re beautiful,” or “You’re pretty,” or “I like your dress,” or things like this. When it’s a boy, you are more likely to say “What did you do today?” or “What did you learn at school?” or things like this. [Deanna].

However, informants also described the ways in which school personnel supported their IT career interest. Sally’s guidance counselor invited an engineer to speak to students. The nuns at Maire’s school allowed her to take courses at the nearby boys’ school. What made the biggest impression on her was not what she learned as much as the teacher who supported them.

There were only four of us girls doing honours maths and about twenty boys. And every time one of us would try and ask a question the boys would start making noise and passing smart remarks and laughing. But the priest who taught us copped on to it pretty fast and put an end to that. He actively encouraged us. [Maire]

At a time when cultural norms about women pursuing technical careers were in flux, women spoke about other significant adults encouraging them to resist negative messages. As the IT sector expanded so did role models and mentors. The oldest informants had only role models from traditional Ireland: stay-at-home mothers. But incremental change in role models evolved from increasing numbers of mothers and older sisters working in IT jobs who signaled that women could combine marriage and technical careers. Role models such as Mary Robinson, Ireland’s first woman President elected in 1990, were also appearing (Finlay 1990).

Encouragement of men—fathers, brothers and husbands—was noteworthy, consistent with research on the influential role of fathers in their daughters’ occupational choice (Hellerstein and Sandler Morill 2011). Fathers’ encouragement of a career in a traditionally male-dominated field provided girls with social legitimization and normative sanctioning (Ferry 2006; Kniveton 2004; Rokeach 1976).

My family were extremely supportive. Again, my brother was technical. I was used to seeing him messing around with engines. I wouldn’t have thought there was anything un-girly about being technical but there is apparently with some (laughs). The thing is that with those stereotypes, if you are brought up in an environment where y’ know ... “Let’s be a nurse,” or “What about being a kindergarten teacher?” or “What about being a teacher?” .... If you are in that kind of environment maybe you will think that it’s [IT work] more a guy kind of thing. Resisters and circuits, all that kind of stuff is for the lads (laughs). I think that an influence on girls in general would be their environment, what signals are they getting? What messages are coming into them? [Gillian]

By the 2000s the informants’ discussion of influential people included support in the workplace. Grainne spoke of work place support to resist family and technology stereotypes. Maire complained about insufficient support systems at work. Lara, who was pregnant with her first child at the time of the interview, appreciated her workplace support system directed at promoting, sponsoring and retaining women. Seeing women in high level professional positions coping with work– life balance was also beneficial. The women’s discussion of workplace mentors and advocates indicates the incremental nature of change brought about by influential people in their work lives. It also shows the interaction with culture insofar as these women discussed the need for both a national culture supportive of women and a similarly supportive workplace culture.

Significant life experiences were also influential. What motivated Aoife to become career- rather than job-focused was her father’s death when she was young. She saw her mother working as a receptionist and she, herself, had a job selling ice cream. Sally’s motivation to have a successful career was bolstered by watching her mother take on babysitting jobs in order to pay for her education. Maire saw a career in IT as a route to financial freedom. She witnessed her mother’s economic dependence on her father and did not want that for herself.

## Personal Characteristics

Two types of personal characteristics—women’s internal motivation and their resilience—grew as factors influencing change in Irish women’s engagement with IT careers. These personal traits increasingly enabled women to resist inhibitors to IT careers. In the earliest decades, respondents conveyed ambivalence about IT careers, especially in the face of motherhood pressures. But over time women wanted to continue working after having children, and not just for the income; they enjoyed the work. Women’s interests and abilities nourished their motivation to pursue an IT career in the face of demotivating cultural messages and other barriers. This represents a change from a time when personal characteristics played no role in IT career decisions. Those women who took jobs in the early IT sector sought income, not personal fulfillment. They were not encouraged to act upon their individual interests and capabilities. The culture reinforced this absence of personal motivating factors. According to Patricia it was acceptable for a woman to work for an improved standard of living. But working because she chose to work, would be viewed by some as a selfish act.

As more women entered the IT field their interest in IT careers extended beyond economic security. They articulated intrinsic motivation to work in this sector, and discussed the traits associated with being successful in this field: an inherent interest in IT, and being competitive, persistent, tenacious and risk taking. These personal traits enabled them to prevail in the face of technical gender stereotypes. Their perseverance was fueled, they said, by their interest and capability, because in order to disprove gender stereotypes in the culture about technical ability, women felt they needed to know more and work harder.

If a woman is not interested in IT, she won’t go because that’s not for her. It’s only if she has good skills, good marks or good things, then she will go. A guy is different. For them it’s more natural to go into this field, so even if they are interested but not that much, they will go because it’s guy stuff. [Deanna]

Indeed, contemporaneous data show higher third-level education rates for women (Donnelly 2019) and significant increase in salary for women with advanced degrees (OECD 2018).

Personal agency was the other influencing factor that grew in importance over time. Over the course of the study women’s resilience in the face of barriers reflected changes in the economy and culture, as well as the influence of parents, teachers, and role models. Support from significant others helped the women to resist institutional barriers. Angela did not see barriers and never felt restricted because she is a woman. Sally and her classmates resisted the expectation that girls limit themselves to studying liberal arts and become either teachers or secretaries. These women were empowered to persevere in a career of their choice. They were interested in the subject matter, were good at mathematics and science, and were drawn to the logic and problem-solving aspects of programming.

By the late 20<sup>th</sup> and early 21<sup>st</sup> centuries women were empowered, confident, well-educated contributors to the new Irish economy. Their mothers’ restrictions were unimaginable to them (Macdona 2001). An engineer in her late 30s who was interviewed in 2003 had never heard of the marriage bar and could not believe such a thing had ever existed in Ireland! Nevertheless, they acknowledged the challenges of advancing into higher management. They recognized issues of power, politics and authority in the workplace and what it meant to be a woman in a masculine, technical field (Wickham et al. 2005). Their resilience is illustrated in the response of Sally. The same determination that motivated her in the 1980s to study engineering came into play in the 2000s. When she perceived that she had reached a “glass ceiling” in her advancement, she co-founded a startup company, a phenomenon just beginning to be noticed (Duffy and Pruchniewska 2017; Dy et al. 2017; O’Connor et al. 2006).

## Discussion

## Contribution

This research was motivated by the desire to better understand not only what societal, organizational and individual factors interact to affect gender equity in the IT field. We also sought to understand how and why these factors exert their influence over time. Hence, we undertook a longitudinal study in order to gain further insight into the nature of change regarding these factors. What resulted is a dynamic extension to the individual differences theory of gender and IT, which now provides IS researchers with a theoretical vehicle for examining the nature of change in factors affecting gender equity in the IT sector.

We interrogated the life histories of women in the IT workforce at four points in time in order to examine how and why alterations in gender relations were connected to changes in the nation’s socio-economic environment. Our analysis of women’s collective reflections on the first five decades of Ireland’s information economy, using the guiding theory, accomplished two goals. First, we demonstrated the relevance of applying the constructs of the guiding theory to identify specific environmental and individual factors that were affecting gender equity in the Irish IT sector. Second, and most importantly, our investigation showed that a static consideration of factors provides an incomplete understanding of them. In order to account for variation in the way environmental and individual factors enhance or inhibit women’s participation in IT it is also necessary to understand the change process itself. Our results show that some factors affect change quickly, others slowly. Factors result in both abrupt and incremental transformation. From this research we now know that there is a dynamic dimension to the influence of the factors represented in the constructs of the IDTGIT. Our research has produced seven new themes that indicate the way in which these factors contributed to changes in women’s relationship to the Irish IT sector.

The addition of this change dimension directly addresses a gap in the literature employing this theory: the need to move beyond associations among factors and women’s participation in IT careers, to explanations regarding how these factors have their effect. As such, it represents a major extension of this indigenous theory about gender in the IS field. The dynamic dimension adds significantly to our understanding of the gender imbalance, its causes, and ways to ameliorate it. This theoretical extension is consistent with the “explanation” goal of theory “intended to promote greater understanding or insights by others into the phenomenon of interest” (Gregor 2006, p. 619).

The specific environmental and individual factors from the guiding theory that emerged from this research point to concrete interventions that can be undertaken to redress the gender imbalance. At the same time the seven themes in the new change dimension suggest ways in which these interventions could be deployed and expectations calibrated. The educational infrastructure is a gender barrier when girls’ schools do not offer sufficient STEM courses. Parents and IT professionals interested in gender equity can help to create awareness and influence educational policies to bring about change. Gender stereotypes about technical competence prevalent in educational and workplace cultures, represents another barrier. As the informants noted, this places a burden on a girl or woman to prove herself. Networking groups can be deployed to address disempowering educational and workplace cultures. Indeed, one corporate survey showed a correlation between involvement in a women’s network, her advancement, and closing the gender pay gap (Shook and Sweet 2018). Two-thirds of fast-track women were found to participate in women’s networks. Where such networks are available, the majority of women chose to participate. Unfortunately, half of the women surveyed worked for organizations without them. Networking groups illustrate change occurring through the interaction of culture and individual influence factors.

Our results provide a clear articulation of personal influences, thereby pinpointing possible interventions to change outcomes. The findings confirm the importance of parental influence, particularly a father’s encouragement of what has been traditionally viewed as men’s work. School guidance counselors can enhance or inhibit girls’ technical career interest based on the provision of adequate career guidance. The indirect influence of negative stereotypes in the culture is in evidence when girls unconsciously internalize these messages imposed upon them by significant others. The findings of this research also point to specific interventions that can be undertaken by managers in the workplace to address the gender imbalance. Insufficient role models and mentors affect both recruitment into IS careers and advancement within them (Young et al. 2013), a theme that arose during each decade of this study. Indeed, a multi-country survey (Shook and Sweet 2018) found three times more women on the fast track in organizations having at least one woman in senior leadership. Companies can enact incremental change by establishing specific gender equity goals such as increasing the share of women executives to a fixed number (e.g., 25 or 30 percent), which would increase the number of role models (Flabbi et al. 2016).

As this study shows, motherhood was the dominant identity factor influencing women’s IT careers. Hence, work–life balance policies and practices represent a significant way to support women IT professionals (PwC 2018). However, wide variation has been shown in the implementation of such work practices (CIPD 2018, Eurofound 2017). Further, the provision of maternity leave is not just a matter of instituting abrupt change through corporate policy. It can impede women’s career progression if it is not an acceptable part of the corporate culture (Shook and Sweet 2018). One way to address this is to encourage paternity leave, which not only facilitates a mother’s return to work, but also reinforces a societal expectation about a father’s role in childcare (Irish Times 2018). However, research conducted in the UK (Taylor 2019) found that less than a third of first-time fathers availed themselves of shared parental leave; some men reported being tacitly or explicitly discouraged from taking leave. To counteract this, countries such as Sweden require that the maximum amount of parental leave can only be obtained if fathers take a share of it. The advancement of both men and women who have availed themselves of parental leave and flexible working arrangements could send a visible message of encouragement to others.

## Limitations and Future Research

There were some differences in data collection methods across the study time periods. The 1990 interview protocol differed from that used in subsequent interviews because the guiding theory and its constructs were not yet in the IS literature. Also, three different individuals conducted the interviews. While arguably this can minimize the potential for undue interviewer influence, it can also be viewed as a limitation. Finally, some of the interviews were conducted face-toface, while others were mediated (i.e., telephone or skype).

From a societal perspective, boundary conditions exist with respect to applying our findings about Ireland’s gender equity path to other settings. More research employing this dynamic individual differences theory of gender and IT should be conducted in other regions of the globe in order to determine the applicability to gender and IT workforce equity issues in other cultural contexts. While our research was carried out in the context of norms about gender roles grounded in Western European and North American culture, the variation in IT workforce patterns and societal norms regarding gender roles in other cultural contexts warrants further research about the nature of change. For example, policy changes in some Muslim majority countries have resulted in these countries now producing more women engineers than is the case in Western countries (Charles and Bradley 2009), and in some cases, leading to a reverse gender gap with more women earning science degrees than men.

Applying this dynamic individual differences theory of gender and IT to transitioning economies would be another relevant line of future research. We provide a theoretical framework for examining how the intersections of environmental and individual factors in conjunction with gender have influenced IT career choice in a developing economy. Because Ireland’s position as a first world country with a strong IT footprint is a relatively recent phenomenon, this study’s methodology and findings represent promising avenues of investigation for countries seeking to foster gender equity in the context of advancing an IT sector. Ireland’s rapid transformation over recent decades suggests relevance in similar socio-economic settings. Finally, societal-level research based upon our findings can also be used to explore possible effects of recent geopolitical circumstances arising from right-wing populism and the COVID-19 pandemic on women IT professionals. This study offers methodological and theoretical guidance for doing so.

From an organizational perspective, future research is needed to explore corporate culture and policies, as well as individual influences coming from role models, mentors, peers and sponsors. One topic that particularly merits further investigation in different contexts is work–life balance, what was arguably the most consistently reported theme across the decades of this longitudinal study. Cech and Blair-Loy’s (2019) study of parenthood among American STEM professionals found that nearly half of new mothers left full time work compared to fewer than one quarter of new fathers. This points to parental responsibility as a significant component of the gender imbalance in STEM employment. Further research is needed to better understand the career trajectories of mothers and fathers in view of changing societal norms regarding parenting.

Finally, we note that this research about women in the IT field is situated within a broader category of social inclusion research. There is currently a paucity of gender research focusing on men and masculinity in relation to the construction of IT work, which suggests an opportunity for future research. The extended theory, methodology and findings offer approaches that can be taken to examine underrepresentation of other groups based on characteristics such as ethnicity, disability or sexual and gender identity. There has been a limited amount of research on the topic of race and ethnicity (e.g., Cain and Trauth 2017; Joshi et al. 2016; Windeler and Riemenschneider 2016) and even less about disability (Annabi and Locke 2019; Loiacono and Ren 2018; Trauth et al. 2015). While there is almost no research on LGBTQ<sup>20</sup> issues in the IS field, findings from other STEM fields suggest this as a promising area of IS research as well. STEM careers have been shown to be less likely chosen by LGBTQ students due to environmental factors such as a heteronormative climate in higher education (Bilimoria and Stewart 2009; Hughes 2018; Patridge et al. 2014). Because the extended IDTGIT includes gender and sexual minorities as part of the identity construct, and societal and organizational cultures as part of the environmental construct, it provides a useful framework for investigating alterations of factors influencing LGBTQ retention in the IS discipline. In sum, research that examines changes in environmental and individual factors affecting a variety of marginalized people is needed in order to examine similarities and differences in the experiences of various underrepresented groups in the IS field (Trauth 2017; Trauth et al. 2018).

## Conclusion

Barriers to IT careers are not uniformly experienced by women. Hence, efforts to achieve gender equity in the IS field require a nuanced understanding of the various ways in which both extrinsic and intrinsic factors are differentially affecting girls and women. In response to this need, we undertook an examination of changes in environmental and individual factors affecting gender equity in the IT sector. By conducting a longitudinal study of women IT professionals, we were able to observe and analyze these changes over time. Ireland was chosen as the context because of the explicit way in which Irish institutions brought its IT sector into existence and the significant socio-economic transformation that accompanied it.

What resulted was a set of seven themes about the nature of change. Taken together, they constitute a new, change dimension added to the guiding theory. The resulting dynamic individual differences theory of gender and IT now provides a theoretical mechanism for investigating how and why societal, organizational and individual factors bring about gender equity in the IT field. It does so by juxtaposing themes about the change process against environmental and individual factors represented in the constructs of the guiding theory. Our findings show that these factors affect women both directly and indirectly. They effect both abrupt and incremental change. Some factors are consistently influential. Some change results from the interaction of several factors. Finally, transformation is often manifested as the impact of changes in factors occurring elsewhere.

This significant extension of the guiding theory now enables investigation of the way in which the theory’s constructs interact to account for the varied effect of factors influencing women in the IT sector. Over the course of our study economic factors were shown to affect gender equity in the IT sector through interaction with other environmental factors (i.e., policy, culture, infrastructure), individual identity factors (e.g., motherhood), and individual influence factors (e.g., family and personal agency). The results revealed both gradual and dramatic changes in an evolving picture of women in this sector, against the backdrop of the peaks and valleys of Ireland’s economy. Both transformational and enduring images emerged from this look at Ireland over five decades.

The tension between transformation and resistance that played out in Ireland over 50 years reveals universal themes that continue to challenge efforts to redress the gender imbalance. Recruitment barriers to entering the IT field come from educational institutions and cultural messages about femininity and working in a technical field. Retention and advancement barriers in the IT profession speak to the persistent influence of workplace power and politics. But as the transformation in Ireland has revealed, gender inclusion offers benefits not only in its value as a social good. It is also an important component of an economically healthy and innovative IT sector.

## About the Authors

Eileen Trauth is Professor Emeritus of Information Sciences & Technology, and Gender, Women’s & Sexuality Studies at The Pennsylvania State University. Her work focuses on barriers to inclusion in the information technology profession experienced by wmen and other marginalized groups. Her research in Europe, Africa, the Asia Pacific, and the United States has been supported by the Fulbright Foundation, the National Science Foundation, and Science Foundation Ireland. Her findings have been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information Technology & People, Information and Organization, and The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems, as well as in several books, and a play, iDream. She has advised EU gender projects, is co-editor of Handbook of Gender and Technology, was co-editor-in-chief of Information Systems Journal, and was founding president of the AIS Special Interest Group on Social Inclusion.

Regina Connolly is Professor of Information Systems at Dublin City University Business School, Ireland. Her research focuses on the nexus of the interlocking challenges and opportunities related to digital transformation in the public sector, with a particular emphasis on issues related to inclusion. She also studies the human resource implications of mandatory technologies on employees and their relationship with the organization, from an information privacy perspective. Her work has been published in journals such as European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, Information Systems Journal and Government Information Quarterly, among others. She is a lead partner in research projects that have received over €21 million in EU Horizon 2020 funding to date and is an Investigator with LERO, the Irish Software Research Centre.
