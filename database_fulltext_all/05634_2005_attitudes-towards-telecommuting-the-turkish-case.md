# Attitudes towards telecommuting: the Turkish case

Omer Faruk Iscan, Atilhan Naktiyok

Ataturk University, Erzurum, Turkey

Correspondence: OF Iscan, Ataturk Univeristy, IIBF 25240, Erzurum, Turkey. E-Mail: Omer\_iscan@hotmail.com

## Abstract

Examines the effects of demographic characteristics (gender, marital status), household attributes (small children, largeness of home and distance of home and workplace), support factors (supervisor, colleague and technological-bill support), and perceived advantages and disadvantages of telecommuting to individuals, organizations and society on individuals’ attitudes towards telecommuting. The sample comprised 664 IT professionals working in several internet (portal) companies in Turkey. Results suggest that women, married employees, employees who have children less than five years old, employees whose house is big enough, employees whose house is relatively farther to the workplace, and those who perceived more advantages accruing from telecommuting, to themselves, to their organization or to the society, have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting. On the other hand, employees who perceived more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to themselves or their organization have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting. In addition, colleague support and technological-bill support positively influence the attitudes of employees towards telecommuting. Journal of Information Technology (2005) 20, 52–63. doi:10.1057/palgrave.jit.2000023 Published online 30 November 2004

Keywords: telecommuting; advantages; disadvantages; attitudes; technology and gender

## Introduction

D evelopments in telecommunications and unpredictable, rapid changes in the business environment as a result of global competition, have led to a new thinking in the design and structure of business practices. The advent of the Information Age with the increasing availability of new computer and communication technologies, along with the internet, have transformed the ways in which many individual work, travel and conduct their daily activities today (Nagurney et al., 2002: 1629). As the creation, storage, transfer and management of information became the source of knowledge-based competitive advantage, organizations no longer needed to be located at the same place (Atkyns et al., 2002: 269).

Today because of the above developments, workforce is able to have different arrangements from the traditional nine-to-five day in the office settings (Mokhtarian and Salomon, 1997: 41; Mills et al., 2001: 50). Many job and career changes may be instituted by an organization and its management to accommodate employees’ reduced physical proximity (Raghuram et al., 2003: 181). Many organizations are now aware that they need to be proactive in adapting to the changing environment and circumstances by applying business practices, which encourage maximal flexibility. It is possible that through the adoption of unconventional work methods such as telecommuting, organizations can become flexible (Teo and Lim, 1998: 253; Teo et al., 1998: 329; Belanger, 1999: 139; Lim and Teo, 2000: 560).

The term telecommuting is not one that is clearly defined, and it is often used to encompass a number of different styles of work. In other words, various terminologies for the subject have been used in the literature. Usually, the term teleworking is used as a more general concept. Although telecommuting is used more often, networking, remote working, flexible working and homeworking are among those concepts that are used with similar or same meanings (Lindstrom and Rapp, 1996; Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998; Ward and Shabha, 2001; Obra et al., 2002). Collectively, these phrases describe teleworking as a form of flexible work arrangement which entails working remotely from an employer for a large proportion of employees’ time (Teo and Lim, 1998: 254). Telecommuting can be defined as the practice of working from home, or a location close to home, instead of traveling to work during the normal workday (Stanek and Mokhtarian, 1998: 53). In other words, telecommuting can be practised as either home based or from a telecommuting center. It refers to a form of work arrangement involving the use of computers and telecommunication technologies which allow employees to substitute telecommunication for physical transportation thereby permitting jobs to be performed at a remote work site (Tung and Turban, 1996: 227; McInerney, 1999: 69). Employees maintain contact with their organizations through the use of telephones, facsimile machines, computer modems as well as electronic and voice mail systems (Ahmadi et al., 2000: 84). They do not necessarily work at home every day but for one or more complete workdays a week in lieu of working in the office. They will spend the remaining days in the office (Lim and Teo, 2000: 561). By using telecommuting, geographical and organizational boundaries of the traditional, centralized workplace are removed.

Telecommuting can be considered from the telecommuter contract point of view by taking into account the employer–employee relationship. However telecommuting can be studied from several other perspectives: strategic, organizational, human resource management, and economic, social, legal political and technological aspects (Obra et al., 2002: 786). This led us to evaluate the subject by taking into consideration many studies belonging to different disciplines.

Much of the current research on telecommuting focuses on employees and organizations in the Western context. The phenomenon is very new among companies in Turkey. Societal changes that have taken in recent years have triggered the interest in telecommuting as a new work arrangement. Turkey has a high potential with a young and energetic population of 70 million, 3 million Internet users, an amazing growth rate in information technology (Intersection, Istanbul Chamber of Commerce, 2002). According to the investment bank of Salomon Smith Barney that conducted an investigation in Turkey, e-commerce and technologically driven sectors and companies are growing (Sanli and Celebi, 2000: 13). A rise in the number of dual career families and in the number of females in the workplace are also among such changes. Although today 48 percent of women are employed in Turkey, it is expected that this rate will have risen to 60 percent by 2010 (International Labor Organization, 2000). Telecommuting permits a more effective balance of work demands with responsibilities at home (Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 61). Most organizations now accept that a satisfied employee is a productive employee and providing flexibility for the working parents to manage their own work hours results in more satisfied and thus more productive staff (Sturgeon, 1996: 27). In such a situation, it is more important for organizations to build flexibility about where and when employees want to work and telecommuting represents a work option, which allows for this flexibility. Telecommuting is beneficial to the employee who wants more control over his or her work life (Sturgeon, 1996: 27).

Other reasons described in the literature for individuals to decide to telecommute include reduced commute time; less interruptions; and, cost savings through lower or no transportation, lunch and business clothing costs (Mokhtarian et al., 1995: 284; Belanger, 1999: 141; Hill et al., 2003: 2; Workman et al., 2003: 200).

In spite of these advantages the telecommuting ‘boom’ has not taken place. In the 1970s and 1980s, telecommuting was perceived as the work arrangement of the future. But despite the optimistic predictions about it, telecommuting has not had the success predicted for it (Jackson and van der Wielen, 1998). This is surprising, as recent developments in information and communication technology have reduced the coordination and control costs associated with the geographic decentralization of the workforce’s location (Illegems et al., 2001: 276). This can on a large extent be attributed to some disadvantages brought by telecommuting. For example, Kurland and Bailey (1999) identify several factors associated with telecommuting that tend to negatively influence job motivation: negative impact on social networks, decreased teamwork, and resentment by those who do not telecommute. In that case it becomes difficult for managers to create team synergy and to overcome the absence of informal, interactive learning (Pliskin, 1998: 73; Perez et al., 2002: 777). In addition, some research (Kraut, 1989) has shown that job satisfaction did not differ between telecommuters and non-telecommuters. Negative influence on career progress (Teo and Lim, 1998), less external structure (Workman et al., 2003), security issues (Sturgeon, 1996), professional isolation (Kurland and Bailey, 1999; Ward and Shabha, 2001), career anxieties created by spending less time in the office and being passed over for a promotion (Judiesch and Lyness, 1999) are among such problems documented by research.

This study examined attitudes towards telecommuting among information technology professionals in Turkey. We selected IT professionals because it is clear that IT personnel represent a dynamic workforce in a high growth industry of the future. With the increased implementation of computing and communication technologies in the workplace, the nature and amount of white-collar employment has changed. Work processes are becoming more complex and information intensive. Workers are becoming more focused on specific knowledge domains, sharing expert knowledge with peers and relying heavily on information technology (Pliskin, 1998: 73). Telecommuting is not suitable for every kind of worker. One of the major characteristics that differentiate today’s telecommuters is that more and more of them are knowledge workers (Belanger, 1999; 139). If such a work arrangement is to be successful, it is important that potential telecommuters have appropriate personal qualities that allow them to work unsupervised or supervised remotely. These include selfmotivation, self-discipline, commitment to telecommuting, adaptability, self-organization and ability to work with little social contact (Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998: 369). So, characterized by variety and exception rather than routine and performed by professional or technical workers with a high level of skill and expertise (Davenport et al., 1996: 54) IT sector is very suitable for telecommuting.

When we look at the working statistics, we see that turnover rates of cities, in which IT personnel mostly work, are very high. For example in I<sup>˙</sup>stanbul for 1999, turnover rate is 14.54 per cent (Turkish Employment Organization, 2000). This rate is the highest rate in Turkey. Employee turnover presents a wide range of problems for organizations. Frequent displacement of skilled IT personnel may lead to a low morale among survivors. Also this situation may escalate costs of organizations, as new employees need to be recruited and trained.

In the context of qualitative manpower shortage and high turnover rates, the aim of this study is to examine various factors, which may affect IT personnel’s attitudes towards telecommuting in Turkey. The results of this study will have important implications for researchers and practitioners about the feasibility of telecommuting. With this understanding, we believe that it will be possible to conceptualize and implement more appropriate and effective programmes. In addition, we hope that both management and operation staff will be encouraged for the adoption of telecommuting.

Variables, which may potentially affect people’s attitudes towards telecommuting, can be categorized into four headings: demographic characteristics, household attributes, support factors, and perceived advantages and disadvantages to individuals, organizations and society.

## Research hypotheses

## Demographic variables

## Gender

Gender is a vital social factor shaping organizational life and focusing on gender differences in terms of the attitudes towards telecommuting can offer a different perspective for understanding the organizational and broader societal context of information systems and information technology (Wilson, 2002: 139).

Presently, only 48.6 percent of women in Turkey aged between 15 and 64 are employed. In contrast, for a similar age group, 81.5 percent of women in Sweden are employed in the labor force, 77.8 percent in Denmark, 65.6 percent in Britain, and 62 percent in France (International Labor Organization, 2000). This may on a large scale stem from the fact that in many Western countries the state, aiming to tackle the problem of the double burden of women’s role in the family, has invested a tremendous amount of money, time and effort to provide socialized childcare. But even in that case, focusing on strategies, which encourage flexible work arrangements, may be considered as necessary because of the relatively low female labor force participation rate in Turkey. But this should not be at the expense of arguing better public childcare and challenging the existing division of labor in the home.

Despite the significant growth in the IT profession, it is stated that there remains a gender imbalance; there is evidence of under-representation or a decline in the participation of women in the IT profession (Trauth, 2002: 98; Wilson, 2002: 139). According to some, this is due to the social construction of the IT profession as ‘men’s work’ (e.g. Murray, 1993). Female workers are understood to be subordinate figures to male workers because of characteristics associated with their femaleness (Wilson, 2002: 141; Woodfield, 2002: 134). Some others (e.g. Trauth, 2002) mention that despite cultural features gained by means of family, society and public policy shape women’s identities and their feelings of acceptance or alienation within the IT field, individual differences may also be influential in terms of the attitude of men and women towards IT. These are not the main focuses of this paper, although they should nevertheless be addressed here.

When domesticity is discussed, it is usually done with a strongly gendered interest and in conjunction with the life of women. In other words, ‘managing the domestic’ is still considered women’s contribution to the household’s economy (Habib and Cornford, 2002: 160) For this reason, women often have to assume major responsibilities for their family and career. And most of the time such responsibilities restrict their mobility and participation in the labor market. In this respect, telecommuting may have favorable effects on relations with family. Because telecommuting offers the flexibility for individuals to better schedule time with their family members (Teo and Lim, 1998: 255). But even if we are not considering it in this paper it is also stated that by means of technology women’s work is often depicted as being of an ever-expanding nature and technological developments such as telecommuting have been seen as intensifying the traditional sexual division of labor and as reinforcing the boundaries around what are commonly regarded as masculine and feminine roles within the household (Habib and Cornford, 2002: 161).

Investigating such theoretical considerations, some researchers found that preference to telecommuting was in fact related to gender and women, with a greater proportion, favor this work alternative compared to their male counterparts (e.g. Mokhtarian et al., 1995; Belanger, 1999). Thus, gender has often been discussed as a clear determinant of telecommuting potential.

These thoughts are hypothesized as follows:

H . There will be a significant difference between men and women in terms of favorable attitude towards telecommuting. Women will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting than men.

## Marital status

Some investigations showed that married employees favor telecommuting more than their single counterparts (Teo et al., 1998: 335). The reason of this largely is the time flexibility that allows work to be fitted around family commitments. Married employees who use telecommuting do not have to travel to work daily and thus the time that is saved can be spent with family members (Nilles, 1996: 16– 17). This situation leads to improved family relations (Hall, 1989: 25). Telecommuting may provide a solution for married employees who need to juggle their time between work and family responsibilities (Handy and Mokhtarian, 1996: 233).

In addition, the presence of children is also an important factor for most mothers (Handy and Mokhtarian, 1996: 234; Stanek and Mokhtarian, 1998: 67). Mothers of young children tend to opt for flexi-work arrangement in order to increase their involvement with family and nurture their children without sacrificing their careers. For this reason, telecommuting is a good substitute way of working for women. More specifically, some investigations found out that married employees are more receptive in admitting that telecommuting will increase their work productivity than are the unmarried employees (Abdul Azeez and Supian, 1996).

Some studies have also suggested that telecommuting can also be a good substitute way of working for men. By telecommuting they can participate actively in caring for the family and children (Lim and Teo, 2000: 564). In this way, there can be a balance between their quality of work life and quality of family activities. And in some cases, spending more time with family members may be more important for men than career advancement.

Thus we can propose the following hypothesis:

$H _ { 2 } .$ There will be a significant difference between married and unmarried people in terms of favorable attitude towards telecommuting. In other words, married employees will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting than unmarried ones.

## Household attributes

As the number of children in the family increases, the household becomes more complex to manage, and flexibility becomes more important to handle family needs (Mokhtarian and Salomon, 1997: 41; Illegems et $a l . ,$ , 2001: 279). In terms of Turkey, average number of children per married woman is 3.14 (Turkish State Statistics Institute, 2001). So there will be a more positive attitude towards telecommuting in terms of parents (Mannering and Mokhtarian, 1995: 50; Handy and Mokhtarian, 1996: 234). Moreover, telecommuting, by placing workers in the home and eliminating the commute to work, serves to blur distinctions between family life and work life. For individuals specially those whose workplaces are far from their home and whose houses are larger and suitable for telecommuting, telecommuting provides time and cost savings (Mannering and Mokhtarian, 1995: 59; Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998: 367). Telecommuting programs can save an estimated 5.000 dollars to 10.000 dollars per year per telecommuting employee (Dannhauser, 1999: 53).

In the light of the above statements, followings are hypothesized:

$H _ { 3 } .$ Individuals who have children less then 5 years old, will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting than those who do not have children less than five years old.

H . As the physical distance of work and home increases, the positive attitude towards telecommuting will also increase.

$H _ { 5 } .$ Individuals whose houses are larger and suitable for telecommuting will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting than those whose houses are not large enough and suitable.

## Perceived advantages of telecommuting

Existing technology allows the telecommuter to work at a remote site without having to sacrifice the technical support available in the corporate environment. Telecommuting also allows workers increased flexibility in their work schedules, rather than binding them to traditional nine-tofive schedules (Mokhtarian and Salomon, 1997: 41; Mills et al., 2001: 50). The increased flexibility in work schedules allows individuals to engage in non-work activities to a much larger extent than otherwise possible. With the help of telecommuting, employees can balance work–family relationship and they can get more job satisfaction (Nilles, 1996: 19; Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 57).

Some studies showed that telecommuting increases productivity since individuals can avoid interruptions at the office and get work done faster (Lindstrom and Rapp, 1996; Teo and Lim, 1998). Reduced stress levels in employees, and improved morale/motivation are also perceived benefits of telecommuting (Lupton and Haynes, 2000: 326). Telecommuting helps to reduce commuting costs and time as individuals can minimize the hassle of commuting to and from work (Teo and Lim, 1998: 256).

In terms of organizations there are advantages resulting form telecommuting such as reduced building costs, reduced running costs, reaching employees with special skills, improved staff retention and reduced office politics (Mills et al., 2001: 52). In addition, it is beneficial in terms of organizations in that it causes greater productivity, lower absenteeism, better morale, greater openness, fewer interruptions at office, reduced overhead, lower turnover and regulation compliance (Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 56).

In addition to individual and organizational advantages, telecommuting has also social advantages. It may decrease traffic congestion on strained highways and reduce automobile related air pollution. It is considered as $\mathbf { \dot { a } }$ green activity’, primarily because of the reduction in travel, the consequent fuel savings (Mokhtarian et al., 1995: 287; Henderson and Mokhtarian, 1996: 34; Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998: 367; Mokhtarian and Bagley, 2000: 224; Mitomo and Jitsuzumi, 1999: 742; Mills et $a l . _ { \cdot }$ , 2001: 50; Atkyns et al., 2002: 267). Moreover, by working at home telecommuters discourage community crime that might otherwise occur in bedroom communities. Further, because of flexible time schedules and time savings, telecommuting enables telecommuters to spend more time building community ties (Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 61).

Thus it is hypothesized that:

$H _ { 6 a } .$ Employees who perceive more advantages resulting from telecommuting to themselves will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

$H _ { 6 b } .$ Employees who perceive more advantages resulting from telecommuting to their organization will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

$H _ { 6 c } .$ Employees who perceive more advantages resulting from telecommuting to the society will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Perceived disadvantages of telecommuting

Some investigations showed that individuals perceive that there are less promotion possibilities for telecommuters (Illegems et al., 2001). In other words, one of the most frequently reported disadvantages of telecommuting is that it may potentially impede individuals’ career growth. Employees believe that their reduced visibility in the office will have a negative effect on their career development and growth prospects. In addition, employees miss out on technical discussions and peer interaction, which are important to development of technical expertise. Their interaction with business contacts may also be reduced and they may be likely to be passed over at promotion time because their supervisors do not see them often enough (Teo and Lim, 1998: 258).

Another disadvantage may be the feeling about the lack of companionship and social interaction (Kurland and Cooper, 2001: 2). Telecommuters may feel that they are isolated, cut off from the flow of things back in the company and unable to keep up with both career and company developments and that they are losing a sense of belonging to the company, which forms such an important foundation in their commitment (Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 57; Ward and Shabha, 2001: 62).

In addition, telecommuters face the difficulty of separating work and home activities while telecommuting. Distractions at home may be harder than those in the office (Mills et al., 2001: 52). Self-discipline and motivation to work may be a problem as it may be stressful to work and to look after children simultaneously.

Telecommuting may result in reduction or loss of office workspace. Furthermore, employees may be required to share their workspace as a result of telecommuting. This can be a significant disadvantage since any reduction or loss of workspace can adversely influence perceptions of privacy and status at the workplace (Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998: 367).

Telecommuting may also have an adverse effect on others. For example, non-telecommuting colleagues may be resentful and supervisors may feel loss of control over telecommuters. In addition, family and neighbors may perceive telecommuters as not having a ‘respectable’ job (Teo and Lim, 1998: 258).

Some investigations showed that since the work is being done away from physical supervision, managers may find it difficult to control and monitor telecommuters (Kurland and Cooper, 2001: 2; Ward and Shabha, 2001: 68).

In addition many organizations worry that letting workers telecommute will increase security risks by creating more opportunities for computer hackers and equipment thieves (Mills et $a l . ,$ 2001: 52). For all organizations, certain data are confidential and any leakage of such data to unauthorized parties may cause companies to lose their competitive advantages in a highly competitive business environment. Another disadvantage is the issue of designing an equitable compensation scheme. Problems pertaining to issues accountability for repairs and maintenance of equipment placed at employees’ homes may arise (Lim and Teo, 2000: 569).

In terms of social challenges, it may be stated that telecommuting needs to develop its own culture. Within existing cultural environment, it is assumed that anyone at home during the day is free to chat, to watch a child or to run an errand (Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 62). It is also feared that by transforming work from a sensual activity to an abstract, computer mediated one, telecommuting may cause a gradual degradation of a person’s ability to interact with other people in a personal, intimate and human manner. In addition to above considerations, it is stated that telecommuting may promote decline of clothing industry, dispersion of housing and this increases commuting distances (Tung and Turban, 1996: 104; Mills et al., 2001: 529).

Thus, we propose that:

$H _ { 7 a } .$ Individuals who perceive more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to themselves will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

$H _ { 7 b } .$ Individuals who perceive more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to their organization will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

$H _ { 7 c } .$ Individuals who perceive more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to the society will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Support factors

## Supervisor relationship

Positive attitude and support of supervisors is very important in influencing an individual’s attitude towards telecommuting. Studies showed that factors such as working well with one’s supervisor, appreciation of employee’s work by the supervisor, effective communication between the supervisor and the employee, positively effect employee’s attitude towards telecommuting (Stanek and Mokhtarian, 1998; Ahmadi et al., 2000; Mokhtarian and Bagley, 2000). Telecommuters regarded their supervisors’ understanding, concern and acceptance of telecommuting as valuable and important to their telecommuting attitudes. Such results show the important role of supervisors in promoting a favorable attitude towards telecommuting (Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998: 370).

Therefore we may expect that:

$H _ { 8 a } .$ Employees who perceive that their supervisors are supportive of telecommuting will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Colleague support

Getting along with one’s co-worker and having an effective work team appears to be a positive factor in terms of attitude towards telecommuting (Mokhtarian and Bagley, 2000: 232). On the other hand if an employee believes that his or her colleagues may react negatively towards telecommuting, he or she may reject the work option. Some non-telecommuters may believe that telecommuters are not working when they are not regularly seen in the traditional office. As it is the case in the negative effect of supervisors, this is because employees feel that the organization is not interested in telecommuting and thus this new work arrangement is unlikely to be adopted even though the technology is available (Ahmadi et al., 2000: 84; Lim and Teo, 2000: 566).

Thus we may state that:

$H _ { 8 b } .$ . Employees who perceive that their colleagues are supportive of telecommuting will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Technological-bill support

Individuals, in order to have a positive attitude towards telecommuting, must be sure that basic technologies, such as e-mail access and responsive communication links will be available (Mokhtarian and Salomon, 1997: 44; Ahmadi et al., 2000: 84). In addition, managers and employees may need to improve their ability to communicate effectively with electronic media (Hobbs and Armstrong, 1998: 370; Kurland and Bailey, 1999: 65). Technology in the form of a computer, a modem, a telephone and voice messaging service, has given us a runner up in terms of effectiveness and efficiency and has made telecommuting an alternative working option (Handy and Mokhtarian, 1996: 229; Ahmadi et al., 2000: 84).

Another aspect, which may affect attitudes towards telecommuting, is bill support. If employees know that some kind of support will be made in terms of payment of telephone bills, electricity bills and stationary expenses, their attitudes towards telecommuting may be more positive (Teo et al., 1998: 340).

Thus it is hypothesized that:

$H _ { 8 c } .$ Individuals, who perceive that there will be technological infrastructure in their organization, will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Method

## Sample and procedure

The sample comprised 664 IT professionals working in several internet (portal) companies in Turkey. We selected portal companies because such internet companies have formalized programs to continually track advances in IT and to acquire new and promising technologies. These organizations are indeed, very suitable for telecommuting in terms of the content of the work and the infrastructure.

Data were gathered through the use of questionnaire surveys. We conducted a pilot-test and asked an expert group to comment on the representativeness and suitability of our questions. Comments and suggestions received were then used to fine-tune the presentation of the final questionnaire. The questionnaires were distributed in packets, each of which contained a letter from the researchers assuring participants of a complete confidentiality, and a questionnaire. Completed surveys were mailed by the respondents directly to the researchers in preaddressed envelopes. In all, 900 questionnaires were distributed but 200 of the questionnaires were not returned and 36 others were incomplete. So, 664 questionnaires were considered for statistical analyses (Response rate is 74 percent).

## Instrument

Items measuring the variables in this study were derived from an extensive review of the literature on telecommuting, general work environment support and household attributes. Wherever possible, scales that operationalized constructs were adapted from past research where psychometric properties were already established.

## Perceived advantages of telecommuting to individuals, organizations and society

Variables related to individual and organization were measured with scales adapted from Yap and Tung (1990). Individual advantages were measured through 13 items and organizational advantages were measured through six items. Societal advantages were derived from extensive review of the literature about this subject (Mokhtarian et al., 1995; Henderson and Mokhtarian, 1996; Tung and Turban, 1996; Salomon, 1998; Mitomo and Jitsuzumi, 1999) and were measured through seven items. Cronbach’s alphas for the scales on advantages of telecommuting to individuals, organizations and society were 0.87, 0.80 and 0.73, respectively.

Perceived disadvantages of telecommuting to individuals, organizations and society

Variables, related to individual and organization were measured with scales adapted from Yap and Tung (1990). Individual disadvantages were measured through 16 items and organizational disadvantages were measured through seven items. Societal disadvantages were derived from extensive review of the literature about this subject (Tung and Turban, 1996; Kurland and Bailey, 1999; Mills et $a l . ,$ 2001) and were measured through three items. Cronbach’s alphas for the scales on disadvantages of telecommuting to individuals, organizations and society were 0.89, 0.82 and 0.69, respectively.

## Work-based support

This variable was assessed with the work support scale adapted from House (1981). Four items were used to assess supervisory support and four items to assess work colleague support. Technological and bill support was assessed through nine items which are developed from extensive review of the literature about this subject (Teo et al., 1998; Belanger, 1999; McInerney, 1999; Illegems et al., 2001). Cronbach’s alphas for the scales on supervisory, work colleague and technological and bill support were 0.71, 0.68, and 0.79, respectively.

## Demographic individual and household attributes data

Respondents were also asked to provide their age, gender, marital status, educational experience, whether there are children less than five years old, largeness of home, and distance of home from workplace.

## Attitudes towards telecommuting

Attitude towards telecommuting was assessed with two items. Cronbach alpha for this scale was 0.79.

Five-point Likert scales ranging from (1) ‘Strongly Disagree’ to (5) ‘StronglyAgree’ were utilized to assess the construct in the present study.

## Results

Demographic, individual and household attributes characteristics of individuals

A total of 75.6 percent of the respondents were male, 74.4 percent were married. The mean age of the respondents was 33. Majority of the respondents had a university degree (72.9 percent). In total, 59.3 percent of the respondents have children less than five years old. The mean largeness of respondents’ houses was 110 m<sup>2</sup>. The mean distance of respondents’ homes and workplaces is 13 km.

## Demographic variables

Gender. Table 2 shows that men and women differ in their attitudes towards telecommuting. Thus, H1 which postulated that women will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting than men is supported. This is an interesting finding and it is contrary to the claims of some studies which state that technologically driven facilities such as telecommuting are primarily perceived as masculine (e.g.Wilson, 2002). This can be considered as the positive side of the findings. The negative side is related to the division of labor at home. Indeed, our findings show that traditional roles of males and females did not change in Turkey. In other words, it seems that most of the homerelated problems are still seen as the responsibility of women and for this reason women favor telecommuting more than men.

Marital status. H2 which predicted that married employees will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting than unmarried ones is supported in regression analysis $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 , P < 0 . 0 5 )$ . On the other hand, the relationship between marital status and telecommuting is weaker than the relationship between gender and telecommuting. In spite of a relatively weak relationship we may conclude that telecommuting enables married individuals to have more time flexibility to balance between work and family responsibilities.

## Household attributes

Small children. As predicted, the results of our study suggest that individuals who have children less than 5 years old, have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting thus supporting H3 $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 , \ P < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The interesting point about our findings is that while the relationship between having children less than five years old and telecommuting is weaker than the relationship between gender and telecommuting, it is higher than the relationship between marital status and telecommuting. A possible explanation for this could be the fact that about 15 percent of the sample did not have children less than five years old. This shows that employees who are both married and have children less than five years old have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting. This finding shows that telecommuting helps such people to balance their work and family needs by providing a flexible work environment. For example, the individual may arise early, work from home for a few interrupted hours, then provide family members with breakfast and get the children off to school or to other care arrangements. This flexibility might also explain the results suggesting that workers with flexibility in the location of work can work more hours without impacting work–family balance.

Largeness of home. H4 which postulated that employees who have larger houses will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting is supported $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 , \ P < 0 . 0 5 )$ The basic reason behind this could be the fact that individuals who have larger houses, since they have enough space to establish a home office, prefer telecommuting more than those whose houses are not large enough. If the house is large enough, other elements of home atmosphere (small children, number of individuals etc.) may have less negative effects on the working conditions at home.

Distance of home and workplace. H5 which predicted that as the physical distance of work and home increases, the positive attitude towards telecommuting will also increase is supported but the relationship between this distance and telecommuting is not as high as the relationship of other demographic and household attributes variables $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 , \ P { < } 0 . \dot { 1 } 0 )$ . A possible explanation for this weak relationship might be the fact that most of the employees in our sample (76.4 percent), have personal vehicles. Another explanation could be the effectiveness of mass transportation system in big cities of Turkey such as I<sup>˙</sup>stanbul and Ankara.

## Perceived Advantages

## Perceived advantages of telecommuting to individuals

Our regression analysis showed that individuals who perceive more advantages accruing from telecommuting to themselves have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting, thus supporting $\mathrm { H } _ { 6 a } ~ ( \beta = 0 . 2 2 , ~ P < 0 . 0 5 )$ This finding suggests that respondents perceived advantages such as increased work life quality, better family relationships, savings in commuting time and cost, and improved efficiency. This leads us to conclude that the more potential advantages accruing from telecommuting to individuals, the more favorable their attitudes towards telecommuting.

## Perceived advantages of telecommuting to organizations

$\mathrm { H } _ { 6 \mathrm { b } }$ which postulated that employees who perceive more advantages resulting form telecommuting to their organization will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting is empirically supported $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 , \ P < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Thus, individuals’ attitude towards telecommuting is significantly and positively affected by their beliefs about advantages accruing to their organizations. For example, organizational performance may increase; turnover rates and absenteeism may decrease as a result of telecommuting. In addition, advantages of telecommuting such as reductions in office space requirements, capital savings for the organization and a possible retention of scarce skills and talents in an organization may motivate organizations to adopt telecommuting. Parallel to this, employees who perceive such kinds of advantages to their organization will also have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Perceived advantages of telecommuting to society

As we predicted, employees who perceived more advantages resulting from telecommuting to the society have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting. Thus, $\mathrm { H } _ { 6 \mathrm { c } }$ is supported empirically $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 , \ P < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Thus individuals’ beliefs about advantages accruing to their society, positively and significantly affect their attitude towards telecommuting. For example, as a result of telecommuting there will be a decrease in the demand of transportation and air pollution. Also, telecommuting may cause energy saving and decreased traffic congestion. Correspondingly, employees who perceive such kinds of advantages to their society will also have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Perceived disadvantages

Perceived disadvantages of telecommuting to individuals $\mathrm { H } _ { 7 \mathrm { a } } ,$ which posited that individuals who perceive more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to themselves will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting, is supported by the results of regression analysis $( \beta = - 0 . 2 0 ,$ $P { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ . This shows that individuals’ attitudes towards telecommuting are significantly and negatively affected by their beliefs about disadvantages associated with telecommuting. For instance, telecommuting may cause professional and social isolation. Individuals may perform their tasks independently at home by means of telecommuting but since telecommuting reduces their workplace interaction they may still be hesitant towards telecommuting. As such, individuals who perceived a potential reduction in workplace interaction may have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

In addition, individuals may get confused with home and work responsibilities. Also they may experience difficulties in establishing self-discipline and motivation for their tasks. Furthermore, they are concerned with the potential loss of office workspace if they telecommute. As a result, as potential disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to individuals increase, they will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

Perceived disadvantages of telecommuting to organizations $\mathrm { H } _ { 7 \mathrm { b } }$ which predicted that individuals who perceive more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to their organization will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting is supported but the negative relationship between perceived organizational disadvantages of telecommuting to organizations and telecommuting is not as high as the relationship of perceived individual disadvantages $( \beta = - 0 . 0 9 , ~ P \tilde { < } 0 . 1 0 \tilde { ) }$ This finding shows that individuals do not see organizational disadvantages, such as controlling performance, loss of data security and equipment accountability, as important as individual disadvantages. This might be attributed to the fact that the process of institutionalization or institutional thinking has not been completed in Turkey. For this reason every fact, factor or case is evaluated on the basis of individual paradigms. Nevertheless, we can conclude that individuals who are concerned with such organizational disadvantages tend to have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting.

## Perceived disadvantages of telecommuting to society

Table 2 shows that our $\mathrm { H } _ { 7 \mathrm { c } }$ hypothesis which predicted that individuals who perceive more disadvantages accruing from telecommuting to the society will have a less favorable attitude towards telecommuting is not supported. This shows that negative perceptions of individuals about disadvantages of telecommuting to society, does not affect their attitude towards telecommuting.

## Support factors

## Supervisory support

$\mathrm { H } _ { 8 \mathrm { a } }$ is not empirically supported as our results showed that telecommuting is not significantly related to beliefs about supervisors’ telecommuting attitudes. This finding indicates that individuals are doubtful in their attitudes towards the kind of support they expect from their supervisors during telecommuting. A possible explanation may be that, since telecommuting is a very new form of work option (specially for Turkey), employees are not sure about the reactions of their supervisors. Another explanation may be that colleague support may play an even more influential role in employees’ attitudes towards telecommuting.

## Colleague Support

$\mathrm { H } _ { 8 \mathrm { b } }$ which posited that employees who perceive that their colleagues are supportive of telecommuting will have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting is empirically supported $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 , P { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . It is interesting to see that while there exists no important relationship between supervisory support and attitudes towards telecommuting, there is a strong relationship between colleague support and attitude towards telecommuting. This may be thought as a reflection of Turkish culture which gives great importance to collectivism and not being socially isolated. This finding is also consistent with our findings about perceived individual disadvantages about social isolation.

## Technological-bill support

As predicted, individuals, who perceived that there would be technological infrastructure and bill support in their organization, have a more favorable attitude towards telecommuting. Thus $\mathrm { H } _ { 8 \mathrm { c } }$ is empirically supported $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 , \ P < \mathsf { \bar { 0 } } . 0 1 )$ . This finding is also as interesting as the findings about supervisory and colleague support. One possible explanation of this finding may be the fact that in the home atmosphere, usage of technological instruments such as personal computers, faxes, and printers are not very common and such instruments are very expensive in Turkey. For this reason, employees expect support in terms of such instruments. In addition in Turkey bill costs as a result of technological instruments are high and the salaries of employees are relatively low. Thus, it is very difficult for Turkish IT personnel to telecommute without technological and bill support.

## Conclusion

Telecommuting appears likely to expand and evolve in an information-driven society. But a review of the scientific and popular literature, leads to the conclusion that telecommuting forecasts have generally been overly optimistic and do not take socio-cultural features of countries into consideration. So the question remains whether telecommuting will eventually reach the high levels envisioned for it. In order this to happen, it seems necessary to study both employees’ and employers’ decisions as complements. In other words, the future of telecommuting depends on the willingness and desire of employers and employees.

This study contributes to the literature about telecommuting by examining a wide variety of factors, which may affect IT professionals’ attitudes towards telecommuting. Our results reveal that telecommuting should not be viewed merely as a technology. As broad social and institutional aspects are involved, they fall more in the context of social forecasting than that of technological forecasting. In addition it seems that telecommuting is an emotional issue. Personal circumstances of individuals are very important in assessing it. In other words, a personal standpoint is necessary. When coupled with the perception–reality gap that surrounds the advantages of telecommuting, this means that organizations are in a very hard position to improve their competitive advantage.

An important finding that must be mentioned is the finding about negative relationship between perceived organizational disadvantages of telecommuting to organizations and telecommuting. Although this relation is meaningful, it is not as high as the relationship between other variables and telecommuting. This finding shows that individuals do not see organizational disadvantages, such as controlling performance, loss of data security and equipment accountability, as important as other variables.

Interestingly, our results suggest that individuals are doubtful in their attitude towards the kind of support they expect from their supervisors. Also, the effect of perceived social disadvantages to the attitude towards telecommuting is ambiguous. This is perhaps telecommuting is a very new work environment, specially in the context of Turkey, and thus employees may not be sure about how their supervisors would react to telecommuting. This means that the forecasts of future telecommuters are simply that – forecasts. Quite possibly, individual workers and managers may determine the challenges of telecommuting outweigh the advantages and thus decline to set up telecommuting alternatives. It can be inferred that since telecommuting is a relatively new work arrangement, some form of training is required so that employees and their supervisors have a good understanding of this work arrangement. Moreover, the uncertainty and fear of the unknown associated with the change can be minimized by an effective communication program. In addition, since the work option is very new, individuals may not perceive social disadvantages in a culture in which traditional norms are predominant. Because in a country like Turkey in which masculinity culture is prevailing, ‘a man working at home’ may be perceived as not having a ‘respectable’ job by family and neighbors. Nonetheless, in the face of this phenomenon, small businesses, large corporations, state agencies and the public at large must help to develop policies to address the needs, challenges, and ramifications associated with telecommuting.

Our results show that telecommuting may produce effective and practical results for individuals, organizations and society. If necessary infrastructure is prepared, a telecommuting program may help employees manage work and family responsibilities, and it may improve workrelated attitudes such as job satisfaction, job autonomy and job performance. Since women constitute an important percent in the population, and since their involvement in the workforce increases specially because of economic reasons, telecommuting may provide a flexible work arrangement in order to accommodate increased demands from the non-work domain.

But companies need to be alert to the potential disadvantages or problems associated with telecommuting such as impediment of career growth, lack of companionship and social interaction, loss of control and office workspace, and security risks. For this reason, to help alleviate potential liability and employee concerns before constituting a telecommuting program, organizations are advised to consider the issues presented in this article and institute telecommuting agreements with their employees. In order to gain a competitive edge, companies should explore the possibilities surrounding telecommuting and develop a strategic plan before implementing a program. The negatives about telecommuting can be greatly minimized by taking the proper steps to maximize the chances of success.

Two important limitations exist in this study. First, respondents comprised 664 IT professionals working in several internet (portal) companies in Turkey. Therefore it is necessary to investigate whether these findings can be extended to other professional groups or non-professionals employed in other industries. As a result, examination of the topic in the future could be directed towards individuals employed in other industries.

Another limitation is that potential predictors of individuals’ attitudes towards telecommuting are more than those of our study. Thus, future attempts may take into consideration the potential impact of more factors such as support from the family and union.
