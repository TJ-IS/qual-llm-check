# Enacting new temporal boundaries: the role of mobile phones

Elpida Prasopoulou<sup>1</sup>, Athanasia Pouloudi<sup>1</sup> and Niki Panteli<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

This paper examines how the use of mobile phones influences the temporal boundaries that people enact in order to regulate and coordinate their work and non-work activities. We investigate both the structural and interpretive aspects of socio-temporal order, so as to gain a fuller appreciation of the changes induced by the use of mobile phones. With specific reference to professionals working in traditional, physically based and hierarchically structured organizations, we found that mobile phone users are becoming more vulnerable to organizational claims and that as a result ‘the office’ is always present as professionals, because of the use of mobile phones, become available ‘anytime’. This is enabled by the characteristics of the technology itself but also by users’ own behaviour. In the paper, we discuss the properties of the emerging socio-temporal order and show how mobile phones may render the management of the social spheres in which professionals participate more challenging.

Keywords: mobile phones; temporal order; structural and interpretive properties; temporal boundaries; information systems and time

## Introduction

The introduction of mobile phones in our work and wider societal practices has triggered great interest in the ways these technological artefacts are incorporated and potentially reshape the established order of our social interactions (Peters & ben Allouch, 2005). Mobile phones, as communication technologies, present intriguing properties since they are at the interface of the private and public spheres of life (Arnold, 2003) while their ability to compress space and time has the potential to change the very framework in which society lives (Fortunati, 2002). Thus far, research in this field has focused on the spatial aspects of this mobility which serves the restructuring of organizations towards network forms and enables novel working arrangements (e.g., Kakihara & Sorensen, 2002).

The temporal aspects of this ever-availability have received limited attention (e.g., Fortunati, 2002; Green, 2002). Social order is established through the dual process of attributing societal activities to discrete physical locations while enacting rigid temporal boundaries which facilitate the symbolic segregation of human activities (Zerubavel, 1981). Therefore, the collapse of space is bound to have implications on the existing temporal boundaries (Green, 2002) which need to be further examined. In this paper, we use Zerubavel’s (1981) notion of sociotemporal order, to study the impact of mobile phones on the structural and interpretive aspects of the temporal order of users with specific reference to the temporal boundary segregating their public from their private sphere of life. Thus far, the collapse of time triggered by mobile phones has been mostly praised (Fortunati, 2002) and not adequately examined. Our research shows that many of the paradoxes attributed to the use of mobile phones that is, liberated and leashed, private and public (Arnold, 2003), essentially express the re-enactment, currently underway, of traditional temporal boundaries regulating users’ various spheres of activity. Interestingly, intense use of mobile phones gradually makes users aware of the role of these devices in the reenactment of temporal boundaries despite the fact that the temporal properties of information technologies are rarely acknowledged by organizational members (e.g., Lee & Liebenau, 2000).

In the next section, the theoretical underpinnings of socio-temporal order and the influence of information technologies on temporal boundaries are analyzed. Our research design and data collection methods are then described. Presenting our empirical findings, the following section focuses on the analysis of the way mobile phones induce the restructuring of temporal boundaries in people’s everyday practices as well as on the interpretations around the new modalities. The Discussion section examines the implications of the study. The paper concludes with areas for further research.

## Socio-temporal order and information technology

Time is one of the fundamental parameters of the orderliness characterizing human involvement with the world (Dubinskas, 1988). Through the establishment and preservation of specific temporal boundaries people organize their activities and attach meanings to their experiences. To better understand the importance of time in the organization of human experience, we focus on the constituting elements of socio-temporal order. Then, we discuss the role of information technologies in this context.

## Temporal boundaries: the importance of segregating activities

Time, especially in highly industrialized societies, functions as a ‘symbolic nexus around which coalesce issues of order’ (Dubinskas, 1988, p. 3). Social entities in their effort to infuse daily experience with a sense of stability and continuity organize their activities in ways which are neither natural nor inevitable but rather an instantiation of how time is perceived and handled by collectivities (Zerubavel, 1981). The existence of specific temporal boundaries in almost every societal occasion reinforces the idea that ‘time is a contributor to the dynamic and subjectively formed context within which the social actors define and plan their activities’ (Sawyer & Southwick, 2002, p. 236).

The temporal regularity characterizing societal activities can be traced through the examination of specific structural properties, which permit the delineation of any situation or event. According to Zerubavel (1981), these properties are:

\- Sequence: the order in which events typically occur. Not all events can take place simultaneously. The order can be random; however, it is often prearranged and irreversible.

\- Duration: how long events or situations last. For practical purposes, most of organizational or social events have a fixed duration, which explains the primacy of timetables and schedules in our society.

\- Temporal location: when the events usually occur. According to Lee and Liebenau (2000), locating an event in the continuum of time is the basis of scheduling and every other planning activity.

\- Rate of recurrence: how often the events occur. This property enables us to trace patterns and cycles of activities.

It follows that people use structural properties to draw specific temporal boundaries around their various spheres of activities. These temporal boundaries serve as a shared scheme against which people construct their meanings and evaluate their experiences (Barley, 1988) during the course of the day. As Dubinskas (1988, p. 15) notes ‘[time functions] as a cosmological framework for understanding this regulation [of social action] as a [God] – given order of the social universe.’ A schematic representation of the structural and interpretive properties of sociotemporal order is given in Figure 1.

Studying the construction of temporal order within bureaucratic organizations and more specifically hospitals, Zerubavel (1979) observed that the work of doctors and nurses was characterized by events with rigid sequential structures, fixed durations, standard temporal locations and uniform rates of recurrence. This ‘temporal rigidification’ of organizational activities permitted doctors and nurses to better organize their daily activities at the hospital and draw definitive temporal boundaries (especially in the case of nurses) among their work and private life. The existence of specific temporal boundaries has also enabled, from a symbolic perspective, organizational members (i.e., doctors and nurses) to anchor-specific meanings to their actions and situations. Nurses, for example, were not assuming their chores before the official start of their shift even when present at the hospital. The formal nature of the temporal order also gave doctors the ability to legitimately distantiate themselves from the obligation to be always on call.

The idea of a temporal order with specific structural properties against which people anchor their activities uncovers the socially constructed nature of time. Zerubavel did not incorporate in his analysis the role of organizational technologies. Yet, his concepts have been extensively used in information systems (e.g., Barley, 1988; Lee, 1999) since they provide ‘a sturdy analytic scaffold’ (Barley, 1988, p. 126) for a detailed appreciation of the role of technology in the establishment of temporal order. Current research on the impact of information technologies on the socio-temporal order is reviewed in the following section.

## Temporal inscriptions on ICTs: their impact on human activities

The order characterizing human involvement with the world can also be traced to the technological artefacts of our society (Dubinskas, 1988). Following Zerubavel’s concepts and ideas, many researchers (e.g., Barley, 1988; Lee & Liebenau, 2000) examined the interplay of information technologies with the constituting elements of temporal order. The increasing deployment of information technologies was found to significantly alter the structural properties of temporal order by accelerating business processes (Lee & Liebenau, 2000), by inducing more rigid temporal patterns to non-bureaucratic organizations like universities (Pollock & Cornford, 2004) or by simply functioning as interruptions of the flow of work (Rennecker & Godwin, 2005). The interpretive aspects of time are also affected (Barley, 1988). In the aftermath of any technological implementation new temporal boundaries are drawn (Barley, 1988) and events acquire new meanings in tune with the rhythms of the machines (Sawyer & Southwick, 2002). Ultimately, people start to perceive differently their tasks, roles and relationships within the organization (Scott & Wagner, 2003).

Differences, therefore, among the temporalities inscribed in information technologies with the temporal order of the hosting organizations occasion its gradual re-enactment. However, Zerubavel’s ideas, with a special focus on the role of information technologies, have been empirically tested by Barley (1988) in the same type of organizations, that is, hospitals, as the original study, which are characterized by high procedural standardization and very strict temporal order. The interplay of technologically induced change in the temporal order of alternative organizational forms (e.g., business organizations) where temporal structural properties are still easily discernible, yet not so tight, has received limited attention to date (e.g., Lee & Liebenau, 2000). Moreover, the nature of the information technologies investigated (i.e., intended for organizational use only) gives no insights on how professionals in business organizations manage the impact of these technologies on the temporal boundaries segregating work from non-work activities. This issue has been examined only in the context of alternative working arrangements such as teleworking (Tietze & Musson, 2003) or professionals with increased mobility (e.g., Kakihara & Sorensen, 2002) where the nature of work blurs temporal boundaries anyway.

Mobile phones operate at the interface of the organizational world and wider society since a single device serves both organizational and personal needs (Srivastava, 2005). Users are endowed with more autonomy and flexibility while they can still be reached by others (Kakihara & Sorensen, 2002). Hence, they have the ability to move between different locations (Fortunati, 2001), arrange more lax working conditions and be distant yet close. In this sense, their mobile phone number becomes their fixed point of reference permitting instant connectivity and communication (Arnold, 2003) regardless of location. These characteristics of mobile phones set an interesting new context for studying the impact of ICTs on the socio-temporal order and especially the temporal boundary between the private and public spheres of life. Yet, there are limited efforts to understand how the use of mobile phones, as communication devices, affects or even restructures the temporal boundary segregating work and non-work activities (e.g., Green, 2002; Srivastava, 2005). A detailed description on how we examine this issue is presented in the following section where we analyse our research approach.

## Research approach and method

This research is exploratory and interpretive in nature, as our aim is ‘to understand how members of a social group [professionals], through their participation in social processes [mobile phone use], enact their particular realities [relation of public and private time] and endow them with meaning, and to show how these meanings, beliefs and intentions of the members help to constitute their social action’ (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p. 13). The interpretive research epistemology adopted enables capturing differences in interpretations among research participants, leading to a richer understanding of mobile user behaviour (cf. Klein & Myers’ (1999) principle of multiple interpretations). To this end, our research method is qualitative in nature, examining ‘humans within their social settings’ (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p. 14). We collected and analysed empirical data using the idiographic methods described below, in line with Miles & Huberman’s (1994) suggestions for qualitative data analysis.

To better capture the impact of temporal properties of mobile phones on the lives of users and address a gap in earlier research, we chose to collect data from professionals working in business organizations. The choice of this occupational group was based on the fact that these professionals are still maintaining a definitive temporal boundary segregating work from private life.

We invited people from a range of companies, including software and publishing houses, banks, public relations companies and telecoms. In all, 15 professionals participated; about one third of those were project managers, one third were IT managers, two were CEOs and the rest had other middle management roles in their respective organizations. The majority of these professionals participated, in the last 2 years, in executive postgraduate and training programmes at the Athens University of Economics and Business, where two of the authors are based.

The focus of our research was to capture both the observable characteristics of temporal order (i.e., structural properties) as well as the interpretations cast on them by the individuals involved. To this end, we collected data through the use of log files followed by debriefing interviews. This data collection method, introduced by Perlow (1999) and used also in the study of people’s use of mobile phones (Gant & Kiesler, 2002), gives a thorough account of the participants’ activities. We asked the study participants to track their use of mobile phones (incoming and outcoming telephone calls and text messages (SMS)) for 24 hours on randomly chosen days. For each phone call and SMS, they were asked to record:

\- When it happened

\- What they were doing at the time

\- The content of the phone call or SMS (work-related or private)

\- In the case of a phone call, its duration

During the one-hour long debriefing interviews, the participants presented a detailed account of the day they kept the log files. They also described their feelings towards the mobile telephone calls/SMS they made or received during that day as well as the effects they had on their activities. In line with Klein & Myers’ (1999) principle of contextualization for interpretive field research, we also asked the interviewees to describe the nature of their work, the responsibilities and tasks it entails, so as to understand in more detail the working practices of the organization in which they work. Moreover, we prompted the interviewees to describe their general patterns of mobile use in the context of their work as well as in their private time in order to capture their attitude towards the medium.

Two of the authors coded the data, working independently, noting patterns in the logs and interviews (Miles & Huberman, 1994). The coding schemes were then compared and consolidated with reference to our conceptual foundations (cf. Figure 1). In case of disagreement the third author viewed the data and reached a decision. As a result of this process we first analysed changes in the structural properties in existing temporal boundaries by the use of mobile phones. Then, we traced the new interpretations emerging because of these new modalities. Finally, in order to ensure the plausibility of our findings, we discussed our own interpretations with the participants themselves as well as other academic peers.

## How mobile phones change temporal order

The use of mobile phones appears to affect the temporal order of our respondents in both its constituting elements (i.e., structural properties and interpretations). To this end, we first present the new structural properties of our respondents’ temporal order. Then, we examine how these affect the way people draw temporal boundaries among their work and private activities as well as the interpretations they cast around them.

Changes in the structural properties of temporal order Our respondents have the opportunity to organize their activities around a fairly stable schedule, which permits them to better control their accessibility and most importantly segregate their private life from their work. Our data showed that in many occasions, mobile phones facilitated our informants in their work (e.g., microcoordination before an important meeting). However, their effects on the existing temporal boundaries among work and private activities are quite detrimental. Information sharing and communication activities can now be performed irrespective of time and location by simply calling colleagues on their mobile. This gradually creates a temporal order with increasingly random structural properties which prevents people from drawing definite temporal boundaries around their activities.

Calls from colleagues on the mobile, especially after working hours, as we witnessed in the log files, allow work-related activities to penetrate time segments devoted to other spheres of life. There is a growing number of issues (e.g., exchange of ideas for next day meetings, debriefing on late meetings with clients, last minute assignments) which evade scheduling and occur at a growing rate after working hours. It follows that the temporal location of these activities changes as well since a part of the work of these professionals is transferred into their private lives. Duration also is affected since private activities are interrupted and more work might occur after the phone call. Moreover, the duration and temporal location of the call itself, which depends on the issues discussed or the identity of the caller, cannot be anticipated. This means that users cannot know in advance the effects of a phone call on their activities.

By answering work-related phone calls during their private time our respondents started to have a sense of long working hours even when they were away from the office. This was because they gradually allowed work issues to invade their private time enabling the institutionalization of an ‘anytime, anywhere’ availability quite detrimental to the preservation of temporal boundaries which are re-enacted in favour of work. The concession to a socio-temporal order with no clear boundaries is also evidenced in the fact that most of the participants revealed that they are unwilling to turn off their mobile phones even at home where they can be legitimately inaccessible. As one of the participants stated:

I’m not strong enough to turn off my mobile phone! I can turn it to silent tone but I definitely prefer to keep it on even if this means that they will probably call from the office.

It follows that since the structural properties of the established temporal order are changing because of the use of mobile phones, the interpretations people cast around various events and on the role of mobile phone itself are also transformed, as illustrated in the following section.

## The emergence of new social meanings

One of the most immediate effects of the new structural aspects of the emerging socio-temporal order involves changes in the social meaning of calling a colleague during hours which have traditionally been considered as private time. People perceive as more ’tangible’ the temporal boundary between home and work when calling on the landline phone that is destined for household and family use. As Geser (2004, p. 32) put it, ‘by articulating differences in location, fixed landline phones have contributed to a more pronounced segregation between different social spheres.’ As personal devices, mobile phones are lifting much of people’s diffidence and lead to the gradual retreat of the temporal boundaries to the detriment of private time. As stated during the interviews:

Business partners usually stop calling at 19:00 or 20:00 pm y that is the norm. When it comes to my close collaborators then we can call each other until one o’clock in the morning. On our mobiles of course y so we won’t disturb the rest of the family. Especially when I’m out on a business meeting or they are out for business purposes we tend to call each other afterwards for debriefing purposes. Even when I work late and they are at home I tend to call them to exchanges ideas. [y] After 1:00 am I don’t call them. I don’t turn the mobile off and I guess they don’t either but I’m not sure. What I can tell you for sure is that we tend to leave our phones on and be available until 1:00 am at least.

Even during hours that normally belong to someone’s private sphere, mobile phones can intrude through the use of SMS. Ironically, this can happen because the sender feels this ‘is not so intruding,’ as stated by one participant:

Sometimes, when I finish work very late and I need something to be done by some team member in the morning, I send an SMS. It is not so intruding and it is considered OK. The advantage is that you get the work done with minimum disturbance.

What also changes through the growing use of mobile phones is who people think is legitimate to call outside working hours. Mobile phones help people to easily transcend social distance, which used to function as a protective shield against disturbances. Our respondents asserted that mobile phones allow people from different social spheres to intrude in different aspects of their lives. Since, in the work settings that we studied, mobile phone numbers tend to be widely distributed, people become accessible to colleagues that are normally quite distant in social terms and would never call them on their landline phone number. In this respect, the temporal boundary between work and private activities does not retreat only in favour of a limited number of close collaborators. It becomes also more vulnerable to intrusions from a larger number of people. As stated during the interviews:

During an election period, I was responsible for the Internet campaign of one of the candidates. During this period, which fortunately lasted only for two months, I was getting calls literally 24 hours a day. I received urgent calls at 4 o’clock in the morning by people I didn’t even know quite well to inform me on special developments of the campaign. It was quite annoying but it had to be done.

In most of the cases presented so far we see that mobile phones trigger the restructuring of temporal boundaries between private and work time in favour of the latter. This temporal distinction is not a natural property of the temporal order but the outcome of negotiations among the individual and the organization. Most of our respondents admitted an implicit understanding among their close collaborators regarding their ‘anytime availability. As they could be reached at any time, these professionals have linked ‘anytime’ communication with their ability to handle organizational issues under any circumstances even during their family time. They further admitted answering their mobile phone not only for important organizational problems, but for minor issues too. This is because they could not know the content of the phone call in advance. Most importantly, over time they also developed a growing sense of inevitability regarding organizational claims. As one executive stated:

Sometimes I can’t be bothered to answer my friends’ calls. In these cases I let the phone ring. When it comes to business calls I always reply. No matter what time it is, I will definitely take the call. There is no way to escape from it. Even if I don’t answer it immediately, I will be forced to reply to the caller later on; so I prefer to deal with the issue on the spot.

It is therefore evident that people start to develop new interpretations of their social world, particularly with respect to their social accessibility. The implications of the changes induced by the use of mobile phones are further discussed in the following section.

## Discussion

Our study has shown that the introduction of mobile phones in everyday life, for both individual and organizational purposes, occasions significant changes in the structural and interpretive aspects of the current sociotemporal order. Even in cases where professionals have standard working hours, as in our research, organizational activities claim some of the time intended for family purposes. This observation unveils a number of useful insights regarding the repercussions on societal practices of mobile phones as temporal devices, discussed in detail in this section.

Mobile phones provide increased accessibility and instant communication to users which might feel empowered by the possibility to independently organize parts of their work since they can be reached at all times. The very same properties of mobile phones turn them also into electronic leashes (Arnold, 2003) which enslave users since, without active control of their temporal boundaries, they become more vulnerable to organizational claims surpassing standard working hours. The capability of users to preserve their private time appears to be closely linked to their position in the hierarchical structure of their organization. Most of our respondents were middle executives involved in jobs with increased need for communication and coordination among colleagues, clients or associates. The use of mobile phones may have facilitated their work in many ways, especially in terms of coordination, yet it further increased communication for even minor work issues in segments of time usually devoted to private activities.

The growing number of calls after work hours, either from senior executives or colleagues, on the mobile phones of our respondents, created a sense of urgency regarding organizational claims. It follows that our respondents became increasingly reluctant to draw a definitive temporal boundary between their work and non-work activities by turning off their phone or by not answering business calls. This practice is quite different from that of other social groups like teenagers. In the latter case, young users evade control of their activities from their parents by pretending that the mobile phone is not functioning properly (Ling & Helmersen, 2000). Interestingly enough for some of our respondents who, during the course of the research, have moved into more senior positions, this trend seems to be reversed. They admitted that they were actually more meticulous in the preservation of their private time. After leaving the office they would not answer any business related phone calls. The attempt to actively manage the temporal boundaries between work and non-work activities observed in the behaviour of some of our respondents confirms Peters and ben Allouch’s findings (2005) that over time, having permanent access appears to be a less manifest reason for using the mobile communication device which consequently becomes more latent.

Mobile phones are initially perceived by users as a tool for a more direct organization of their activities since there would be no need for detailed planning (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005). Over time, they realize that this impedes scheduling – the primary tool for the establishment of temporal order – and the preservation of strict boundaries between their various activities. It follows that the temporal properties of mobile phones become more pronounced to their users than other technologies. Even more so, since mobile phones, as technological artefacts/ communication devices are exclusively linked to one person (Arnold, 2003) and as such could become much more intrusive to one’s life than any other mobile technologies. As stated earlier in the literature review section, previous research (e.g., Barley, 1988; Lee, 1999) has not provided clear evidence on whether users are aware of the role of information technologies as enablers of changes in their temporal order. However, in our research there are clear indications that users, as they embed their mobile phones into their everyday activities, become more aware of the effects of mobile phones on the temporal boundaries segregating work and non-work life. In many instances, our respondents, especially after an intensive period of mobile phone use, which made them more experienced with the device, decided to be more active in taking measures to ‘protect’ their time.

The strategy of our respondents in managing their work–private life temporal boundary needs also to be taken into account for a fuller appreciation of the role of mobile phones in their lives. Previous research on the temporal boundaries between work and non-work time (Nippert-Eng, 1995 as cited in Perlow, 1998) has documented two types of strategies deployed by employees in the enactment of these boundaries. One strategy is to physically separate work and non-work life (‘segmenting’) and thus signal a definite time-space boundary between the two social spheres. The second strategy consists of the synergy (‘integrating’) of these two spheres, which is achieved by intertwining work and non-work activities and the absence of definite boundaries between the two spheres. Our respondents managed their private and public spheres of life by segregating them through quite distinct and solid spatial (e.g., work in the office) and temporal boundaries (e.g., the end of the working day). The increasing use of mobile phones in their daily practices brings them before a very strange situation; from a spatial point of view, their work and private activities remain separate. However, the temporal boundaries are blurring by the gradual integration of work and private time leaving them with a sense that their office is always present, even when it is physically distant.

It is therefore evident that the diffusion of mobile phones has important consequences in the temporal aspects of people’s non-work lives. These go well beyond their intended role as enablers of the spatial mobility, which increasingly characterizes the work of many professionals. The ability of time to facilitate the establishment of order in daily activities is gradually subsiding since temporal boundaries that were distinct (i.e., public vs private time) become more lax and fluid. This does not mean that professionals return to the situation of pre-modern societies where they were inseparable from what they did. However, the multiple roles they assume during their participation in the social world can no longer be clearly separated. The growing use of mobile phones enables a more complex organization of the social spheres in which professionals participate. More importantly, our research indicates that it is the professionals themselves who often determine the extend to which work-related mobile phone use penetrates their private sphere of activities.

## Conclusions

This paper examined the impact of mobile phones on temporal order and especially on the temporal boundary segregating work from private time. The spatial flexibility and independence enabled by the use of mobile phones signifies the removal of the rigidity and fixity characterizing the aforementioned temporal boundaries. Current research on time and mobile phones has mostly praised the collapse of time and space since its primary focus was professionals on the move. Such individuals have already fluid temporal boundaries separating their work and nonwork time, because of the nature of their job. However, the diffusion of mobile phone use in other occupational groups is triggering unintended consequences, which are worth studying.

To this end, this study contributed to a deeper understanding of mobile user behaviour by focusing on a group of workers with properties quite distinct from what is documented in existing literature. Our respondents are professionals working in business organizations; not strict bureaucracies as hospitals (Barley, 1988) nor novel organizational forms as the K-Net researched by Lee & Liebenau (2000). Moreover, their jobs are performed in specific physical locations (i.e., their offices) not at home like teleworkers (Tietze & Musson, 2003) nor on the move like salesmen and policemen (Sorensen & Pica, 2005). Their working conditions enable and even reinforce a rigid temporal boundary, separating work from private life, whose symbolic value turns its deregulation to a very stressful experience.

The study has shown that the intrusive character of mobile phones and their ‘attachment’ to a single user make their temporal properties more evident. Professionals become conscious of the impact of mobile phones on their work-private life temporal boundary integrating accordingly, over time, their various activities.
