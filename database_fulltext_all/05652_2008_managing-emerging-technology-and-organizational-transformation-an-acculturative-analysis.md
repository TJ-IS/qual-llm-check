# Managing emerging technology and organizational transformation: An acculturative analysis

Shan L. Pan <sup>a</sup>, Gary Pan <sup>b,\*</sup>, Paul R. Devadoss <sup>c</sup>

## Abstract

From 1992 to 2005 Singapore National Library Board (NLB) successfully incorporated IT into its working procedures and customer service. The organization has adopted RFID and other technology applications; transformed its operating routines and provided access to millions of publications for its library users. A key issue in this transformation was how the organization managed its cultural change and took advantage of emerging technology. The success can be considered remarkable since, in the past, public libraries in Singapore have carried the stigma of traditional public sector organizations in tending to resist new technology. We examined the cultural changes resulting from introduction of IT in order to show how the Singapore National Library Board managed its employees’ adaptation to the new technology. A number of key factors emerged; these show how an organization can manage this type of cultural adaptation.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations are increasingly required to incorporate new technology into business practices to improve competitiveness. This type of innovation is essential for the success of any organization. Recently, public sector organizations have started to transform the way in which the public interacts with them. The IT transformation has become worldwide, and substantial investment has been made to improve both the infrastructure and delivery of services to citizens. But despite their potential, these projects still carry the risk of failure in implementation. Among the challenges faced by public sector organizations, managing the cultural changes is among the most difficult [3] due to the emphasis on bureaucracy and hierarchy in their organizational culture [13]. Further, the introduction of IT often fails due to lack of preparedness for cultural change [17]. It is therefore important to understand how public sector organizations manage cultural change to capitalize on the benefits of technological innovation. To achieve this goal, we examined the cultural change during the introduction of new technology by Singapore’s National Library Board (NLB). We assessed how it adapted to the new cultural environment, and the ways in which new ideas, values, conventions and behaviors were introduced. The NLB was chosen as the base for our study because it had successfully achieved the transformation and had enhanced its service delivery by adopting innovative technology. We believed that a description of the handling of cultural change during the process could provide a valuable lesson.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Introduction of IT in public sector organizations

The rapid introduction of IT into public sector organizations is in sharp contrast to the traditional view of them as being rigid, risk-averse and being satisfied with the status quo [2]. Public sector organizations are often criticized for choosing the easy route to transformation [7]. Nevertheless, with increasing political pressure from interest groups and constituents, IT has become a major factor in the transformation [18]. Most countries world-wide have introduced egovernment in an attempt to improve service delivery and administrative procedures, but the literature on IS suggests that its introduction is complicated by a lack of fundamental structures and human and technical resources, and by the size and bureaucratic nature of governmental bodies [12].

Public sector organizations need to modify obsolete practices [4]. Consistent management and IS changes are needed in order to enable change [1]. Further, the attitude of users towards new technologies is important in the effectiveness of the new systems [16]. In particular, the most challenging issue in public sector organizations is in modifying users’ beliefs, attitudes and ambitions and how they can adapt to the new experience of using IT. Further, the organization plays a role in IS effectiveness because the adoption of the technology is affected by the culture [15].

## 2.2. The concept of acculturation

Acculturation occurs when the meeting of two or more different cultures results in the combining of existing cultural elements, generating new features and forming a new culture [11]. A more precise definition is: ‘‘Acculturation comprehends those phenomena which result when groups of individuals having different cultures come into continuous first-hand contact, with subsequent changes in the original culture patterns of either or both groups’’ [14]. Acculturation theory has been adapted, for example, to study cultural interaction among organizations as a result of mergers or acquisitions [10]. When users perceive the new system to be effective and believe that the system would enhance their job performance; it is likely that they will adopt a positive attitude towards its subsequent use. Consequently, users are more likely to abandon the old ways and adapt to the new, leading to successful acculturation.

The acculturation process may involve five stages [5]:

\- Initiation: Two or more cultures or subcultures meet and adapt to the presence of another;

\- Fascination: Individuals find the new environment exciting, seeing the benefit in utilizing the new environment to improve their job;

\- Disenchantment: The differences between the new and previous state become apparent and lead to annoyance and resistance;

\- Mental isolation: Individuals wish they could return to the old ways of operating. The gradual adaptation, fascination, disenchantment and mental isolation may loop several times before continuing to;

\- Adjustment and recovery: When individual has finally altered his or her expectations, and accepts the new environment and become comfortable in the new setting.

In our application of acculturation, we treated technologies as cultural artifacts, with embedded cultural tenden cies, such that its use became a cultural interaction. However, our use of the stages of the acculturation process centered on the users allowed us to examine the cultural changes among users [6]. In other words, we felt that this perspective was useful in assessing user interactions with technologies.

## 2.3. Research approach

Our strategy was to study the mechanism of the transformation of NLB due to the introduction of IT. This case study approach was particularly appropriate for an exploratory study, since it simplified our analysis of the organizational dynamics and social influences that needed to be understood [22]. Its strength also lay in its ability to explain the phenomena by interpretation of the data [20]. Between September 2003 and March 2004 we collected the data. Semistructured interviews and informal discussions were conducted with all involved. These interviews were tape-recorded, with interviewees’ permission and transcribed immediately. Twenty-eight interviews were conducted, each lasting an average of 1.5 h. The interviewees were selected from users of a wide variety of business functions across the organization. The interviews focused on interviewees’ perceptions of what had taken place and the reasons for it; how decisions had been made; how conflicts were resolved; participants personal attitudes; their experiences of previous transformations. The questions were openended and exploratory, allowing opinions to be expressed, and enabling the investigator to interpret the interviewees interpretation of events, as well as their beliefs. Other sources of data included documentary evidence, such as the NLB website, press releases, internal reports and the organization’s annual reports. The fact that data were collected from various sources helped to increase their reliability.

As the first step of analysis, one of the investigators used the interview transcripts to prepare a summary of the transformation process. Major events, key participants and the actions taken during this process were identified and summarized. Collier’s model of the acculturation process was used in analyzing our data. An iterative process for comparing the data to the process model was adopted, and an analysis prepared [21]. Acculturation theory was used to explain the cultural change process observed in the case data. Data were validated to avoid researcher bias by use of a peer review by individuals familiar with the project background but not involved in the data collection. The data analysis was iterated many times, with each iteration following the double hermeneutic circle principle [8].

## 3. Managing cultural change at Singapore’s National Library Board

## 3.1. The background and evolution of the NLB (1992– 2005)

In June 1992 the Singapore government conducted a public library review to determine how IT could be exploited to allow public libraries to play a greater role in the information society. Several strategic initiatives were identified, including the extension of the public library system to bring information services closer to the community. Another was the development of a network of borderless libraries with a wider and better range of quality services to allow access to information and resources from anywhere at any time. To achieve these initiatives, three key elements were identified:

\- Leadership: To set up a new statutory board to spearhead the formation of policies and strategies;

\- People: To conduct effective staff development, since skilled manpower was crucial to the successful implementation of new technologies;

\- Technology: To invest in new technologies such as RFID, multimedia, knowledge navigation and networking systems.

In 1995 a new statutory board was created to spearhead the implementation of the strategic initiatives. Dr. Chia was transferred from the National Computer Board (NCB) to become the new chief executive (CE) at NLB. In 1995, the NCB was responsible for IT policy and its development in Singapore. Its mission was to help Singapore excel by exploiting IT extensively to enhance the nation’s economic competitiveness and quality of life. Infocomm Development Authority was now the agency in charge of IT policy and its development. The CE, who was familiar with IT capabilities, drove the transformation of NLBs technology infrastructure. In addition, top management, senior managers and key process owners from various divisions joined together in setting performance targets. The transformation involved improvements in several operational areas:

(1) Time to checkout: The time customers spent on searching for books was excessive. This took approximately 1 h per book.

(2) Time to market: The time between new book launches and their availability for borrowing took up to 8 months. This was considered unreasonable.

(3) Time to shelve: A returned book was not shelved for approximately 6.5 h, because of the batch-shelving approach.

(4) Time to provide information: Handling customer enquiries was time-consuming. General enquires usually took 30 min, enquiries on reference matters took approximately an hour, but research enquiries would often occupy an entire day.

(5) Time to set up a new library: Establishing a new library usually took from 18 to 36 months.

During the transformation process, RFID was identified as a key technology for managing NLBs high usage volume. It was to be incorporated in functions such as the borrowing and return of reference materials, the cataloguing of books and for optimizing the supply chain. This was intended to ensure that new publications were on the shelves quickly. The senior manager from the Division of Services Innovation and Development commented:

‘‘The RFID system is called Electronic Library Management System (ELiMS). In the system, smart labels which emit 13.56 MHz radio waves are used in several applications— tracking and monitoring items, shelving and stock-taking. For library users, these applications save precious queuing time. For library staff, tracking misplaced items is no longer necessary. The adoption of RFID has enhanced service levels; generated greater productivity; provided significant cost savings.’’

In November 1998, EliMS was successfully piloted in Bukit Batok Community Library (BBCL). ELiMS was designed to manage the tracking, distribution, circulation and flow of library materials using RFID technology. ELiMS combined radio frequency components with complete library management front-end software. It enabled various functions such as self-borrowing, item return, sorting, stock control, administration, customer service and theft detection, to be integrated and automated. Prior to its launch, library users had to wait 45 min to borrow an item from the library. After its introduction this dropped to a few minutes. The EliMS comprised a network of multimedia personal computers (PCs) which supported various functions in the libraries and catered for the growing needs of library users. In addition, library users had access to built-in catalogues to search for items in all NLB libraries. With the EliMS, the search process was expedited and library users could see whether books were available for borrowing. Fig. 1 illustrates the automated book loaning system, using EliMS.

In 1999, ‘book drop’ counters were installed using RFIDs. When library users returned their books, the RFID reader captured the electronic signature and sent it to the back-end system for loan cancellation. This was followed by a beep sound and flashing light, acknowledging return of loan items and the updating of the users’ records. Fig. 2 shows the new book drop process.

Overall, the adoption of RFID helped NLB achieve a large savings of 47 million Singapore dollars (approximately US\$29 million). These savings consisted mainly of: manpower reduction despite increased loan volumes; reduced waiting time for library users; faster stock-taking; easier tracking of misplaced items. The adoption of RFID successfully eliminated loan and return queues and enhanced service quality. By April 2002, NLB had deployed EliMs in all of its 21 public libraries.

In addition, other technological improvements were introduced; these included: self-service kiosks with a borrower’s enquiry terminal (BNQ) and ‘cybrarian’ enquiry kiosk (CNK). The BNQ terminals allowed users to access their records and pay fines simply by inserting a cash card and selecting the transaction options on the touch screen. The CNK operated by the use of a personal computer cobrowsing facility that linked up with the librarian’s phone. When a user was connected, he or she was automatically linked to a librarian at a remote site. The librarian could then use the co-browsing facility on the PC screen to demonstrate how to search for books and other materials. In March 2001, NLB launched a new corporate website which included a suite of standard library services. Users could browse the NLB catalogue online, place reservations, make renewals, verify the status of loans and report lost items. The website was also used to disseminate information on new services and online facilities. In September 2001 a new library portal, eLibraryHub, was launched. This allowed library users 24-h access to information such as membership status, library transactions and a range of electronic resources and library catalogues. The digital library comprised 24,000 titles, including more than 700 CD-ROM and reference videos, 13,000 e-magazines, e-journals and online databases, and 10,000 e-book titles that members could access online or at the library branches. By December 2001 almost all library transactions were automated. The new facilities relieved staff of many of their mundane daily routines, such as manning library loan counters.

## 3.2. The culture change at NLB

Initially, most library employees felt that it was a challenge for them to understand and utilize IT. Their fear and insecurity were obvious during the initial stages adoption. For example, when IT systems failed, users were reluctant to learn how to fix them, despite efforts by technicians to teach simple troubleshooting techniques. This later improved, and an IT technician commented:

‘‘When I visit the libraries to fix servers, I try to teach them some tricks so they can do it themselves. At first they were reluctant, but now they can do simple things themselves or I guide them over the phone.’’

Given low IT adoption within NLB, the management was slow to add new systems. The implementation was carried out in phases, with experimentation in one library and incremental development before it was implemented in other libraries. This approach helped reduce technological failure. Further, it prevented problems in one library being replicated in other locations. However, there were still some technological weaknesses, such as occurred in early versions of CNK, a remote PC controlled by a library call center through which library visitors were able to seek help in discussion with experienced librarians. Its deployment was impeded—much to the satisfaction of some librarians. The CEO summed up NLBs approach as one where new technology should be adopted to improve the business process.

Gradually however, the technological transformation within NLB resulted in significant cultural change in the organization. The human resource manager commented:

‘‘Traditionally, our processes were mainly manual and all of a sudden, we were given PCs. A lot of us had phobia in the beginning. To avoid using the PC, I had even considered early retirement. But in the end, I decided that I would give it a shot.’’

NLB utilized a project-centered approach in which a project team was created with the deputy chief executive as the main driver of the project. Project members were selected from across the organization, involving staff at all levels. Such a cross-functional team helped to develop common trust and communication, minimizing apprehension within the organization. A number of ‘Meet the CE’ sessions were arranged so that staff could hear at first hand the CEs vision and commitment to the project. Focus groups were also organized to obtain feedback from students, retirees, professionals, senior government officials and other relevant contributors. During these sessions, concerns centered upon what library users saw as the ideal library for the next decade, and the kind of library services they expected to be available. In course of time the CE became a role model with whom all library staff could identify. According to one service manager:

‘‘Our CE is inspirational and knows exactly where we ought to be heading. He is also well respected for his vast IT experience.’’

A further advantage of the system was its user-friendly interfaces. They helped users to overcome learning barriers. To better facilitate the transformation process, intensive training and job reallocation was offered to allow library employees to work in areas where they felt competent and comfortable. The aim was to transform library staff from passive custodians of information, to knowledge navigators, well versed in search, retrieval and research capabilities. During this process Hay Consulting Group was engaged to review job scopes, career paths and remunerations of the library professionals. The group conducted a satisfaction survey on the adoption of IT. Seventy-five key components of employee satisfaction were included. Three critical areas emerged as important in generating trust and confidence:

\- Helping employees understand the organization’s overal strategy;

\- Helping employees understand how they contributed towards achieving key objectives;

\- Sharing information with employees on how the organization and its various business units were performing, relative to the organization’s strategic objectives.

New job descriptions and professional career tracks for library professionals were created. Salary structures were also revised to reward library staff willing to accept more challenging tasks.

At this stage, the library staff had overcome their initial resistance and realized the benefits of adopting new technologies in their daily work. As they gradually began to appreciate the value of IT, many were fascinated and became enthusiastic. Some of them actively participated in devising new ideas to improve NLBs daily operation. A senior manager commented:

‘‘We use this library (BBCL) as a prototype to launch many ideas and they work. The public love them!’’

To NLB, the benefits obtained from adopting RFID included higher productivity, with lower training and manpower costs. According to the CE:

‘‘Without the RFID system, we might have to hire another 2000 people to resolve 10 min of queuing time across all our branches. Our library staff of 600 is about a quarter of the total number in major libraries in Europe, with equivalent volume of book loans and library visitors. The introduction of technology has improved customer response time, productivity of the library staffs and also cost reductions.’

The transformation effort has changed the scope of some jobs significantly. A branch manager commented:

‘‘NLB could not simply insert IT into the libraries and expect to capitalize on its competency immediately. We have to make sure that we understood our staffs’ needs and design the system around them.’’

Besides daily responsibilities of managing loan counters and ensuring that books were returned to the shelves, library staff participated in the improvement of the system. An example was the ask stupid questions (ASQ) forum, a platform for the sharing of ideas and knowledge. Another idea which encouraged innovation was implementation of the aspiration pathfinder (AsPi) project. In this, the library simulated the operation of a tour agency which exposed its participants to a series of unconventional learning experiences, thereby fostering creativity and altering the way they worked.

Increased responsibilities and the desire to innovate put many library employees under great pressure. Nevertheless, like many organizations it was inevitable that a small number of people would not accept the new practices: some left the organization because they could not accept the changes. To relieve stress and improve difficult situations, NLB introduced a stress management program. Library employees were encouraged to balance work and play. Individuals’ reactions and handling of stress depended on their personality, including previous experience in handling problems. To address these issues, senior managers were able to help reduce employee fear, confusion and uncertainty by providing support. By involving the library employees in the transformation process and encouraging them to accept ownership of the project and processes, many became motivated and receptive towards change. In addition, incentives, such as service awards, were introduced to give recognition to employees who proved exemplary.

Senior managers acted as role models to their subordinates, providing support and demonstrating their own willingness to embrace change. The employees were committed towards innovation and were expected to possess traits such as adaptability and flexibility. According to one service manager:

‘‘It is important to have a change management mindset. However, this may not be sufficient. More importantly, one should not underestimate the changes needed to adapt to the new culture and having the assumption that it would be easily integrated.’’

According to the CE, NLB would continue to invest in new technologies to complement its services:

‘‘We’ve been prototyping a number of the services we want to launch, but one has to wait for the right timing. Six years ago, for instance, not many people had access to The Internet, so it would not work. But it is an appropriate time now to introduce these services.’’

In July 2005, NLB announced its strategic directions for the next 5 years—Library 2010, known as the L2010 Report. This strategy addressed current challenges faced by Singapore’s society and economy, and marked the next phase of development in NLB. The CE commented:

‘‘We have received many compliments. The library has become more visible and we have a sharp increase of visitors. Book loans have increased as well (Fig. 3). Yet, most importantly we do not have to hire additional staff.’

Overall, NLB has undergone a huge organizational transformation. Thanks to the new processes and working culture, the number of visitors has quadrupled and member ship has doubled. Loans have increased from 10 to 25 million items without a corresponding increase in staff numbers. Queuing time has been reduced from 60 to less than 5 min. At present, NLB has a national reference library, three regional libraries, 18 community libraries and 18 children’s libraries.

## 4. Factors underlying Singapore National Library Board’s successful cultural adaptation

NLBs experience demonstrated sound management of cultural change during technological transformation of the organization. In particular, it provided valuable lessons in the management of the stages of the change in culture (see Appendix B for an illustration of this process in NLB). Seven critical success factors in NLBs cultural adaptation were identified.

## 4.1. Select a strong and visionary leader

The process of culture change needs new management and objectives. NLBs board of directors appointed a CE whose vision and experience were crucial for achieving successful change. The CEs appeal to the library staff was pivotal in persuading the organization to accept his vision. He earned the trust of the employees through his declaration that IT would improve their work situation, and that no one would lose his job as the result of the transformation.

Under the CEs leadership, other senior managers supported the initiative unequivocally. They encouraged prototyping and experimentation, tolerated initial failure and moderated the resistance to change.

Overall, effective leadership played a very important role in encouraging the adoption of new technology. Our findings suggested that social influences will help the adoption of technology by modifying the attitude of the users.

## 4.2. Develop a robust project plan, including a cultural adaptation strategy

NLB ensured that its staff understood the goals and visions of the organization, and how introduction of new technology would improve their daily work. It used a project team approach, which emphasized the importance of teamwork and ownership during change. The CE provided strategic foresight and participated in developing a robust project plan which helped to activate the transformation process. Every employee was trained in basic project management skills. This gave them a common language to share ideas. The project team consisted of individuals who specialized in different functions at various library branches. The project encouraged sharing of experience and broadly based decision making. This was very different from the strict hierarchical style adopted in the past. The involvement of staff in the development, planning and execution phases encouraged them to accept ownership helping them acculturate with the use of technologies [19].

A number of approaches were used to gain the commitment of staff to the adoption of IT. Examples included the CEs direct appeal and numerous communication sessions with senior management, extensive training and rewards for adopting the technology early. The robust project plan brought together a consistent recognition of priorities across the organization and helped individuals focus on the strategic initiative while reducing uncertainty. In order to increase acceptance of new technology, NLB moderated the speed of development, choosing to develop services incrementally. Since traditional public sector bureaucracy tends to inhibit communication, the effective management of a project of this nature provided an excellent opportunity to achieve effective cultural change [9].

## 4.3. Develop a systematic and effective change management process

During the early stages of IT implementation, NLBs employees were apprehensive that the transformation would affect them adversely. Senior managers were determined and patient in attempting to addressing individuals’ anxieties before moving the project forward. Aware of the severe implications of mishandling user resistance to change, NLBs management developed good rapport with its staff through the implementation of the change management process. Managers promoted an open culture. Communication was essential as it significantly reduced the resistance of library staff to change, resulting in smoother process acceptance. The key to change management was, therefore, to determine the effects of new technology on users, anticipating points of resistance and being prepared to address them as the need arose.

Proper planning and effective change management techniques can help reduce the shock at the initial phase. Two initiatives were set up.

\- The ASQ forum was used as a platform for ideas and knowledge sharing;

\- The AsPi project provided library staff simulation exercises involving a series of unconventional learning experiences, encouraging their creativity and acceptance of change.

The change management plan allowed library staff to better understand the rationale behind the transformation, helping them adapt to new cultural changes with little resistance. 4.4. Ensure effective communication between senior managers and library employees

Effective management of relationships enabled a high level of internal coordination and process integration. Conducting effective programs to promote awareness and interest in the new system was challenging. To attract interest, the new system was announced as bringing about a better working environment and more efficient work processes.

Senior managers were willing to listen and understand their subordinates’ needs and to integrate the system into work processes. Focus groups were organized to obtain feedback. In these sessions, discussions centered on library users’ vision of the ideal library in the next decade, and the library services they expected to see.

The trust developed between library staff and senior managers helped to improve staff morale and enhance customer service. The staff understood the strategic vision and knew their individual role in achieving the goals.

## 4.5. Instill a strong sense of ownership in both project and work processes and promote training

The fascination stage is the period during which individuals overcome their initial resistance to change. At NLB, it began as staff saw the new cultural environment more clearly. They realized that they would benefit from the changes and started to enjoy some of the promised benefits. This increased feedback about possible further improvements. From NLBs experiences, involving staff as early as the design phase resulted in easier acceptance of technology. It instilled staff with a strong sense of ownership. Fascinated with significant work process improvements, staff were enthusiastic about future developments and began to participate actively in generating new ideas. Three critical factors which created a sense of ownership among staff were:

\- Communicating the organization’s overall strategy to staff;

\- Involving staff in developing new performance measures;

\- Enabling communication among staff on organizational performance and strategic objectives.

Training was critical in preparing employees for the transition to a new technology. From our experiences with NLB we found that senior management had not imposed change, and that individuals are allowed to search for solutions, with support from senior managers. In most situations training was required before the new technology was introduced. This increased the perception of benefits. In addition, staff could participate by including their own ideas on improving customer service.

## 4.6. Initiate stress management program to relieve the pressure of changes in expectations

As the transformation process progressed, several milestones were achieved. Then senior managers were able to set higher targets as expectations were fulfilled. NLB focused its efforts to increasing productivity to offset the cost of introducing the new technology and retaining staff. NLB also concentrated on developing excellence in customer service. Staff were expected to keep up to date with new sources of information, new databases and search engines, to remain familiar with NLBs increasing data bank. The growing expectations of senior managers kept most library staff under pressure, since they were not only expected to understand IT but also to keep up with new developments. The pressures inevitably led to some negative feelings. To ensure smoother acceptance, senior managers introduced stress management programs.

Lack of knowledge is often the cause of alienation, which can lead to social and cultural isolation. Furthermore, without a proper coping strategy it is likely that distressed members of staff will choose to leave.

Generally speaking, stress in the workplace increases work pressure and reduces productivity; it can even make people ill or result in litigation against employers, with consequent bad publicity and loss of reputation. At NLB, the project team made strenuous efforts to ensure that the new technology was user-friendly, and adapted in response to feedback. Prototypes were designed, and used during staff training. This helped to overcome fear of the new technology. It was shown that once individuals saw how little effort was required to use IT they had little difficulty adopting it.

## 4.7. Cultivate a culture of innovation within the organization

At NLB there was a noticeable improvement in employees’ IT skills, mostly due to the help given by IT personnel and other members of staff. Library employees began to feel comfortable with IT. However, open dialogue was necessary to allay anxiety about loss of their jobs. At NLB the recovery phase was characterized by the facilitation of staff acceptance and their personal contribution to the process of innovation.

Striving to be innovative and to constantly challenge the status quo, NLB took every opportunity to develop and implement new ideas. In addition, it persevered with of the use of prototypes and new innovative services were constantly being added to libraries. Three new or completely revamped libraries were introduced every year, with each experimenting in at least one innovation.

With rapid growth in the use of the internet, NLB has been subjected to a continual increase in the availability of information. At NLB every staff member has been encouraged to learn how to satisfy its users’ needs. Table 1 summarizes the phases and the critical success factors.

## 5. Implications and limitations of the study

The study had a number of important implications for research and practice. For research, it may be of value in investigating organizational re-engineering by providing an insight into the culture changes involved. Using acculturation as an analytical tool, we attempted to identify the gradual change in users’ behavior within the organization in relation to IT and the adoption and use of new technologies. We have highlighted the phases that occur and the factors that have an influence on the participants. While some of these issues have been highlighted previously, our analysis may provide a fresh appreciation of their role in the acceptance of cultural change.

Our analysis of the process of cultural change also has important practical implications. For example, by understanding the factors involved, managers are in a better position to anticipate and be prepared for unexpected events. Our conclusions are made in the context of the public sector. They will, however, be relevant to other organizations when introducing new technology.

There were some limitations in our conduct of this case study. The data were collected retrospectively from participants over the period 1995–2004. While most of the participants served with the organization during these years, their memory may be subjective and biased. A longitudinal ethnographic study might provide a better insight into the subtleties of cultural changes in the organization. In addition, the participants were not able to review their interview transcripts. Some bias in interpretation is therefore possible, despite the researcher’s best efforts.

## 6. Conclusions

The process of the acceptance of culture change highlights the importance of communication and trust. It further illustrates the need to understand a range of issues, each generating a variety of demands and expectations. Trust and transparency must be developed if staff are to become committed to a project. Our case showed that managing cultural change can be challenging, but awareness and preparedness for change is necessary. Key issues have been identified and examined in the context of increasing importance of social factors in adopting and using IT. Attention to these issues is essential to ensure success.
