# A study of the roles of trusts and risks in information-oriented online legal services using an integrated model

Vincent Cho

Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Hong Kong Received 4 June 2003; received in revised form 1 November 2005; accepted 20 December 2005 Available online 14 February 2006

## Abstract

This study examined the effect of the four constructs – trust in online media, trust in online services, perceived risk with online media, and perceived risk with online services – on the use of information-oriented online legal services. Using past theories on adoption studies, a three-level framework was developed. This framework helped to formulate a new research model. A quantitative survey was conducted to identify the important factors affecting the intention of customers to adopt information-oriented online legal services in Hong Kong. Findings indicated that there is a hierarchical relationship among the four constructs. Perceived usefulness was the most important belief that drove people to adopt the online services. Perceived risk and trust were less important at present, probably because online legal services are still somewhat immature and mainly focus on less risky areas, such as information dissemination and marketing. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Adoption; Trust; Online legal services

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has enabled e-commerce to flourish. Nevertheless, it is still not as popular as face-to-face transactions. Customers are uncomfortable when they are physically separated from the sales persons and response is not rapid. These effects make e-commerce seem risky due to fear of monetary loss or privacy exposure. In order to reduce the perceived risks, online service providers attempt to establish trust through means such as encryption, firewalls, or assurance from a trustworthy third party. Many studies have explored the impact of perceived risk and trust in the Web retailers on the attitude of adoption of e-commerce [40,41] and have investigated the antecedences of trust [19]. Though past studies stated that the perceived risk with online media was different from that with online services [47], and trust also differs in these two aspects [28], but no research has put these four constructs together. My work attempted to explore the relationships and impacts of these on attitude towards adopting online services. However, according to the three-level framework which I described later, other factors such as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, compatibility, and facilitating conditions are needed to be included in order to provide a full picture.

The application domain of the study was online legal services. In the past, lawyers were the only interface between people and the law. This channel has been hindered by the high hourly charges normally imposed by lawyers. The emergence of the World Wide Web has, however, allowed people to gain access to legal information through online facilities and provided transparent legal practices, lowering the charges (see Appendix A).

Surprisingly, few have been using such new services. Indeed, online legal services are in the beginning stage and mainly focus on information dissemination and marketing. They do not provide full-fledged legal counseling through interactive online media. Moreover, customers may perceive a high level of risk if they must provide details of their personal information and particulars of their case before an online legal adviser can provide information or advice. Thus, past studies on online banking or Web retailing may not apply to information-oriented online legal services.

## 2. Formulation of the framework

A variety of theoretical perspectives have been used to study the determinants of IT usage. The use of intention-based models is one way of predicting usage by identifying the determinants of the behavioral intentions of individual users. TAM, TRA, TPB, and the Triandis model are examples of intention-based models while another approach is the use of innovation diffusion-based models, such as the Innovation Diffusion Theory model (IDT); for a brief description of these, see Appendix B.

Besides studying the adoption of technology, we may classify studies on adoption, as shown in Fig. 1, into three levels: technological, system, and application. At the technological level, standalone technology is the main focus. Davis [11] developed the constructs of PU and PEOU to explain the adoption and use of a given technology in the TAM model.

In addition, the technology and its environment involve social and facilitating support as a system and should be studied under the Triandis framework, which includes an examination of technological and environmental concerns. Karahanna and Straub [26] explored the psychological origins of PU and PEOU, and suggested the inclusion of social determinants in their study on the adoption and use of e-mail systems.

The technological and system levels are mainly focused on the technology itself. Its application would be related to services or products that help people to accomplish their daily tasks. Thus, e-mail is regarded as a tool to facilitate communications as well as the performance of complicated tasks such as online negotiations, which represent a change in business practice and may not be compatible with normal business style. For that reason, the adoption and use of online activities may be perceived as difficult and, in general, the task-technology fit should be considered; studies on new technology adoption should include an examination of its compatibility with the normal style. Moreover, the success of a change will be highly dependent on the extent to which the technology itself can be adopted and this, in turn, is a prerequisite for its application.

IDT, which concentrates on both technology and its compatibility, provides some understanding of the adoption of a standalone technology in the application level. For instance, the adoption of an e-photo album that can store up to 20 GB and allow people to view digital photos in a 3 in.  4 in. LCD screen, could be studied by IDT by assessing the effect of its replacing a traditional photo album.

Chen et al. [8] combined TAM and IDT in their study on online consumers. Dishaw and Strong integrated the TAM and task-technology fit models to study software development tools for software maintenance. However, they did not include social and facilitating conditions. Table 1 attempts to classify the factors in the adoption of different major theories into the three-level framework. For a standalone technology such as a wireless mouse that is highly compatible with the wired-mouse, TAM is a reasonable predictor of its adoption. For a technology such as short message service in a mobile phone, Triandis is more relevant. For a technology involving the application of a new idea, IDT is more suitable if it is standalone. Otherwise, it is necessary to combine TAM, Triandis, and IDT to predict adoption. In the application level, compatibility or TTF should be an influential factor affecting the attitude, intention, and behavior of people in adopting a new technique.

In this study of online legal services, PEOU and PU were adopted in the integrated model. Past studies had indicated that PEOU was a better predictor of attitude for inexperienced users while PU was a better predictor of the intention for experienced users [20].

Given that trust and perceived risks, being elements in the IDT [44] are the primary focus in this study, their effect in online media and online services was incorporated in the integrated model as beliefs that shape the behavioral intention of customers.

Chen et al. showed that compatibility, which influences PU and attitude, can improve the TAM model; the integrated model also included compatibility from IDT, while the other attributes of innovation (trialability and observability) were discarded because they had not been shown to be consistently related to adoption.

From the Triandis model, the construct of facilitating conditions was incorporated, though the construct had a broader meaning, including users’ self-efficacy and objective factors that facilitate behavior. Self-efficacy here used the definition that it is ‘‘the belief that one has the capability to perform a particular behavior’’ [33].

As online legal services are still in an early stage and not many people have experience with them, habit, affective feeling, and social factors were discarded in the model.

## 3. An integrated research model

TAM, the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), the Triandis model, and IDT provided a fundamental framework for technology adoption at the application level. In particular, trust and risks were integrated into the comprehensive framework as shown in Fig. 2.

## 3.1. Trust

Trust has long been an essential variable of economic and social interactions where uncertainty, delegation of authority, and fears of opportunism are present [32]. It is especially true in the uncertain environment of e-commerce [30]. Following Stewart [48], McKnight et al. [37], Parlov, and Gefen et al., trust is defined here as the belief that allows individuals to be willing to react after having taken the characteristics of the providers and the underlying Internet infrastructure into consideration. This definition, consistent with Tan and Thoen [50], captures both the traditional view of trust in a specific party and reliability of the transaction medium.

Trust in online media is a behavioral belief relating to security in using the Internet. This may be guaranteed by a security service provider or by using encryption. Trust depends on past experience and social cues about the ability of the system designers. On the other hand, trust in online legal services is a behavioral belief that the service provider is competent, has integrity, and is benevolent.

Strengthening trust by reducing environmental uncertainty should have a direct effect on the trust in the online service. Moreover, trust should depend on the trust in online media, in general. Once a customer has a feeling of insecurity about accessing the Internet, worrying that information may be trapped during the transmission, the customer will also lack trust in using any online service. DeLone and McLean [13] have applied this argument for the links among user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact in their study of system effectiveness. People need to establish a belief of trust in online media before they can establish the belief of trust in online services. Thus, the hypothesis is:

H1a. The customer’s trust in online media will positively affect his/her trust in online legal services.

The relationship between trust and attitude draws from ‘‘perceived consequences’’ [51] and the Theory of

Reasoned Action. Trust enables favorable expectations that no harmful outcomes will occur if a trustor undertakes a behavior and the trustee will protect the trustor’s interests [3,22]. Consumers are more likely to have a positive attitude towards buying from a merchant they trust. In sum, trust creates favorable perceptions about the outcomes of the service provider’s actions, thus creating positive attitudes. This was also shown in studies by Ruyter et al. [45], Jarvenpaa et al. [25], McKnight and Chervany [36], and Pavlou: trust has a positive impact on consumers’ attitudes towards both traditional and Internet stores.

In terms of obtaining information from an online legal service provider, trust creates positive expectations that the service provider will post credible information and trust in online media attenuate infrastructure concerns [21]. According to Pavlou and McKnight et al., the trust of online media is an antecedent to the trust of online services; and the influence on attitude towards online legal services will depend on the trust of online services. Based on this, the hypothesis is:

H1b. The trust that customers have in the online legal services is positively related to their attitude towards these services.

## 3.2. Perceived risk

Murphy and Enis [38] defined perceived risk as customer’s subjective assessment of the negative consequence when making a mistake in a transaction. Jacoby and Kaplan [23] classified consumers’ perceived risk into five types: physical, psychological, social, financial, and performance. In general, it represents an individual’s assessment of the relative probability of positive and negative outcomes of a given transaction or situation [10]. As online legal services involve the exchange of more personal information, they will be perceived to have a greater privacy risk [29]. Two forms of risks are naturally present:

(a) behavioral risks from the online service providers; (b) environmental risk derived from the underlying technological infrastructure [4].

Behavioral risk arises because Web retailers have the chance to behave in an opportunistic manner and the government’s inability to adequately monitor all transactions. Examples of opportunistic behavior could include exposing the user’s personal data to a third party and the fear of receiving low quality and unreliable online legal advice.

Environmental risk mainly exists because of the unpredictable nature of the technology that is beyond the control of the online service provider or individual. While retailers have an important influence on the security of the medium, there is still a possibility for third parties to compromise the process.

Therefore, the hypothesis is:

H2a. The perceived risk with online media will positively affect the perceived risk with online legal services.

Once the perceived risks associated with an online service are reduced, customers will have a more favorable attitude towards the online service and will be more willing to engage in transactions. This idea was supported by Jarvenpaa and Tractinsky [24] in their study on online shopping.

When engaging in an online transaction process, consumers are alarmed about the two types of risks (with online media and online services). The overall expectations will be influenced by the service providers behavior and ability to protect critical information during the transaction [43]. Thus, the hypothesis is:

H2b. The perceived risk with online legal services will have a negative effect on the attitude of customers towards engaging these services.

## 3.3. Trust and perceived risk

Trust is influenced by the way that risk is evaluated and perceived. The level of perceived risk is offset by the level of trust [17,39]. Trust is also linked to increased risk-taking behavior [34] and a reduction in opportunistic behavior. When an online legal service provider can be trusted to show competence, integrity, and benevolence, there is much less apparent risk in interacting. Thus, trust in online media and trust in online services have been considered especially important for mediating the respective risks. Hypothesis 3 predicts:

H3a. The customer’s trust in online legal services will negatively affect the perceived risk with these services.

H3b. The customer’s trust in online media will negatively affect the customer’s perceived risk with these media.

Moreover, a trusted service provider should take steps to reduce environmental uncertainty and related risks in the Internet infrastructure by imposing encryption, firewall, and authentication mechanism. In general, trust in the services should improve the consumer’s beliefs about online service providers and the associated infrastructure, attenuating the perceived level of risk associated with the online services and media. Hence, the hypothesis is:

H3c. The customer’s trust in online services will negatively affect his/her perceived risk with online media. However, the Internet media, which consist of routers and public wirings, are not under the control of the service providers. Thus, the hypothesis is:

H3d. The trust in online media will negatively affect the perceived risk with online legal services.

## 3.4. Behavioral intention and attitude

Behavioral intention refers to ‘‘instructions that people give to themselves to behave in certain ways.’’ Therefore, here it refers to a customer’s intention to adopt information-oriented online legal services.

According to Fishbein and Ajzen [16], ‘‘attitude is a learned predisposition to respond in a consistently favorable or unfavorable manner with a given object.’ Attitude is directly related to behavioral intention because people will only intend to perform behavior for which they have positive feelings. Thus, the hypothesis is:

H4. Attitude is positively related to the customer’s intention to adopt online legal services.

## 3.5. Perceived ease of use

PEOU is the belief that a particular technology would be applied with no effort [15]. It should directly influence the user’s attitude towards a particular technology. Applying this to online legal services, a Web interface perceived to be user-friendly will have greater appeal to customers than one that is not, and will facilitate use of the services. In some studies, PEOU was found to have an indirect influence on attitude through PU. Here, PEOU refers to the ease of navigating, searching for information, and obtaining services from legal websites. Thus, the hypotheses are:

H5a. Perceived ease of use is positively related to the customer’s attitude towards online legal services.

H5b. Perceived ease of use is positively related to the perceived usefulness (PU) of online legal services.

## 3.6. Perceived usefulness

There are some relative advantages to using information-oriented online legal services compared with traditional legal services. The PU of online legal services is measured by savings in money and time, increased flexibility, and greater access to information about a legal case. It influences both attitude and behavioral intention [2]. The more useful it is perceived to be, the more positive the user’s attitude will be towards the adoption of online legal services, and the greater the intention to make use of the services.

H6a. Perceived usefulness is positively related to the customer’s attitude towards online legal services.

H6b. Perceived usefulness is positively related to the customer’s intention to adopt online legal services.

## 3.7. Compatibility

This has not been included in most studies on the adoption of technology. The exceptions are IDT and a study by Chen et al. Compatibility refers to the congruence of innovation with the values and beliefs of users and with previously introduced ideas and needs [35]. It is worth considering when the task makes use of new methods or approaches. Thus, adoption of a new application depends on its compatibility with existing practice. Here, compatibility represents the congruence of the online legal services with the people’s lifestyle in seeking legal advice or services. Thus, the hypotheses are:

H7a. Compatibility is positively related to the perceived usefulness of online legal services.

H7b. Compatibility is positively related to the customer’s attitude towards online legal services.

## 3.8. Facilitating conditions

These are the environmental factors that make a behavior easy or difficult. Users with sufficient skills and ability will find a new application easy to use. Therefore, aside from objective factors such as the availability of resources, implicit factors like the confidence of users in their use of a technology are considered as facilitating condition.

In the Triandis model, facilitating conditions are important determinants of behavior. If habits are well established and physiological arousals are optimal, there may be no action/behavior once objective factors do not warrant user behavior. The facilitating conditions in the Triandis model are similar TPB, which suggests that the skills of users and the availability of resources directly affect the intention of users to perform a behavior [14]. Empirical investigations have shown that facilitating conditions could also have a significant positive impact on attitude [7]. Thus, the hypotheses include:

H8a. Facilitating conditions are positively related to the customer’s attitude towards using online legal services.

H8b. Facilitating conditions are positively related to the customer’s intention to adopt online legal services.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Developing the measurements

A questionnaire was constructed (see Appendix C) for collecting user perceptions of online legal services.

## 4.2. Trust

Most often, trust is viewed as a three-dimensional construct of competence, integrity, and benevolence [5,18]. Competence is the belief in the trustee’s ability to do what the trustor needs. Integrity is the belief that the trustee will be honest and keep his or her promises. Benevolence is the belief that the trustee will not act opportunistically. In the area of online legal service providers, the information must be valid, credible, and accurate [42]. A trusted online legal service provider fears that false information may expose a consumer to adverse outcomes. Thus, the trust of online services has two perspectives—competence (ability) and integrity (honest). The three items for these aspects were adopted from Gefen and Lee and Turban [31]. For the trust in online media, the measure was adopted from the latter.

## 4.3. Perceived risk

PR here is about the risks to security and privacy that may deter consumers from making use of the services. In the first three questions, the respondents were asked to rate their perception of Web security (perceived risk with online media). The measurement scale was adopted from that of Salisabury et al. [46]. The respondents are asked about the level of risk in searching for legal information, downloading legal documents, and seeking legal guidance. The measurement scale was based on that devised by Pavlou.

## 4.4. Perceived ease of use

PEOU stresses how comfortable customers will be with the Internet as a purchasing medium for legal services. Its measurement scale was based on the work of Chen et al. and Davis. The measurement has three items focusing on the respondents’ perceptions of the ease of using an online medium and two items on the ease of learning to use online legal services.

## 4.5. Perceived usefulness

PU emphasizes the expectations of value derived from using online legal services: the relative advantages of increased flexibility, efficiency, effectiveness, etc. The measurement scale was also based on those of Chen et al. and Davis.

## 4.6. Compatibility

Compatibility was measured by five items underlining the congruence of using online legal services with the existing value and beliefs, and task needs. The items were adapted from Chen et al. and Davis; they measured the compatibility of using online legal services, which include searching and downloading legal information and seeking legal advices, with respect to the respondents’ lifestyle.

## 4.7. Facilitating conditions

The facilitating condition had four items in its construct, consisting of the objective factors that make the behavior easy or difficult. For online legal services, facilitating conditions were considered to be accessibility to the Internet and self-efficacy of users. One item adapted from Chang and Cheung measured ease of access to the Internet. The other three items for measuring the self-efficacy of users were adapted from Tan and Teo [49].

## 4.8. Attitude

Fishbein and Ajzen’s suggestion that attitude should be predicted from a person’s beliefs was followed here. A four-item scale is adopted from Chen et al. and Davis to measure the attitude of respondents towards using online legal services.

## 4.9. Intention to use

One item was used to measure the respondents intention to use online legal services. Since most of the respondents did not have prior experience in using online legal services, this question asked whether they intended to use such services in the future.

## 4.10. Sampling target and research method

The scope was restricted to online legal services and ‘‘virtual lawyers.’’ Based on current online legal services, which primarily delivered informationoriented services, we could only interpret the result for this specific range of services. The population of respondents to the survey was potential users of online legal services; they were chosen to be Internet-literate, having adequate experience in using legal services and in shopping online. The sample involved part-time postgraduate students, law students in local universities, and employees in public organizations and government departments. All subjects were educated and Internetliterate and potential users of online legal services.

Since the objective was to study the behavioral intention of customers, measures of beliefs, attitudes and behavioral intention were more appropriate than direct observation. Approximately 300 questionnaires were distributed to the target subjects by mail or e-mail.

## 5. Findings and discussion

## 5.1. Reliability and validity

Factor analysis was used to reduce the number of items in each construct to maintain its reliability and discriminant validity. Factors were extracted by using the principal component method and varimax rotation. Variables were eliminated if they were not factorially pure.

Thus, the following items were discarded:

\- Two items in the PEOU: ‘‘It is easy to interact with the Internet’’ and ‘‘It is easy to shop on the Internet.’

\- Two items in compatibility: ‘‘Using the Internet fits my lifestyle’’ and ‘‘Using the Internet fits my practices in seeking information.’’

\- One item in facilitating condition: ‘‘I am confident of being able to communicate my legal problems effectively with the Internet.’’

After removing these items, the constructs focused on online legal services and they examined a person’s perception of online legal service in various ways. Table 3 shows the items that remained for modeling the structural equation and also summarizes the result of a reliability analysis of the variables. All factors had quite high a values (over 0.7), except for trust in online media (a = 0.69) which was still acceptable. This may be due to the relatively small number of questionnaire items (two) used to measure the scale of trust in online media. Overall all of the variables were reliable. Moreover, all of the measures of constructs had been used in past studies, and had been validated.

Average variance extracted (AVE) determines the average variance shared in a construct. It is calculated by the sum of the loadings squared, divided by the number of items in the construct; e.g., the AVE of PEOU is 0.52. It should be greater than 50% in order to be considered reliable.

If the items in a construct correlate more highly with each other than with items measuring other constructs, the measure is considered to have adequate discriminant validity. Table 4 presents the squared intercorrelations among the study variables. They represent the shared variance among the variables and did not exceed the average variance explained. Thus, discriminant validity was again justified.

Compatibility, facilitating conditions, PU, and attitude receive relatively high mean scores (all items were above 3.5 except for ‘‘Online legal services are very useful’’ and ‘‘Online legal services are suitable for seeking legal advice’’). This indicated that the respondents regarded online legal services as useful and compatible with their way of seeking legal services, and that they had a positive attitude towards trying out these services. This is justifiable in view of the high level of education of the respondents and of the widespread access to the Internet in Hong Kong. At the other extreme, the score for the perceived risk with online media was relatively low, which suggested that the respondents felt that the Internet was not a secure medium for conducting transactions and exchanging personal information.

## 6. The structural equation model

We formulated an SEM using AMOS 5.0 [6] to analyze our model. Significant paths were found. A number of statistics were generated to evaluate the overall fitness of the model. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value of 426 (d.f. = 45) with a p-value of 0.000 indicated a good fit of the model. However, as the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test is sensitive to sample size and may not be very stable, supplementary measures, such as goodness of fit index (GFI), normed fit index (NFI), and comparative fit index (CFI), were investigated. For the SEM, the GFI was 0.92, the NFI was 0.91, and the CFI was 0.95. All are over 0.9, which shows a good fit. The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was also computed to measure the expected error of the model; its value was a little less than 0.20, and this is acceptable. As expected, our integrated model was consistent with previous studies and most of the hypotheses were supported as shown in Table 5 and Fig. 3.

## 6.1. Trust

There is no significant influence of trust in online media to trust in online services $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 , p = 0 . 6 8 )$ this suggests that the link between two beliefs due to the effort of the service providers enabling a better secure environment with encryption, firewall, and authentication may not be adequate to join them. On the other hand, there is a temporal and logical sequence between them though they are conceptually different. Though there may be some overlapping between these two beliefs, it may be too weak to be validated.

Nevertheless, our findings show that trust in online services has a significant though weak impact on the attitude towards adopting online legal services $( \beta = 0 . 0 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). This can be explained by the fact that most providers of online legal services use their website for marketing and for the dissemination of information. Thus, trust in using the service provider is still not a major concern at present.

Perceived risk with online media had significant impact on that with online services $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ Negative consequences could be expected when using the online services because they are dependent on the online media.

## 6.3. Trust and perceived risk

The survey validated the view that trust and perceived risk are closely related. The trust in online media has a significant impact on the perceived risk of online media $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Similarly, the trust in online services has a significant impact on the perceived risk of them $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Because the items in perceived risk were reversely coded (e.g., ‘‘The Internet is a secure means through which to send personal information’’), the coefficients bs in both of the above relationships were positive.

Results showed that trust in online media had a significant but moderate impact on the perceived risk with online services $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Thus, trust in online media due to the competence of the Internet technologies’ designers or user’s experience on the Internet reduced the perceived risk with online services. On the other hand, trust in online services had no significant impact on perceived risk with online media $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 , p = 0 . 2 1 )$ .

## 6.4. Attitude and intention

The findings agree with TPB and other attitude– intention models: the attitude of adopting online legal services had a positive impact on the intention to use the services $( \beta = 0 . 3 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ .

## 6.5. PEOU and PU

Although perceived ease of use was not significantly related to attitude $( \beta = 0 . 0 4 , \ p = 0 . 4 8 )$ , there was an indirect impact from PEOU on attitude and intention through PU $( \beta = 0 . 3 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . As predicted, PU had strong correlation with attitude $( \beta = 0 . 4 3 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and intention $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The results demonstrated that PEOU does not engender customers intention to use online legal services.

In designing a legal website, developer should pay attention to functionality and the respective PU. Responding to the open-end questions, respondents stated that the scope of service, responsiveness of feedback, and extent of interaction with virtual lawyers determined the usefulness of online legal services.

## 6.6. Compatibility

Table 5 indicates that respondents are prone to agree that online legal service is compatible with their lifestyle: the means of the items are all above 3. In the path analysis, which was consistent with the studies by Chen et al., compatibility was strongly correlated with PU $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . It also evinced positive influence on intention through attitude $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Apparently, early adopters of the online legal services are people with a ‘‘wired lifestyle.’’

## 6.7. Facilitating conditions

These do not appear to have significant effect on intention $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 , p = 0 . 7 8 )$ . Although they did not have a significant factor for adoption intention, it was a significant factor affecting the attitude to adopt online legal service $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ due to environments and self-efficacy. Thus, skillful people having good Internet access will have a positive attitude towards the adoption of online legal services. However, its impact was moderate $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ .

## 7. Conclusion

This study was developed to investigate the relationships among the four constructs of trust and perceived risk in the online media and online services and how they influence the attitude and behavior intention to adopt the information-oriented online legal services. A three-level framework was developed to explain the adoption of information-oriented online legal services at the application level. The results of an experiment showed that once the trust had been established, the perceived risk would be reduced. Also, perceived risk with online media, trust in online media, and the trust in online services were the fundamental beliefs that influenced the perceived risk of online services. We found that the intention to adopt an information-oriented online legal service was significantly affected by a customer’s trust in the online service. In anticipation of a new vista for online services, these findings may give law firms some insight into customer behavior.

The results confirmed that trust in online media is an underlying belief affecting the perceived risk of online media. Also, the perceived risk with online services was affected by trust in online media, perceived risk with online media, and trust in online services.

In practice, our research shows that online service providers should pay more attention to the value of their website as a means of disseminating information or of conducting transactions. Its usefulness is the most important factor in its adoption. Also, a website must be compatible with the lifestyles of people seeking legal advice and the online legal service provider may need to consider using more verbal or visual aids in disseminating information or consider using video conferencing for online discussions. Some respondents stated that it is hard to read or understand legal terms. Moreover, the provision of personalized and follow-up services would help to create a ‘‘personal feeling’’ that would boost the trust of customers and bolster the reputation of the legal service provider. Finally, perceived risk with online services did not significantly affect the users’ attitude in adopting of online legal services.

## 8. Limitations of the study

Some limitations existed in this study. First, as online legal services were still in their infancy and many respondents were not familiar with them, therefore the study could not include the relationship between the intention to use and actual behavior (actual usage). Second, cultural, ethical, or regulatory issues, which are probably relevant to legal services, were not considered.

## Appendix B. Brief descriptions on TRA, TPB, TAM, Triandis, and IDT

## B.1. Theory of Reasoned Action and Theory of Planned Behavior

The Theory of Reasoned Action and the Theory of Planned Behavior have been widely adopted as the theoretical basis for studies on the adoption of IT systems and the Internet. TRA is a well-accepted model to explain the attitude–intention–behavior relationship.

## B.2. Technology Acceptance Model

TAM was derived from TRA to explain general human behavior in IS usage. The constructs of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (PEOU) are two beliefs that form its basis.

## B.3. Triandis model

This model assumes an attitude–intention–behavior relationship but includes a number of relevant variables, such as habit, social factors, and facilitating conditions (the necessary resources and support to perform a behavior—time, money, expertise, hardware, software, network connection, etc.). The Triandis model was widely adopted in studies of social and consumer behavior. In recent studies, the Triandis model has been applied to the adoption of technology such as World Wide Web usage [9].

## B.4. Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT)

IDT also deals with the adoption of online professional services. It helps to explain the innovation decision process, the factors determining the rate of adoption, and different categories of adopters using five attributes: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. A sixth attribute of perceived risk has been used in other studies (www.comp.nus.edu.sg/gohky/Ibank/ITUsage.htm). Generally, only relative advantage, compatibility, and complexity are consistently related to the adoption of innovations.

## B.5. Similarities among different theories

Although these theories focus on different determinants to explain consumer behavior in the adoption of technology, they have similarities:

\- TRA, TPB, TAM, and the Triandis model assume an attitude–intention–behavior relationship which, in turn, influences behavioral intention and actual usage.

\- Perceived usefulness in TAM is similar to relative advantage in IDT and, to a certain extent, to perceived consequences in the Triandis model.

\- Perceived ease of use in TAM is obviously close to the complexity construct in IDT.

\- Perceived behavioral control in TPB refers to a person’s perceptions of control over a process and access to the resources to facilitate a behavior. In the Triandis model, this is also considered, but it posits that facilitating conditions only affect the actual behavior, while perceived behavioral controls in TPB affect both the behavioral intention and actual usage.
