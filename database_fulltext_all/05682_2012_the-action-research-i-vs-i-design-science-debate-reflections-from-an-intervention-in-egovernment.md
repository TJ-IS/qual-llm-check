RESEARCH ARTICLE

# The action research vs design science debate: reflections from an intervention in eGovernment

Nikolaos Papas<sup>1</sup>, Robert M. O’Keefe<sup>2</sup> and Philip Seltsikas<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, University of Surrey, Guildford, UK; <sup>2</sup>Faculty of Management and Economics, Royal Holloway College, University of London; <sup>3</sup>University of Sydney Business School, University of Sydney

Correspondence: Robert M. O’Keefe, Faculty of Management and Economics, Royal Holloway College, University of London, Egham, TW20 0EX, UK. Tel: þ 44 1784 414147; E-mail: bob.okeefe@rhul.ac.uk

## Abstract

As Design Science (DS) establishes itself as an acceptable approach to Information Systems research, many have commented on the similarity, or otherwise, between DS and Action Research (AR). Most of the writing on this topic has been conceptual, and not grounded in practice. In this paper, we present a piece of completed research that was perceived and executed as AR, but also reflected upon as DS. The research produced a new method for diagramming electronic workflows and creating the associated digital signatures, within the domain of eGovernment. Our conclusion is that AR that produces an artefact can be quite easily, and perhaps superficially, presented as DS. Epistemologically, there is little to separate the two methodologies. However, there are some subtle differences in practice, especially with regard to the role of the artefact, the structuring of the process, the focus of evaluation of the intervention and research, and the emphasis on learning and knowledge. We provide guidance to researchers contemplating either approach, and also consider the role of pluralist attempts to combine the approaches. Keywords: research methods; Action Research; Design Science; pluralism

## Introduction

Approaches to research in Information Systems (IS) exhibit a considerable plurality, with numerous quantitative and qualitative approaches evident. Qualitative approaches have been dominated by case studies, embedded in either a positivist or post-positivist (typically interpretive or construc-tivist) method of analysis. However, over the past 15 years or so, research methods that are interventionist in nature, merging research and praxis, have matured and grown to become acceptable approaches for IS researchers.

Action Research (AR) is ‘an interventionist approach to the acquisition of scientific knowledge that has foundations in the post-positivist tradition’ (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). It aims to ‘solve current practical problems while expanding scientific knowledge’ (Baskerville & Myers 2004). Although various forms of AR exist (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998, distinguish 10 separate articulated forms of AR), most AR as used in IS research follows an approach whereby researchers interact with practitioners through a series of steps or stages that can be cycled through in their entirety or in part (Davison et al, 2004). Although the number of stages can vary, a five-stage model of (1) problem diagnosis, (2) action planning, (3) action taking, (4) evaluation and (5) learning is not uncommon (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). This is frequently referred to as canonical AR due to its prevalence in the AR canon, and is often credited to Susman & Evered (1978).

In Design Science (DS), ‘understanding of a problem and its solution are achieved in the building and application of the designed artefact’ (Hevner et al, 2004). Researchers engage with practitioners in the design of an artefact that is produced to address a specific problem, while at the same time possibly embodying a test of extant theory. Hevner et al (2004) state that artefacts can include constructs (vocabulary and symbols), models (abstractions), methods (algorithms and practices) and instantiations (implemented or prototype software), but have a clear propensity for physical artefacts. We see this definition of ‘artefact’ as a wide-ranging term that covers any type of ‘research product’ capable of being used to ‘serve human purposes’ (March & Smith, 1995). Like AR, definitions of DS methods are often reduced to a set of steps that allow for a mutual understanding of the research process. Peffers et al (2007) present a DS ‘methodology’ that includes the stages (1) problem identification, (2) definition of objectives for a solution, (3) design and development, (4) demonstration, (5) evaluation and (6) communication. In a memorandum arguing for more visibility for DS, O<sup>¨</sup> sterle et al (2010) present an even simpler meta-process for DS: (1) analysis, (2) design, (3) evaluation and (4) diffusion.

IS researchers have considered the apparent similarities between the two approaches. For example, Peffers et al (2007) consider ‘action research, as y an alternative or complementary paradigm through which to design IS research artefacts’, indicating that AR and DS are research methodologies that may complement each other when applied to certain research problems. Ja¨rvinen (2007) states that ‘action research and design science are similar’, based on the fact that AR and DS are both often executed as cyclical processes with stages that are similar in content if not in name. Ja¨rvinen then outlines key aspects of both AR and DS and identifies six areas where they yield equivalent, or very similar, results. This comparison leads Ja¨rvinen to conclude that ‘action research and design science should be considered as similar research approaches’.

Cole et al (2005) see a close relationship between AR and DS and show this similarity by capturing the development stages of AR and DS and then conducting a ‘cross-application of criteria’ (ibid.), where AR criteria are applied to a notable DS paper and DS criteria are applied to a notable AR paper. Their comparison reveals some subtle differences, but they conclude that AR and DS are epistemologically and ontologically similar. Conversely, others have focused on differences rather than similarities. Most notably, Baskerville (2008) states that ‘design science is not action research’, since ‘action research is clearly centered on discovery through action y design science is clearly centered on discovery through design’. Winter (2008) presents a strong and differentiating view of DS as a reflective cousin of Design Research, rather than an interventionist approach such as AR.

This paper is motivated by experiences in the use of various and varied approaches to both AR and DS. We question whether it is possible to conduct research that can satisfy the mechanics of both AR and DS, while remaining true to a philosophical orientation. Put more succinctly:

Is it possible to conduct research that satisfies the evaluative criteria of both AR and DS?

We are not proposing that research should be conducted so that it satisfies both; our reasoning is that if research can satisfy both criteria then distinctions between the two approaches may be no more than a differing set of semantics, perhaps reinforced by differing communities. Instead, we rely on the premise that researchers attempt ing to select an appropriate research approach should be aware of their conformance or otherwise. We conduct our debate through the presentation of a research intervention in the area of legal workflows and Digital Signatures in eGovernment undertaken with the Catalan Certification Authority (CATCert) in Spain. The core objective of this work was to provide a capability for ensuring that a Digital Signature conforms to the necessary legal restrictions relating to particular uses for specific documents in a process-driven workflow.

As we draw on this case, we take a pragmatic stance in this paper, noting and discussing differences and similarities especially with regard to four issues: (1) the role of the artefact, (2) the structuring of the process, particularly with respect to the research cycle and development methods, (3) the focus of evaluation of the intervention and the research, and (4) the emphasis on learning and knowledge. These issues emerged post-hoc from our reflection on our intervention, but to aid presentation in this paper we consider these before we introduce the intervention.

In the next section we consider the AR vs DS debate further. We then briefly introduce the work with CATCert, covering the background to the intervention including the restrictions placed on the research team, and note the outcomes of the intervention. Following this, we focus on our four issues, framing our consideration of research approaches both within our intervention and more broadly within the discourse of research methods. After this, we consider the choice of AR vs DS, the possibilities of pluralist or combined approaches, and our conclusions.

## Action research vs design science

It also covers the generation of theory and selection of methods. Cole et al (2005) present a coherent argument that suggests that AR and DS are epistemologically and ontologically similar. AR is arguably rooted in constructionism (the creation of a mental model that aids understanding of the world), whereas DS exhibits a pragmatism (a concern for practical outcomes) that harks back to the philosophy of Dewey. Table 1 also suggests that they are axiologically similar, since both purport to improve practice in addition to their research aims. In terms of theory, both are highly contextual, but generalisation to theory may be possible, often through multiple interventions (AR) or a transferable design or reusable artefact (DS).

In terms of method, most researchers would consider both AR and DS to be post-positivist, but there are writings that suggest, for example, that a positivist hypothesis testing stance can be embedded in AR (DeLuca et al, 2008) or that a formal Design Research method (which moves from idea through prototype to fitness-for-purpose) can be at the heart of DS. It is also worth noting international differences, with AR in Europe almost always contextual, interpretive and less concerned with generalisation, whereas suggestions for a positivist approach and efforts to effect generalisability originate in North America. Irrespective of this, both AR and DS have seen a move towards the creation of principles aimed to help the researcher use research methods that are rigorous within the context of the intervention (see, for example, Davison et al, 2004, on AR or Kuechler & Vaishnavi, 2008, on DS).

Many researchers will draw upon varied approaches and methods, combining them as necessary. We can identify three approaches to combining methods in interventionist research. In a pluralist approach, methods are varied based upon context. In a review of published AR research, Chiasson et al (2009) show that while many studies have AR as the dominant research approach, other research methods may be included ‘to examine and explain research questions’. For example, a large AR project may allow for data collection methods, such as experimentation, to be embedded within the larger project.

Second, researchers may opt for a multi-methodology approach where from the outset multiple approaches or methods are built into the research effort (Mingers, 2001). These can be enacted in parallel, or one might be dominant. Chiasson et al (2009) identify the sequential use of methods as common in AR within IS, such that field work, for example, might precede AR, or AR might be a precursor to a more focused piece of research using another approach.

A third effort can be termed the production of metaapproaches, where the meta-approach contains elements of both AR and DS, or even other approaches. Sein et al (2011) have built an extended approach to DS that draws upon the action and organisational aspects of AR, appropriately enough called Action Design Research (ADR). ADR is in part driven by a desire to better integrate DS views on design with AR views on evaluation, moving away from a limiting software development approach to DS that fails to capture organisational aspects of the intervention. Baskerville et al (2009) covers similar ground, using Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) as a way to combine AR and DS into a ‘soft’ version of DS that combines AR concepts of ‘change’ with the DS focus on an ‘artefact’. They note that all designs have to be ultimately acknowledged as a form of ‘organisational action’.

These efforts represent relevant options for interventionist research that we consider as part of our conclusions. For now we return to our four issues on the role of the artefact, the research process, evaluation of the intervention and the research, and the role of learning and knowledge. As mentioned, these issues emerged from our reflections on the intervention at CATCert and they will serve to frame our analysis and discussion of contributions.

## The role of the artefact

Typically, for the practitioner, AR leads to improved practice or solved problems. For the researcher, observations may be made that lead to specific insights that inform knowledge, or to generalisations within or across AR interventions (see ‘Generation of theory’ in Table 1). Knowledge is constructed from the rich set of data, and the interpretation thereof, emerging from the intervention, building upon the theory that is brought to bear. DS, on the other hand, specifies that the same must be achieved through an artefact. The artefact may embody the knowledge such that it is reusable or (better still) transferable to other contexts (March & Smith, 1995).

Following from this, AR’s philosophy implies that AR was successful if an improved practice was observed, whereas DS’ philosophy implies that DS was successful if an artefact improved practice. This suggests that in DS, the use of an artefact to effect change is key, whereas in AR the change does not require an artefact at all; it is the mere result that is key. This has been observed by others; for example, Henfridsson (2005) has made the same observation when he investigated six AR publications and realised that the IT-artefact was ‘basically never a significant part of the contribution’ (ibid.), leading him to conclude that ‘the IT-artefact has a marginal role in IS AR’ (ibid.). Conversely, it is possible that researchers that embark on AR, when finding themselves needing to produce one or more artefacts, tacitly move to a research approach more akin to DS or that even becomes labeled as DS. The role of the artefact, and its position within the intervention and relationship to practice, is thus central to our reflection.

## Structuring of the process

Both AR and DS see the research intervention go through multiple iterations of stages or steps within an overall cycle. This might be referred to as an ‘action’ cycle within AR or a ‘design’ cycle within DS. As noted in the introduction, the stages within the cycle are often given a fairly simplistic naming convention for reasons of mutual understanding with practitioners. Cycles can be highly iterative in both AR and DS. Further, in both AS and DR the intervention is likely to be sustained over an appropriate temporal period and level of collaboration.

Sustaining both is liable to the usual difficulties in interventionist research, where collaborators may lose interest or even withdraw.

In terms of how the research is managed, AR is clearly a ‘broad church’, enveloping varied approaches to research cycles. Davison et al (2004) review various types of AR from a process perspective, and note that (at least in theory) AR can be linear, short term and with limited practitioner participation. They argue coherently for canonical AR that is ‘iterative, rigorous and collaborative (ibid.). McKay & Marshall (2001) note that the ‘problem solving interest cycle’ in AR can be viewed separately from the ‘research interest cycle’, suggesting that each can be managed as a different (albeit connected and informing) set of steps within the overall process.

In DS it is possible that the use of a systems development methodology will colour the management of the research cycles. DS is closely associated with systems development, and can be considered a modernisation of calls to allow systems development as a recognised method for IS research (Nunamaker et al, 1991). Use of existing systems development methods, or attempts to employ methods that are novel within the collaborating organisation, may limit the research. It may be, for example, that systems development freezes the research at the point of prototyping, or makes it more difficult to recycle through the various research stages. How the process is structured, and how this interacts with development methods, is thus part of our reflection.

## The focus of evaluation

According to Gray (2009), the epistemological position of evaluation in interventionist research is actually a combination of constructivism and pragmatism. That is, a model of reality is constructed, but it has to be developed and (perhaps) reduced so that collaborators can agree (or at least agree to disagree) on the success or otherwise of the intervention. Moreover, the phrase evaluation is used inconsistently within interventionist research. Often used to denote the evaluation of the research cycle so as to inform the next cycle, it can be used by practitioners to refer to the overall assessment of the value of the engagement with researchers, and used by researchers to assess the value of the engagement to the generation of new knowledge. Thus, although one might expect AR and DS to draw on the same methods for evaluation, what is being evaluated is often not clear. For clarity, we will use ‘evaluation-step’ to refer to the in-cycle evaluation of the intervention, ‘evaluation’ as defined by Gray (2009) to refer to the success of the work within its context, and ‘research evaluation’ to refer to the a formal evaluation performed by researchers.

Within AR, each evaluation-step within a research cycle contains feedback from the stakeholders and the analysis of the implemented intervention (Baskerville, 1999). Since the evaluation-step informs the planningstep in an AR project, the input from the collaborator provides the researcher with the necessary data to be informed of the next planning and implementation steps. McNiff & Whitehead (2009) suggest that each evaluation-step in a research cycle must evaluate whether there is ‘evidence of improvement’, whether ‘the solution actually solves the problem’ or whether a new plan is ‘clearly specified’ or required. To summarise, in AR, a final evaluation of the project is not a formal necessity since the researchers have completed this already through the repeated evaluation steps in the AR cycle. However, some integration of these evaluation-steps, and a research evaluation of the intervention, is not unusual.

DS, subtly, suggests a more technical and distinct approach to evaluation and research evaluation. That is, Peffers et al (2007) and other’s efforts to bring more rigour to DS, have evaluation as a formal component, rather than evaluation-steps within the research cycle. They suggest that user acceptance, testing, and other artefactoriented evaluations may be necessary. March & Smith (1995) stress that evaluation must answer ‘why and how the artefact worked or did not work within its environment’, whilst Peffers et al (2007) place evaluation in a six-step model of DS, stating that research ‘may’ have to iterate back to previous activities if the developed artefacts are not of sufficient quality and efficacy. Sein et al (2011) note that this can enforce a ‘design and then build’ mentality into the research process. Pragmatically, in DS the evaluation of the artefact and research evaluation may be distinct activities that consume research resources. In AR the evaluation is embedded within the research cycle and resourced within the overall effort. Thus how evaluation is undertaken, and how it interacts with the research process and the artefact, is part of our reflection.

## The emphasis on learning and knowledge

Cole et al (2005) posit that ‘the common philosophy that DS and AR share is pragmatism’, which is a common trait of interventionist research (although we argue above that AR owes more to constructionism). As part of this pragmatism, researchers and collaborators learn about their problem and its solution, while researchers learn about technology, organisational structures or other varied aspects of IS. This can be generalised to produce knowledge through whichever research methods accompany the research approach (again, see Table 1).

We would argue that DS has largely confused learning with knowledge. Ja¨rvinen (2007) uses the terms almost interchangeably, suggesting that AR ‘produces knowledge to guide practice’ and DS ‘produces design knowledge’. Kuechler & Vaishnavi (2008) note that theory can be seen as accumulated knowledge, while defining DS as ‘learning through the act of building’. van Aken’s (2005) view of DS as a research approach applicable to a broad range of management research focuses on actionable knowledge that produces field tested and grounded technological rules. Hevner’s (2007) suggestion for a rigour cycle in DS suggests ‘creative insights’ as an output from the intervention.

AR (at least in Europe) has its roots in Action Learning. This was founded by Revans and others in the 1940s, and formalised in the 1950s. Hence within IS, AR has largely emerged from what Rapoport (1970) calls the Operational Research (OR) view of AR, which draws on Action Learning to bring together technical approaches and organisational methods with a view to problem solution through shared learning; what is occasionally referred to as a less technical and more humanist variant of OR. (This is in contrast to the development of AR in the U.S. as a more social oriented method dating back to Lewin.) The output of much AR is therefore learning. Revans (1983) suggests that a key aspect of this learning is that communities, within and beyond the research, learn from the interventions. Davison et al (2004) argue for reflective learning as a key component of AR.

Evaluation (in all its forms) is often confused with learning. Following Revans (1983), we view reflective learning as a broader community activity that produces actionable knowledge – knowledge that practitioners can carry forward into their work, and knowledge for researchers about interventions and how to enact them in addition to the informing of scientific knowledge. Evaluation addresses ‘what happened?’ while learning addresses ‘what knowledge can we develop from what happened?’ It is possible that the need to develop the artefact can become a race to design and build rather than a formative approach that shapes the learning by those involved in the research, and thus this needs to be part of our reflection.

## The intervention with CATCert

To be able to be more specific in the AR vs DS debate, we now turn to our research undertaken with CATCert. In 2007, the authors were approached by CATCert in Spain to help create an improved method of creating Digital Signature Policies (DSPs). CATCert were exploring the use of DSPs as an electronic document validation tool for Catalonia’s new eGovernment infrastructure. The research problem posed was: Can a method be produced that allows legislation to be converted into valid and adherent digital signature policies in a precise and repeatable manner?

Digital Signatures, in combination with Digital Certificates, can provide a statement of who digitally signed a document (Hazari, 2002), ‘proving’ that a document received by a party is the same document as was sent by the sender (Broderick et al, 2001). In many government transactions, electronic documents similar to their paper counterparts need to adhere to legislation that governs the information contained in the document, as well as any approvals or authorisations that are related to the government process that the document relates to. Legislation that controls the use of documents needs to be updated when documents ‘become digital’ so as to provide guidelines and rules for how Digital Signatures are to be applied, how secure they must be, how long they are valid for, and what type of Digital Certificates may be used.

Within Europe, the ETSI TR 102 038 standard has been developed (European Telecommunications Standards Institute, 2002) to define the concept and makeup of a DSP. This is operationalised as an electronic document that uses the extensible mark-up language (XML) to capture the legal requirements and constraints of a Digital Signature. The general aim of a DSP is to define all rules and meanings of all the signatures that can be found in a particular electronic document that is used in a particular context. Thus, a signature policy can be used to verify whether the Digital Signature and Digital Certificate for a specific digital document comply with the applicable legislation that governs the use of a particular electronic document for a specific transaction.

A major difficulty with using DSPs as the electronic equivalent of the relevant legislation is that legislation is created and exists in natural language. Turning natural language legislation into machine-readable DSPs is typically a lengthy and complicated process that involves elements of trial-and-error, requires extensive testing by both technical and legal personnel, and is therefore difficult to produce for each and every legislative act.

## Actions and outcomes within the intervention

In the initial problem diagnosis stage of the intervention, we worked with CATCert to define a range of requirements that had to be met by a potential IS. A series of meetings took place in which we used SSM thinking (see Winter et al, 1995, on SSM and links with development and methods) to elicit initial requirements. Only after this did the AR approach emerge as the means of intervention.

Four initial requirements emerged from an extended diagnosis stage. First, it was agreed that the solution must be capable of representing legal acts and processes that combine to form one uniform legal workflow. A legal workflow will usually comprise a combination of several legal acts and processes that interconnect activity between different legal acts. This view of activity within a legal workflow should therefore allow the depiction of the sequence of legal acts and processes that combine to form the actions of the legal workflow. Second, any solution must be capable of showing how individual legal acts affect legal resources, such as Digital Signatures and documents. That is, Digital Signatures and documents must be able to be depicted as individual and separate entities in any representative model. Thus, it must be possible to see how a Digital Signature and its legal requirements are shaped by which legal act and how this affects a specific document in a legal workflow. Third, for each legal act in a legal workflow, as well as for each document and digital signature used, it must be possible to submit a large number of associated (attribute) data. The make-up of this attribute data is determined by the requirements of the standard that the DSP is meant to adhere to and will make up the bulk of the DSP. Fourth, it must be possible to view how individual signatures or documents are passed through a series of acts and processes.

In an action planning phase the project considered the resources and tools necessary to proceed. Only when engaged in action taking did another key requirement emerge, implying a need to recycle back to diagnosis. CATCert decided to mandate the use of a prototype method developed by them called PADS (Processes/Acts/ Documents/Signatures). This was a key event within the intervention. PADS is a method that was aimed at satisfying most of the above four requirements using a paper-based template, but failed to do so. Its manifestation was through the use of a lengthy questionnaire aimed at helping an administrator extract the requirements of a piece of legalisation as described in natural language. Using these ‘captured’ requirements and printed copies of the ETSI DSP standard, the DSP would be drafted by hand using the legal information elicited in the PADS questionnaire. This method was not suitable in a production environment for several reasons. First, it required the administrator to be well versed in both extracting legal information from a drafted piece of legislation, and in creating documents in XML. Second, notwithstanding the complications concerning procedural aspects of the flow of information with respect to a legal act, it was not always clear how this information could be drafted as part of a DSP, if at all. Finally, the questionnaires were discarded after use, which meant that the organisation lost specific knowledge for recreating or updating existing policies.

CATCert decided to insist on PADS being part of the ‘new’ solution as they hoped to reduce the complexity of the transition between the ‘old’ and ‘new’ method so that administrators could be trained quickly on the new method. Despite being made aware of recent automated legal information extraction research, they decided that a more conservative approach would present less of a risk.

We followed our action planning phase with a number of action planning, action taking, evaluation, and learning steps, in which we iteratively designed the graphical elements, the metadata model and the behaviour of both the graphical elements and the data model within its own self-contained environment. As initially the collaborators lacked a clear understanding of the problem and the technology, we built a number of prototypes and descriptions, which we used to kick-start discussions with the collaborators. Using their feedback, we were able to learn more about the intended use of the solution and could thus adapt the artefacts to suit their needs. In terms of data, we focused almost exclusively on semi-structured interviews, using prototypes to engage in discussions about visibility, visual design, usage and so forth. It became clear that the four initial requirements identified in our initial diagnosis stage were not sufficient to describe the future stage, and that we had to continuously use evaluation steps to ascertain whether our work was sufficiently adapted to the needs of the client organisation.

As the intervention continued, actions focused on development of both a method to capture the data from PADS, and to display and manipulate the workflow. What emerged was a workflow notation, incorporating both the ETSI TR 102 038 standard and the PADS method. Our intervention resulted in the physical artefacts of a graphical modelling notation, a questionnaire and a software tool that generates XML from the notation. We also obtained an overall method that prescribes how the developed instantiations are to be used in order to yield valid DSPs.

Following what in retrospect can be perceived as an evaluation-step, CATCert pushed for a ‘graphical tool’. This was the second key event in the intervention. They saw the development of a graphical tool as, for them, the key change. They were also very cognizant of the need to produce XML compliant with the ETSI standard, and together with the decision to produce a graphical tool their view of the intervention became artefact-focused.

The subsequent workflow notation was based on previous work by the researchers aimed at developing a process modelling notation specific to the eGovernment domain (Seltsikas & Palkovits, 2006), which had been adapted for the DSP problem domain. CATCert had also been involved in this research. Following an evaluationstep, it was recognised that there was other contextual information that cannot be easily captured with a graphical workflow alone, and therefore the notation was extended to capture the properties of the concepts that record a variety of contextual and legal information. In order to ensure that this information is correctly handled across all aspects of the solution, as part of this evaluation we performed a gap-analysis with CATCert to compare the data provided by the notation’s predecessor (the PADS questionnaire) and the data required for a complete ETSI TR 102 038 compliant DSP. The graphical notation was then designed in such a way that it could capture all data that had been collected by the PADS questionnaire, as well as data that were required by the ETSI standard. The final result was that our notation included three concepts (Acts, Signatures & Documents), each of which is equipped with a range of properties aimed at satisfying the data requirements of the ETSI DSP standard. We captured the data and the relationship between the concepts and their properties in a metamodel of the notation.

Evaluating the work from the perspective of the collaborators, we achieved a significant practical result in providing CATCert with tools that allow administrators to convert legal text into both a business process model (using our notation), and automatically into syntactically valid and standards-compliant DSPs. Alamillo et al (2007) considers the technical validity and generalisability of the work. CATCert have learnt how to progress efficient production of DSPs from legislative acts expressed in natural language.

From our perspective, a key output from the research is not so much the artefacts produced, as the learning associated with their development. Thus, we can claim that we have learnt how to develop the workflows and tools necessary to produce DSPs. As a more specific example of learning, much discussion took place around the definitions of Citizen, Administrative Worker and Administrative Authority, i.e., definitions of these had to be made more formal over the course of the project. As an adjunct to this work, CATCert, having learnt of the importance of getting key definitions established and agreed, created a schema to formalise the definitions.

As discussed in the introduction, our intervention with CATCert was constructed and planned using AR. However, as the project progressed we considered if our approach was perhaps more in line with the approach of DS. Our reflection became formalised as we started to write up the work; we now turn to this reflection. We evaluate the intervention against developed criteria for AR and DS, which allows us to answer the research question. We consider specifically the four issues of (1) the role of the artefact, (2) the structuring of the process, particularly with respect to the research cycle and development methods, (3) the focus of evaluation of the intervention and the research, and (4) the emphasis on learning and knowledge.

We developed evaluative criteria for DS using the work of Peffers et al (2007) and the criteria presented by Hevner et al (2004). Table 2 compares our intervention to the developed criteria. We have ordered the table to follow Peffers structured method, and then align this against Hevner’s criteria where there is some overlap. Superficially, we can present our work as DS as it appears to meet many of these criteria. To be more precise, we can determine actions that we took that are within the realm of DS methods, and make post-hoc justifications of these actions with regard to methods.

We also evaluated our work against AR criteria. We developed evaluative criteria for AR using the general principles outlined by Baskerville & Myers (2004) and McNiff & Whitehead (2009), while also considering the stricter guidelines presented by DeLuca et al (2008). ## Revisiting the four issues

We now consider the issues in more detail. Beyond the existence of methods and tools, we could argue that the main artefact in our intervention is the overall method that creates DSPs using these physical artefacts, i.e., we have created a meta-method that can be transferred (perhaps with local instantiations) into other workflow situations where DSPs must be created. As we focused on creating an improved method of developing DSPs, it may be judged that our research’s primary aim was the improvement of organisational practice, not the creation of specific artefacts. It meets the criteria of ‘Socially situated’ and ‘Relevant’ for AR (see Table 3), but also meets the DS criteria (see Table 2) for both ‘Design as an artefact’ and ‘Design and development’. We suggest that although the production of an artefact might indicate that research should be DS rather than AR, it does not dictate this.

A distinctive feature of our research process was the occurrence of an apparent dual-research iteration loop. That is, while the physical IS artefacts (modelling notation and graphical transformation tool) underwent several iterative AR cycles, there were at least two iterations that covered the complete method of creating DSPs, and caused changes in all physical artefacts. We would therefore argue that our progression of research was very much in line with AR, such that we iterated through cycles of investigation related to specific aspects of the intervention. We undertook ‘Practical action in the problem setting’ (see Table 3). However, if the intervention had been conceived as DS we do not think the research cycles would have proceeded too differently. In fact, as noted above, CATCert became keen to view the work as production of artefacts (especially software tools), and less keen to revisit key decisions about these. They would have perhaps been willing to go along with a ‘Design and development’ followed by ‘Demonstration’ approach, as per Peffers and Table 2.

Within our intervention, the progression from understanding, through notation and design through to the XML meta-model and the resulting transformation tool can be considered as an ad-hoc development method, not dissimilar from any small software project. Moreover, the requirements of funded research, reporting mechanisms and deadlines will lead to a formalism that makes AR more structured than might be desirable (what Avison et al, 2001, call ‘formal AR’). Any production of a software artefact will require systems development. Our reflection on this is that key events can freeze decisions within the work. These decisions have to be taken within software development for progress to be made, and will be firmer where very structured systems development methods are used, but they might also occur in areas of AR where there is no artefact. Moreover, such ‘freezing’ can occur when the artefact is any part of the interdependent methods, models, constructs and software. Hjalmarsson et al (2010), for example, consider the use of AR to help unfreeze various barriers that emerge within the DS process.

With regard to evaluation, we used AR-style evaluationsteps, and struggled to engage CATCert in any formal ‘Design evaluation’ stage (a DS criteria in Table 2). Further development of the methods and artefacts could have led to more formal user testing, providing some evaluation that is design rather than action oriented. Our project records show that some collaborators in CATCert were very keen to generate what might be called ‘use cases’ and treat evaluation primarily as software testing. In the closing phases of the project, they were keen to pursue a validation of the correct functioning of the modelling tool and the software tool generating the DSPs, instead of focusing on the correctness of the overall method and any possible generalisation of it, or any further investigations into procedural or organisational changes required to better accommodate the new tools. This tension is likely to exist whenever software developers engage in collaboration. We suggest that more formal evaluation of the intervention, such as software testing, is liable to be a function of the artefact within its context, and a successful project (AR or DS) that creates such artefacts will lead the practitioners to focus on what needs to be further done to get the artefacts embedded in the organisation and seriously used. Conversely, research evaluation will be led by the researchers. As McKay & Marshall (2001) note, those interested in solving the problem may take separate steps and actions from those interested in research outcomes.

Turning to learning, the outputs from our research can be viewed as both supporting practice through the design of artefacts (the overall method that creates DSPs) and an improvement of practice through the intervention, resulting in actionable knowledge. Using Mathiassen’s (2002) Collaborative Practice Research imperatives we (a) ‘improved practice’ in that CATCert is now better placed to produce DSPs, and we (b) ‘learnt about what it takes to improve practice in this area’, as particularly evidenced by one specific outcome – an identification of the need to develop a proper schema around definitions of Citizen, Administrative Worker and Administrative Authority. But imperative (c), the building of some new knowledge is, as noted in Table 3, unclear. By Chiasson et al’s (2009) classification, our project was more ‘problem-solving dominant’ than ‘research dominant’. However, use of literature and concepts from the domain of DSP is rigorous (although not covered in this paper), and a generalisable method for the construction of DSPs was developed, suggesting we addressed ‘rigour in many ways’.

Generated knowledge such as the creation of a new schema may meet the AR criteria of ‘Informing theory (see Table 3) or the DS criteria of a ‘Research contribution (see Table 2). However, the AR criteria to ‘Generalize the theoretical contribution’ was certainly not met in our work, and this appears to be a stricter criteria than the DS requirements for research and communication. Then again, creating knowledge that is generalisable and transferable outside of the context in which it is gained (i.e., it has some external validity) and thus contributing to theory is never trivial in interventionist research that achieves results of relevance to practitioners.

Returning to what we identified as efforts to combine and deploy mixed interventionist research approaches and methods (pluralism, multi-methodology and metaapproaches), our reflection suggests that our intervention with CATCert might be considered as pluralist. That is, it started with some SSM to elicit initial requirements, embarked upon AR, and then became more akin to DS as requirements and project objectives became firmer. Chiasson et al (2009) would have perhaps classified our project as a sequential use of methods within AR, with SSM as a method to elicit requirements and systems development as a formal method to deliver the graphical method and transformation tool.

## Conclusions

In concluding, we start with some general observations, and then address our four issues. We then return to our research question, before considering the choices open to IS researchers that engage in interventionist research.

Current writing that indicates strong similarities between AR and DS often takes an epistemological stance. Table 1 also suggests an ontological and axiological concordance. Yet, remaining true to a philosophical intervention is more than an epistemological stance. Due to the very pragmatic nature of any intervention, good researchers will find that collaborators wish to impose methods, tools and techniques that require their stance to be, at the very least, tweaked to meet the situation. Moreover both AR and DS will meet with required formalisms imposed by project funders, line management, and even journal editors.

The artefact has a role to play in both AR and DS, but in AR the artefact is normally the by-product of the research intervention, not the goal of that intervention. A DS intervention can only be a success if an organisational practice or human state is changed through the use of rigorously developed artefacts. On the other hand, an AR intervention can only be a success if organisational change has been effected and the research and evaluation focused on the details of organisational acceptance of revised processes or artefacts – not a design evaluation and assessment of the actual artefact per se. Thus, where AR produces an artefact as a (shall we say) second order effect, which is evaluated as a piece of design in addition to the organisational change, or where DS leads to second order organisational changes, then the criteria of both AR and DS approaches may both be satisfied. Writers such as Cole et al (2005) and Peffers et al (2007) are correct in seeing a conformance between AR and DS, but suggestions that they are ‘complementary or alternative’ ways to design artefacts are too simple.

The structuring of the research process in both AR and DS is often achieved by reference to a simple set of steps, in AR often using canonical AR, and in DS often referring to Hevner et al (2004) or some derivation thereof. However, as shown in our reflection against evaluative criteria for both AR and DS, any particular intervention will make compromises and be stronger in some parts of the process than others. This certainly appears to be the case in both Tables 2 and 3, where our reflections show varied conformance to the developed criteria. Moreover, we suggest that consideration of the interaction between the problem solving interest cycle, the research interest cycle (McKay & Marshall, 2001), and how evaluation interacts with each is more fundamental and important than any ‘methodology’.

The role of evaluation in the two approaches can be considerably different. Even if artefacts are produced by both AR and DS, the mechanics of AR are only really satisfied if evaluation occurs within the research cycles (what we call evaluation-steps in the above). Design evaluation of the artefact within DS is likely to be more design focused and occur after the design has reached a certain level of sophistication, allowing software testing and validation to take place. However, the necessary adoption of a means of systems development could structure evaluation in both AR and DS to look similar.

We suggest that wherever an artefact is constructed, a race to design and build can take preference over the creation and sharing of knowledge by those involved in the research. Using AR may diminish an unwelcome focus on building artefacts, but it may not overcome it. It is thus no surprise that ADR (Sein et al, 2011) gives considerable attention to reflection and learning, realising that reflection, learning and generalisation are all important and related aspects of a research intervention. AR’s approach to learning is more sophisticated than DS, especially when structured as a form of Action Learning, but this may be due to maturity rather than fundamental differences.

Is it possible to conduct research that satisfies the evaluative criteria of both AR and DS? The simple answer must be yes. However, we would suggest that how and why IS research is evaluated against the requirements of both AR and DS is an issue of overlap and pragmatism. If evaluation in AR is based upon what has been learnt, and evaluation of DS is based upon the creation of knowledge embodied in an artefact, then a piece of research must be able to determine both learning and generated knowledge to satisfy both AR and DS. There are no doubt situations where researchers and collaborators learn something about actions and change, while also creating knowledge about technical solutions or organisational change. But, as noted by writers on both sides of the debate (e.g., Cole et al, 2005, Baskerville et al, 2009, Sein et al, 2011), there are also situations where learning from action or knowledge generated from an artefact will dominate.

Finally, we would conclude that as a less mature approach, DS is perhaps overly prescriptive. Its recent appeal may be part of this prescriptive nature, with simple methodologies such as Peffers et al (2007) appearing to differ little from similar methods that focus on design in, for example, OR (O’Keefe, 1995). We would suggest that DS would benefit from some of the reflective thinking more prevalent in AR (such as Davison et al, 2004, and Chiasson et al, 2009) and less prescription.

What do our reflections and conclusions mean for the future of research methods? We conclude with three suggestions for the future use of interventionist research in IS. First, where researchers wish to make a firm choice of an established research approach, we suggest that those embarking on AR (where an artefact might be

present) or DS should be cognizant of the other approach. That is, they should be able to defend their choice and understand the requirements of labelling their research as AR or DS. We would agree with Baskerville & Wood-Harper (1998) that the more defined a research approach and the more carefully managed, then the stronger the claim for validity. To support this first stage, we have produced the questions in Table 4 (which combines our experience from this research and our reflection with the criteria from Tables 2 and 3). These will help in determining the research approach and how it is enacted in a particular context, guiding the required pragmatism. The Table is not designed to portray AR or DS as ideal approaches in specific contexts, where one must be selected, but more to provide guidance where both are being considered.
