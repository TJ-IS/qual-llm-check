# Web performance scale

Ming-Hui Huang

Department of Information Management, National Taiwan University, Taipei 106, Taiwan

Received 1 April 2004; accepted 20 June 2004

Available online 8 December 2004

## Abstract

What does a high performance website look like? And how can we measure its performance? The present research develops and validates a new scale that measures Web performance as perceived by consumers. This scale discerns the formation of Web performance into the hedonic and utilitarian aspects reflecting Web users’ entertainment and information needs. Content domains for the two aspects were developed based on marketing and information systems literatures, and then tested for reliability and validity in two studies involving a total of 912 Web users. The measure was purified through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses and MTMM models, resulting in one three-item scale for hedonic (reliability = 0.867) and one four-item scale for utilitarian (reliability = 0.850) Web performance. The scale thus developed not only has face validity, but also displays adequate construct reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and nomological validity. It achieved reliability and validity using only seven items for the two aspects of Web performance making it highly feasible for implementation and particularly appealing to Web managers for tracking and improving site performance. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Hedonic; Utilitarian; Web performance; Technology acceptance model; Flow

## 1. Introduction

Understanding the needs of Web users and being able to capture their feelings and opinions towards the performance of a website are essential for market success. Previous measures of Web performance have been designed primarily to assess site efficiency rather than consumer evaluation, such as server logs, consumed time per page or visit, sales transactions, and Web traffic. These non-consumer-based measures may fail to capture more meaningful indicators of online behavior such as consumers’ cognitive and attitudinal evaluations [31]. Spiliopoulou [37] argued for the importance of subjective evaluation. Because perception is a human characteristic, she concerned that not all recorded activities are appropriate for studying how users perceive a site.

Web users not only visit a website to search for specific information to solve problems, but also surf for entertainment, stimulation, and socializing [21]. A review of recent literature indicates that there is probably more to this variable than overall performance as captured by a unidimensional global evaluation. For example, several recent studies have demonstrated that perceived usefulness a primary and perceived fun a secondary determinant of computer technology acceptance [22,23]. In this paper, building upon both marketing and IS literature, a reliable and valid measurement of subjective Web performance that incorporates the hedonic and utilitarian aspects is developed. Such disaggregation in the dimensions of Web performance and integration with IS characteristics will permit the study of the possibly differing effects of each of these Web performance components, which is critical for tracking and improving site performance.

The next section presents the theoretical foundations of the two evaluative aspects guiding this study, which draws on marketing, IS, and Web literature. Each of these conceptual streams is briefly discussed. The following two sections report the two studies in developing and validating the proposed scale, including the assessment of convergent and discriminant validity through the MTMM approach. The final section discusses the results of both studies and their implications for research and practice.

## 2. Hedonic and utilitarian web performance

Web performance was used here to refer to Web users’ subjective evaluative judgment toward a particular website. It is website success or failure evaluation criterion. Web performance is the key outcome variable in Web experiences that a website can provide to consumers. Behavioral outcomes, such as technology acceptance or computer usage, are a function of performance evaluation.

The two aspects identified for Web performance are hedonic and utilitarian. In the physical market, this duality of evaluation has been well articulated [6,8]. In the virtual market, the two aspects are more commonly conceptualized as motivational determinants of Web performance [14,24].

The hedonic aspect of Web performance is the positive evaluative outcome of a website, according to Web users’ assessment of how much fun, playfulness, and pleasure they experience or anticipate from the site visit. Visiting a website, with or without online transaction, can generate hedonic responses.

Alternatively, the utilitarian aspect of Web performance is the positive evaluative outcome of a website, according to Web users’ assessment about the instrumental benefits derived from its functional attributes. Utilitarian performance results from Web users visiting the site out of necessity rather than recreation. It depends on whether the particular navigating need is accomplished.

## 3. Theoretical foundations

The theoretical foundations of the two aspects of Web performance is indicated by several diverse lines of research. The following review two theories in the IS domain and two marketing theories concerning the two aspects of performance. The two IS theories view the two aspects as motivations, while the marketing theories see the two aspects as evaluations.

## 3.1. The extended technology acceptance model (TAM)

The original TAM model relates to the utilitarian aspect only, but its later extension incorporates the hedonic motive. Davis [10] advocated this model where perceived usefulness was proposed to be a fundamental determinant of user acceptance of information technology. The perceived usefulness scale developed in the study has been replicated in various settings, including electronic mail, voice mail, word processing, spreadsheets, and graphics [1]. Davis et al. [11] extended this model to include perceived enjoyment as an additional motivational determinant of system acceptance. They demonstrated that the degree of enjoyment people experienced in using the computer per se influenced their intentions to use computers in the workplace.

## 3.2. The theory of flow

The importance of assessing the hedonic aspect of Web performance is supported by Csikszentmihalyi’s [9] motivational theory of flow. According to Webster and Martocchio [42], flow is concerned with the subjective experiences of enjoyment during playful interactions with tasks. Flow is conceptualized as an intrinsically enjoyable experience, and is operationalized as the enjoyment or the intrinsic interest of human–computer interactions. Ghani and Deshpande [18] found that flow is closely related to user satisfaction and the acceptance of information technology. Watson et al. [41] pointed out that websites that entertain visitors with games have the potential to generate experiential flow, which creates or enforces excitement. In an investigation of optimal flow experience in Web navigation, Novak et al. [32] considered that the state of mind arising as a result of achieving flow on the Web is extremely gratifying.

## 3.3. The ad attitude theory

The ad attitude theory from marketing literature is relevant to both aspects of Web performance. Ad attitude is used as an indicator of advertising effectiveness and is defined as a ‘‘predisposition to respond in a favorable or unfavorable manner to a particular advertising stimulus during a particular exposure situation’’ [26].

Extending the concept of ad attitude to website attitude, Singh and Dalal [36] proposed that Web home pages can be viewed as ads and these home pages can be evaluated using the conventional attitude measures such as attitude toward the home page (i.e., ad attitudes) and attitude toward the sponsor of the home page (i.e., brand attitudes). Two studies provided empirical evidence for such application. Chen and Wells [7] argued that while it is important to know a site’s overall attitude score, it is also important to know the underlying perceptual dimensions that contribute to and account for that evaluation. They developed a three-factor attitude toward the site scale reflecting the dimensions of entertainment, informativeness, and organization. Stevenson et al. [39] considered that attitude-toward-the-website is worth including in studies of websites. They found that as attitude-toward-the-website improved so did attitudetoward-the-ad, brand attitude, and purchase intention. Balabanis and Reynolds [5] drew items from measures of ad attitudes to assess attitudes toward a website, and found the need to remove several items in order to achieve an acceptable level of fit.

## 3.4. The shopping value perspective

Research on the shopping value also suggests that shopping can provide both hedonic and utilitarian value through responses evoked during the experience.

Value is ‘‘all factors, both qualitative and quantitative, subjective and objective that make up the complete shopping experience’’ [44]. Value in this form is considered the key outcome variable in a general model of consumption experiences. Thus, a shopping experience could evoke value either through successfully accomplishing its intended goal (e.g., the utilitarian value) or by providing enjoyment and/or fun (e.g., the hedonic value). Babin et al. [3] recognized that shopping value is related intimately to hedonic responses as well as other more tangible consequences. They developed a scale assessing consumers’ evaluations of a shopping experience along the two important dimensions: utilitarian and hedonic value. Ducoffe [12] demonstrated that advertising on the Web can provide value to consumers. He found that both information and entertainment are benefits users derived from Web advertising. Web advertising not only should be able to provide users with product information so that purchases yielding the greatest possible satisfaction can be made, but also should fulfill users’ needs for escapism, diversion, aesthetic enjoyment, or emotional release.

## 4. Scale development

The accumulated body of knowledge regarding technology acceptance, flow, ad attitude, and shopping value provides theoretical support for the distinction of the two aspects of Web performance. The next section describes the process used to establish the content of each aspect and validate the scale psychometrically and theoretically. A psychometric approach is adopted in the development of the Web performance scale. Fung et al. [17] argued that user requirements and feedback on an information system may not always be easily observed or categorically captured; instead some of them may be latent and have to be extracted through carefully designed human sensors. The psychometric approach can be used to measure the subjective Web performance level as perceived by the users with known degree of reliability and validity.

## 4.1. Generation of scale items

Bipolar semantic differential items were developed based on the argument that both the hedonic and utilitarian aspects are bipolar in nature and vary in magnitude [4]. For example, hedonic aspect includes both painful and unpleasant feelings; while utilitarian aspect includes judgments about foolishness and irrationality as well as wisdom and rationality. Bipolar rating scales patterned after the ‘‘evaluative’’ factor of the semantic differential [34] has been commonly used in measuring consumer attitudes [30].

An encompassing list of 42 evaluative semantic differential scale items was collected based on pilot interviews with Web users and through a review of relevant IS and marketing literature. Items such as pleasant–unpleasant, enjoyable–unenjoyable, interesting–boring, and entertaining–weary were used to assess the hedonic aspect, whereas items such as useful–useless, wise–foolish, and reliable–unreliable were used to capture the utilitarian aspect.

## 4.2. The development sample

Two hundred and forty three graduate and undergraduate students at a large university served as the development sample. Effective sample size was 229. Their responses were used to purify the measures and provide an initial examination of the scale’s psychometric properties. A little over half of the respondents were male (59.3%). Their ages ranged from 18 to 51, with the average being 21.76. They tended to spend more than 5 hours on the Web per week (53.7%), and were more likely to navigate the Web both for work and entertainment (75.3%).

## 4.3. The procedure

The 42 scale items were administrated to the development sample. They were instructed to rate all scale items on the basis of their most frequently visited website. This approach covers all types of websites, an important requirement for a general Web performance scale. It discounts automatically the effects of familiarity and meaningfulness because respondents own Web experiences provide the benchmark of the responses. The websites that they most frequently visited fell primarily into the categories Entertainment (19.2%), News and Media (18.5%), Business and Economy (16.6%), Reference (11.3%), and Computers and Internet (9.9%).

## 4.4. Scale purification

The scale items were analyzed using principal component exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation to examine their dimensionality. The sharp decrease in eigenvalues after the second factor suggests the predominance of 2 factors in the data with 22 items loaded on the utilitarian and 20 items loaded on the hedonic factors. Only three hedonic items had factor loadings lower than 0.50, which were dropped from further analysis. To reduce redundancy, expert interviews were then conducted to assess the semantic content of the items. Those items that best fit the definition of the two aspects of Web performance were retained, yielding 22 items with 11 items representing the two aspects, respectively. Factored this reduced set of items obtained a clear two-factor solution with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. The application of Cattell’s scree test confirmed eigenvalues became homogeneous after the second factor. This 22-item two-factor solution revealed a hedonic factor (48.1 percent) and a utilitarian factor (12.1 percent). All corrected item-total correlation for the two factors exceeded 0.60. The factor solution is presented in Table 1.

## 4.5. Convergent and discriminant validity

The 22 items were subjected to a series of twofactor confirmatory factor analyses to examine more closely the scale’s psychometric properties. During each iteration, the compound item (loaded on both factors, as suggested by the modification index) with the lowest squared multiple correlation coefficient (individual item reliability) was dropped. In an attempt to carry forth enough items to the validation study to ensure valid construct measurement, and because items would be subjected to additional purification analyses in the validation study, the iteration procedure stopped at the point when all the items remained in the model were sufficiently significant and the overall model fit were acceptable.

The final model consisted of seven items for each aspect and produced a chi-square of 229.54 $( \mathrm { d . f . } = 7 6 ,$ $P = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , a goodness-of-fit statistic (GFI) of 0.87, a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) of 0.09, a normed fit index (NFI) of 0.85, and a comparative fit index (CFI) of 0.89. All t-values for loading estimates were significant $( P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and were larger than 6.0. Likewise, the estimates of reliability for the hedonic (0.876) and utilitarian (0.833) factors are high, with both values sufficiently greater than 0.75, the cut-off value recommended by Nunnally [33]. The variance extracted estimates reflect the amount of variance captured by a measure relative to random measurement error; a level of 0.50 or above has been advocated for these estimates. This level was achieved for the hedonic factor (0.50) and fell slightly below for the utilitarian factor (0.42). Discriminant validity in this two-factor solution is evident in that the variance extracted estimates for each of the two factors are greater than the square of the parameter estimate between them $( \phi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 6 3 )$ [16]. ## 5. Scale validation

## 5.1. The validation sample

The validation sample was used to validate the scale’s psychometric properties obtained from the development sample. Announcements for the study were made in class to undergraduate and graduate students with majors in a variety of fields from a large university. Students were invited to participate and were encouraged to look for potential respondents who are representative of the demographic of Web users. Remuneration of \$ 5 was offered. Six hundred and sixty nine Web users participated in the validation study. Effective sample size was 583. Males (49.3%) and females (50.7%) were approximately equally represented. Respondents ages ranged from 12 to 57, with an average of 23.30 (S.D. = 6.84). A total of 66.1% of respondents reported 2 years or more of Web use experience and 39.1% reported 5 h or more of Web use each week. The respondents were more likely to navigate the Web for entertainment (35.4%), gathering information for personal needs (14.5%), wasting time (13.0%), or education (10.7%).

## 5.2. The procedure

The data collection procedure employed in the development study relied on the memory of Web users. The validation procedure remedied this problem by asking respondents to navigate two randomly assigned websites belonging to the same product category. Eight websites were selected to represent four major product categories conducting business on the Web, including books, computers and peripherals, and clothes. They are the online booksellers Amazon.com and Barnesandnoble.com; the fashion clothing venders Landsend.com and Gap.com; the computer hardware sites Dell.com and Compaq.com; and the software sites Netscape.com and Microsoft.com. Respondents were asked to visit the two assigned websites through their own computers in familiar environments. They were instructed to visit the sites for as long as they liked and using their usual method of navigation. When they finished the visit and exited from the sites, they were asked to rate the 14-item Web performance scale.

## 5.3. Additional purification

The purification process resulted in seven items representing the hedonic and utilitarian aspects of Web performance, respectively. To better reflect the conceptual definition of hedonic performance, the item ‘‘fun-frustrating’’ was added based on the extended TAM model and Web attitude study. The 15 items were then analyzed through confirmatory factor analysis for convergent and discriminant validity [4,25]. All squared multiple correlation coefficients exceeded 0.50, except for the item ‘‘effective–ineffective’ (0.26). However, the hedonic and utilitarian factors appeared to lack discriminant validity, with the squared of the estimated correlation between the two factors $( \phi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 8 3 )$ well exceeded the variance extracted in each factor (0.60 and 0.52). The fit of this two-factor model was marginal: x<sup>2</sup>(89) = 580.19 (P < 0.000), RMSEA = 0.10, RMR = 0.08, GFI = 0.88, AGFI = 0.84, NFI = 0.91, CFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.93.

## 5.4. The alternative model

The additional item purification process aimed at increasing the discriminant validity of the two aspects of Web performance. However, it was at the cost of dropping valid items that might capture the overall aspect of Web performance. To test the alternative hypothesis that there were three factors underlying the Web performance, a three-factor model was fitted treating the dropped items as the indicators of the overall Web performance. A similar iteration procedure was undertaken and the final model retained four reliable items. The right column of Table 3 reports the solution of this three-factor model.

It showed that this model fitted reasonably well. All squared multiple correlation coefficients exceeded 0.50. The average variance extracted was 0.69 for the hedonic, 0.59 for the utilitarian, and 0.62 for the overall factors, all above the minimum of 0.50. Composite construct reliabilities were high, 0.87 for the hedonic, 0.85 for the utilitarian, and 0.87 for the overall factor. As expected, the hedonic and the overall factors, and the utilitarian and the overall factors appeared to lack discriminant validity. This suggests that the overall factor captured both the hedonic and the utilitarian aspects.

## 5.5. MTMM models for convergent and discriminant validity

Five MultiTrait–MultiMethod models were compared treating the 10-item two-factor attitudes to the Web Likert scale as the alternative measure for Web performance. The matrices for the baseline model $\mathbf { M } _ { 1 }$ were constructed following the procedure described by Marsh and Grayson [27]. First, the two traits, hedonic $( \xi _ { 1 } )$ and utilitarian (j<sub>2</sub>) $( T = 2 )$ were measured by both semantic differential scale $( \xi _ { 3 } )$ and Likert scale $\left( \xi _ { 4 } \right) \left( M = 2 \right)$ . Second, $T \times M$ observed indicators were used to infer $T + M$ a priori factors. Third, each observed indicator loaded on one trait factor and one method factor only. Fourth, correlations between trait factors and between method factors were estimated without constraints but correlations between trait and method factors were fixed to be zero. Fifth, the uniqueness of each scale was also estimated with constraints but assumed to be uncorrelated with the uniqueness of other scales.

Four alternative models were estimated and were compared with the baseline model to examine the effects of trait and method factors on the two types of Web performance scales. $\mathbf { M } _ { 1 }$ is the baseline model that assumes both trait and method effects for the two types of scales. $\mathbf { M } _ { 2 }$ is the trait effect model assuming no method effect and $\mathbf { M } _ { 3 }$ is the method effect model assuming no trait effect for the semantic differential scale. Similarly, constraining either the method or trait effect to zero, we can form $\mathrm { { M } } _ { 4 } ,$ , the trait effect model, and $\mathbf { M } _ { 5 } ,$ , the method effect model, for the Likert scale. The four constrained models $( \mathbf { M } _ { 2 }  – \mathbf { M } _ { 5 } )$ can thus be compared with the unconstrained model $\mathbf { \left( M _ { 1 } \right) }$ respectively to test the superiority of the semantic differential and Likert scales in measuring the two aspects of Web performance. The difference in chi-square estimates approximates a chi-square variable that tests whether there is a significant improvement in fit for the less restrictive model.

The first criterion construct is flow. According to past studies, flow results in positive mood and satisfaction in human and computer interaction. Flow can result from experiential and utilitarian behaviors, but more clearly relates to the consequences of pleasant subjective experiences. Nomologically, if these two Web performance factors are indeed measuring what they should theoretically, the hedonic aspect should relate more strongly to flow. A structural equation model is specified to examine the relationship between the two aspects of Web performance and flow. As expected, hedonic performance was the only aspect that had a significant positive impact on flow $( \gamma = 0 . 2 0 , t = 2 . 3 7 )$

Second, longer average time spent on a site was found to be a measure associated with experiential visit to the site. Miller [29] claimed that Web users navigating for hedonic benefits view the navigation process as an end in itself and a valid use of time, whereas those who are seeking specific information will not waste time on surfing as long as their goals have been achieved. Consistent with this line of thought, the correlation between site dwell time duration and the hedonic aspect of Web performance was more manifest (r = 0.20, P = 0.000) than the correlation between site dwell time duration and utilitarian performance (r = 0.17, P = 0.000).

A third criterion variable assesses the overall performance of the site. It asks respondents to compare the visited site with other sites along the 7-point scale ranging from ‘‘one of the worst’’ to ‘‘one of the best’’. Sites performed better in the two aspects should received higher rating in terms of this overall performance scale. As expected, the pattern of correlations between the overall performance and the two aspects of Web performance were consistent with this prediction. Both the utilitarian $( r = 0 . 4 8 ,$ $P = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ and the hedonic (r = 0.55, P = 0.000) performance were related significantly to the overall performance scale. Thus, the two aspects of Web performance not only possess convergent and discriminant validity, but also measure what they are supposed to, thus possessing nomological validity.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Conclusions

Through a rigorous scale development procedure and incorporating Web characteristics, the proposed scale communicates effectively to Web managers and makes the proposed scale empirically relevant. The two studies reported here involving more than 900 participants. The results indicate that evaluative responses toward websites have two discriminable aspects, hedonic and utilitarian. The diagnostic value of partitioning the Web performance along hedonic versus utilitarian lines can be enhanced by the proposed scale.

The Web performance scale achieved both convergent and discriminant validity through an MTMM examination. The proposed scale is grounded in both IS and marketing theories and provides conceptual links to the literature. It also demonstrated nomological validity in the two aspects of Web performance related to flow, site dwell time duration, and overall site evaluation as theoretically predicted. Clearly, sites that score high on the Web performance scale attracted consumers to stay longer and to enjoy the site more. In the post-Internet bubble era, websites that score high on the two aspects of Web performance are expected to be long-term winners.

The Web performance scale has good psychometric properties and yet can be easily administered. It consists of two dimensions that can be captured using seven items. To allow assessing the full spectrum of Web performance researchers and managers may wish to utilize the 11-item three-factor scale that includes the overall performance factor.

## 6.2. Future research

The following implications for information studies are brought out by the current findings. First, the relationship between the two aspects of Web performance merits further investigation. The two aspects were found in the two studies to be discriminable but significantly positively correlated. Two possible directions of associations have been suggested. This view implies that the two aspects are equally and simultaneously important in meeting Web users’ needs. The second is to treat them as two consequential, causally related stages of Web performance, with the occurrence of one leading to the other. This view suggests that the hedonic performance is an extension of the utilitarian performance; therefore, utilitarian performance can bring about hedonic performance, contributing to total satisfaction.

Second, the reliability coefficients were at 0.87 and 0.85 for hedonic and utilitarian performance, respectively, both being higher than those of general IS research. The development of reliable scales represents a useful starting point for improving the quality of IS research; nevertheless, additional testing is needed. In the development study, Web users’ most frequently visited sites were sampled. In the validation study, eight websites represent the four major product categories conducting business on the Web were sampled. The sampling frame captures the successful sites and the findings demonstrated the importance for websites to be both hedonic and utilitarian. Further studies can be undertaken to validate the proposed scale in a wider range of situations and using broader categories of websites.

Third, a clearer distinction would need to be made between emotions and evaluations in refining Web performance measure. Some items that are used to measure hedonic evaluations are more commonly seen and used for gauging emotions, such as happy, sad, pleased, and irritating. For example, Mehrabian and Russell’s [28] pleasure–arousal–dominance (PAD) emotional scale is the most influential scale for the measurement of emotional experience in the consumer research literature [13,20], and its application has been extended to the Web environment in Novak et al.’s study. The item ‘‘excited’’ in this scale belongs to the dimension of arousal and is considered to be high in pleasure and arousal [15].

## 6.3. Managerial implications

The findings of this study provide the following implications for Web managers to assess Web performance. First, the proposed scale captures the characteristics of specific Web factors, tailoring to the need of measuring Web performance. It uses items incorporating Web characteristics, such as safe–risky, fun–frustrating, providing a relevant assessment for Web applications and helps to determine the aspects for their success or failure. Managers should not only use the scale to measure the perceived performance of their sites, but also to benchmark their site’s performance against competing sites.

Second, the proposed scale achieves reliability and validity using only seven items for the two aspects of Web performance making it highly feasible for implementation and particularly appealing to Web managers. It provides a reliable and valid measure for the subjective aspect of Web applications. From the end-users’ perspective, returns from Web applications are often subjective, and such subjective utility cannot be converted to monetary value without a proper subjective measure. For example, Turban et al. [40] stated that the benefits generated by a Web project include both quantifiable and non-quantifiable items. Although the cost of resources, such as hardware, software, and human resources, for a Web project is relatively easy to quantify, the returns (benefits) are difficult to measure, as seen in many other information technology projects. As an attempt to this issue, this study develops an evaluative measure for Web performance, taping into the traditionally non-quantifiable aspect of Web performance. It helps to assess the competitive advantage of Web applications and the benefits generated by Web projects.

Third, according to the current findings, managers assessing the performance of Web applications should attempt to measure both hedonic and utilitarian aspects, and explore empirically possible implications of each aspect to develop attribute, preference, and behavior models for use in managerial decisions. Consistent with Web users’ evaluative duality, Web application providers can design websites to deliver either hedonic or utilitarian value, or both. Designers of websites should therefore be aware of the type of appeal–rational, experiential, or mixed-that would be most effective in attracting their target users.

## 6.4. Contributions

The Web performance scale is a psychometrically rigorous instrument that measures the perceived performance of a website. This simple scale is reliable, valid, multidimensional, and parsimonious. It will benefit website designers and researchers, and may be used to evaluate and track site performance. The current evidence suggests that the hedonic aspect can be most validly measured by the semantic differential items of fun–frustrating, enjoyable– unenjoyable, and interesting–boring; the utilitarian aspect by items such as safe–risky, ordered–chaotic, wise–foolish, and reliable–unreliable; and the overall aspect by items such as useful–useless, pleasant– unpleasant, entertaining–weary, and nice–awful. These results thus provide empirical support to theoretical ideas that have appeared earlier in the literature, and suggest measurement scales for future research.
