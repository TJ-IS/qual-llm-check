OPINION PIECE

# Managing as designing: ‘opportunity knocks’ for the IS field?

David Wastell

Nottingham University Business School, Nottingham, U.K.

Correspondence: David Wastell, Nottingham University Business School, Wollaton Road, Nottingham NG8 1BB, UK. Tel: 078105 22749; E-mail: david.wastell@nottingham.ac.uk

Accepted: 24 April 2010

## Abstract

In response to anxieties about our discipline’s decline, this opinion piece ‘looks to the future’, arguing a strong prospectus for the IS field, based on its direct relevance to general management practice. The thesis set out is inspired by the recent upsurge in interest in ‘managing as designing’. Reformulating management as design, I argue that we have a formidable knowledge-base of tools, theories and critique relevant to any manager, not just the MIS-inclined specialist. A case study of yet another failed information system (in U.K. children’s services) is presented to show just how uniquely relevant our discipline is, in the workplace of today. Other pertinent issues are explored, such as synergies with the growing vogue for evidence-based management. The paper concludes by considering implications for research, including the need for a less exclusive approach to publishing and dissemination emphasising the need to make our work more accessible to lay practitioners. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 422–431. doi:10.1057/ejis.2010.31; published online 1 June 2010

Keywords: information systems; managing as designing; evidence-based management; IS failure; child welfare; design science

## Prologue

When it was known in Manchester that Mr. Drinkwater had engaged me, a mere boy without experience, to take the entire direction of this new mill, the leading people thought he had lost his senses y I at once determined to do the best I could and began to examine the outline and detail of what was in progress. I looked grave, inspected everything very minutely, examined the drawings and the calculations of the machinery. I was in with the first in the morning, and I locked up the premises at night. I continued this silent inspection for six weeks y and during that period I did not give one direct order about anything. But at the end of that time I felt myself so much master of my position, as to be ready to give directions in every department.

The epigraph is from the autobiography of Robert Owen, management pioneer, successful entrepreneur and social reformer of the early factory age (see Owen, 1967). At the callow age of 18 (having left the family home in the Welsh marches to seek fame and fortune 10 years previously, in 1778) Owen had audaciously applied to manage a new mill being established in Manchester, a mill employing the latest manufacturing technology of the day. How he proceeded as an untutored manager in a sticky predicament is instructive; ‘ethnographically’ we’d call it in today’s terms. And the strategy paid off handsomely. Owen apparently pulled off, again using the modern argot, a spectacular piece of ‘re-engineering’:

I soon perceived the defects in the various processes and improved the quality of our manufacture. In about a year, I had y gained the means to increase the

fineness of the finished thread from 120 to upwards of 300 hanks in the pound. I was now known as the first fine cotton spinner in the world.

Radical change indeed and a fascinating story to boot, but no mere historical curiosity. In this paper, I wish to make a case for our discipline, whose decline is much lamented, by addressing its direct relevance to management practice. That we are needed more than ever shall be my riposte to the Jeremiahs. I will proceed parabolically. Two true stories will underpin my thesis. Owen furnishes our first moral tale, and I have said all that needs to be said in this pithy opening. In the next section, I will recount my second narrative at somewhat greater length. It is a modern, altogether more convoluted affair, with a less happy denouement. Having set out both, I shall then juxtapose and contrast the two tales to complete the parable and put forward my case.

## The tragicomedy of the Integrated Children’s System (ICS)

In the Punch-and-Judy show of this century, there are no responsible men. It is always, ‘We couldn’t help it’ and ‘We didn’t really want that to happen’. Everything is dragged along in the sweep of events. But the tragic is still possible even if pure tragedy is not y We can bring it forth as a frightening moment, as an abyss which opens suddenly. (Duerrenmatt, Problems of the Theatre, 1955)

In early 2006, in concert with academic social work colleagues at the Universities of Huddersfield and Cardiff (and now Lancaster), ESRC funding for a project on frontline decision-making in child welfare was gained. The original focus of the work was on patterns of error and blame in the context of the ubiquitous imposition of ‘performance management’ in the U.K. public sector (Bevan & Hood, 2006). The potential conflict, between the targets and indicators of the new managerialism and the traditional imperatives of the professional task, formed our primary interest. In the course of our ethnographic field-work, we soon became aware of the disruptive impact on professional practice of a national computer system (the Integrated Children’s System – ICS). The results of our research on the ‘failure’ of the ICS have been published in a series of papers in social work and social policy journals (viz. Broadhurst et al., 2009; Wastell et al., 2010; White et al., forthcoming) and these papers should be consulted for detailed method and findings. Suffice it to say that this work generated a substantial body of evidence (from five local authorities in England and Wales) showing all too clearly the dysfunctional impact of the ICS on child protection practice. In this paper, I will attempt to summarise the main outlines of this work for an IS audience.

As a major national initiative, ICS is well worthy of investigation. In the context of public sector reform, where technology features so centrally as the instrument of ‘modernisation’ (so-called ‘e-Government’ – Irani et al., 2007), we believed that the problems inherent in the ICS were unlikely to be unique and that there were important general lessons to be learned. There are 150 social services departments in England and Wales, with the total children’s services workforce numbering over 30,000 professional staff. With such a multitude of users, getting the design right is absolutely critical, and getting it wrong, a failure of profound consequence, demanding careful analysis.

ICS was originally (mis)conceived in 2000 (White et al., forthcoming). It is probably best seen as an instrument of policy reform aimed at making fundamental change to the way social workers practice, in order to afford ‘at risk’ children greater protection and support. Deconstructing its design rationale, we may descry two ‘figures in the carpet’. The first is a formally defined, measurable ‘standard process’. The imposition of this through software enables workers to be monitored and held to account for their performance against defined targets. The second core element is a comprehensive assessment framework for ‘Children in Need and their Families’ (Cleaver et al., 2008). The latter is operationalised in a highly prescriptive and detailed set of electronic recording forms (the exemplars). The policy aspiration seems to have been that, by embedding and enacting these innovations electronically, practice would be re-configured and professional rigour enhanced, thus making children safer and pre-empting tragic deaths, such as that of Victoria Climbie (Laming, 2003). Such appalling incidents periodically punctuate the conscience of the Nation, evoking moral panic, political embarrassment and, inevitably, the clamour for ‘something to be done’.

Since its conception, the development of the ICS evolved over a number of years, before ‘roll out’ (a rather ironic term for a system which does anything but roll!) on a national basis from early 2007 onwards. Although there is an espoused emphasis through its development on piloting and staff involvement, our research (White et al., forthcoming) found that, in reality, its design has been highly centralised, led by a small cadre of senior civil servants (now in the Department for Children, Schools and Families, the DCSF) and expert advisers. Input from social work practitioners has been very limited, and feedback from the field has had minimal impact on design; oft-heard are tales of requests to simplify the exemplars, for instance, going routinely ignored. The following quote is typical, from an ICS project manager:

The main message was that the forms were too complicated. We spend a lot of time making the forms more user friendly. At that stage it wasn’t clear that there was to be no negotiation, that the forms couldn’t be changed. This caused a lot of disappointment - staff thought they were shaping things.

Throughout its ill-starred life, adverse reactions to the ICS have been consistently dismissed as ‘teething problems’ rather than symptoms of fundamental design flaws (see Cleaver et al., 2008). When things go wrong, unreliable IT platforms, inefficient local authorities, or ‘confused practitioners’ are always to blame, never the ICS (White et al., forthcoming). The following response is typical. Reporting on negative feedback from a pilot study, the system’s architects concluded that ‘the findings suggest that practitioners’ resentment to the change owes much to unresolved problems with IT systems and the unfamiliarity with new systems’ (Cleaver et al., 2008, p. 177). In contrast, an independent evaluation of the ICS was highly critical; its conclusion was stark: ‘ICS has not shown it is fit for purpose. Its problems must be addressed’ (Bell, 2008). But the DCSF took no action to review or adapt the ICS; their response, published in early 2008 (DCSF, 2008, pp. 2–3), set aside the findings as follows:

[The evaluation] was based upon participating local authorities’ commitment to implement the ICS fully within a seven-month period. There were, however, significant delays in the implementation process. By the conclusion the ICS had only been fully implemented in one of the sites. It was [thus] not possible for all the aims and objectives of the evaluation to be achieved y [therefore] the research does not provide a sound basis on which to judge the potential value of the ICS. Instead the study provides an informed assessment of the challenges which need to be overcome if this potential is to be realised.

As a display of ‘oracular reasoning’ (Mehan, 1999), in which all data are interpreted as consistent with an a priori major premise, this is a tour de force. Not only are the vicissitudes of the ICS reinterpreted as implementation challenges, but these same tribulations are invoked to undermine the evaluation itself; an incorrigible but fatally deluded position. Further indications of ICS’s deep-seated troubles were reflected in press reports of industrial unrest, such as the following from the Unison trade union to which many social workers belong:

UNISON wishes to draw attention to the seriousness of the problems being experienced by social work staff with the Integrated Children’s System y we have reports of a number of industrial disputes or collective grievances brewing and in many more cases staff are voting with their feet and not using the system when they can get away with it. (Unison, 2008)

Returning to our research, we were especially interested in the ‘front-door’ of statutory children’s services, as ‘referral and assessment’ practices have been singled out for particular attention in the enthusiasm for ‘e-reform’. Initial assessment (IA) emphasises the importance of the first professional response, and the ICS imposes a highly prescriptive workflow over this initial phase, leaving behind an indelible, electronic trace for audit purposes. A standardised IA form prompts workers to collect information in a systematic way, and a range of targets and associated timescales have also been stipulated. Within 1 day of a referral being received, social service departments are mandated to make a decision about the requisite response. When an IA is deemed necessary, this must be completed within 7 days. irrespective of the complexities of the case and the opportunities for investigation.

In several papers, we drew attention to the unsafe work practices produced by these inviolable time-scales, which recall the dysfunctional ‘computer workarounds’ well known in the IS literature (e.g. Azard & King, 2008). Although there were variations across sites, we found a number of distinct commonalities. In one paper, we dwelt on the array of risky improvisations and short cuts engendered by the ICS, at all stages of the IA process (Broadhurst et al., 2009). Two quotations will suffice as illustrations:

Team leader: Being a bit cheeky y we contacted the health visitor and said when did you last see the child and lucky enough the health visitor had seen the baby recently and it wasn’t as bad as the grandmother had alleged y so we didn’t take it any further, no further action

Social worker: If it’s not looking that serious y sometimes you don’t get all the information and the temptation is then to take a short-cut and maybe not contact the school, you say I think I’ve got sufficient information to make a decision - no further action.

The assessment records themselves constituted another area of difficulty. The IA is a long and complex, multisectioned form, requiring copious information that is difficult to glean from one home visit and from other professionals. Unsurprisingly, users had become experts with the ‘copy-and-paste’ function, as material was routinely and mechanistically repeated. We also found that an expedient method of ‘front and back-ing’ had spontaneously sprung up across all our sites, wherein middle sections of the document were omitted altogether.

In the above-mentioned paper (submitted to the British Journal of Social Work, BJSW, in the summer of 2008) we gave voice to our profound concern that, far from increasing child safety, the ICS has paradoxically put children at greater risk. Its ‘electronic cage’ had produced a range of unsafe working practices: premature case categorisations, short-cuts, and so on. Professional discretion had been seriously curbed. A further paper (Wastell et al., 2010) focused on patterns of professional resistance which had grown up in response to the totalising regime inflicted by the ICS. Drawing on Fleming & Sewell (2002), we used the term S<sup>ˇ</sup>vejkism (inspired by the eponymous hero of Hasˇek’s comic novel, The Good Soldier S<sup>ˇ</sup>vejk) to designate a distinctive form of passive ‘resistance’ based on excessive obedience. S<sup>ˇ</sup>vejkism relies on comic effect to destabilise managerial power by revealing the absurdities of the organisational regime. The following example has been taken from an interview with two social workers in a London borough. The ‘five outcomes’ mentioned are the overarching welfare aspirations of government policy regarding children and families, as set out the in the iconic Every Child Matters policy paper (DfES, 2004).

First social worker. The five outcomes came in and instead of being creative they just stuck it on the end of the form y [both laugh derisively] y and they just appeared. You don’t have an analysis with your recommendations, your recommendations are the five outcomes [more laughter] y you write the 5 outcomes, strengths, weaknesses and risks under each thing, and then your analysis and recommendations are strengths, weaknesses and risks without a box for saying why y

While the BJSW paper was under review, major developments supervened: the ‘abyss opened’ with the conclusion of the ‘Baby P’ trial in November 2008. Baby P was a 17-month-old child, brutally murdered in the London Borough of Haringey over a year earlier. The trial of the child’s mother, boyfriend and another man was widely reported in the British press and caused considerable moral outrage. Some press reports drew attention to the deficiencies of the ICS and the role it had played. If ever the time was ripe for action, this was it, and our mouse roared! The same week we wrote a long article for the Guardian (a leading national newspaper) entitled ‘Computers can hinder child protection’. It was published in online form, with a pre´cis in the printed newspaper. Considerable press interest in our research followed, fuelled by further press releases from the Team, some of a rather inflammatory nature it has to be said! At the time of our final report to the ESRC in the summer of 2009, we tallied over 30 pieces of press coverage, including radio interviews, letters, mentions in articles etc. The BJSW article itself received expedited publication and was made freely available; it has attracted over 5000 downloads, at the last count.

Following our media campaign, an overture was made to the DCSF in late December 2008. The olive branch was accepted and we presented our findings to relevant civil servants early in the new year. As a result, the Team’s principal investigator (Professor Sue White) was invited to join the Social Work Task Force, a body set up by the Government to review all aspects of social work training and practice in response to the Baby P outcry. The urgent need to review the design of the ICS was designated as a key priority for the Task Force, and Sue determinedly pressed the case for change. Six months into the deliberations of the Task Force, the failings of the ICS were finally acknowledged by its progenitors in the DCSF. A circular to local authorities announced that a fundamental review of its design was to be launched, foreshadowed in a report published in June (DCSF, 2009). The report had a notably reluctant tone, but dawn seemed finally to be dawning!

[T]he Task Force highlights a number of current problems with the local implementation of ICS systems and says these have been exacerbated by overly prescriptive national requirements. The Task Force makes a series of recommendations which, it believes, will rapidly have a positive impact on how frontline workers experience ICS. The Task Force says its proposed changes will enhance the positive elements of the system by making it more flexible and by supporting its record-keeping capacity, while stripping out other, unnecessary features.

The final report of the Task Force (published December 2009) proclaimed a more whole-hearted endorsement of the need for change (Gibb, 2009). It urged both the DCSF and local authorities ‘to continue to improve the ICS by y making ICS more straightforward and effective for frontline social workers in children’s services’. It is early days, but there are optimistic signs that the new design spirit is paying off. In a working group set up by one of the suppliers (which we attended) a social worker observed that the participative approach had produced ‘very much simpler and easy to read forms based on a family approach’. Early feedback from staff using the redesigned forms in real situations is encouraging, as affirmed by comments from a senior manager at the same meeting:

This is a joy to read! I’ve spent far too long reading ICS assessments where I’m no clearer at the end of the document as I was at the beginning what the issues were, whereas this was so easy to read and understand. I really think we are on the right track.

## (Re)enter the manager-as-designer

Design rules! Design is about passion, emotion and attachment, and it must be at the heart of every business y Design thinking and systems thinking are one and the same. In great design, form and function come together seamlessly. Every part contributes to the whole in a way that seems inevitable. So too in a great system. (Tom Peters, 2005).

Having set out our two stories, one situated at the inception of management as a profession, the other over two hundred years later, it is time to decrypt the parable. What is done well in the first vignette, which is done so badly in the second? The Greeks have a word for it, not the usual one, though hubris certainly looms large in the fiasco of the ICS. The word I have in mind is sw-edio (schedio), modern Greek for design, a word whose etymology is fascinatingly discussed by Terzidis (2007). Owen’s performance as a designer is a tour de force. Simon (1969) famously defines design as ‘courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones’. How striking is this ‘design orientation’ manifest in Owen’s proceedings. In his role as manager, he clearly sees his pre-eminent goal as the design of the workplace, to find the best way of doing things by harnessing the new technology of the mill to its fullest potential. But to intervene effectively, Owen also saw that he must first thoroughly understand the organisation he was freshly steward of; such an understanding was a sine qua non. Nowadays we appreciate the importance of ‘ethnography’ in design, Owen knew it instinctively. The contrast with the ICS narrative is stark; as an exercise in how not to design, it is a cautionary tale to end all such tales. I shall say more of this shortly.

Owen is not alone among the management pioneers in seeing his primary responsibility as design. Many of the early managers were engineers, who naturally saw the design of the organisation as their prime concern. First published over a century ago, Taylor’s Shop Management (1903) is, from cover to cover, a book quintessentially about design, in both senses of the word, as noun and verb. Regarding the former, a set of normative principles for the design of the workplace is systematically adumbrated; concepts such as the first class man, functional foremen, the planning department. Taylor also speaks to the methodology of design, with the ‘Time Study’ being the outstanding example. Although the subject of much opprobrious comment, the Time Study’s insistence on rigorous empiricism (Taylor gives page after page of meticulous ‘how to’ instructions) reflects a very clear position on how design work should be done, that is, that it should be grounded on systematic direct observation rather than fashion, prejudice or ideology. Today we’d call it a design methodology, and Taylor, like him or loathe him, an evidence-based manager par excellence.

But what has all this to do with our discipline? It is time to come to the point. In our existential travails, lack of relevance to practice is often invoked (Kaplan et al., 2004). In this we are not alone; the management academy as a whole is wracked with kindred angst (Starkey & Madan, 2001). Martin comments: ‘business schools are under intense criticism and have reached a point of crisis. Both academics and management practitioners criticise MBA programmes for their lack of relevance to practitioners’ (Dunne & Martin, 2006, p. 512). And the remedy? For Dunne, the call for reform is answered by nothing less than a fundamental reformulation of management education, predicated on a ‘new’ concept of managerial practice:

we are on the cusp of a design revolution in business y today’s business people don’t need to understand designers better, they need to become designers. (ibid, p. 513)

Not so new, as we have seen, but nonetheless an important opportunity for our beleaguered discipline. If the resurrection of design is to provide the ground for the rehabilitation of management education, then we have a central part to play, as leading characters not bit parts. More than any other management discipline, we know about design, a strong claim, but one to which I will return. Happily, IS scholars are prominent in the design renaissance, paladins indeed. In 2004, Richard Boland and Frank Collopy convened an interdisciplinary colloquium at the Weatherhead School of Management to celebrate the design vision of Herb Simon. In advocating the case for design, Simon (1969) notably bemoaned the ascendancy of pure science in professional schools:

Design is the core of all professional training. Schools of engineering, architecture, business y medicine are all centrally concerned with the process of design y It is ironic that in this century the natural sciences have driven the sciences of the artificial from professional school curricula y Medical schools have become schools of biological sciences; business schools have become schools of finite mathematics.

The raison d’eˆtre of the colloquium was to proselytise the idea of ‘Managing as designing’, and its published proceedings (Boland & Collopy, 2004) constitute an important intellectual landmark. Boland has subsequently co-edited a special issue of Organisation Studies further championing the cause of design (Jelinek et al., 2008). In the opening chapter of the 2004 proceedings, Boland and Collopy contrast two paradigms of management practice and education. They characterise current orthodoxy as the decision attitude: ‘concerned with the various techniques and methods managers use in making choices, starting from the assumption that a good set of options is already available’. In contrast, they proclaim the design attitude in appealingly romantic-heroic terms:

A design attitude views each project as an opportunity to question basic assumptions, a resolve to leave the world a better place. Designers relish the lack of predetermined outcomes, the opportunity to go back to those assumptions that have become invisible and unnoticed, looking for the real thing we are trying to accomplish, unvarnished by years of organizational habit. A design attitude fosters a problemsolving process that remains liquid and open, celebrating path-creating ideas about new ways to use technology and new work processes y A good design solution solves many problems, often ones not envisioned in its development!

Although not one of Mintzberg’s (1973) celebrated clutch of managerial roles, designing is manifestly an integral part of the mundane practice of management. Arguably, ‘systems design’ is (should be) the manager’s primary task, construing system in its broadest sense as an organisational configuration of processes, people and technology constituted to accomplish a desired function. At whatever level, organising the work activities under his/her jurisdiction as efficiently as possible, for the general good of the enterprise, is surely the chief responsibility of the manager. Yet ‘design’ is not an appellation which managers spontaneously use to describe their work, any more than they are formally trained in the methods of design. Nor is design a term much in vogue in scholarly writings about the managerial role, and it has yet to gain currency among management researchers, though there are notable exceptions. Van Aken (2004, 2007) calls for management research to be based on a ‘design science’ paradigm, in order to redress ‘serious utilisation problems’. Romme (2003) makes a similar argument, also calling for more ‘action research’ to address the relevance gap. Such exhortations resonate with similar programmatic arguments within our field in favour of the design science ‘turn’ (e.g. Iivari, 2003). Two further special issues are also noteworthy. One of these addressed ‘Design and Organisational Change’ in Design Issues, winter 2008 (Buchanan, 2008); the second, devoted to the rejuvenation of Organisational Development through the design-based approach, was published in the Journal of Applied Behavioural Science (2007, volume 43, issue 1).

## Venceremos – towards victory!

And so, from hour to hour, we ripe and ripe,

And then from hour to hour, we rot and rot:

And thereby hangs a tale (As You Like IT, Act II, vii)

That our knowledge of design, as IS scholars, is compendious and stands ahead of the other management disciplines is a strong claim, as I have acknowledged. But it is a justified one. We can see this in the ICS case study. There is nothing new here from the perspective of our knowledge base, an ‘old story’ indeed. As IS onlookers we are bursting to interpret, comment, reprove and advise. It hard to hold back, hard not to play the obnoxious wiseacre! Another failed project, and all the critical failure factors are there: disengaged senior management, excluded users, weak project management, and so on. Blind faith ‘in IT as a magic bullet’ predominates (Markus & Benjamin, 1997) and, as we well know, those organisations ‘that mechanically insert IT into their businesses’ will not enjoy the hoped-for guerdon (Brown & Hagel, 2003). The ICS is a story of ‘escalating commitment’ if ever there were one. Many of Keil’s (1995) noxious ingredients are there. To name a few: a ‘large scale, longterm project promising high pay-off’, extreme emotional ‘over-attachment’ causing negative feedback to be reinterpreted, the reckless impulse to continue at all costs, and the general laxity of management controls. Keil offers his framework as a risk-management tool-kit for detecting and pre-empting escalation. It is hard not to see the ICS as bearing all the classic hallmarks of an escalating project, and to lament that it was not subject to the sort of critical review that Keil recommends. If only we had been there!

Other ideas from the eclectic knowledge-base of IS have helped inform the Research Team’s diagnosis of the failings of the ICS. Basic systems concepts have been particularly helpful, especially regarding the nature and management of error. Coining the notion of ‘latent conditions for error’, Reason (2000) argues that the analysis of errors in organisational settings should focus on general systemic weaknesses, rather than mistakes made by particular individuals on particular occasions. Through its imposition of a rigid work-flow and other infelicities, we have argued that the ICS has exacerbated latent error conditions (Broadhurst et al., 2009). Another core systems concept we have invoked is the Law of Requisite variety (Wastell et al., 2010). Pithily the Law proclaims ‘Only variety absorbs variety’. In systems terms, the curbing of professional discretion through electronically enacted administrative control has effected a reduction in requisite variety. Such discretion, as Lipsky’s (1980) concept of the ‘street level bureaucrat’ emphasises, is essential in order to get the job done in the public services, as the situations faced by workers ‘are too complex to reduce to prescribed responses’. The implications for safety are made stark in Weick’s (1987) classic paper on the design of ‘high reliability’ systems; failures inevitably occur when ‘the variety that exists in the system to be managed exceeds the variety in the people who must regulate it’.

And it is not mere critique that we, as IS scholars, have to offer. As a discipline, we do know how to design, what works and what does not. Quoting the BJSW paper (ibid):

Although this paper challenges the huge investment in systems of performance management and IT, we are not arguing for a wholesale Luddite abandonment of new technology. The remedy, we believe, lies elsewhere, in a radically different approach to design. Radical it would seem for present times, in the public services at least, though hardly ground-breaking; rather the reclaiming of an older design wisdom, sundered-off, ignored, or never encountered. Sociotechnical systems design (STSD) embodies much of what we have in mind. Core principles of STSD include: user participation, minimum critical specification and the optimization of local autonomy y Above all, it is essential to focus the design of systems on the needs of users, founded on a rigorous understanding of their working practices.

My case here is thus a simple one, a call-to-arms to jump aboard the management-as-design ‘bandwagon’ and take our rightful place in the vanguard; no ‘full sea perhaps but certainly a ‘current which serves’. All our existential angst is dissolved in a trice, our relevance to practice becomes incontestably obvious, for managers in general not just the IS specialist. For those of us in Business Schools, our identity and standing are transformed, no longer to be marginalised as technological outsiders. Idealistic perhaps, but surely there is a decisive opportunity here to be confidently seized. Our knowledge-base is certainly formidable, ranging from practical design methods and tools, knowledge of particular classes of artefact and their impact, knowledge of the design process itself and its potential dysfunctions. Our pluralism is also a source of strength; all research traditions fly under the IS flag. Long may it last! Hevner et al. (2004) distinguish between the behavioural science and the design science versions of our field, but both can unite around our knowledge of design and its relevance to management. As Hevner et al. write, the ultimate aim of behavioural theories is practical, to provide understanding of ‘the interactions among people, technology, and organizations that must be managed if an information system is to achieve its stated purpose, namely improving the effectiveness and efficiency of an organization’. In other words, design will be as much enhanced by the accumulation of social theory as by research located in the pure design science mode. In the words of Lewin’s much-repeated apothegm ‘there’s nothing so practical as a good theory’.

There are other developments afoot in management education that make this a propitious time for design, and for suturing the gap with practice. The recent vogue for evidence-based management (aping prior developments in other professional domains, medicine in particular) is what I have in mind (Pfeffer & Sutton, 2006; Wastell, 2006; Rousseau & McCarthy, 2007; Baskerville 2009). The synergies between evidence-based management (EBM) and design are potentially very strong, and there is a real sense in which EBM is paving the way for the design movement. When Pfeffer and Sutton talk of organisations as ‘unfinished prototypes’, and espouse the need for experimentation, this is ‘design talk’ par excellence. Design, I would argue, provides the natural domain for putting EBM into practice. Unlike the hurlyburly of everyday decision-making, design work typically takes place over a longer duration, allowing room for reflection and research. Effective design should always be informed by the best evidence. This should derive from two main sources. Local knowledge is critical, that is, the sort of systematic understanding of actual work practices which we see Owen so diligently seeking out. But there is also room for the evidence-based manager to look further afield, to study other organisations facing similar issues, and, above all, there is time and opportunity to consult the knowledge-base of management scholarship, including the fruits of our toil.

The design movement in management education is still at the fledgling stage. Being realistic, it is clear that a profound shift will be needed in the mind-set (metanoia) of both the Academy and in the sublunary world of Practice. Changing the managerial mind-set is a challenge. Managers often invoke the ‘day job’ when I speak of design. The following remark of an executive MBA student is typical: ‘Design is a good construct but hard to apply y someone is always breathing down your neck to get things done’. Others have commented in a similar vein. Reflecting on her teaching experiences at the London Business School in the mid-1990s, Van Stamm (2003) ruefully looks back:

I got involved in teaching ‘Tools for design minded managers’. The aim was to expose MBA students to some design techniques and practices on an experiential level y While the link is utterly obvious to me, it became clear that this was not shared by the students. One said – Bettina don’t worry, some people get it and some just don’t, whatever you do.

But if managers fail to take responsibility for design and if organisations fail to create the necessary mandates and rewards, then disasters such as the ICS will continue to occur, and the productivity paradox will persist unabated. Design is too important to leave by default to software vendors and consultants. Research has shown that the best and most innovative ideas do come from the middle layers of the organisation and the front line (Wastell et al., 2007), but the culture of the ‘learning organisation’ is needed for design to flourish; Senge himself speaks of the new role of the manager ‘as researcher and designer’ (Senge, 1990, p. 299).

If we can not reform the whole of management education, then we can at least rethink the way we teach

MIS, founding it squarely on the concept of management as design, emphasising the use of technology as the instrument of innovation, sans pareil. Tools and methodologies provide a particularly important way of influencing practice, as the EBM proponents have noted (Rousseau & McCarthy, 2007). Not only are IS researchers prolific as tool-makers, the tools we produce typically do not require technical knowledge beyond the reach of the motivated manager, and can be readily taught in short courses for instance. Soft systems methodology (Checkland, 1981) and Mumford’s socio-technical approach (Mumford, 2003) are two techniques which come immediately to mind. My own SPRINT methodology, for instance, has been designed for the general manager, and provides the foundation of my MIS teaching (Wastell et al., 2007).

## W(h)ither IS research: cathedral or bazaar?

He has been eight years on a project for extracting sunbeams out of cucumbers which were to be put into vials hermetically sealed, and let out to warm the air in raw inclement summers. Another Professor shewed me a large Paper for discovering Plots and Conspiracies. He advised great Statesmen to examine into the Dyet of all suspected Persons y [to] take a strict View of their Excrements. (Gulliver’s Travels, Voyage to Laputa)

In this final section, let us focus on research. Lest alarm be caused, making the case for design should not be seen as another unwelcome attempt to impose hegemonic order on the field; no such ‘supremacist tendency’ is intended and should certainly be resisted anyway. Conferences provide ‘epistemological experiments’, and in reflecting on an event I co-organised in 2007, the greater concern was not lack of coherent identity, but lack of movement and diversity in the discipline (Wastell & McMaster, 2008). The writ of Requisite Variety is ineluctable; it runs for research communities too, just as for child welfare, and the attempt to exert more control will only beget dysfunctional behaviour and threaten our ability to respond to change, and thereby our viability. On this point, perhaps we should desist from speaking of the ‘discipline’, as the connotations of the word smack of control and are not exactly congenial. Foucault (2002) argues that the unities in any field are apt to be exaggerated; he prefers to speak of ‘systems of dispersion’ and ‘discourse formations’, though the discourse formation of IS hardly trips off the tongue!

Making design our common interest furnishes a ‘broad church’ in which all are welcome; better still, the metaphor of the ‘bazaar’, to borrow from our ‘open source’ friends! And it is not necessary to do ‘design science’, as its votaries would define it, to produce designrelevant knowledge. There are roles for all to play in the bazaar of design: a Marxian critique of oppression and resistance in call centres, for instance, is ultimately about design; it bears design’s normative quality, that the world could be made better, just as our ICS study does, although beginning in a non-design mode. Even if the original scholars eschew design, others with a design mentality can certainly garner useful insights. Design is holistic, interdisciplinary and inclusive of all research approaches: as Romme (2003) argues ‘the pragmatism of the design mode’ provides the common ‘epistemological ground’ on which all research traditions can meet, combine forces and make joint cause. Unifying around design, we may declare the ‘method wars’ well and truly over.

A more liberal and inclusive structure is assuredly needed than Benbasat & Zmud’s (2003) ‘nomological net’. Focusing on the artefact is too narrow. We should certainly take up Alter’s (2003, 2008) excellent suggestion of replacing ‘IT artefact’ with ‘IT reliant work system’. We could indeed go further and drop ‘IT reliant’, for the simple reason that it is redundant, especially if we adopt a technology-neutral position. I see absolutely no reason for restricting our field of interest to one class of IT, namely the digital computer. Whether an information system is computer-based or paper-based, it is still an information system, and the same core issues of design and epistemology still apply. So-defined, all work systems are IT reliant, for the straightforward reason that information flow is analytically integral to the very concept of the viable system. The incessant circulation of information, feeding forward and back, provides the control infrastructure mediating internal coordination and purposive action, without which the system could not survive (Beer, 1994). All systems, by definition, are thus IT-reliant. But overall, there is a case for keeping the ‘ITreliant’ prefix. Digital technology does have a special place within our field, not the least as a tool for enabling organisational innovation.

Design itself should also be understood in its broadest sense. An effective design process covers the whole of the systems life cycle, and its inherent feedback loops. Simon’s deceptively simple definition makes this clear when he talks of ‘all courses of action’. As well as design in its narrowest sense, that is, the abstract configuration of the artefact, the definition embraces not only its realisation, but implementation, ‘change management’, evaluation, and so on in a necessarily circular, iterative process. Without this end-to-end scope, how can design secure its ultimate aim of desirable change? By the same reasoning, all ‘stakeholder perspectives’ must also be considered, the individual, the organisation, and society as a whole. By its very nature, design effortlessly subsumes the whole of the nomological net, and encompasses much more besides.

Although Alter’s definition of an IS as a system for producing ‘information products and/or services’ is useful conceptually, it is too narrow to provide the exclusive focus for our research, or indeed teaching. Alter acknowledges as much: ‘the processing of information may be so intertwined with the work system that it is barely meaningful to speak of the IS as a separate system’ (2008, p. 451). From a design point of view, the separation is as impossible as it is undesirable. Given that the role of the information system is to enable the effective operation of the work system in which it is embedded, only design of the whole system makes pragmatic sense. Pure information systems are rarities indeed. Scientific research is about as close as it gets to a system designed exclusively to generate information products: papers, books, reports, charts etc. But even here, there is a wider work-system, of Science itself, in which these informational activities are embedded. Design of the ‘IT enabled work system’ as a whole must thus be our overarching concern.

As an aside, let us pause briefly and follow the logic here, as there are important implications in thinking of ‘doing research’ and ‘doing IS’ as the same thing. From this perspective, doing any form of research, ‘scientific’ or otherwise, can be re-cast as the design and implementation of a bespoke information system. What else is a survey other than an IS, or indeed a laboratory experiment, or an ethnographical study for that matter; they are all designed to produce information. The whole gamut of research methods are thus properly speaking IS design tools, which is why questions of epistemology have figured so largely within our ‘discourse formation’, at least in earlier times (Mumford et al., 1985). Why then is it unusual to speak of techniques for experimental design, survey design, statistical analysis and so on as IS tools? Why do we not teach research methods as a core part of Information Systems? This is territory to which we certainly have claim, and important territory to boot in the brave new world of learning organisations, management as design and evidence-based practice.

Excursus done, time to come back to the point. Being soi-disant experts on design, of information systems in particular, naturally invites some final reflections on the design of the primary information systems within our discipline, that is, its journals and its conferences. How fit-for-purpose are they? They may support our scholarly requirements, though this is arguable, as witnessed by recent debates in this Journal (Lyytinen et al., 2007; Galliers, 2008; Paul, 2008). Take our conferences as another example, where the high rejection rate for papers is often held up as the touchstone of quality. We are unlike many other disciplines (e.g. medicine, social science) in this regard, who adopt much more inclusive policies. Wastell and McMaster (2008) comment: ‘to an outsider, it would seem perverse to mark prestige in this way’; how, we asked, is the advancement of knowledge to be promoted by excluding the majority of candidate contributions? Drawing on Mary Douglas (2002), we interpreted such exclusive editorial policies as primitive purification rituals. Their sacred thrall tellingly reveals their true nature.

But what have we to fear? The greater danger is surely the loss of diversity, of ‘the speculative, the unorthodox, the interdisciplinary and the maverick’; back to requisite variety again. Our information systems have also signally failed from the point of view of management practice. Making ‘scientific knowledge’ more accessible to the ‘lay’ practitioner is a key challenge acknowledged by evidence-based evangelists in all domains, management included. We are not well-served by the plethora of MIS texts with their kitschy production values; typically technologically orientated, they are tenuously related to general management practice, lack critical edge (Baskerville, 2009) and do little to ‘show-case’ the research that most of us do. The design of our primary information system, the journal system, also works against us. An overemphasis on theory, often arcane, exacerbated by ‘methodological showiness’, hardly helps to close the relevance gap (Wastell and McMaster, 2007). Rousseau and McCarthy’s general point, targeted at management research as a whole, applies very pertinently to our niche.

The existing mass of management research needs to be distilled into readily accessible, updatable repositories y Management scholars are rewarded often for new ideas, as opposed to assembling cumulative evidence regarding ideas other scholars might already have entertained. Even the Academy of Management Review pursues the editorial policy of rejecting papers unless they develop new theory. Providing systematic evidence of what we can know with confidence is a recipe for rejection. Failure to target summaries to non-specialists and in particular to practicing managers further compounds the problem.

In the context of design, the need for reforming the primary information systems of our discipline is particularly vital.
