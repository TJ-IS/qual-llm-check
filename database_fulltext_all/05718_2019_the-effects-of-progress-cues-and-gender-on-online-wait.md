## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cognitive absorption theory Progress cue Gender Online wait Perceived waiting time

## A B S T R A C T

A wait screen is one of the proactive mechanisms that have been suggested to manage online wait. Despite the presence of significant gender differences in traditional wait, a lack of studies in online wait context has beer acknowledged. This study proposes a research model of online wait management based on the cognitive absorption theory and gender literature. We test the efects of online interface designs in a wait screen on waiting time perceptions across gender by manipulating the salience and framing of a progress cue. A total of 535 subjects participated in two controlled experiments. MANOVA and subsequent ANOVA tests were conducted. We found significant main and interaction effects of wait screen design cues and gender on the relationships betweer the design cues and cognitive absorption variables. Detailed theoretical and practical implications are provided.

## 1. Introduction

Just as wait in traditional environments such as banks, airports, malls, clinics, or restaurants is a commonplace and frustrating experience [35,56], wait in online environment is ubiquitous and can negatively afect users' attitude and behavior (e.g., [8,49,67]). Despite the advancement of networking technology (e.g., WiFi and 5G), computing systems, and image compression tools that significantly contribute to the reduction of loading time and increase of task completion speed online, online users still wait while searching products (e.g., best airfare), downloading music, video or software, and completing online transactions. In addition, while we normally experience fairly steady Internet connectivity with WiFi or good coverage with cellular networks nowadays, from time to time people can encounter unexpected delay, wait, or even disconnection while they are online. Wait on the Internet has thus been reported as one of the major impediments of ebusiness [18,53,64] because it leads to users' frustration and negative evaluations of the site, the e-service, and its providers [32,81], website abandonment [49], and switching to other sites [65]. Approximately 60% of online users switch from one website to another due to long waits, with 40% waiting no longer than 3 s for a website to load [45], resulting in significant losses of potential sales [53]. A number of industry-based studies have noted the importance of proper management of online waits. For example, based on a study of Walmart, Mital [53] reported that “Every 100ms improvement led to a 1% increase in incremental revenue and every 1 second improvement led to a 2% boost in conversion rates.” Therefore, it is crucial for e-businesses to prepare solutions (e.g., website design improvement) for these waiting situations and use them to potentially alleviate users' frustration and dissatisfaction of wait. In spite of the importance of this topic, most writings related to online wait are practical speculations of the causes and efects, with a paucity of theoretically driven research [35,67].

E-businesses have attempted to reduce objective waiting time by implementing expensive technical solutions, including advanced and powerful servers, databases, and networks, but to date such eforts have not turned out to be as efective as expected [65]. This is because reducing “objective” waiting time is extremely dificult due to an exponential increase in Internet users, slow implementation of the intermediary infrastructure [64], and rapid growth of database volume [78]. As a result, studies focusing on managing “perceived” wait rather than objective waiting time have started to emerge (e.g., [8,12,20,35,44,49]). Buell and Norton [8] found that simply showing the appearance of efort on a web page during online wait (e.g., delivering the results instantly but invisibly when searching an airline ticket at Kayak) can significantly increase users' willingness to endure waits and their preference for a site. Hong et al. [35] developed wait interfaces by using diferent visual content (travel/clock picture) to manipulate the amount of information and direction of attention in a long vs. short wait context and found a positive efect of visual content on perceived waiting time and afect toward the site in a long wait but a negative efect in a short wait context. While these findings indicate that even a simple manipulation of design components in a wait interface can significantly influence users' wait experience, there remains a need of scientific studies that systematically investigate the full potential of wait interface design on managing online wait perceptions [35,48]. One promising approach to control perceived waiting time involves designing a wait screen (a filler screen or a wait interface) – a web page shown to users during wait situations where it can consist of various interface cues, including progress cues, image, text, and multimedia. Lee et al. [49] designed and tested the efect of wait interfaces by manipulating text and images in a simulated online travel site and found that the presence of image and text, relevant text and images, and moving images in a wait interface helped trigger a shorter perceived waiting time. While a few recent studies (e.g., [35,49]) have confirmed significant positive efects of wait interfaces on reducing perceived waiting time and providing positive user experience, this study at tempts to extend this stream of research by further investigating different manipulations of web interface design components.

Meanwhile, prior research has suggested that individual factors, such as gender, age, race, and cultural background, can play a crucial role in afecting users' waiting time perceptions [10,35]; however, a lack of related investigations has been reported. Specifically, even though IS researchers have speculated the presence of significant gender diferences toward website design components, attitudes (e.g., online trust), and behaviors (e.g., online purchases) [17,40,61], limited empirical studies have been conducted (e.g., [52]). Page et al. [60] commented that “it is surprising that researchers have only recently started to treat sex as a boundary condition moderating web use (p. 908).” Similarly, Tuch et al. [77] lamented that “studies investigating gender-related design preferences are rare. Thus, it is important that scientists and practitioners examine possible interactions between empirically identified design variables and gender (p. 1831).” Indeed, to our knowledge, the impact of gender in relation to online wait has been investigated in only one study (i.e., [10]). Following this lead, one goal of the present study is to investigate how gender moderates the relationship between wait screen design components (i.e., progress cues) and perceived waiting time.

Based on the cognitive absorption theory, this study specifically manipulates the salience of a progress cue (explicit vs. implicit) and the framing of a progress cue (utilitarian vs. hedonic), and assesses their efects on four cognitive absorption variables. We choose to investigate the salience and framing of the progress cue because these two design alternatives have been pervasively utilized in various e-businesses as well as can be a cost-efective solution to be easily applied in practice; however, they have received a limited attention from academic researchers. More importantly, these manipulations can be linked theoretically to the four cognitive absorption constructs but have not been examined in previous studies that use cognitive absorption theory as their theoretical base. While a handful of past studies (see the summary table in the Supplementary document) related to this topic exist, they are diferent from our current study in several aspects. For example, some of them do not refer to or use any theoretical support or an overarching theory (e.g., [8,12]). Some of them do not examine any mediating variables between progress cues and perceived waiting time (e.g., [44,55]). Most of them do not investigate one or both aspects of salience and framing of a progress cue (e.g., [44,48]). We developed a research model to investigate the efects of progress cue designs on four cognitive absorption variables – perceived control, focused attention, time distortion, and heightened enjoyment. Importantly, the study ex amined the moderating role of gender between wait screen design cues and cognitive absorption variables.

Our contribution is multifold. First, this study extends prior research of wait interface design and perception of wait by providing new in sights into the efects of two theory-based manipulations of a progress cue in a music download wait context. Second, given the lack of scientific investigation of gender diferences in online wait, this study contributes to the advancement of the online wait management and gender literature. Third, we contribute the advancement of cognitive absorption theory with exploring antecedents of cognitive absorption. In addition, this is the first study to incorporate perceived control dimension of cognitive absorption and investigate its efect on perceived waiting time in an online wait context. Finally, by proposing and validating a research model of online wait management based on an established IS theory (i.e., cognitive absorption theory), our study provides a solid foundation for better understanding the online wait phenomenon and for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

We first describe the theoretical foundation that we adopt in building the research model, and then develop relevant hypotheses based on this theoretical base.

## 2.1. Theory of cognitive absorption

Technologies increasingly include hedonic and experiential features, such as multimedia, for use by both businesses and individuals. Fittingly, IS researchers have proposed and applied theories that explain users' holistic responses to experiencing these technologies. One such theory is the theory of cognitive absorption [1]. Cognitive ab sorption, defined as “a state of deep involvement with software” ([1], p. 665), originated from three closely related streams of research: (a) the personality trait dimension of absorption, (b) the state of flow, and (c) the notion of cognitive engagement [1,42,76].

By integrating these theoretical bases, Agrawal and Karahanna [1] developed cognitive absorption as a multi-dimensional construct consisting of five dimensions: (a) temporal dissociation, (b) focused attention (or immersion), (c) heightened enjoyment, (d) control, and (e) curiosity. Temporal dissociation refers to the phenomenon where a person engages in an activity and loses track of time passage. Focused attention refers to the situation where a person devotes her full attention to a specific activity and ignores other surrounding activities. During a person's interaction with an activity, increased pleasure is referred as heightened enjoyment; perception of being in charge is referred to as control; and simulated sensory and cognitive interest is referred to as curiosity.

Cognitive absorption has been found to successfully predict individuals' IT acceptance behaviors [1]. For example, Saade and Bahli [68] reported that it had positive influence on the perceived usefulness and ease of use of an Internet-based learning system. In a virtual environment, Rutkowski et al. [66] found that virtual teams with focused immersion and time distortion showed higher performance whereas Goel et al. [28] found it had a significant impact on willingness to return to virtual worlds. Similarly, cognitive absorption has been found to influence perceived usefulness, satisfaction, and ease of use of an elearning service [62].

While research consistently supports the influence of cognitive absorption on technology-related beliefs and acceptance, less research has examined the determinants that may induce cognitive absorption. Prior studies have tested individual diference variables, personal innovativeness, and playfulness as antecedents of cognitive absorption [1], but we have very limited knowledge of the actual design artifacts (e.g., wait screen) that may induce cognitive absorption [49].

In brick-and-mortar business environments, diferent design elements such as color, furnishings, lightening, temperature, and music contribute diferently to customers' wait perceptions [5]. Similarly, in an online business environment, a wait screen can be designed with diferent design elements such as progress cues, text, images, and music, which can have diferent efects on cognitive absorption and perceived waiting time. Aiming to extend our understanding and application of this theory, our study intends to explore (1) the formation process for cognitive absorption by manipulating wait interface design elements (i.e., salience and framing of progress cues) and examining its interaction with gender, and (2) whether these cognitive absorption variables can afect perception of waiting time in online environment. Therefore, this study examines how two relevant designs of progress cues (antecedents), namely salience and framing, afect individuals' cognitive absorption, namely perceived control, focused attention, time distortion, and heightened enjoyment,<sup>1</sup> which in turn afect their perceived waiting time. In addition, based on the existing literature on waiting, we hypothesize the nomological network between the cognitive absorptions and perceived waiting time. We present this conceptual model in Fig. 1.

## 2.1.1. Antecedents of cognitive absorption: salience of a progress cue

In online wait environments, HCI researchers generally provide usability guidelines about the information or feedback to be presented to users as they wait, including requests under processing, remaining waiting time, and progression of the wait situation. Feedback on waiting time progress and the remaining wait can be provided in a text format, a visual or graphical format, both text and visual formats, or through other means such as tactile feedback (e.g., touch) [7]. The use of progress cues is a common practice in online interface for wait situations as they can be designed to provide related wait information. A simple text message (e.g., “Your request has been processed …”), a continuous filling bar or circle, or random blinking dots that do not explicitly showing the percentage or exact progression of the processing (i.e., presence of accumulation to an end) may be classified as implicit progress cues. On the other hand, explicit progress cues consist of progress cues (usually in a visual or graphical format) whereby a rectangular bar or a circle is gradually filled or a character is moving from a starting point to an ending point to show online processing progress.

A few studies have examined the efects of progress cues online, but they mainly used a simple rectangular progress bar or circle and investigated its efects by manipulating its presence (e.g., [55,56]) and accumulation speed (e.g., [33]) with some exceptions. In our supple mentary document, we provide a comprehensive summary table of past studies related to using cues to manage perceived waiting time. Due to this lack of theoretical support and scientific investigation of the efects of diferent designs and uses of progress cues in wait situations especially in the online setting, there is an obvious need for IS researchers to fill the void of our understanding of using progress cues to manage online wait.

From the existing literature, one major classification of progress cues can be based on their level of salience. Salience represents the extent to which a progress cue is visually identifiable and purposefully notable to users as they view it while waiting. While some progress cues simply convey that a task is in progress by exhibiting the repeated movement of symbols (e.g., dots that flicker to convey movement), others convey task progress as well as additional information about the percentage of waiting time that has passed and/or the percentage that remains. The former is classified as implicit (low salience) progress cues while the latter is called explicit (high salience) progress cues. For example, Apple® products usually show distinct bars continuously moving clockwise in a circular form, Orbitz.com simply displays three moving dots, while Hotels.com displays a progress cue showing the amount of progress.

A progress cue with high salience level that provides additional information about the wait situation, based on the cognitive control model [3], should influence users' perception of increased control. The cognitive control model [3] suggests that we experience a loss of con trol while waiting, which creates stress and influences our psychological and physical reactions to the wait. The provision of any information about the wait makes the wait felt more predictable and controllable and, therefore, may result in more positive afective responses to the wait. In addition, the provision of waiting time information also facilitates a cognitive-reappraisal efect, such that when customers have to wait, any information regarding the wait is likely to stimulate cognitive reappraisal and result in them perceiving the wait as more acceptable. For example, Taylor [75] reported that the degree to which customers perceive having control over the wait and the degree to which their waiting time is filled, positively afect service evaluations. Similarly, Nah [56] found that the continuous update of waiting time information provides people with opportunities to adjust their waiting time expectations while making them feel that they have more control over the wait, resulting in a more positive wait experience. Schwartz [69] suggested that wait may create a feeling of helplessness and a sense of loss of control, whereby the person in the wait situation is essentially “immobile” with fear of losing her spot in the queue; on the contrary, getting explicit wait-related information might reverse this feeling.

In addition, the explicitness nature of a progress cue should also attract more attention than just an implicit progress cue. For example, providing more information about the wait situation with explicit progress cues should attract more users' attention to the wait screen [41]. The resource-allocation model [50,83] postulates that (a) we have a limited pool of processing resources (e.g., attention or cognitive effort); (b) we are naturally occupied with the passage of time and are actively engaged in time estimation during the entire waiting period; and (c) increasing task demands can limit the mental processing resources available for time estimation. That is, if resources are allocated to process other objects (or events) such as emotional expressions, voices, music, or actions, fewer resources are available to attend to the duration of an event [83]. In the context of time perception, this implies that we pay less attention to the waiting time if distracting stimuli are introduced. Because more resources are consumed for processing explicit (non-temporal) information, fewer resources are available for processing time duration [75].

Therefore, when people receive explicit information about the wait situation and waiting time, they perceive having more control during the wait. By providing additional information regarding the passage of time and the amount of time left, explicit progress cues can also attract more users' attention. Thus, we predict that:

H1. During an online wait, users perceive MORE control (H1a) and MORE focused attention (H1b) when viewing a wait screen with an explicit progress cue than with an implicit progress cue.

## 2.1.2. Antecedents of cognitive absorption: framing of a progress cue

Traditional research in the field of HCI has focused on the utilitarian aspects of interface design such as utility and usability. As more and more hedonic design components are embedded into web user interfaces and influence users' perceptions and behavior, researchers are showing a greater interest in examining the relationship between hedonic web interface design and users' perceptions toward a website [77,87]. Despite many best practice guidelines for hedonic interface design, scientific investigation is still lacking to confirm the belief and speculations.

In addition to salience of progress cue, another common progress cue design that could afect waiting time perception is how to frame its intention. Framing of a progress cue represents the extent to which people perceive this visual object as useful and/or afective as they view it while waiting. Therefore, a progress cue can be framed as a hedonic and a utilitarian cue based on the use of different shapes or symbols. For example, hedonic progress cues might use symbols such as an animated airplane followed by clouds or a cartoon character to invoke fun and enjoyment, whereas utilitarian cues would simply utilize functional symbols such as plain dots or rectangular bars; both types are evident on the Internet. Research has shown that objects which spur emotions draw more attention than those that ofer only functional value. For example, Norman [58], in his book entitled Emotional Design, indicated that more attention and value are allocated to products that are visually attractive than to those that are only function-oriented. We expect that hedonic progress cues attract more attention than utilitarian progress cues, as the afective shapes or symbols are more eye-catching and attractive [41]. Zakay [83] pointed out that viewing cues that attract attention may cause individuals to be less aware of time passing. Therefore, we predict that seeing hedonic progress cues, users tend to be unconscious of the passage of time and have a tendency to lose track of time (i.e., time distortion). Further, hedonic stimuli are likely to provide fun, excitement and enjoyment [57]. Cober et al. [14] found hedonic design factors greatly enhance afective feelings toward a website. Given the attractiveness of a hedonic progress cue, we also expect that users will perceive greater enjoyment during the wait when they see hedonic progress cues than their utilitarian counterparts. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## 2.2. Moderator: gender

Despite an equal number of male and female Internet users, studies have reported that more men are engaged in online shopping than women [17,34], drawing attention to the role of gender in e-commerce and factors afecting online purchases between men and women. Previous gender studies in online environment can be summarized in two groups, those investigating (a) the presence of gender diference in online environments and (b) the relationship between website design factors and gender. First, studies were conducted in various online contexts to investigate and confirm the presence of gender diference on attitude, perception, and behavior of online users [13,54,60,86]. For example, previous research found that women showed greater computer anxiety and lower self-eficacy [21] and higher risk aversion [25], and were more focused on trustworthiness [17,61], assurance [4], and social norms [40], whereas men were more influenced by enjoyment [40], convenience [74], and value acquired through purchases [63]. In general, men had greater positive attitude toward online shopping [78,82]. Second, recognizing that “In the case of an Internet vendor, the website is perhaps the only way a firm communicates with its custo mers. Therefore, its appearance and structure encourage or discourage a consumer's purchase intentions” ([11], p. 310), researchers have ex amined the potential role of gender in the relationship between website design factors and online users' perceptions and behaviors. For ex ample, Cyr and Bonanni [17] found that men are more attracted by animations and interactive design components. Similarly, Stenstrom et al. [72] found significant sex diferences in navigation, object location, spatial rotation, and perception of color, form, and movement in conducting experiments with mock online bookstores. Further, Tuch et al. [77] found that men favored symmetrically designed websites, whereas women's judgment was not afected by website symmetry. Simon [70] indicated that men prefer extensive graphics and animation, whereas women like websites that are less cluttered with less graphics and more information.

Studies have been conducted on gender difference in various traditional wait contexts (e.g., [6,30]). A meta-analysis of 87 studies by Block et al. [6] confirmed the existence of gender diferences in waiting duration judgments. Females were found to focus attention on time more than males, with the result that they accumulated subjective temporal memory units at a faster rate. Espinosa-Fernandez et al. [22] reported similar findings. However, they mainly investigated the efect of gender on time estimation but not the role of gender in waiting time perception or overall wait experience.

To the best of our knowledge, Chebat et al. [10] conducted the only study which investigated gender diferences in online wait. They manipulated music tempo (fast vs. slow) and waiting duration information (presence vs. no presence) in the context of downloading a web page to examine the efect of gender between the manipulated variables and waiting time where a significant efect was found. Specifically, for women, significantly diferent amounts of pleasure for diferent music tempos was found. This finding was not the case for men; instead, significantly diferent amounts of arousal for diferent music tempos was found only for men. In addition, for women, pleasure was afected by their cognitive response (attention and memory) but not for men. This study is important in that it empirically validated the presence of significant efect of gender in online wait context. However, it has several limitations, including (a) background music is scarcely ever used in commercial websites and design guidelines (e.g., [38]) recommend not to use it and (b) the researchers used text messages on a web page to manipulate the presence of waiting duration information without directly using more proactive design cues such as progress cues.

To extend past research of gender diference, we intend to further investigate gender efect in wait contexts of online environment while people experience a diferent variety of design cues. Specifically, we investigate the role of gender with the relationship between progress cue designs on wait interfaces, and wait perceptions during an online wait situation. Previous studies (e.g., [17,18,25,31,46]) confirmed the presence of significant gender in online context. Specifically related to perceived control, Dabholkar and Sheng [18] found that women are more likely to react to the perceived speed of a Web site download based on perceptions of control in the context of download delays. Garbarino and Strahilevitz [25] found that women experience higher levels of risk with online purchasing and are inclined to seek more control. These findings suggest that women are more likely to develop and rely on their “control” perception to guide their online interaction. Wang [80] found that browsing frequency has a positive direct efect on perceived control of female consumers but does not have a significant impact on male perceived control. Finally, Cyr and Bonanni [17] sug gest that women are either more skeptical of information presented online, or require more information to make a purchasing decision. Based on the cognitive control model [3], additional information about the wait situation should influence users' perception of increased con trol. Therefore, we conjecture that cues with high-salience level $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ explicit progress cue) on online interfaces can provide users with “more” information which is preferred by women to feel in charge. We hypothesize that:

H3a. During an online wait, the diferences in perceived control between an explicit progress cue and an implicit progress cue is larger for females than males.

Gender is believed to be one of the individual factors that influence online attitudes and behavior. The term “hedonic,” derived from Greek where hedonism, means pleasure; hedonic design elements can cause an afective state of pleasure or heightened enjoyment [15]. In terms of gender diferences, Hasan [34] suggested that the afective attitude of women toward online shopping seems to be lower than that of men. Simon [70] also found that, in general, females provided lower aesthetic ratings than male of websites. Further, Hwang [40] found that males are significantly influenced by perceived enjoyment while searching and purchasing products or services online. In addition, previous studies indicated that men show more immersive behavior and spend more time (resulting in losing track of time) while playing computer games and navigating virtual worlds sites [23,37] (which deems to be hedonic activities) than females. Thus, we hypothesize that males would perceive more enjoyment and lose track of time compared to females when they see hedonic (compared to utilitarian) progress cues.

H3b–c. During an online wait, the diferences in time distortion (H3b) and heightened enjoyment (H3c) between a hedonic progress cue and a utilitarian progress cue is larger for males than females.

## 2.3. Consequences of cognitive absorption: perceived waiting time

Having hypothesized how diferent progress cues can influence time perceptions, we now turn our attention to how these four wait perception constructs, namely perceived control, focused attention, time distortion, and heightened enjoyment influence perceived waiting time.

Time has always been an integral part of human experiences, and in today's hectic environment, people tend to regard time as more precious than ever before. While we may refer to time from a clock (i.e., objective time), we rely upon and emphasize perceived time, which is a product of cognitive functioning. That is, many time-related experi ences are based on perceived (or subjective) time such as memory, estimation of duration, and “felt” speed of events [64], as evident in questions such as “how much time did we use?” and “how long did we wait?” In fact, individuals use perceived, internal, subjective, psychological, and apparent waiting time interchangeably [2], representing “the temporal value used by the subject in making his judgment (p. 341).”

It is important to recognize that perceived time is sensitive to the conditions under which it is measured [84], including the external $\mathit { \Omega } ^ { \mathrm { { e } } } \mathbf { c } \mathbf { u } \mathbf { e } \mathbf { s } ^ { \gamma }$ in the environment and how they influence the internal perceptions of time. Thus, the passage of time can be perceived diferently based on people's cognitive reactions to the type and number of cues present in the environment. For example, Gilliland et al. [27] claimed that time estimation depends on the number of cues that occur or seem to occur during a wait.

A wait is perceived to be shorter when people perceive that they have control over the wait situation. Researchers have found that a sense of control significantly influences individuals' reactions to wait (e.g., [39]). People can maintain control in a waiting environment through information gain and reappraisal of the stressful situation. If any waiting time information is given, people perceive the wait as more manageable. In addition, if waiting time information is provided in a situation where wait is inevitable, it stimulates reappraisal of the wait, resulting in the wait being more acceptable. In our study, progress cues explicitly and implicitly provide information about the wait situation, reducing stress and helping users perceive that they are in better charge. This perception of better control, in turn, helps to reduce the perceived waiting time.

When a person is immersed in an experience to such a degree that her attention is distracted from other things, this focused attention may reduce perceived time because she does not attend to the passing of time. That is, if an individual's attention is focused on cues (or tasks) while waiting, she has fewer cognitive resources available for temporal information processing [85]. Thus, when users' attention is focused on a progress cue in a wait screen, fewer time signals accumulate in the cognitive counter, resulting in a perceived shorter waiting time. A number of studies have empirically tested the negative relationship between focused attention and perceived waiting time $[ 1 , 5 , 2 6 , 7 6 ]$ Thus, we conjecture that when a progress cue attracts users' attention $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ distract users' attention from time), their perception of waiting time is reduced.

Time distortion occurs when the thought of time passing does not enter into person's consciousness and the estimate of time duration, therefore, is distorted. Research has found that while people are engaged in an interaction, they are unable to register the passage of time accurately, and thus can perceive that time is flying by quickly $[ 1 , 1 6 , 7 1 ]$ . Kellaris and Mantel [43] observed that music distorted the listener's internal timing mechanism and thus reduced perceived waiting time. To this extent, progress cues on a wait screen may induce time distortion for users who are engaged in the wait screen, and thus successfully reduce their perceptions of waiting time.

Finally, heightened enjoyment is another factor that can influence perceived waiting time. To the extent that cues provided in wait screens are enjoyable, empirical studies suggest that perceived waiting time can be reduced. For example, Hornik [36] found that happy events are perceived to be shorter than somber ones, and Campbell and Bryant [9] reported that the more excited a person is about an imminent event, the faster time seems to pass. Similarly, Fraisse [24] noted that listening for a minute to more interesting prose passages provided better enjoyment and was judged be to shorter than less interesting passages. In addition, North and Hargreaves [59] found that individuals perceived les waiting time when they encountered stimuli that they liked (e.g., music). Given this, we expect that progress cues designed to provide fun, enjoyment, or excitement would result in a shorter perceived wait. Based on the above reasoning, we hypothesize that:

H4. Users' perceptions of MORE control (H4a), focused attention (H4b), time distortion (H4c) and enjoyment (H4d) while viewing a wait screen with a progress cue is related to SHORTER perceived waiting time during an online wait.

## 3. Research methodology and results

To examine the efect of progress cue designs and gender on cognitive absorption (perceived control (PC), focused attention (FA), time distortion (TD), and heightened enjoyment (HE)) and to investigate the relationships between cognitive absorption and perceived waiting time (PWT), we conducted a pre-test and two controlled experiment studies. A pre-test was conducted with two treatments $( 2 \times 1$ design) to examine the diferences in perceived waiting time between (a) a wait screen without a progress cue and (b) a wait screen with a progress cue. The goal of the pre-test was to investigate whether a wait screen with a progress cue is more efective than one without a progress cue with regard to cognitive absorption. After confirming the benefit of a wait screen with a progress cue, Study 1 and Study 2 were conducted to examine the efects of progress cue designs – Study 1: salience-level (explicit vs. implicit progress cue), and Study 2: framing (hedonic vs. utilitarian progress cue) – on cognitive absorption, and to test the nomological network for cognitive absorption and perceived waiting time. A simulated online music download website was designed to test re search hypotheses.

Subjects were recruited by distributing invitational fliers and making class announcements to undergraduate business students at a large U.S. university. In the pre-test, 113 subjects were recruited, resulting in 100 usable responses due to no shows and incomplete surveys (88.5% response rate). Two weeks after completing the pre-test and confirming significant diferences between the treatments (wait screen without a progress cue and wait screen with a progress cue), 301 subjects were recruited for Study 1. Then 278 subjects signed up for Study 2 one month after Study 1 was completed. Subjects were randomly assigned to one treatment condition to investigate the efect of diferent progress cue designs. After removing no shows and incomplete responses, a total of 535 usable responses (Study 1: 279, 92.6% response rate; Study 2: 256, 92% response rate) were included in the data analysis (Table 1).

On average, subjects were 21.59 years old and 46.3% were females. Subjects had purchased or downloaded songs from online music sites the previous vear an average of 5.10 times. Finally, 94.4% of the sub. jects had used medium and high-speed Internet services. For all studies, participation was voluntary. Incentives to participate included receiving both class credit and an opportunity to win a small music download gift card.

We created a simulated online music download site by mimicking major online music sites such as 7digital.com and eMusic.com. Except for the control (no progress cue) condition, all experimental treatments were designed to include three web pages: an input screen, a wait screen, and an output screen. The input and output screens were identical across treatments. The only diference among treatments was the design elements included in the wait screen. The input screen consisted of a company logo and three drop-down boxes for music search. Subjects were asked to make selections for favorite music (songs), quality (Best, Regular, etc.), and file format (MP3, WAV, etc.) in addition to promotion ads, acceptable credit card types, social media, and security seals. The top 15 songs listed in a major music chart (i.e., Billboard) one week before starting the experiment were chosen as favorite music to be selected and purchased on the site. After choosing the song and selecting a payment option, subjects clicked the “Download Now” button to download music and waited 16 s to finish downloading music while seeing a wait screen.

The wait screen included a company logo and a progress cue. We designed diferent progress cues by manipulating their presence, salience level, and framing. After the wait was over, an output screen was presented to inform users that the download process was successfully completed. Subjects were then requested to click “Continue” button that directed them to an online questionnaire.

In the no progress cue condition, a wait screen with only a company logo was displayed during the wait. In the other treatments, the progress cues were based on those currently used on online music download sites. Specifically, the implicit condition was implemented by using a location-moving dot in the background as the implicit progress cue. For the explicit progress cue condition, an accumulative rectangular progress bar was used. Finally, the hedonic condition was designed using an incremental display of pop star images appeared to move across the screen. Details on the experimental procedure checks are provided separately, by study, in the sections below.

## 3.1. Pre-test

## 3.1.1. Method

Before the main study, a pre-test study (n = 100) was conducted in a controlled lab environment. The two versions of simulated music websites were installed onto the lab computers in order to precisely control the delay experienced by the subjects. As previously described, the two conditions were (a) a wait screen with no progress cue $( n = 5 7 )$ , and (b) a wait screen displaying a progress cue (n = 43). Subjects arrived at a computer lab that has PCs installed web applications for experiments and connected with high-speed network. Every monitor was set with the same resolution, brightness and contrast level. Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the two conditions and received instructions for participating in the study. They were told that they would be searching for/downloading favorite music on a new music website and would be asked to evaluate the website after they completed the purchase/download task. Subjects were asked to fill in the requested information on the input screen, click on the “Download Now” button, and then see the download confirmation message provided on the output screen. Subjects waited 16 s and either viewed a wait screen with a progress cue or one without it while waiting. Subjects were then requested to click on the “Continue” button that directed them to an online questionnaire site. The total amount of time taken to complete the study was no > 20 min.

The scales used to measure the cognitive absorption dimensions were developed according to a formal instrument development procedure [73]. Instrument items were based on an extensive literature review: three perceived control items [1], four focused attention items [47], four heightened enjoyment items [47,48], and three time distortion items [71] were selected (see Table 2). A pretest was conducted with seven experts, consisting of faculty members, doctoral students, and users who had experienced diverse online music sites. Wording and questionnaire formats were modified based on the experts' suggestions in order to improve content validity. All items were measured using a 7- point Likert-type scale anchored with strongly agree/disagree.

## 3.1.2. Results

The psychometric properties of the instrument were first examined by calculating Cronbach's alphas and running an exploratory factor analysis (EFA). All constructs exhibited alphas over 0.8, indicating high reliability. In the EFA, all factor loadings were > 0.903, and crossloadings were < 0.117 suggesting good convergent and discriminant validity.

We then examined the diferences between the progress cue condition and no progress cue condition with respect to perceived control, focused attention, heightened enjoyment, and time distortion using MANOVA and found a significant efect of the progress cue's presence (Wilks's $\lambda = 8 . 7 3 2 , p \ < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Subsequent ANOVA test showed that online users reported more perceived control $( F = 2 7 . 7 5 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , $\mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { P R } } = 4 . 4 3 9 , \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { N P R } } = 3 . 2 7 0 )$ , and focused attention $( F = 4 . 4 6 0$ , $p \ < \ 0 . 0 5$ , Mean = 3.862, Mean = 3.227) when the wait screen included a progress cue than when it did not. The findings were con sistent with those of previous studies [17,72]. Meanwhile, no sig nificant diferences for heightened enjoyment $( F = 1 . 8 9 4 , p > 0 . 0 5 ,$ $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { P R } } = 3 . 8 0 3 . } \end{array}$ , Mean<sub>NPR</sub> = 3.495) and time distortion $( F = 0 . 3 3 3 ,$ $p > 0 . 0 5 ,$ $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { P R } } = 3 . 2 0 2 , } \end{array}$ $\mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { N P R } } = 3 . 3 5 4 )$ . It implies that there needs extra design efort for progress cues to induce users' greater enjoyment and time distortion. The findings of the pre-test motivated us to further investigate alternative progress cue designs that may induce greater cognitive absorption and stronger influences on waiting time perception.

We used the same simulated online music download site as in the pre-test. Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the experimental conditions and completed the experiment and questionnaire by following the same procedure used in the pre-test (see Fig. 2 andTable 3).

## 4. Discussion

This research applies the theories of time perception, cognitive ab sorption, and gender study to investigate antecedents of individuals perceived waiting time in an online wait context, expanding our understanding of online wait management. We first examined the relationship between diferent progress cue designs and waiting time perceptions, and found that they had a significant influence on subjects waiting time perceptions, including perceived control, focused attention, time distortion, and heightened enjoyment. Additionally, several significant efects of gender emerged on relationships between progress cue designs and waiting time perceptions. All hypotheses were sup ported with our data except H3a. In summary, the findings of our experimental studies validated the proposed model and explained substantial variance in perceived waiting time.

First, from Study 1 we found that an explicit rather than an implicit progress cue efectively afected waiting time perceptions – increasing users' sense of control and focused attention. This finding confirms that when a clearly recognizable, high salience-level progress cue with extra temporal information (percentage of wait completed and remained) rather than an implicit cue for progress is presented on a wait screen, users perceive more control over their wait that the wait will eventually end as well as pay more attention to the explicit progress cue while waiting.

Second, from Study 2, we found that hedonic progress cues, as compared to utilitarian progress cues, had a significantly stronger efect on focused attention, time distortion, and heightened enjoyment. These results imply that a progress cue with a hedonic presentation attracts more attention, arouses feelings of more fun and excitement, and distorts users' estimation of time duration. As such, we confirm the findings of previous wait studies, where time was distorted for people that saw a hedonic time filler while waiting in physical environments (e.g., live piano performance in a bank). These findings further validate the postulations from the cognitive absorption model. That is, the study empirically validated the significant efect of cognitive absorption on perceived waiting time in a simulated online music download context. The results suggest that perceived waiting time can be managed by designing progress cues that distract the attention of users from waiting time. The significant influence of time distortion on perceived waiting time also provides us with useful insights as users are less able to track the passage of time when viewing hedonic progress cues.

Third, this study found several significant interaction efects of gender on cognitive absorption. We found a significant interaction effect between gender and framing of progress cues with respect to heightened enjoyment and time distortion. This finding indicates that males perceive more enjoyment and are prone to lose track of time more than females when they see a wait screen with a hedonic progress cue. These results extend previous findings on the higher pursuit of enjoyment [40] and higher time distortion [23,37] of males while reacting to higher aesthetic in an online environment to the online wait context. Meanwhile, we did not find significant interaction efect between gender and salience-level of progress cues in regards to perceived control. One possible interpretation of this insignificant findings is that the task of this study (i.e., music download) is not highly risky and thus female subjects do not perceive a great need to control the task. Future replicate studies with high risky tasks (e.g., expensive product pur chases) will be required to further clarify this finding.

Finally, the study confirmed that four cognitive absorption variables significantly afect perceived waiting time, explained by its large amount of variance. These findings confirm that when we display a wait screen with progress cue designs that appropriately influence users' wait perceptions, they perceive a shorter waiting time. The nomological networks in our model can thus richly address (a) how to help users develop a sense of control, (b) how to explain or manipulate users immersive behavior, (c) how to distort users' estimation of time, and (d) enhance users' enjoyment while they wait. Specifically, from Study 2, we found that heightened enjoyment had the strongest efect on perceived waiting time, confirming the importance of framing interface features as more hedonic. Considering that utilitarian progress cues are predominantly designed and investigated in academia and industry, the present study provides useful empirical evidence that facilitates further exploration of the design of efective hedonic progress cues.

## 5. Implications and conclusions

## 5.1. Limitations

As with any research, this study has limitations that must be acknowledged prior to discussing study implications. This study is limited in external validity with regard to subjects, the controlled setting of the study, and the experimental website. Specifically, students from one U. S. University served as subjects, and the study was conducted in a lab environment in order to control for potential diferences in computer performance and network bandwidth, which could have influenced objective waiting time. While student subjects can successfully represent the target population of our study (i.e., purchasers of music online), future work with actual consumers conducting real e-business transactions should test the generalizability of the research findings with other populations. Specially, a replicate study with gender-ba lanced subjects is recommended. The experimental wait screen is also limited in precisely representing real-world wait screens. For example, we selected a 16-second waiting time, and thus did not compare the findings under shorter and longer wait conditions. This study fixed the music (song) download scheme and manipulated only a progress cue. Since it is an exploratory study, we separately examined the efects of salience and framing of progress cues. We do suggest future work to examine the main and interaction efects among them to discover potential interactions between these design components on cognitive absorption and further validate the findings of this study. In addition, it is possible that nature of task or complexity of task which a user is undertaking can potentially afect his/her perception of wait online. Future study should investigate waiting time perception in diferent task environments with progress cues presented. This study did not investigate other demographic variables such as age and culture be cause we recruited student subjects from one U. S. University only for conducting controlled experiments. It is recommended for future stu dies to investigate the efects of these demographic variables for testing the generalizability of the findings of this study and examining the interactions between them and progress cue designs to deepen our understanding of the efects of those boundary conditions.

## 5.2. Implications for research

Despite these limitations, given the empirical support for our model and hypotheses, this study contributes to the wait literature in several ways.

First, this study extends prior research of waiting interface and perception of wait by providing new insights into the efects of two theory-based manipulations of a progress cue in a music download wait context. We investigated the main efect of these two progress cue design alternatives and the interaction efect between them and gender based on cognitive absorption theory. As shown in the summary table of the supplementary document, only a few past studies related to this topic were conducted and they are diferent from our current study in distinct ways. Specifically, none of them focus on the investigation of gender diference on cognitive absorption variables with progress cues online. Therefore, with theoretical support and by empirically documenting the significant influence of progress cue designs and gender on perceived waiting time, we believe that this study contributes to the advancement of the online wait management literature.

Second, by proposing and validating a research model of online wait management based on cognitive absorption theory, our study provides a solid foundation for better understanding the online wait phenomenon and for future research. Specifically, we contribute the advancement of cognitive absorption theory in two ways. One, we identified the efects of web design components (antecedents) and its main and interaction efects with gender on four cognitive absorption variables. Despite of the request from Agarwal and Karahanna [1] to identify antecedents of cognitive absorption constructs, only a few studies have been conducted to investigate these potential antecedents. Second, we incorporated the perceived control dimension of cognitive absorption which surprisingly was not examined in previous online wait studies. Considering that perceived control is crucial during online wait because people with perceived control during waits feel more calm, comfort, satisfied, confident, and less PWT, it was a very valuable efort to in vestigate and validate its efect.

Third, this study also contributes to the gender literature by confirming the significant role of gender on the relationship between progress cue design and cognitive absorption variables. Prior research has suggested that individual factors, such as gender, age, race, cultural background play a crucial role in afecting users' waiting time perceptions ([10,35]), but a lack of related investigation specifically on gender has been frequently reported. For example, Van Slyke et al. [79] pointed out that “among the many characteristics that impact the use of e-commerce, one that has received relatively little attention is gender. Extant research suggests that men and women difer in their beliefs … including e-commerce. However, less is known about how gender moderates the impact of various beliefs on behavioral intentions (p. 17).” Specifically, to our knowledge, the impact of gender in relation to online wait has been investigated in only one study (i.e., [10]) and thus we have very limited understanding on the influence of gender in online waits. By testing and validating the complex interplay among progress cue designs, gender, and cognitive absorption constructs, we believe that this study contributes to expand the theoretical boundary of gender and the advancement of the online wait management literature as well as opens the door for future studies to further examine gender diference in various online wait contexts using diferent design components including multimedia, fonts, typeface, layout, and color.

Finally, this study reconfirms the validity of cognitive absorption as a multidimensional measure of wait-related perceptions and sheds light on the design components that contribute to its formation. Research ha called for additional exploration and psychometric validation of cog nitive absorption [1], but to date few studies have answered this call. Cognitive absorption is a state that can be influenced by the particular configuration of systems, users, and situational factors. In this study, we focused on system features and identified progress cue design compo nents that diferentially afect cognitive absorption factors. Consequently, we validated the significant influence of interface design on users' cognitive absorption, opening the door for human-computer in teraction researchers to explore how other website designs can influ ence cognitive absorption. Further, we contribute to the theoretical advancement of the cognitive absorption construct by applying it to a wait context and examining the novel relationship between cognitive absorption and perceived waiting time.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

This research yields important implications for e-business practitioners in their eforts to design interfaces that can efectively manage users' online waits. First, our study provides important evidence that the inclusion of progress cues in online wait screens can improve users waiting time perceptions. Results from our study clearly show that users' perceptions of waiting time were reduced by the presence of a wait screen with the inclusion of diferent progress cues on the wait screen. This is an important finding that can provide (a) e-business managers with confidence in investing their limited resources on wait screen design and (b) interface designers with evidence to persuade managers to devote more resources to designing usable wait screens.

Second, our findings also provide useful implications for custo mizing online wait interfaces to efectively manage online wait. That is, our results suggest that specific progress cue design components such as salience and framing diferentially afect individuals' online wait perceptions. For example, we showed that the use of a hedonic progress cue can distract users' attention from wait, distort users' estimation of time duration, and provide fun and excitement, whereas the use of an explicit progress cue provides users with greater perceptions of control as well as more attention. These findings can clearly guide designers in customizing progress cues to meet target users' needs for managing online wait.

Third, by empirically validating the role of gender between wait screen designs and cognitive absorption, this study ofers useful information for designing a gender-relevant website that can provide a greater satisfaction for online customers. As Meyers-Levy and Sternthal [51] stated, gender is still a very important segmentation strategy consideration since (a) gender is easily identifiable, (b) gender segments are accessible, and (c) gender segments are large enough. Simon [70] suggested that females online may exhibit lower levels of favorable perception and satisfaction if websites fail to provide suficient or attention getting information. Therefore, to target women who visit a website to purchase products or services, wait screens can include an explicit progress cue and provide more wait related information. This is consistent with what Dittmar et al. [19] suggested; that is, while functional benefits are facilitators for women to shop online, it is specifically important to increase emotional aspects for them. Meanwhile, to target men who visit a website, wait screens should have a hedonic progress cue. Understanding the design factors impacting the perception of time, in particular with regard to gender, will help designers to consider new ways of designing the web interface to make users perceive better online wait experience.

Finally, we demonstrated that if users' wait perceptions are improved, their perceived waiting time can be reduced. Overall, this study provides useful insights for managers to design wait screens best matched with users, providing a better online experience.

## 5.4. Conclusion

This study examined the efect of a wait screen with progress cues on cognitive absorption and the nomological networks associated with perceived waiting time in online environments. The critical impact of online wait on e-businesses, as well as the lack of studies on online wait management, was discussed. This study manipulated two design conditions of a wait screen, salience and framing of a progress cue, and validated their diferential contributions to cognitive absorption variables. In addition, we confirmed a significant gender efect in online wait context. Further, by discovering the antecedents of perceived waiting time, we proposed and validated a theory-based research model of online wait. The empirical results provide strong support for the proposed model and highlight the importance of managing users' online wait perceptions and creating an overall positive web experience through efective wait screen designs.

His current teaching and research interests include knowledge management, IT business value, human computer interface design, database management, and business and Web programming applications. His research work appears in Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, Information & Management, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, and international conferences and workshops such as AMCIS, DSI, ICIS, PACIS, and WITS.
