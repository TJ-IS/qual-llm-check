# Data warehouse-in-practice: exploring the function of expectations in organizational outcomes

Silvia Massa<sup>\*</sup>, Stefania Testa

Department of Communication, Computer and System Sciences, University of Genoa, via Opera Pia, 13, 16145 Genova, Italy

Received 17 October 2003; received in revised form 20 February 2004; accepted 5 June 2004 Available online 4 August 2004

## Abstract

Traditionally, data warehouses (DW) have been counted among the most powerful problem-solving tools to enable easy access to information and enhance the effectiveness of decision-making processes.

Nevertheless, as noted by some authors, the outcome of any Information and Communication Technology (ICT) tool is emergent, since there will always be scope for improvisation in technology use. ICT tools are expected to be used in different ways, either ignoring certain properties, working around them, or inventing new ones that may go beyond or even contradict designers’ expectations and inscriptions. In order to cope with this, the term ‘‘technology-in-practice’’ is frequently used. Adopting this perspective, the aim of the authors was to broaden the empirical basis of DW-in-practice, showing how different uses of the same tool could lead to different outcomes. We, therefore, examined DW-in-practice in three manufacturing and services organizations.

Keywords: Technology-in-practice; Data warehouse; Power user; Boundary objects

## 1. Introduction

Data warehouses (DW) are commonly considered powerful problem-solving tools in organizational context [8,39]. Nevertheless, as noted by some authors (see e.g., [5,10,29]), the outcome of any Information and Communication Technology (ICT) tool is emergent, since there will always be scope for improvisation in technology use.

This paper broadens the empirical basis of DWs by providing evidence of how different uses of the same tool lead to different outcomes. We examine DW-inpractice in three manufacturing and services organizations by means of case studies. Such a qualitative approach is appropriate for detecting social dynamics [2,27,38].

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Structuration theory on technology

In understanding technology, structurational models have been strongly influenced by the intellectual tradition of social constructivism. Such models posit that technology is developed through a socio-political process in which the disposition of users inside organizations is the major factor. Researchers began to use the notions of innovation and learning to understand the organizational implications of new technologies, introducing the role of the emergence and improvisation in technology and its use [9,11,33]. Thus, rather than focusing on technologic aspects, ‘‘embodied’’structures and their influence on use, this view starts with human behavior and the enactment of emergent structures in the use of technology. According to Orlikowski [34], technology is comprised of two aspects: technology as artifact (material and symbol properties) and its use, which is shaped by the users’ experiences with other technologies and their participation in social communities. On the basis of a large body of empirical evidence [21,31,47], technology use is strongly influenced by users’ understanding the properties of a technology and this, in turn, is strongly influenced by their expectations. It follows that different user groups enact different technologies-in-practice.

## 2.2. The DW technology

A data warehouse system [16] is a repository of integrated information, which can be utilized for query or analysis.

Inmon et al. [18] defined a DW as ‘‘a subjectoriented, integrated, non volatile, time-variant collection of data organized to support management needs’’. Data warehousing involves extracting data from source systems (e.g., legacy or external), cleaning (e.g., eliminating inconsistencies), and transforming the data (e.g., applying business rules or summarizing it), and loading it on a data store (e.g., a database). It also includes the tools and applications that end-users apply to access and analyze the data [43,44].

DW design and implementation require several competencies that logically belong to different organizational units: content experts, content designers, architecture designers, and developers, software experts, maintainers and users. Some authors speak of DW stakeholders [19] as the DW decision makers, users, and technicians.

DW is a technology that often requires the technical expert’s intervention between the user and the artifact. Traditionally (e.g., [30]) the emergence of technology structures is related to open-ended technologies, in the sense that users (with or without assistance) have the means of making changes. In this case, the mediation of the technical expert limits direct interaction between the user and the artifact, and puts the problem of the emergence of technologies-in-practice in a new light.

Some authors recognize the role of DWs in reducing information ambiguity by providing ‘‘a single version of the truth’’ [22] reinforcing the control of centralized information flow.

Notwithstanding, the vendors underline the role of DW in decentralizing information and authority – the so-called ‘‘information democracy’’ [26]. The role of information technology in decentralizing information and authority rather than in reinforcing the centralization of authority seems to depend on both management philosophy and organizational culture [28].

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Research setting

We examined DW-in-practice in three manufacturing and services organizations (Table 1) [6]. These included two from the private and one from the public sector, that have been using the same DW tool (Business Objects) for 4 years with full and regular use.

Alpha is an Italian University, Beta is the Italian subsidiary of a Japanese consumer and professional electronics firm and Gamma is an Italian fashion firm. Through an examination of these three diverse organizations, we tried to explain the differences in their usage.

We deliberately sought a variety of settings and types of users, in order to enhance generalization. While replication selects cases with similar expected results, we wished to study different types of results [14].

In this study, we interviewed three categories of system’s stakeholders in different work units, including project and management teams, departments, and committee (see [49]). The first category of stakeholders consisted of the decision makers involved in the initial decision to adopt the technology. We use the term ‘‘decision maker’’ for them. The second category is made up of the DW users, belonging to several work units. The third is made up of the technicians. They maintain and upgrade the system.

## 3.2. Data collection and analysis

Data was primarily obtained by using semi-structured interviews supported by documentation and observation. Clearly, the use of multi-method approaches, designed to compensate for any single-method’s weaknesses, are ways of increasing both reliability and validity. The study thus incorporates Kanter’s [20] suggestion that different sources of data be used to validate each other. Multiple informants were interviewed, providing further opportunity for triangulation. They came from all the categories of stakeholders as well as spanning all hierarchical levels (see Table 2).

Whenever possible, documents were used to gather information about the introduction of the DW into the company. This was a way to confirm or refute data obtained through other means and to guard against bias in informants’ data [25].

The interviews lasted from 1 to 4 h. The individual interviews ended with group discussion whenever possible. The interviews focused on ways of using the DW, prior expectations, and evaluation of formal and informal organizational changes induced by its adoption. Changes included modification of the organizational chart, role-players involved, functions, horizontal and vertical coordination mechanisms, power relationship, etc. [13].

The checklist for interviews included open-ended questions. All interviews were split into two parts: expectations to be fulfilled by DW adoption and perceived organizational outcomes induced by DW.

The data collection was conducted over a period of 2 years; standard techniques were followed [48]. After the transcription of the interviews, data was coded to identify themes, recurring comments, and parameters that could be analyzed with respect to the research issue.

Data analysis proceeded in three phases, the first two involved in-study analysis and the third consisted of a cross study analysis. First, we searched for the expectations of each group of stakeholders. Second, we examined the perceived organizational changes induced; we were particularly interested in instances where unforeseen relationships or behaviors occurred. Third, we compared the results across the three sites and determined similarities and differences.

## 4. Case Alpha

## 4.1. Decision makers

The decision makers belong to the Budget Administration and Information Systems (IS) Departments.

## 4.1.1. Expectations

They interpreted their goal as integrating the data sources in an ‘‘institutional’’ way, and spoke about the adoption of the DW as an ‘‘obligatory choice, the natural evolution of information systems’’. The decision makers aim was found to be to centralize and control the input and output of the data and have a ‘‘unique, certified data source’’.

The decision makers interpreted the DW as a tool to ‘‘put things in order’’ and aspired to a strong hierarchy in order to impose its use. The decision makers foresaw the adoption of ‘‘economic sanctions’’ on departments that refused to use it.

The decision makers considered users’ fears of losing control and their inability to express tacit knowledge as being the main obstacles to DW adoption. They considered the decision-making support as being the ‘‘proper use’’ of the DW. Actually, on the basis of the interviews, the emerging use seemed to be more oriented towards monitoring and controlling activities than decision-making support.

DW was used to prevent conflicts that could arise from the stakeholders’ different culture, language, and practice, as it provided a ‘‘single version of the truth’’.

## 4.1.2. Perceived organizational outcomes

The decision makers identified unexpected and undesired organizational changes. Such changes derived from the ‘‘improper’’ use of the tool that included activities that would be more appropriately carried out by an MIS. Such an ‘‘improper’’ use implied, for example, that the technicians were strongly involved in content definition and mediation between the user and the tool. Therefore, the technicians deviated from their current practice. This resulted in an informal enlargement of their tasks and an ‘‘improper’’ mix of roles, involving extra work for the DW technicians and a substantial deviation from ‘‘best use’’. Every deviation in the use of the instrument was considered a failure.

## 4.2. Users

The interviews involved users from many different work units. A user profile emerged that was characterized by fast learning processes and high interest levels in the DW. In literature, this is considered a power user [32].

Power users assumed the role of interface in mediating between their own work unit and that of the DW technicians and also in interacting with members of other work units on the contents. In fact they spent much of their time helping their colleagues (such a phenomenon has been observed also in [45]).

The decision makers seemed to exploit power users as ambassadors to translate their needs in terms of DW outcomes. Power users functioned as bridges between technicians and decision makers, working with the technicians while speaking the same language as the decision makers. The role of ‘‘ambassadors’’ who worked at the boundaries between work units, the so-called ‘‘boundary persons’’ or ‘‘boundary-breaking managers’’ have been widely recognized in literature [1,12,35,37,40]. Some authors have underlined their fundamental role as people who ‘‘trigger and coordinate knowledge-creation process’’ [23]. Power users take advantage of their privileged relationship with the decision makers to improve the quality and the scope of their job, even if their positions are not easily sustainable in the long run. For example, an interviewee reported: ‘‘At the beginning I was very stimulated. My boss and I were doing a job together, something really useful and my role was absolutely indispensable. But soon I realized I was alone. I could perceive my colleagues’ envy. I had a lot of extra-work to do, without economic or decision making benefits.’

Indeed, management did not formally recognize the role of the power-users. However, the need for a mediation role was largely perceived. As a result, some DW technicians were transferred from DW technician work units to administration units where they were supposed to be DW users with the informal responsibility of mediating between the technical and the administrative staff. This decision was unfortunately made while ignoring the power users that were the natural candidates for the role. An informal role is scarcely effective because it is not legitimized in Alpha context. Furthermore, an unexpected result was that the person, who moved to the new position, was no longer considered a member of the technician community by his ex-colleagues. As one of the transferred technicians reported: ‘‘In a few days I was not allowed to access the DW as administrator, my passwords were changed overnight and I was treated as the lowest ranking of the users.’’ This hostile behaviour may be partly explained by the fear of the technical unit of losing control of the DW, which is clearly perceived as their major source of power. Nevertheless, the administrative management was aware of the potential role of the DW in changing the power map and this resulted in them trying to ‘‘steal’’ it from the technician work unit. Many authors have dealt with these aspects of organizational power in using computer-based systems (see [15]).

## 4.2.1. Expectations

Expectations and different uses surfaced in the relationship between users and power users. According to the users, efficiency improvement, in terms of rapid data retrieval, was the most important result of DW adoption.

The users’ main goals were to answer their superiors’ requests and validate results. Their focus was upstream, to resolve the semantic ambiguity of the incoming requests, and downstream, to validate the results. The users expected the instrument to resolve any ambiguity, thus making activities as pre-determined as possible. The users did not interact proactively with the decision makers, moreover, their relationship with the technician unit was limited to reporting the information requirements of their superiors. Their activity was limited to updating the reports without modifying them in terms of contents or layout. They did not use even the basic computational facilities offered by the tool, preferring previously used tools. As one of them reported: ‘‘When I have to count performance indicators, average values and so on, I prefer to cut values from Business Object reports and paste them into a worksheet. I feel it’s easier and safer.’’ Business object provides a facility to export reports to worksheets that is completely ignored by these users.

However, the power users were autonomous in making and modifying simple reports and viewed the DW as a tool that stimulated and improved analysis, and increased efficiency by simplifying routine activities. They observed that the DW provided faster responses and stimulated new insight resulting from the interaction between diverse units. Soon power users realized that some data was not available and that they might have needed to obtain them from operational systems. Hence, there was a need for the DW to be constantly upgraded and improved based on power users’ feedback.

## 4.2.2. Perceived organizational outcomes

Users and power users evaluated the organizational outcomes differently. As a consequence of the increased efficiency, user activities were enlarged by adding new tasks not necessarily related to previous ones. Furthermore, the job division and the high formalization of Alpha severely limited co-operation among peers. This minimized the impact of the DW on coordination mechanisms.

Power users were aware of the innovative power of the DW but frequently reported scepticism from some of their colleagues and superiors. They enacted a limited use of technology-in-practice with the DW, arising from their fear that its use would threaten their status, increase their workload, or even result in losing their job.

## 5. Case Beta

## 5.1. Decision makers

In Beta, the decision makers belonged to the IS Department.

## 5.1.1. Expectations

Before introduction of the DW, information management was accomplished by in-house developed software. It was complex to use and required the assistance of a technician to develop reports that were not customizable.

The DW was introduced in the Italian branch to respond to local needs. The interviewees expressed the goals of the DW with the slogan: ‘‘Autonomy, democracy and independence through an equal distribution of information to all users.’’

## 5.1.2. Perceived organizational outcomes

A need for an interface between the work units of users and technicians emerged. At the beginning mediation was carried out informally by power users. After a few months, the decision makers proposed formalization of their role by creating the position of data analyst. Data analysts developed advanced skills in DW use that went beyond their traditional ones. They acted as internal sponsors of DW and co-operated to solve the problems concerning its use among different work units.

## 5.2. Users

The interviews involved users from many different work units, such as administration, finance, accounting and human resource management. A power user profile also emerged in Beta.

## 5.2.1. Expectations

Different expectations and uses emerged between users and power users. The users were not really interested in understanding or experimenting with DW. They aimed at improving their work efficiency through the DW and acted autonomously only in 5% of the cases. Most of the time, they resorted to power user’s support.

Some users pointed out that they did not have the technical competencies necessary for autonomy, as they were used to using the previous system that did not provide a user intervention capability. Instead, power users aimed at improving their own technical capabilities to become more autonomous. They understood the users’ needs and were able to communicate directly with the technicians. As one power user reported: ‘‘At the beginning (the users) came to me proposing a solution to their problems but most of the time they found out that their solution was not feasible or that their perceived problem was not a problem at all. Now they come to me with their problems and we find a solution together.’’

## 5.2.2. Perceived organizational outcomes

All the interviewees expressed satisfaction with the new data analyst position. They viewed this role as an effective ‘‘service’’ in the resolution of daily problems in use of the DW.

Power users observed a change in their role after they became data analysts. One data analyst said: ‘‘I felt like I was losing my role in the group, without acquiring a new one. At the end of the semester I would be evaluated on the basis of my formal tasks and I would find that I had been doing double work. Now, as a data analyst, I have more time to do a job with greater value added.’’

Data analysts interacted with technicians and their former departments to improve the contents and the usability of the system. As an interviewee reported: ‘‘The users now come to me expressing their informative needs, not just requiring data. I feel like I am an internal consultant for informative needs.’’

The data analyst role also included training activities that seemed to be appreciated. As a user said: ‘‘They (data analysts) are able to speak our language. They can give examples by using real data without comprehension problems or privacy issues.’’

## 6. Case Gamma

## 6.1. Decision makers

In Gamma, the decision makers belonged to the Marketing and Finance Departments.

## 6.1.1. Expectations

Before the introduction of the DW, information management was accomplished by in-house developed software. This system was inadequate, because of its low quality output reports. Therefore, the main goal of DW adoption was to improve the timeliness and quality of reports.

Gamma decided to outsource the development to a consulting company with the intention of later training its own personnel to maintain the system. The interaction between the would-be users and the technicians during the development phase was limited because of the extremely narrow goal: namely, improvement of the report format, regardless of its content. As one technician reported: ‘‘They (the users) gave us old reports and told us to reproduce exactly the same information in a better format.’’

## 6.1.2. Perceived organizational outcomes

According to the decision makers, the Information Systems work unit acquired a greater internal role by interacting much more often with users.

In particular, the decision makers, who had been completely autonomous due to their long experience with the old software, were forced to turn to technicians for creating their reports. As some said, ‘‘I personally feel like I’ve lost something but I do recognize the improved quality of the reports.’

## 6.2. Users

## 6.2.1. Expectations

The users stated that the tool increased efficiency. This was their only opinion about the instrument. As one reported: ‘‘Now work is faster. Before the DW, we had to batch process all procedures and check them in the morning. Now we don’t have to do that anymore.’’

The DW was used for daily operations, such as stock management and replenishment, and for monitoring activities.

## 6.2.2. Perceived organizational outcomes

The improvement in efficiency left more time for people to dedicate to other activities with consequent job enlargement. As a user reported: ‘‘Before DW adoption I spent time every morning checking procedures; now I’m involved in a new project of quality assessment that was my first interest.’’

## 7. Cross-case analysis and implications

Comparing the three cases, several different uses emerged. They appear to be influenced by the social expectations for the system and they impacted the organization in different ways. As regards social expectations, they seemed to be determined by the decision makers’ goals.

In Alpha, where the goals were directed towards centralization of information and decision making, technology emerged as an instrument of standardization and control. The desire to have a ‘‘unique, certified data source’’ was connected to the broader aim of reducing opportunistic behavior permitted by information asymmetry.

In Beta, where the goal was to increase ‘‘autonomy, democracy and independence’’ the same artifact contributed to the decentralization of the decision making process.

In Gamma, where the goals were limited to the improvement of clearness, readability and timeliness of reports, the DW contributed to increasing the efficiency and effectiveness of information management.

From a standpoint of organizational outcomes, diverse situations emerged.

In Gamma, where expectations towards the DW were limited to increasing efficiency and effectiveness, no major organizational outcomes were detected.

In Alpha and Beta, where the settled goals were farther-reaching than in Gamma, the common and central organizational outcome was the spontaneous emergence of power users.

In Beta, power users were sponsored and turned to better account through the creation of ad hoc positions formally dedicated to mediation. In this new position, they admitted that they could do a ‘‘more creative job, playing with data’’. By bringing their diverse cultural backgrounds, interacting with their own groups, speaking their former colleagues’ language and interacting with technicians, power users created new knowledge by adding new links between data, connecting ideas and perspectives, providing new aggregations, and extracting new rules from data. Power users became the mean to propose new contents and add new perspectives. The process of collaboration between experts working across boundaries, when they were able to appreciate and synergistically utilize their distinctive knowledge, has been referred to in the literature as the ‘‘perspective taking’’ process [3,4,17]. The power user in the formal mediation role acts as the ‘‘boundary person’’ making it possible for the DW to be a ‘‘boundary object’’ [7,41], as first introduced by Star and Griesemer [42]. They are defined as objects that work to establish a shared context: repositories (databases), standardized forms and methods (FMEA, etc.), objects or models (drawings), and maps of boundaries (Gantt chart). Many authors (see e.g., [36,46]) have recognized their importance in supporting shared information systems. Boundary persons activate interaction between diverse work units. These ‘‘knowledge activists’’ or ‘‘boundary-breaking managers’’ start up and coordinate the process of knowledge creation. The importance of creating an ad hoc role for this form of mediation is widely recognized [24]: ‘‘in some cases giving an explicit responsibility for knowledge has been a positive change. . .. The best idea is to identify a member for every micro-community and to give him the role of the knowledge activist.’’

Although some power users emerged in Alpha, their role was not formally recognized, and in such an organization, in which roles and responsibilities are clearly defined, non-formalization is a critical element. A boundary-breaking role in this kind of organization is difficult to perform, as it is frequently limited and opposed by consolidated routines as well as a change-aversive environment.

## 8. Conclusions

This study confirmed the validity of our approach, even though it was applied to non open-ended technology, such as the DW. In fact, it required mediation from the technical staff in order to customize the contents and the interfaces with the technology, and appeared to leave less room for individual improvisation. Nevertheless, different uses and results emerged from the cases and this seemed to be a consequence of decision makers’ different expectations and goals.

Another element that appeared to have an impact on the results was related to the role of the power user. Where one was recognized, motivated, and formalized by the organization, the DW went beyond its function as a mere warehouse of data and began acting as a boundary object.

Taking into account that this perspective is hardly new, we attempted to find new insight into how the expectations that guide the use of technology brought forth different and unexpected results.

While multiple informants from different hierarchical levels, triangulation using different types of data sources and a systematic data analysis served to attenuate many of the problems of reliability; however, generalizability remained an issue. Although they must be treated with caution, the findings of this exploratory study are nonetheless suggestive.
