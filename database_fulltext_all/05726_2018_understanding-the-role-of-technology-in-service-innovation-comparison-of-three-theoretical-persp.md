## Research highlights

► Investigation of the role of “technology in service innovation” and its impact on firm performance.

► Three competing models having different roles of technology are proposed.

► Technology plays multiple roles in service innovation.

► The role of technology differs depending on the specific type of service innovation.

## Abstract

Although prior studies have stressed the importance of technology in service innovation, debates on the roles of technology continue to surface. This study aims to investigate the role of technology in service innovation based on a service innovation framework. After identifying four innovation orientations, we propose three competing models having different roles of technology (direct, indirect, and moderating). Using data from 193 service firms, we determine which model best explains the role of technology. Results show that technology plays multiple roles in service innovation. Our study helps managers effectively coordinate their technology infusion into service innovation to improve firm performance.

Keywords: Service innovation, Technology, Strategic innovation orientation, Firm performance, Resourceadvantage theory, Technology-push perspective, Complementarity theory.

## 1. Introduction

The influence of service innovation on competitive advantage and business performance has increased noticeably in the past decade [90]. Firms must constantly consider revising their service offerings and service processes to survive in their market [11, 23]. Service innovation is an effective method for enhancing firm performance and ensuring long-term survival [69, 90]. However, service innovation fails to guarantee consistency in a successful outcome. Therefore, strategic orientation of service innovation has attracted considerable attention from business leaders who aim to achieve service innovation success and enhance their firm performance [26]. Service innovation orientation (SIO) refers to a strategic direction as to which service innovation activities are created and how to conduct and manage them effectively for a continuous superior performance of the business. SIO is a precondition for successful service innovation.

The effects of technology have been especially profound in those service areas, which traditionally rely on close, personal contact between customers and employees [22, 45]. Innovation expenditure in some service sectors, such as engineering, technical consultancy, computers, and software, is relatively close to the manufacturing average [45]. Technology is dramatically changing how services are conceived, developed, and delivered; it is being embedded into an ever-increasing range of services. Thus, the role and relevance of technology in service innovation are increasingly expanding in the current business environment [4, 39, 66]. This in turn is generating managerial issues and raising challenges regarding how firms can ensure service innovation success by using technology [8, 34, 48].

Although numerous studies have stressed the importance of technology in service innovation, ongoing debates on the strategic role of technology in service innovation continue without a clear theoretical base. Some studies consider technology as a trigger for other innovations [13, 59, 66, 95]; others view technology as an enabler of other innovations [30, 34, 48, 66]; still others consider technology as an innovator in its own right [27, 39, 60, 94]. Despite this contradictory situation in the service innovation literature, limited theoretical and empirical validation has been conducted. There is no theory-based framework to assist us in understanding the role of technology in service innovation.

Therefore, more detailed exploration and understanding are necessary for technology’s role in service innovation to meet the needs of theory and practice.

To bridge these research gaps, the current study aims to investigate the structural relationships among technology, service innovation, and firm performance based on a service innovation framework and determine the role of technology in service innovation and their ultimate influence on firm performance. For this purpose, four strategic SIOs—service creation orientation (SCO), service delivery orientation (SDO), customer interaction orientation (CIO), and technology orientation (TEO)—are identified, drawing from Den Hertog [19]’s service innovation framework. In addition, three different models are proposed. A simple direct effect (DE) model investigates the DE of TEO and three SIOs on firm performance, reflecting technology-push perspective. An indirect effect (IE) model based on the resource-advantage (R-A) theory explores the IEs of TEO on firm performance through the other three SIOs. A moderating effect (ME) model examines the ME of TEO between the three SIOs and firm performance based on the complementarity theory. Each model can be used to identify the different roles of technology in achieving service innovation success and improving firm performance. On the basis of empirical data collected from 193 service firms in Korea, these three models are tested and compared to determine which model best explains the role of technology in service innovation.

This study intends to make the following contributions. First, we offer an integrated service innovation framework that explicates the structure of service innovation from an integrative perspective. We propose the three service innovation models based on three different theoretical perspectives and examine which model best explains the relationships between service innovation dimensions. Second, drawing on these three theoretical perspectives, we attempt to theoretically conceptualize and argue the strategic role of technology in service innovation and its effect on firm performance. Thereby, this study extends the previous research by providing theoretical and empirical validations for the ongoing debate regarding the role of technology and its effect on service innovation. Practically, a better understanding of the multiple roles of technology in service innovation and its effect on firm performance can help practitioners coordinate technology infusion to achieve service innovation success and superior firm performance. Our findings provide valuable information to those who seek practical guidance in implementing successful service innovations in the service sector.

The rest of this paper is organized in the following manner. Section 2 discusses the theoretical basis of SIOs, service innovation dimensions, and three roles technology plays in innovation. Section 3 explains the three competing research models we developed. Section 4 gives the research methodology, and Section 5 explains the analysis and findings. Section 6 discusses the results and their theoretical and practical implications.

## 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1 Service innovation orientation

Service innovation refers to changes in various dimensions related to the characteristics of the service itself [19, 35]. Service innovation dimensions indicate the areas where service innovation occurs [25]. Service innovation dimensions are equivalent to the types of service innovation and are chosen strategically by firms [25]. An organization may be oriented toward innovation not only through single dimension but also through multiple dimensions. Therefore, the current study defines service innovation as a change either in specific innovation dimensions or in combinations of service innovation dimensions.

Innovation orientation refers to the strategic directions implemented by firms to create the proper innovation activity to ensure constant superior performance of the business [91]. Prior definitions agree that strategic direction for innovation involves clarity of purpose and is articulated generally through vision and mission statements and objectives for innovation activities. An important aspect of an innovation orientation is the creation of innovation behaviors and shared values throughout the entire organization. Given that strategic direction in service development processes plays a more critical role than those in product development processes [3], innovation orientation enables service managers to choose and perform specific innovation activities strategically to enhance firm performance [62]. Therefore, SIO is defined in this study as the strategic direction of firms toward service innovation practices to gain a sustainable, competitive advantage.

In this study, SIO should be understood within the service innovation dimension. Unlike innovation in manufacturing, it has been difficult to distinguish clearly between the well-known dichotomy of product/process innovations in service because of the simultaneous nature of services [84]. Thus, researchers have proposed alternative approaches in terms of service innovation dimensions (i.e., Gallouj and Weinstein [35]’s framework and Den Hertog [19]’s four-dimensional model). Den Hertog [19] proposed a service innovation framework and conceptualized four service innovation dimensions: service concept, service delivery, client interface, and technology (see Fig. 1).

![](/api/attachments/NSJVDXMC/fulltext/images/b54af5403bb6ac9b9df035319459c8e5a707d88d9a4b4c13fe1aff125059fd39.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Service innovation framework by Den Hertog [19].

Service concept refers to an interactive process of service prototype that offers a new value proposition in a particular market and covers both the descriptions of customer needs and the service offered by firms. Service delivery comprises sequential activities and an internal organizational arrangement for delivering a new or an existing service. Client interface refers to the interface design between a service provider and its clients. Technology refers to an innovation process to acquire new technologies and employ sophisticated technologies in the development of new products and services.

In this study, some dimensions need to be revised to effectively develop SIOs. First, the “service concept” dimension is redefined as the “service creation” dimension. The service concept involves in creating and offering new functions, contents, designs, and logic of services to the providing firms, the customers, and the market. In terms of the conceptual definition, the service creation activity is more important than the service concept itself, which is appropriate for developing SIOs. Moreover, the “client interface” dimension is redefined as the “customer interaction” dimension. Although similar to client interface, customer interaction includes the broader terms of co-production and customer contact<sup>3</sup> and therefore is more suitable for developing SIO than the concept of client interface. Finally, although optional in the service innovation framework, the technology dimension is redefined as an independent dimension of service innovation on equal terms with the other three service innovation dimensions.

In this study, we focused primarily on four service innovation dimensions to investigate the role of technology in service innovation. Although Den Hertog [19]’s service innovation framework suggested the relationships among four dimensions enabled by organizational capabilities, these capabilities were proposed without any theoretical justifications. Therefore, this study employs the independent four dimensions of service innovation framework (i.e., service creation, service delivery, customer interaction, and technology) to develop the following major strategic SIOs: (1) SCO, (2) SDO, (3) CIO, and (4) TEO.

## 2.2 Identifying the role of technology in service innovation

A steady increase in the infusion of technology into service businesses is occurring. An increasing number of service firms are investing in technological equipment and utilizing new technologies in their innovation process for greater innovation success. Many service innovation studies have posited that technology [e.g., Information Technology (IT)] is an important component/dimension of service innovation [21, 79, 93]. Technology assists firms in effectively creating a new service offering, enhancing, and enlarging the scope of their services, improving the service delivery process, and creating new market value [13, 79]. Technology is thus essential for service innovation success.

Despite numerous studies that stress the importance of technology in service innovation, debates regarding the role of technology in service innovation continue. The differing and conflicting roles of technology in service innovation have coexisted in previous studies, and this coexistence is confusing to researchers and practitioners with regard to the precise roles and effects of technology on service innovation and firm performance. The literature identifies three dominant roles of technology: (1) a trigger of innovation, (2) an enabler of innovation, and (3) a true innovator.

In information systems (IS) and innovation literature, one main stream of research proposes that technology can enhance the value of other firm resources, including innovation activities. It argues that technology alone has little to do with a firm’s competitive success because it can be duplicated and imitated easily by competitors [70]. This approach focuses on the proactive role of technology based on the R-A theory, which asserts that firms with a comparative advantage in resources achieve superior performance by occupying a position in the market place of competitive advantage. Thus, innovation plays a key role in the R-A theory. Some researchers, such as Froehle and Roth [33], Chen et al. [13], Zhang [95], Dougherty and Dunne [24], Nambisan [66], and Lusch and Nambisan [59], considered technology as a trigger of innovation that acts on other resources and practices to produce effects. Nambisan [66] described technology as a trigger or an initiator of innovation, which affects the innovation process and innovation outcome. Similarly, Ray et al. [76] and Dougherty and Dunne [24] emphasized the generative nature of the affordances of technology. They also stated that technology leverages the implementation of innovation, leading to the creation of a competitive advantage. Furthermore, Froehle and Roth [33] found that technology enhances the overall productivity of the service innovation process and assists in generating ideas for new services or service enhancement. Consequently, this approach emphasizes the IE of technology and assumes that technology indirectly induces better firm performance through service innovation activities.

The second approach considers technology an enabler or a facilitator of innovation that supports the implementation of innovation [34, 48, 54, 59, 66]. This approach is based on the complementarity theory [63, 83], which has been used to explain how coordinated rather than uncoordinated activities yield high return, in that, coordinated activities are mutually enabling and reinforce each other’s performance. Thus, this approach regards technology as a complementary factor that mutually enhances other elements of the firm. Technology has mainly played enabling and facilitating roles in innovation, which create favorable conditions for developing better products and services and achieving more profitable business deals [34]. Fichman and Nambisan [30] posited that the firms obtain more value when IT applications are fitted into product development system-specific complementary organizational elements (e.g., strategies, structures, processes, etc.). Huang [48] demonstrated that there is a synergistic effect between service innovation and IT on firm performance. Moreover, some studies from this approach focused on how the value generated by technology enhances the value of other innovation practices [70, 83]. The primary insight from these studies is the importance of integrating technology with other components of service innovation to ensure innovation success and realize the maximum service value from service innovation. Hence, this approach focuses on the ME of technology and regards technology as an enabler of innovation.

The last stream that emerged in the service innovation literature focuses on the strengthening role of a particular technology rather than the initiating or supporting roles in existing innovation activities [12, 27, 60, 94]. This stream finds that technology is being embedded into varied products and services themselves [94], thereby expanding and changing the role and relevance of technology in service innovation through current business environments [12, 66]. Hence, firms should think of technology not only as an integral part of services but also as an essential component of the service package. The third approach regards technology as an innovator in its own right by implementing new or improved services forming new technologies [12, 16, 20, 93]. This approach is based on a technology-push perspective, which posits that consumers prefer technologically superior products and services [36]. The technology-push perspective stems from the recognition of a new technological means for enhancing performance [36]. This approach considers technology as a salient dimension in the service innovation framework, which can directly affect firm performance. When the importance of technology in service innovation increases, the role of technology expands further from a trigger and enabler of service innovation to a more fundamental service innovation dimension. Thus, technology by itself can create new or improved services, which can have a DE on innovation success and firm performance, just like the other service innovation dimensions do. Although few empirical studies regard technology as a true innovator in service innovation, this approach could explain the expansion of technology in service innovation, which has in turn a DE on firm performance.

## 3. Three models of service innovation orientation

This study proposes three causal relationships in the basic form of SIO-firm performance to understand the role of technology in service innovation and its effect on firm performance. We assume that the SIO of firms is positively related to firm performance (a basic form of SIO-firm performance); then we develop four SIOs (i.e., SCO, SDO, CIO, and TEO) based on the service innovation framework proposed by Den Hertog [19]. This study also assumes that only TEO’s role can be changeable, while the four SIOs are independent of each other at the strategic level. The three roles of technology in service innovation are identified as follows: a true innovator (DE), a trigger (IE), and an enabler (ME). This study attempts not only to justify the structural relationships between four SIOs by developing three alternative models from three different theoretical perspectives but also to determine which one is the most appropriate model that can best explain the role of technology through these three SIOs. The three different models are displayed in Figs. 2, 3, and 4, respectively.

## 3.1 Simple direct effect model based on technology-push perspective

In the innovation literature, one main research stream stresses the strengthening role of technology, thereby considering technology an essential innovation dimension in service innovation. Technology in service innovation is a true innovator, which directly affects firm performance. Thus, this simple DE model proposes the basic relationship of SIO-firm performance by simply presenting the four SIOs as independent variables and making firm performance a dependent variable. This model is a simple basic model (i.e., independent-dependent variables), as shown in Fig. 2.

![](/api/attachments/NSJVDXMC/fulltext/images/25b791a5b58e95b719afc4598817b4bf390aabea45d2a9e12406ac76250eacd9.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Simple direct effect model based on technology-push perspective.

Service innovation orientations as independent variables: We employ the four SIOs (i.e., SCO, SDO, CIO, and TEO) based on Den Hertog [19]’s service innovation framework. SCO involves frequently changing service offers to customers and determining how such change creates new value [19]. SCO aims to provide customers with novel value from new services through service creation innovation and thus constantly seeks new opportunities to meet customer needs from new innovative services. Although SCO takes several risks in developing new services, it improves firms competitiveness and increases firm performance. SDO alters where, when, and how a service product is delivered to the customer and thereby offers differentiated services. SDO provides customers with greater convenience, which comes from an effective delivery process resulting from service delivery innovation and frequently offers services to customers at low cost. In turn, an effective delivery process strengthens the firm’s positive image and reputation [13]. Hence, firms adopting SDO can easily improve the efficiency of their service delivery processes to achieve better firm performance. Moreover, the process by which the service provider interacts with its customers could be a source of innovation [20]. In this case, CIO concentrates on how deeply (i.e., customer co-production) and to what degree (i.e., customer contact) customers are involved in the service innovation processes, including service design, production, and consumption. CIO tends to create a personal and open relationship between customers and service providers. Furthermore, feedback from customers can shape other innovation activities [19]. This finding leads to a better understanding of customer needs, increased customer satisfaction, and provision of higher quality services, resulting in better firm performance. Consequently, we present the following hypotheses:

$H _ { I - I \mathrm { : } }$ Service creation orientation (SCO) has a positive effect on firm performance.

$H _ { I - 2 } ;$ Service delivery orientation (SDO) has a positive effect on firm performance.

$H _ { I - 3 ; \ l }$ Customer interaction orientation (CIO) has a positive effect on firm performance.

Business history offers many examples of industries (e.g., lighting, photography, steel, and telecommunication) in which new technologies emerge and eventually overwhelm established technologies [86]. In each industry, some service firms fail to adopt new technologies in their services, so they fail to survive in the marketplace because customers prefer services with higher technologies. In this case, technology is not a trigger or an enabler of innovation but rather a breakthrough innovator that can create different market positions in service firms. For example, FinTech is an emerging and innovative financial service (or sector) that was built on highly innovative and disruptive technologies [55], resulting in disruptive innovation of the financial industry and the creation of new financial service value [56, 60]. Ernst and Young [27] indicated that in the traditional FinTech services (ebanking), IT plays the role of a facilitator, improving a service efficiency of the value chain, while in the emergent FinTech services, IT plays the role of a disrupter, which disrupts or substitutes the existing value chain and offers new services through new channels. Previous studies have suggested technology as an independent variable of service innovation that directly affects firm performance [20, 45, 87, 92, 93].

Given the expanding role of technology in services in the current environment, TEO in SIO has the potential to create new markets, shape consumers’ preferences, and change customers’ basic behaviors. Thus, TEO can contribute significantly to the profitability of a service firm because it provides benefits to mainstream customers. Accordingly, this model regards TEO as an independent service innovation dimension, along with the other three SIOs, which all directly affect firm performance. We therefore propose the following hypothesis:

$H _ { I - 4 } ;$ Technology orientation (TEO) has a positive effect on firm performance.

## 3.2 Indirect effect model based on resource-advantage theory

An IE model is influenced by previous studies from IS and innovation literature, which generally regard technology as a critical precondition of various innovation practices. Technology can purposefully leverage service innovation activities, which indirectly influence firm performance [13, 59]. Hence, the role of technology is a trigger or an initiator of service innovation, which influences other competences. Ultimately, it leads to the creation of a competitive advantage.

The IE model is proposed to provide a better conceptual understanding of firm performance by placing the three SIOs as intervening variables between TEO and firm performance. Thus, this model changes the basic form of SIO-firm performance into a causal model, where TEO is a precondition of the other three SIOs, and these SIOs positively influences firm performance. It suggests that TEO indirectly affects firm performance through the other three SIOs. This causal model is shown in Fig. 3.

Technology orientation as an antecedent variable: Given that SCO focuses on different market positions achieved mostly through the introduction of new services, TEO allows firms to encourage and leverage the development and application of the new services concept [78] by improving the initial base of knowledge through the integration of IS and the tacit skills of human resources [21]; this effectively assists the firm in achieving innovation success [32]. Furthermore, technology can change organizational forms into virtual organizational forms, which are similar to organic structures and promote creativity and innovation [77]. Thus, TEO positively affects SCO.

Competitive positioning to pursue SDO depends mainly on TEO, which can effectively deliver service offerings to customers. Chen et al. [13] emphasized that technology is the key driver that leads to the success of service delivery innovation. Technology facilitates the creation of a new channel or method of service delivery based on a codified knowledge pool [50, 85]. TEO is more critical in SDO because firms pursuing SDO depend on their ability to increase organizational efficiency. Many researchers have also stressed the efficiency-enhancing role of technology in organizations [49, 89]. Likewise, TEO rapidly and efficiently assists organizations in tending to customer needs. Such efficiency in satisfying changes in customer demands improves customer satisfaction [10]. TEO increases the speed of communication to enable a rapid response to changes in customer needs [57]. TEO can also offer a new online channel to customers to provide them with direct access and coproduction, thereby improving customer satisfaction [73]. For example, firms can collaborate with customers by forming distinct online communities in which customers can coproduce services with firms. Given the importance of completely understanding specific customer needs and facilitating effective linkage between service providers and customers in CIO, TEO can assist firms in effectively implementing CIO. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

$H _ { 2 - 4 ; }$ Technology orientation (TEO) has a positive effect on service creation orientation (SCO). $H _ { 2 - 5 } .$ Technology orientation (TEO) has a positive effect on service delivery orientation (SDO). $H _ { 2 - 6 \cdot } \cdot$ Technology orientation (TEO) has a positive effect on customer interaction orientation (CIO).

## 3.3 Moderating effect model based on the complementarity theory

In increasingly dynamic competitive environments, technology leadership is no longer sufficient for service firms to secure a critical advantage [61]. IS and innovation literature suggest that investment in IT systems cannot stimulate productivity and growth without a number of complementary developments [63]. They indicate that synergies exist among these technologies and developments based on the complementarity theory. A ME model specifically suggests that TEO has a ME on firm performance by interacting with the other three SIOs. While the IE model considers TEO as an antecedent of the other three SIOs, the ME model argues that technology is understood as a specific SIO that can interact with the other three SIOs.

#

Subtle differentiations are required for innovative services, which provide the unique selling propositions of each firm. However, difficulties are frequently encountered because new services created by a firm are vaguely defined and tested. From this point, technology enables firms to develop the service concept effectively, thereby meeting both the needs of the firms themselves and of their customers. For example, technological methods such as virtual reality systems<sup>4</sup> [82] and service theaters [40] provide similar test environments for the visualization of service ideas and service concepts at an early stage. Visualizations of the service concept through technology make SCO possible for customers, employees, and managers to obtain a first impression of the new service and to offer a profound feedback for improvement before the actual market launch [61]. Some benefits from the interaction between SCO and TEO result in the development of synergies, which lead to a competitive advantage in the form of service differentiation.

The infusion of technology in the service delivery process has a dramatic effect on the nature of core offerings because service providers traditionally deliver their services from person to person [52]. TEO combined with SDO enhances the firms’ abilities, thereby enabling them to provide their services with shorter delivery times [50] and monitor their deliveries [85], which leads to increased customer convenience and satisfaction. Dabholkar [17] indicated how technology-based services have made new service delivery options available to firms, allowing for possibly wider customer participation. Easy access to services and the introduction of new service delivery methods have been extended through TEO, which allow consumers to use services anywhere. For example, customers simply sitting behind their desk use electronic devices (including smartphones and tablets) to buy a service product and easily monitor the delivery process of their own service through these devices.

Service innovation literature says that technology may enable firms to change their interaction design and coordination practices with customers (e.g., a customer relationship management system). Bitner et al. [5] indicated that technology can be used independently by customers to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of their own service experience by enabling customization, improving service recovery, and providing spontaneous delight, all leading to increased customer satisfaction.

Technology enables customers to make services fit their individual needs. For example, databases allow key customer information to be shared throughout the organization and used by anyone who encounters the customer, which improves customer retention through satisfaction [43]. When failures in service offerings occur, new technologies result in increased customer accessibility to communicate through email, cellular phones, and other communication media, thereby reducing several problems and limiting the customer complaint rate through effective service recovery [7]. The effective use of technology is essential to a firm’s ability to please customers and enhance their satisfaction. Moreover, customer co-innovation and value co-creation are established effectively in the innovation process through technology (e.g., virtual customer environment) [68].

Overall, a strong TEO may have a positive influence on the strength of the relationship between the three SIOs and firm performance. Consequently, we posit that the influence of the three SIOs on firm performance is moderated by TEO, as shown in Fig. 4. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1 Development of measures

In this study, we developed our measures on the basis of an intensive literature review to obtain content validity. Unless otherwise indicated, all measures were based on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from “extremely low (1)” to “extremely high (5).” The structure of all variables used in this study is shown in Appendix A.

Using the service innovation framework of Den Hertog [19], we identified the role of technology by developing four strategic SIO variables: SCO, SDO, CIO, and TEO. Multiple-item measures of the four SIOs were drawn from previous studies on service innovation [13, 19, 28]. No empirically validated measures for service innovation dimensions have been established, and hence, we developed completely new items following the three steps (i.e., item creation, scale development, and instrument testing) proposed by Moore and Benbasat [65]. In the item creation stage, we generated an initial item pool for each SIO item, including items from previous instruments and newly created items. These items were then re-evaluated to eliminate redundant or ambitious items for content validity. In the scale development stage, service innovation experts sorted the items from the first stage into separate SIO constructs based on their similarities and differences. These items were then examined on the basis of the constructs, and ambiguous items were eliminated. The various scales were then combined into an overall instrument for the instrument testing stage, in which we distributed the instrument to a sample of 30 service firms. The responses were then analyzed to ensure the adequacy of the mechanics that were employed to compile the survey results. The scales were further refined before the main survey was conducted.

We then considered firm performance as a dependent variable. In the context of innovation, nonfinancial performance of innovation has received considerable attention because it helps decisionmakers predict future financial performance [58]. In this study, firm performance was measured as nonfinancial performance, such as customer satisfaction based on responsiveness to customer needs, customer satisfaction improvement, and service offering customization [51].

Finally, we incorporated firm age, firm size, industry type, and total sales volume as control variables. Firm age, measured by the number of years a firm has existed, was controlled because this factor affects the firm’s external legitimacy in its relationship with other firms, staying power, and intensity of actions [29]. Firm size, measured by the number of employees, was also controlled because it may reflect the current and past performance of the firm [75]. Industry type was controlled through classifications for transport, communication, computer and software, engineering and architecture, business consulting, and design services. Total sales volume, measured by total sales in 2010, was controlled because it is the most popular measure of sales organization effectiveness [15].

## 4.2 Sample and data collection

Before the main survey, a pilot test was conducted to examine the reliability and validity of the newly developed measures by conducting a focus group interview with 30 managers. The questionnaire was developed including 21 items to measure the SIO variables and seven items related to firm performance. Among the 21 items, on the basis of comments from a focus group interview, five items were removed to improve the survey’s face validity. The pilot test then led to significant refinement and restructuring of the questionnaire and establishing internal validity of the measures. The responses were analyzed for discriminant and convergent validity of the target constructs through factor analysis. During the factor analysis, four items for the SIO variables and three items for firm performance whose factor loading values were lower than 0.5 were dropped from further analysis. Finally, analysis was performed on 11 items that measured the SIO variables and three items for firm performance.

The Korean version of all the measures was created by following Brislin [6]’s translation-backtranslation procedure. In the main survey, data were collected by distributing questionnaires to Korean service firms. The survey focused on the four SIOs and firm performance within the past 3 years. Hence, respondents who had implemented at least one service innovation activity between 2008 and 2010 were asked to complete the entire questionnaire. We randomly selected the survey sample from the entire population of Korean service firms based on the Korean Innovation Survey in the Service Industry (http://kist.stepi.re.kr). The questionnaire was distributed to 856 service firms by email, fax, postal mail, and personal interview. Out of the 205 responses we received, 12 outliers were eliminated. A total of 193 responses were determined to be useful for this study, indicating a usable response rate of 22.5%.

To minimize single informant bias, the study captured respondents' experience in the firm and industry by answering certain questions [18]. We then examined the titles and biographies of these respondents to identify the most appropriate and knowledgeable informant. Furthermore, before the main analysis, common method bias (CMB) was tested. For the CMB, this study followed suggestions proposed by Podsakoff et al. [71] to reduce the potential of skewness in the survey’s design by ensuring anonymity and by separating data sources for the predictor and criterion variables (i.e., four SIOs were reported by innovation managers, while the firm characteristics and performance were reported by operation managers). In addition, we performed three different analyses to test the effect of CMB. First, we performed Harman’s single factor test. If CMB is a serious problem in the collected data, we can expect either a single factor to emerge from a factor analysis or one general factor to account for the majority of the covariance among all measures. We conducted a factor analysis on all items using SPSS. The results demonstrated that five factors (four independent and one dependent) were extracted with an eigenvalue greater than one, and no general factor was apparent in the unroasted factor structure, indicating no excessive CMB. Second, a partial correlation approach was used, following Podsakoff and Organ [72]. The AMOS model included the highest factor from the principle component factor analysis as a control variable on the dependent variable. This factor did not produce any change in variance explained by the dependent variable, which suggests no evidence of CMB. Finally, we performed a single-method factor analysis in AMOS by having indicators measure their theoretical constructs and a common method latent construct, and rerunning the structural model, following Podsakoff et al. [71]. The results did not change. Overall, the results of the three tests suggest that CMB is not of great concern.

Unfortunately, we could not test for nonrespondent bias because several nonrespondents were not under the listed firms. Therefore, obtaining their firm-related information was almost impossible. Hence, we classified our respondents into two different groups, namely early and late responders [1], to examine nonresponse bias across the data sample. The first group (n=80) responded in the first and second weeks after the questionnaires were distributed. The second group (n=113) responded in the third and fourth weeks. The two groups were compared in terms of their total sales and number of employees, as suggested by Babbie [2]. No differences were observed between the two groups in terms of total sales (t=-0.123, p=0.902) and number of employees (t=0.512, p=0.609). Although these results did not totally remove nonrespondent bias, they suggest that the respondents were consistent and homogenous.

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the respondents. The sample was stratified across six subsectors in the service sector. Many of the responses came from engineering and architecture (21.2%), communication (21.2%), transport (18.7%), and computer and software (17.6%). The mean of firm age was 14.0 years with a standard deviation (SD) of 9.3 years. The mean number of employees was 99.1 (SD=211.5). The mean total sales was US\$ 17.3 million (SD=35.0).

## 5. Analysis and findings

Structural equation modeling (SEM) through AMOS 20.0 was used to test the proposed models in Figs. 2, 3, and 4. As recommended by Gefen et al. [37], a two-stage analytical process was employed for data analysis. First, the measurement model was assessed to determine how observed items load on the constructs in the model. Then, the assessment of the structural model allowed for hypothesis testing by examining the relationships among the constructs.

## 5.1 Measurement model

In the first step, we estimated the measurement model using AMOS 20.0. Overall, the resulting fit indices suggested an acceptable fit, as summarized in Table 2(a) [38]. Most indices were above their ideal criterion levels: chi-square/degrees of freedom $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } ) { = } 0 . 8 6 5 .$ , goodness of fit index (GFI)=0.964, adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI)=0.938, normed fit index (NFI)=0.940, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA)=0.000, standardized root mean residual (SRMR)=0.043, Tucker-Lewis index (TLI)=1.000, and comparative fit index (CFI)=1.000. In addition to evaluating each index independently, we followed a strict combination rule proposed by Hu [47]. This rule has the advantage of controlling Type I and Type II errors simultaneously: (1) SRMR < 0.08 and (2) either CFI > 0.95 or $\mathrm { R M S E A } < 0 . 0 6$ . Results in Table 2(a) show that indices of the measurement model comply with the combination rule, further supporting the acceptability of the measurement model.

To validate our measurement model, three types of item validity were assessed: content, convergent, and discriminant [42]. Content validity refers to the representativeness and comprehensiveness of the items used to create a scale. It was first established by interviewing practitioners and pilot-testing the measures. Convergent validity was assessed by assessing the composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) from the measures [42]. As shown in Table 2(b), the obtained CR values ranged from 0.741 to 0.817, which exceeded the threshold value of 0.7 [14]. The AVE ranged from 0.501 to 0.666, which was above the acceptable value of 0.5 [31]. The discriminant validity of the instrument was verified by checking the square root of the AVE, as recommended by Fornell and Larcker [31]. As shown in Table 2(c), the results revealed that the square root of the AVE for each construct was greater than the correlations between it and all other constructs. Overall, these results showed that the measurement models were strongly supported by the data gathered. In addition to the validity assessment, we checked the multicollinearity of the measurement model. Multicollinearity may exist among independent variables. The multicollinearity for all variables was examined with the variance inflation factor (VIF), and the VIF values for the constructs were acceptable (1.065–1.387).

## 5.2 Structural model

With adequate measurement models and an acceptable level of multicollinearity, the three proposed models of Figs. 2, 3, and 4 were tested. Figs. 5, 6, and 7 illustrate the results of AMOS estimation in the three structural models. The best approach to model assessment is to compare the proposed basic model with two other models that explain the different roles of technology in service innovation. This step is relevant particularly in SEM. While a model could have an acceptable fit, this feature alone does not guarantee that a better model does not exist [46].

Therefore, in this study, the simple DE model was compared with two other models in terms of the following criteria: (1) overall fit measures, (2) the percentage of the model’s hypothesized paths that are statistically significant, (3) the ability to explain the variance as measured by the square multiple correlation (SMC), and (4) the model power or the ability to detect and reject a poor model. ## 5.2.1 Testing the simple direct effect model

As summarized in Table 3, the overall model fit of the simple DE model was good. The chi-square result was nonsignificant $( \chi ^ { 2 } { = } 1 3 8 . 6 , p { = } 0 . 1 0 6 )$ . Analysis of the results showed that all absolute fits (GFI=0.928, RMSR=0.063, and RMSEA=0.029) and incremental fit measures (AGFI=0.896, TLI=0.975, NFI=0.879, and CFI=0.980) were satisfactory. Although NFI value (NFI=0.879) was slightly lower than 0.9, the NFI was acceptable [41, 74]. For the parsimonious fit index, one applicable measure for evaluating a single model is the normed chi-square measure. The value for the simple DE model, 1.164, was found to be within acceptable threshold limits (1.0–2.0 or 3.0).

Fig. 5 shows the results of the simple DE model, including the path loadings and significant level of the paths. Among the four hypothesized paths, three were significant (75%) at the levels of 0.05 and 0.01. SCO had a significant positive effect on firm performance $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 0 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , and therefore, $H _ { I - I }$ was supported. SDO was also positively related to firm performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which supported $H _ { I - 2 }$ . Moreover, CIO was found to be positively significant on the degree of firm performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 0 , p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ , and thus, $H _ { I - 3 }$ was supported. However, the results showed that the effect of TEO was not significant with regard to firm performance, indicating that TEO—unlike the other three SIOs—did not influence firm performance directly. Hence, $H _ { I - 4 }$ was not supported. In the simple DE model, none of the four control variables (i.e., firm age, firm size, industry type, and total sales volume) affected the firm performance. The four SIOs explained approximately 30.9% of the variance in firm performance. The power (0.889) of the simple DE model was above the desired level of 0.8 and was acceptable.

## 5.2.2 Testing the indirect effect model

The IE model was tested using the data gathered, as shown in Table 3 and Fig. 6. Table 3 shows that the chi-square of the IE model was not significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } { = } 1 3 8 . 4 , p { = } 0 . 1 2 1 )$ . All absolute fits (GFI=0.927, RMSR=0.065, and RMSEA=0.028) and incremental fits (AGFI=0.897, TLI=0.976, NFI=0.879, and CFI=0.982) slightly favored the IE model. In addition, the parsimonious goodness-of-fit measure of the IE model (PGFI=0.651) was slightly higher than that of the simple DE model (PGFI=0.645). Five of the six (83.3%) hypothesized paths in the IE model were supported, as shown in Fig. 6. By contrast, three of the four (75%) hypothesized paths in the simple DE model were supported at the same level.

In the IE model, TEO was significantly related to SCO $( \beta { = } 0 . 3 0 4 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , which supports $H _ { 2 - 4 }$ Furthermore, TEO had a positive and significant effect on SDO $( \beta = 0 . 3 6 2 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ ), thereby supporting $H _ { 2 - 5 . }$ However, TEO was not found to be significantly related to CIO, thereby not supporting $H _ { 2 - \ell }$ <sub>6</sub>. These results indicated that only two SIOs—SCO and SDO—are more likely shaped by TEO as an antecedent or a precondition of service innovation; CIO does not require TEO as a precondition to improve firm performance. Furthermore, SCO had a significant positive effect on firm performance $( \beta { = } 0 . 3 2 0 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , meaning that SCO could enhance firm performance. SDO was also positively related to firm performance (β=0.259, $p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ . Moreover, CIO was also found to be positively significant on the degree of firm performance (β=0.224, p<0.05). The results indicate that service firms could improve their performance through the three SIO practices (i.e., SCO, SDO, and CIO), supporting $H _ { 2 - l } ,$ $H _ { 2 - 2 , }$ and $H _ { 2 - 3 . }$ None of the control variables were related to firm performance.

The results show that the DE of three SIOs and the IE of TEO explained about 30.5% of the variance in firm performance. Comparing the SMC by firm performance between the simple DE and IE models (30.9% and 30.5%), the difference was only 0.004. Because differences of 0.06–0.09 are considered to be indicative of substantial model difference [42], no difference in SMC between the simple DE and IE models was detected.

As shown in Figs. 5 and 6, little difference in parsimony was observed between the simple DE and IE models even though the roles of technology were different. When comparing the simple DE and IE models using the parsimonious normed fit index (PNFI) and parsimonious goodness-of-fit index (PGFI), the values of the IE model (PNFI=0.690 and PGFI=0.651) were slightly higher than those of the simple DE model (PNFI=0.684 and PGFI=0.645). Given that differences of 0.06–0.09 indicated substantial model difference [42], no important difference was observed in the PNFI and PGFI values between the simple DE and IE models although the IE model had a bigger percentage of significant paths than that of the simple DE model (75% vs. 83.3%). The power (0.905) of the IE model was acceptable. A comparison of the two models shows that the power value of the IE model was higher (0.905) than that of the simple DE model (0.889).

The mediation effect test for the IE model was conducted in two different ways. First, as suggested by Mithas et al. [64], mediation tests were performed for all possible paths in the model to see whether additional direct paths, unspecified in the hypothesized research model, would be significant. As shown in Table 4(a), the first row presents the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values for the hypothesized model, a set of values which serve as a baseline for $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference tests of direct paths from antecedents to consequences. The second row displays the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values for the hypothesized model with a newly added direct path from TEO to firm performance. Considering that the difference in $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value between the baseline model (M1) and the other model (M2) is not significant and CIO has insignificant effect on firm performance, we can conclude that two types of SIO (i.e., SCO and SDO) fully mediate the effect of firm performance.

Second, to substantiate our findings of the mediation effects of SCO and SDO, a Sobel test was conducted [81]. This technique examines whether a mediating variable significantly carries the influence of an independent variable to a dependent variable by computing the ratio of the mediator path to its estimated standard error. If a p-value for this ratio is significant, we can conclude that SCO and SDO mediate between TEO and firm perfomance. Results are presented in Table 4(b). The IEs of TEO on firm performance are significant at the level of 0.05 or 0.01, as indicated by the Sobel test. Overall, the results of mediation analyses show that SCO and SDO fully mediate the effect of firm performance, although the extent of mediation varies depending on the specific innovation types.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1 Discussion of findings

The objective of this study was to incorporate empirical studies into the service innovation literature to investigate the different roles of technology in an integrated service innovation framework. We employed four SIOs (i.e., SCO, SDO, CIO, and TEO) to investigate the different roles of technology founded on the basic form between SIOs and firm performance. Three models were validated in the context of the SIO-firm performance relationship. Although the customary goodness of fit measures exhibited an acceptable fit for all three models formulated, overall fit measures favored the IE model. The IE model showed that the antecedent role of technology influenced firm performance. Moreover, this influence was only achieved through the intervening variables, SCO and SDO. Clearly, the IE of TEO on firm performance through SCO and SDO was significant, whereas that of TEO through CIO was not significant.

In terms of model parsimony, the overall fit of the simple DE and ME models was similar to that of the IE model. The objective of parsimony is not to minimize the number of coefficients or to maximize the fit. Instead, the goal is to maximize the fit contributed by each estimated coefficient, which suggests choosing the model with the maximum value of overall fit among the three models if their parsimonious fit values are equally acceptable [42]. Therefore, the goodness-of-fit measures of the IE model—except for the goodness of fit (GFI) and root mean square residual (RMSR) measures—were higher than those of the simple DE and ME models. The percentage of significant paths in the IE model was higher than those of the simple DE and ME models. In short, among the 14 measures over the four items in the criteria, 10 measures favored the IE model based on an R-A theory (See Table 3).

Our empirical analysis reveals several findings. First, our results suggest that technology is a precondition for conducting both SCO and SDO to achieve better firm performance, but particularly SDO. Technology alone has little to do with firm performance and should precede two types of service innovation activities (SCO and SDO). This result could be interpreted as technology having an antecedent role for encouraging and leveraging service innovation. Technology enhances the initial knowledge base of firms and changes the organizational structure into being flexible. These features could promote creativity and the innovation-favorable condition of firms before service innovation activities [21, 44], which effectively helps the firms implement SCO. Moreover, technology enables the entire firm to increase effectiveness and efficiency. Consistent with the literature [13, 49, 88], our results confirm that the high level of effectiveness and efficiency of firms through technology should precede SDO, which leads to the success of SDO. Our study shows that the effect of technology on SDO is greater than its effect on SCO because technology is closely linked with SDO.

Second, our results show that CIO and TEC have a synergistic effect on firm performance. Many studies on IS and management literature have asserted a moderating role of technology in innovation [5, 61, 63, 66, 82]. Interestingly, the effect of CIO with TEO on firm performance is greater than that of CIO alone, while the effect of SCO and SDO alone on firm performance is greater than that of SCO and SDO with TEO. A possible explanation for this result is that TEO has no synergistic effect with SCO and SDO but is only a precondition for SCO and SDO, consistent with the discussion in the previous paragraph. The synergistic effect exists only between CIO and TEO because technology intervenes in service innovation as an enabler to effectively change the interaction process with customers (e.g., customization, service recovery, spontaneous delight, and customer co-creation) [5, 67]. These features enable firms to differentiate their core services from their competitors and ultimately increase customer satisfaction. Therefore, our findings emphasize the importance of the interaction between CIO and TEO for the successful development of service innovation, which, in turn, could improve firm performance.

Although the role of technology in service innovation is expanding in the current business environment, the emerging and increasing role of technology was not found in this study. The findings of this study indicate that technology leadership in service innovation is not sufficient to directly enhance firm performance. A possible explanation for this intriguing result is that it is limited in capturing the business phenomenon only using an organizational survey because expanding the role of technology in service is still ongoing, but not a general innovation in service firms. Technology can play a role of a disruptor or an innovator when it disrupts or substitutes the existing value chain and offering new service through new channels [27]. At this time, the reorganization of business ecosystems should accompany to expand and strengthen the role of technology in service, which leads to the disruptive innovation of the entire service domain. Thus, the service firms face the challenge of expanding the role of technology in service, not in the short term, but in the long term. Therefore, many service firms still provide their traditional services with technology whose role is to support and improve the efficiency and effectiveness of other organizational activities although the number of service firms which provide emergent services with disruptive technology is increasing.

The results of this study also indicate the importance of nontechnological innovation in service innovation. Our finding lies in two innovation typologies: the nontechnological innovation and technological innovation [9]. New services development in service firms traditionally depends more on nontechnological innovation activities than on technological innovation activities because of the nature of the services [22, 80]. Given the nontechnological characteristics of the three SIOs (i.e., SCO, SDO, and CIO), our results show that nontechnological innovation dimensions are still more critical than technological innovation dimension for service firms in achieving their innovation success and improving firm.

Our results emphasize the key role of SCO in better firm performance. Among the four SIOs, SCO has the greatest effect on firm performance. This is because developing SCO is usually interrelated with other specific innovation activities, such as improving the service delivery process, changing the methods of customer interaction, and adopting equipment with new technology. Other types of service innovation are usually defined together when designing and developing new service concepts to meet customers’ needs. Thus, SCO is the most central and innovative form of service innovation, which leads to superior firm performance in service firms.

On the basis of our findings, we propose one model (see Fig. 8) that best explains the multiple roles of technology depending on the specific types of service innovation. This model suggests that TEO indirectly and positively influences firm performance through SCO and SDO and interacts with CIO to improve firm performance. The overall model fit of the best explaining model was good. The chisquare of this model was not significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } { = } 1 6 4 . 4 , ~ p { = } 0 . 0 5 )$ at the 0.05 level. All absolute fits (GFI=0.921, RMSR=0.066, and RMSEA=0.033) and incremental fits (AGFI=0.890, TLI=0.965, NFI=0.862, and CFI=0.972) were satisfactory. The value for this model, 1.209, was found to be within acceptable threshold limits. In addition, the parsimonious goodness-of-fit measure of the best explaining model (PGFI=0.659) was the highest than those of the three models (simple DE=0.645, IE=0.651, and ME=0.656). As six of the six (100%) hypothesized paths in the best explaining model were supported, the percentage of significant paths in this model was also the highest than those of the three models (simple DE=75%, IE=83.3%, and ME=66.7%).

As shown in Fig. 8, all the proposed hypotheses were found significant at the level of 0.01 and 0.05. TEO was found to be significantly related to SCO (β=0.301, p<0.01) and SDO (β=0.363, p<0.01). We also found that three types of SIOs (i.e., SCO, SDO, and CIO) have a positive significant effect on firm performance (β=0.317, p<0.01; β=0.285, p<0.05; β=0.187, p<0.05). The effect of CIO with TEO enhanced firm performance (β=0.243, p<0.01) beyond what CIO alone improved (β=0.187, p<0.05). ## 6.2 Theoretical and practical implications

The results of this study bear several theoretical implications for researchers. First, this study offers an integrative service innovation framework, which explicates the structure of service innovation based on theoretical bases. The two dominant service innovation frameworks (i.e., Gallouj and Weinstein’s service innovation framework [35] and Den Hertog’s four dimension model [19]) in the previous literature were not sufficient to describe the structural relationships among various innovation dimensions. Therefore, this study conceptualized three competing models based on three different theoretical perspectives and empirically determined which model can best explain the structural relationships among different innovation dimensions. The results of this study show that one conceptual model cannot explain the structural relationships among service innovation dimensions, although the IE model seems to be better than the simple DE and ME models. Therefore, this study derived a “best explaining” model by integrating with two theoretical bases—the R-A theory and complementary theory—which can provide clearer explanations of the structure of service innovation. Offering a new integrative model of service innovation is one of the most important theoretical contributions of this study.

Second, the findings of this study help resolve the ongoing debate in the literature regarding the role of technology in service innovation. Although several studies have stressed the importance of technology in service innovation, few studies have theoretically identified and empirically validated

#

the roles of technology in service innovation. Hence, the effects of technology on service innovation and firm performance have thus been assessed through perceptual rather than actual measures. To fill the gap, this study developed four SIOs based on Den Hertog’s service innovation framework [19] and empirically validated the three roles of technology in service innovation with three different theoretical perspectives. The results of this study indicate that the antecedent role of technology based on the R-A theory is more effective on service innovation and firm performance than the moderating role or the innovator role. However, our findings do not mean that only the R-A theory is appropriate to explain the role of technology in service innovation. The complementary theory is also suitable to understand the role of technology when service firms conduct customer interaction innovations. Furthermore, our findings show that the technology-push theory fails to interpret the current role of technology in new service development, while it does explain the role of technology in the new product development. It indicates that nontechnological innovation factors—such as high informationintangible contents of service products, high qualified employees, efficient delivery processes, service delights, and intensive customer interactions—are more critical for service innovation success than technological ones, thereby suggesting that technology does not directly influence firm performance. To the best of our knowledge, this study is an early attempt to empirically investigate the different roles of technology in service innovation.

Third, the results of this study show that technology can play multiple roles in service innovation at the same time. These results are consistent with previous research showing that technology indirectly or moderately influences firm performance as a trigger or an enabler of innovation, but this study takes these ideas further. Previous research stressed only one static role of technology in the innovation process, but our study illustrates that multiple roles can occur in practice because service innovation in firms is generally conducted as combinations of service innovation dimensions. Our findings show that while TEO indirectly affects firm performance through SCO and SDO, it moderates CIO to affect firm performance at the same time. To our knowledge, no previous research has empirically studied on technology in terms of the multiple roles it plays in the innovation process. This finding is a major contribution to research and effectively leads to better firm performance.

#

Finally, the role of technology in service innovation differs depending on specific types of service innovation. Although technology is closely linked with service innovation for better firm performance, some relationships between technology and service innovation activities are insignificant with regard to firm performance. Indeed, technology produces different effects on each type of innovation condition. This means that investment for technology does not guarantee service innovation success and the desired outcome unless the technology matches the specific type of service innovation. Hence, the challenges of successfully incorporating technology should be recognized. Firms should infuse effectively managed technology into service innovation to enhance firm performance. This study suggests that different infusions of technology into specific service innovations are necessary for successful service development, which in turn enhances firm performance.

The results of this study have several practical implications for firms to facilitate service innovation. First, this study provides an insight into practical managers who value the importance of technology in service innovation for achieving innovation success and improving firm performance. The real value of technology, especially IT, exists in leveraging and encouraging service innovation. Consistent with previous research, the results of this study show that the effects of service innovation with technology on firm performance are greater than that of service innovation alone. Our results confirm that technology reinforces the other service innovation dimensions and leads to superior firm performance. Therefore, the managers in service firms should increase their efforts in using technology during the implementation of service innovation to maximize innovation success and enhance firm performance.

Second, managers should consider the types of service innovation for their technology infusion. Our findings show that the effects of technology are different depending on the types of SIO. For example, TEO indirectly affects firm performance through SCO and SDO but plays a moderating role in the relationship between CIO and firm performance. Therefore, the specific role of technology can change according to the type of service innovation. Hence, to avoid mismatching technology to service innovation, managers should coordinate their technology infusion with the specific types of service innovation when developing and implementing service innovations; this would subsequently help them achieve superior firm performance.

#

Third, the managers should recognize that nontechnological innovation activities are still major contributors to the successful development of service innovation. Despite the importance of technology in service innovation for service innovation success, our findings show that nontechnological innovation dimensions (i.e., SCO, SDO and CIO) are still more critical to achieving innovation success than technological innovation dimension. Thus, managers should not overestimate the value of technology because technology itself does not seem to directly affect service innovation success and firm performance. In other words, nontechnological innovations are major contributors for service innovation success although technological innovation is as crucial as their nontechnological innovations in improving performance. Therefore, managers in service firms should understand the structural relationship between nontechnological and technological innovations in service innovation and pay attention not only to their nontechnological innovation as a major contributor but also to technological innovation as a trigger or an enabler for better firm performance.

Finally, this study provides valuable information to managers who seek practical guidance for service innovation. This study offers strategic directions on utilizing technology to maximize the success of service innovation and enhance firm performance. The proper use of technology in service innovation ensures better firm performance. A deeper understanding of the different effects of technology on firm performance through different service innovation activities can help practitioners select the most appropriate technology infusion to improve performance and ensure the long-term survival of their firms.

## 6.3 Limitations and future research directions

Despite the aforementioned contributions of this study, several limitations exist. First, several of the service firms we surveyed may not have actually been innovative, although they perceived themselves to be so. This gap between perception and reality may have resulted in an overestimation of service innovation effects on firm performance. Future studies should objectively assess innovation to reduce this gap and, thus, provide stronger results. Second, our measures of the four service innovation dimensions were not tested comprehensively. Although they were drawn from theoretical

#

literature and validated by the proposed procedure of Moore and Benbasat [65], further studies are necessary to ensure their validity more soundly and to develop more valid measurements. Third, our findings could be different depending on what kind of technologies firms actually use in practice. This study attempted to identify the role of technology in service innovation at a general level. Thus, the results of this study can be extended by considering the effect of different technology types at a more specific level. Fourth, this is a cross-sectional study that provides a useful snapshot of the innovative activities of service firms at a specific time from a static perspective. Therefore, we cannot show the evolution of the role of technology in service innovation. Future studies can examine the evolution of such a role over time from a dynamic perspective. Fifth, we limited the study to six service sectors: transport, communication, computer and software, engineering and architecture, business consulting, and design services. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to other service sectors. Future research can extend the investigation to other service sectors. Finally, the sample of this study was restricted to Korean service firms, so the results may not be completely generalizable to other countries. While Korean service firms are likely to have some similarities to their American and European counterparts, their practice of innovation and its socio-economic environment may have be somewhat different and may have played a distinct role in the findings of this study. Therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted carefully, while future research can investigate other regions.

## 7. Conclusion

This study is an early attempt to conceptualize and investigate the role of technology in service innovation by developing and comparing three different models from different theoretical perspectives. The findings of this study facilitate future research toward exploring the role of technology in service innovation. We conducted the study with the understanding that each model could enrich theoretical arguments that explain the different roles of technology in service innovation. Our findings also indicate that business managers should recognize the technology infusion in service innovation as strategic-level issues. Specifically, managers should determine which approach can best explain the role of technology in their service innovation. Consequently, this study provides practical steps for managers interested in using technology to achieve success in service innovation and improvement in business performance.
