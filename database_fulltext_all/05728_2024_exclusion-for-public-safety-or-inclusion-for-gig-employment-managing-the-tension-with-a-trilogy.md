# EXCLUSION FOR PUBLIC SAFETY OR INCLUSION FOR GIG EMPLOYMENT: MANAGING THE TENSION WITH A TRILOGY OF GUARDIANS<sup>1</sup>

Arun Rai Center for Digital Innovation and Computer Information Systems Department Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University Atlanta, Georgia, U.S.A.{arunrai@gsu.edu}

Yanzhen Chen Department of Information Systems, Business Statistics and Operations Management Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Hong Kong, CHINA {imyanzhen@ust.hk}

Yatang Lin Department of Economics, Division of Public Policy, Division of Social Science Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Hong Kong, CHINA {linyt@ust.hk}

Gig platforms seek to create income opportunities, particularly for socially and economically marginalized people who find it challenging to engage in regular employment. Alongside this empowerment, safety concerns over unregulated drivers for transportation network company (TNC) platforms such as Uber and Lyft have led to discourse among policymakers on the necessity of background check laws (BCLs) with different stringency to exclude individuals from TNC jobs. Drawing on theories of labeling, routine activity, and rational choice theory, we conceptualize a trilogy of guardians—the government, TNC platforms, and the community—to safeguard ridesharing while mitigating the social costs of excluding marginalized citizens from TNC jobs. Empirically, we document the shifting of crimes into the property domain as an unintended consequence of the exclusion solution (i.e., BCLs by the government). Our findings indicate that digital safety technologies deployed by TNC platforms to deter crimes (i.e., in-app safety features) can serve as an alternative to BCLs. Moreover, we show that resources provided by the community can inhibit the negative impacts of exclusion by stringent BCL (i.e., through alternative income sources) and enable the effectiveness of deterrence by in-app safety features (i.e., through policing). Our study surfaces a holistic social justice assessment that involves examining the risks of excluding marginalized individuals from gig work and showing that digital technologies expand the solution space to achieve the public safety of citizens and inclusivity in gig employment through enabling the role of each guardian as well as their interdependence.

Keywords: Background check laws, ridesharing safety, gig platforms, labor market equality, digital safety technologies, unintended consequences, social justice

## Introduction

Gig platforms account for a substantial share of the labor force and, unlike other labor markets with accessibility disparities, especially benefit marginalized people. A McKinsey report estimated that over 162 million people are employed in the sharing economy in the U.S. and the European Union, accounting for 20%-30% of the labor market (Bughin &

Mischke, 2016). Among these, marginalized groups <sup>2</sup> are particularly engaged in this market; in 2021, Latino, Black, and lower-income people in the U.S. made up a higher share of the gig workforce compared to the general workforce (Pew Research Center, 2021). Job availability on gig platforms is crucial to marginalized people for several reasons. The low entry barriers for skills and flexibility in hours are especially attractive to marginalized groups (Ma et al., 2022; Rizer et al., 2018). Furthermore, the gig platform rating system is one of the few ways for freelancers to fix or rebuild credit. These job opportunities are also profoundly valuable for ex-convicts, who struggle to land jobs in other sectors <sup>3</sup> due to strict labor regulations that may exclude them from opportunities based on their criminal record. The lower search and matching costs on platforms also help alleviate the significant information disadvantages of this group, many of whom are severed from previous personal and professional networks and live in geographically disadvantaged areas where an array of factors creates and perpetuates exclusion and poverty.

Facilitating the successful reentry of ex-convicts through accessible job opportunities, such as gig jobs, is crucial from a social justice perspective as it helps to address systemic racial and socioeconomic inequalities prevalent in the criminal justice system. The disparities in the system are stark; minorities are overrepresented in the prison population and often face harsher sentences.<sup>4</sup> Upon release, these individuals often struggle to find employment due to their criminal record, further perpetuating a cycle of poverty and criminality. On average, 68% of state prisoners are rearrested within the first three years following their release (Alper et al., 2018). Providing accessible job opportunities can play a significant role in breaking this cycle, giving ex-convicts a chance to rebuild their lives, support their families, and contribute positively to society (Skardhamar & Telle, 2012). Moreover, gig jobs can serve as a stepping stone to more stable employment, enabling ex-convicts to gain work experience and build a positive work record. This approach aligns with the principles of social justice, promoting fair treatment and equal opportunities to reintegrate and contribute positively to society, irrespective of background or past mistakes. In essence, facilitating successful reentry through accessible job opportunities is not only a solution to reduce recidivism and enhance public safety but can also promote social justice by creating a more inclusive and equitable society.

The relatively relaxed labor regulations of the gig economy, however, directly affect the risk exposure of clients by facilitating contact between strangers, making them vulnerable to criminal pursuits. As a result, many states and city councils have passed laws that require mandatory criminal background checks that exclude some drivers from ridesharing platforms.<sup>5</sup> While governments set the regulations and standards for background checks, it is typically the responsibility of the ridesharing platforms, often in collaboration with third-party providers, to ensure that these regulations are being properly implemented. <sup>6</sup> The unwillingness of a TNC platform to comply with the regulations may result in their involuntary retreat from the regional market, as in Uber and Lyft’s withdrawals from Austin, Texas in 2016. The collision between TNC platforms and state governments over strengthening criminal background checks has been escalating. Though many states allow companies to decide how they screen their own drivers, some local governments and officials have pushed for more stringent and top-down background check measures. For example, in November 2019, Connecticut Senator Richard Blumenthal urged Uber and Lyft to run fingerprint background checks (Holt, 2019).

The tension between protecting public safety based on exclusive policies for high-risk drivers and an inclusive gig labor market for employment has persisted due to a lack of consensus on the effectiveness of BCLs, as an exclusion safety solution, in reducing TNC crimes. Some believe that the current practices of BCLs are inadequate: both Uber and Lyft have reportedly failed to screen a significant proportion of drivers with criminal records (Zhou, 2018). On the other hand, while past wrongdoings are a useful predictor of future risks, it is unclear whether a criminal record in other settings would correlate with security risks in the ridesharing context and whether BCLs, particularly highly stringent BCLs, might be overkill. In fact, according to data on 3,300 California Uber drivers who were ex-offenders, the passenger satisfaction ratings of those convicted of misdemeanors did not differ from those of other drivers. The effectiveness of BCLs and the particular types of crimes that should disqualify offenders from driving for TNC platforms remain to be empirically evaluated.

From a social justice perspective, however, BCLs as blanket policies are likely to be detrimental to the welfare of marginalized groups, despite their potential safety benefits to the general public. Racial disparities notoriously exist at every stage of the criminal justice system. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) systems have been shown to demonstrate bias by underpredicting recidivism for White defendants and overpredicting it for Black defendants (Angwin et al. 2016). The issue of disparities is highly relevant to BCLs since many of them are based on arrests rather than convictions (Rodriguez & Emsellem, 2011). In addition, the current practice of background checks, which excludes workers from employment opportunities based on their ability to produce a clean criminal record, may affect not only ex-offenders but also undocumented immigrants who have no criminal record.<sup>7</sup> The reduced job opportunities for ex-offenders, including those convicted of crimes that have no risk implications for the jobs that are accessible to them, perpetuate a system of bias and inequality, trapping marginalized groups into financial exclusion, dire stress, incarceration, and stagnation.

Beyond the criminal justice argument, prohibiting marginalized individuals with criminal records from employment on gig platforms could create an unintended security risk. When traditional job markets fail these individuals, gig platforms become especially vital for their livelihood. Denying them this opportunity might push them toward illicit activities, like drug trading, organized crime, or property crimes, for financial survival. Given the prevalence of property crimes, we center our attention on this aspect. The potential shift from being excluded from the gig labor market to committing property crimes aligns with Gary Becker’s (1968) foundational model of criminal behavior. This model suggests that as legitimate job opportunities decrease, the opportunity cost of engaging in criminal activities also drops, leading to an increase in crime. As such, the unclear socioeconomic complications of BCLs of low and high stringency and the endogenous response from potential offenders motivate our first research question: What are the impacts of BCLs of low and high stringency on TNC crimes and property crimes?

These potential downsides of BCLs should urge us to not only reevaluate the design of BCLs in terms of their stringency (such as reevaluating the types of past crimes that are disqualifying) but also think beyond the government as the only guardian of ridesharing safety. Informed by criminology theories, we develop a holistic trilogy of guardians framework, encompassing three guardians—the government, TNC platforms, and the community. We suggest that digital safety technologies by TNC platforms to monitor gig work can deter TNC crimes and serve as an alternative to background checks, particularly stringent ones. Indeed, leading ridesharing platforms, have been deploying in-app safety features ranging from reminding riders to check the driver’s license plate to features that can dial 911 from within the app and detect unusual stops during the trip.<sup>8</sup>

Accordingly, we propose that to effectively address the challenge of ensuring both access to ridesharing jobs and consumer safety, it’s essential to examine BCLs of varying stringency alongside digital safety solutions initiated by the TNC platform. This brings us to our second research question: How do platform-initiated in-app safety features moderate the impacts of BCLs, whether low or high in stringency, on TNC crimes?

Drawing from the IS literature on how context affects the success of technology implementation and social outcomes (e.g., Sharma & Yetton, 2003, Hong et al., 2014, Venkatesh et al., 2016) and insights from law enforcement (e.g., Chand & Schreckhise, 2015) and economics (e.g., Barrios et al., 2021), we explore the importance of complementary contextual resources, positioning the community as the third guardian. A factor that could mitigate the unintended adverse impact of BCLs on property crimes is alternative sources of income in the community for ex-convicts, such as reentry support, unemployment support, and other gig work opportunities. Such alternative sources of income could provide a safety net during the transition time when exconvicts are banned from the TNC labor market due to BCLs. For in-app safety features, law enforcement workforce is likely crucial to strengthen the credibility of these features as a deterrent to TNC crimes. Overall, we ask: How do BCLs and platform-initiated in-app safety features intertwine with community resources in impacting TNC and property crimes?

In our empirical study, we comprehensively assess the influence of BCLs on TNC and property crimes, how the stringency of BCLs moderates these effects, the role of platform in-app features as a substitute for BCLs, and the use of community resources as a complement to bolster ridesharing safety and counteract the negative effects of BCLs on property crimes. To start, we exploit the staggered introduction of BCLs on TNC workers in different states from 2010 to 2017 as a quasi-experiment. Our identification utilizes the significant variations in the time gap between the introduction and passage of state bills on background check laws, ranging from over 300 days in California to less than 30 days in Idaho. These time gaps make the timing of BCL passage much less correlated with state trends on TNC or property crimes. Further, the relative time model estimates reveal no divergent pre-trends in property crimes between treated and control states pre-BCL. A battery of falsification tests yielded consistent results, lending support for the identification assumptions.

We found that while BCLs reduce TNC crimes, they backfire by increasing property crimes. Dividing BCLs into high-stringency and low-stringency laws reveals that the high-stringency BCLs are not more effective in reducing TNC crimes but are solely responsible for the unintended adverse effect on property crimes. Our findings suggest that this unintended adverse effect can be reduced through community programs that offer alternative income such as reentry programs, unemployment benefits, and other gig work opportunities. Interestingly, the effectiveness of BCLs in reducing TNC crimes was smaller in counties where inapp safety features are adopted. Moreover, the police workforce in the community increased the effectiveness of in-app safety features as a deterrent for TNC crimes. In sum, our study reveals that overreliance on high-stringency BCLs can backfire when social welfare beyond the narrow and immediate policy effect on TNC safety is considered.

To develop holistic community solutions that address the tension between economic inclusion with public safety, government-implemented BCLs should be combined with the initiatives of the other two key stakeholders: the TNC platforms and the community. By enhancing the effectiveness of each guardian’s role and coordinating their roles, digital technology can play a crucial role in broadening the solutions needed to manage the tension between the exclusion, due to public safety concerns, and the inclusion of marginalized individuals in the gig labor market.

## Background Literature

Our paper lies at the intersection of the societal impacts of the gig economy and, more broadly, platforms, BCLs, and fairness in the gig labor market, and is informed by the literature on these topics.

First, our research relates to a growing stream of IS literature on the societal impacts of platforms. Previous studies have documented the positive effects of ridesharing platforms on employment (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014; Hall & Krueger, 2017; Burtch et al., 2018), entrepreneurship (Barrios et al., 2021) and the potential to reduce traffic incidents and sexual assaults (Greenwood & Wattal, 2017; Park et al., 2021). Another stream of IS literature explores the dark side of platforms, especially regarding their role in promoting illegal transactions (Chan & Ghose, 2014; Chan et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2019, Liu & Bharadwaj, 2020). To manage the dark side of platforms, extant IS literature has explored platform regulations, such as privacy regulations (Adjerid et al., 2015; Goldfarb & Tucker, 2011). Recent work has also looked at platform self-regulation on societal outcomes, such as Han et al. (2022), which examined the impact of Airbnb’s self-regulation on crimes. We consider both the positive and negative sides of ridesharing platforms in society—providing jobs to marginalized groups versus exposing passengers to safety hazards—and propose and evaluate a holistic perspective on platform regulation. This perspective entails broadening the scope of guardians beyond the government to include TNC platforms and the community and coordinating their initiatives and efforts.

Second, our study is closely related to work on mandatory background checks in labor markets and fairness in the gig labor market. A few papers have explored the “ban the box” (BTB) laws—which delay employers’ access to candidates criminal records until the final stage of the hiring process. The evidence is mixed. Doleac and Hansen (2020) and Agan and Star (2018) documented the unintended consequence that BTB policies encourage racial discrimination. Sabia et al. (2018) showed that BTB laws are associated with increased criminal incidents involving Hispanic men, likely attributable to reduced employment opportunities. Luca (2018) found that access to online criminal records led to a small net reduction in property crime rates but also a marked increase in recidivism. While the literature focuses on changes in background check regulations at the extensive margin, such as access period and method, our unique setting allowed us to explore newly implemented BCLs in an emerging industry where gig platforms can also play an important safety risk-mitigation role.

Third, our study relates to the role of gig platforms in offering employment opportunities to marginalized groups. These platforms particularly aid those who face challenges in the conventional job market, acting as a safety net during periods of unemployment or job displacement (Cook et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2019). In this vein, our research relates to equitable opportunities to secure work within the gig economy, as highlighted by Chan and Wang (2018) and Galperin and Greppi (2017).

Positioned at the intersection of these bodies of literature, our study aims to shed light on the social cost of introducing disparities in job access through BCLs in the TNC labor market, and how combined guardianship by the government, platforms, and the community can promote both safety and inclusivity within the TNC labor environment.

## Theory Development

## Trilogy of Public Safety Guardians

To begin, we evaluate whether framing the problem solely as ridesharing safety and focusing the solution on government-imposed background check regulations is overly narrow. We broaden the scope by considering not only ridesharing crimes but also property crimes and by delving into how platform and community initiatives can interact with government-imposed background check requirements. In doing so, we add a new twist to the current solution of ridesharing safety—from the regulation of TNCs to regulation with TNCs, emphasizing collaboration from three guardians: the government, TNCs, and the community. This expanded framework allowed us to theorize and assess a broader solution space to achieve both public safety and economic inclusion in the TNC labor market.

We conceptualize a trilogy of guardians (Table 1). These public safety guardians seek to address the exclusioninclusion tension in different ways. First, by implementing BCLs, the government aims to exclude “risky drivers,” using their criminal records as a basis. With high-stringency BCLs, past offenders, with a broad definitional scope, are seen as likely future criminals and threats to public safety, warranting their exclusion from the TNC labor market. Second, TNCs seek to deter TNC crimes by developing and deploying in-app safety features. Third, the community provides resources, including support for returned citizens and a police workforce, which can deter crimes. Reentry programs, unemployment benefits, and other gig employment can help mitigate the detrimental financial impacts of BCLs on the affected individuals, reducing their exclusion, while the police workforce can augment the deterrence effectiveness of in-app safety features, leading to better crime prevention.

To establish a solid theoretical foundation for the trilogy of guardians and investigate their roles and interdependencies, we integrated three seminal theories from psychology, criminology, and economics of crimes, which we discuss next.

## Theoretical Underpinning

Various criminology theories have been proposed to explain patterns in different types of crimes with behavioral, social, and economic determinants. We draw on labeling theory (LT), routine activity theory (RAT), and rational choice theory (RCT) to elucidate the channels through which BCLs (exclusion actions) and in-app safety features (deterrence actions) affect TNC and property crimes and examine the community resources that moderate these effects. Next, we overview the theories and detail their logical linkages to our study (Table 2).

Labeling theory of crime (LT) focuses on the role of social labeling in the development of crime and deviance. It suggests that once individuals have been labeled as deviants, they face new problems from the reactions of self and others to negative stereotypes that are attached to the deviant label (Becker, 1963; Wellford, 1975). In the language of LT, BCLs that exclude people from jobs amplify the stigmatizing label (e.g., “Once a criminal, always a criminal”), which will not deter but rather instigate deviant acts such as property crimes.

Routine activity theory (RAT) connects patterns of offensive behaviors to day-to-day social interaction. This theory has been used in IS research recently to analyze the impact of introducing ridesharing on sexual assault in cities (Park et al., 2021). According to Cohen and Felson (1979) and Felson and Boba (2010), crimes often arise as intersections of three elements: a motivated offender, suitable targets, and a lack of guardians who can prevent the crime from happening. To mitigate TNC crimes, at least one of the three links must be broken. In a ridesharing environment, government-led exclusion actions (i.e., BCLs) and platform-initiated deterrence actions (i.e., in-app safety features) target different vertices of the crime triangle: BCLs change the pool of risky offenders through exclusion, while in-app features, including automatic contact with safety agents and authorities through RapidSOS, reminders to check the driver’s license plate, and the detection of unusual driving patterns, act as “suitable guardians” by changing the environment in which a potential target encounters a potential offender. RAT suggests that BCLs and TNC in-app safety features are both effective in reducing TNC crimes.

Rational choice theory (RCT), in contrast, suggests that people weigh the benefits and costs of crimes to assess the economic incentives to engage in criminal activity. They tend to commit fewer crimes when the benefits, such as the remuneration of criminal actions, are lower, and when the costs are higher. The cost of committing crimes includes both the direct cost, such as the probability of being caught and the severity of punishment, and the opportunity cost, which involves the expected returns on legitimate activities. This theory has two implications in our context. First, when legitimate pathways to income are blocked by BCLs for TNC drivers with criminal records, they may find criminal actions with remuneration, such as property crimes, to be a more attractive option than legitimate work. Second, the adoption of in-app safety features increases the probability of a TNC criminal being caught, and the resultant deterrence effects help reduce TNC crimes.

In sum, LT helps us rethink BCLs as both a public safety problem and a solution because BCLs stigmatize people. Between RAT and RCT, the former has a sharp yet narrow focus on TNC crimes without considering the broader social outcomes and interdependencies among public safety guardians, which RCT covers. In other words, both RAT and LT apply to all types of criminal acts, but the logic of RCT primarily applies to the shifting of TNC crimes to property crimes.

## Hypotheses Development

We draw insights from LT, RCT, and RAT to formulate hypotheses, which we organize by guardians. We start with the intended and unintended consequences of government guardianship of ridesharing safety through BCLs, followed by a potential alternative solution led by platform guardianship, and, finally, integrate community guardianship as a moderator.

First, we consider the role of the government in safeguarding rides. According to RAT, the practice of screening out potential high-risk drivers actively diminishes public interactions with these groups, subsequently enhancing the overall safety of rides. Furthermore, both LT and RCT suggest the possibility of an unintended effect—BCLs increasing property crimes though through different channels. RCT predicts that BCLs will increase the financial incentives for property crimes if exoffenders lose their TNC jobs, both because of a restricted choice set (i.e., TNC jobs are no longer accessible) and the lower opportunity costs of crime. At the same time, according to LT, BCLs reinforce the social identity of ex-offenders as criminals because of a persistent prejudiced label (“once a criminal, always a criminal”) and thus may push people into shadier pursuits like property crimes. We anticipate that while BCLs decrease TNC crimes, they may inadvertently shift TNC crimes into property crimes. Thus, our hypotheses are:

## H1a: BCLs will decrease TNC crimes.

## H1b: BCLs will increase property crimes.

We further consider the stringency requirement of BCLs, and especially, how a proper stringency level of BCLs strikes a balance between their costs and benefits. While past wrongdoings can be a useful predictor of future risks, the correlation differs across crime types and time elapsed (Maltz, 1984; Kurlychek et al., 2006; Bushway et al., 2011). Different types of past wrongdoings also indicate different levels of perceived risks on the passenger side. As a TNC driver, a repeat sex offender poses a much greater threat to riders than a financial fraudster or a shoplifter. In light of LT and BCL, apart from the mechanical fact that high-stringency BCLs exclude more people from gainful employment, thereby inducing more crimes, BCLs also exclude people who otherwise present a low risk to public safety and can economically reintegrate into society post-incarceration. As such, the stigmatization due to the labeling as ex-offenders and the economic incentives due to the reduction in opportunity cost are both stronger for this group, which is excluded by high-stringency BCLs (Rasmusen, 1996; Tittle et al., 2003; Moran, 2012; Moore et al., 2016). Combining these arguments, we hypothesize:

## H1c: High-stringency BCLs will escalate property crimes at a higher rate than the low-stringency laws.

Further, we consider both gig platforms and the government as guardians. We suggest that platform-initiated in-app safety features, such as real-time location sharing and emergency response, are effective in reducing TNC crimes for two reasons. First, based on RCT, in-app safety features raise the perceived costs of committing TNC crimes by raising the risk of getting caught, as suggested by Becker (1968). Second, TNCs act as guardians, akin to a safer environment created by security cameras in traditional RAT (Hollis-Peel et al., 2011). Accordingly, we hypothesize:

## H2a: In-app safety features will reduce TNC crimes.

Moreover, the impacts of BCLs and in-app features, when employed concurrently, can intersect. BCLs and in-app features address different aspects of the offender-target-guardian triangle in TNC crimes. Once an exclusion solution is activated, the value of deterrence diminishes, as noted by Eck (2003).

Drawing from RCT, the introduction of in-app features increases the perceived crime costs due to amplified fears of apprehension, reducing the need to exclude drivers from the TNC labor market through BCLs. Conversely, guided by RAT, with the exclusions provided by BCLs, deterrence through inapp features might lose its potency as the roster of “motivated offenders” contracts, thereby reducing the need to address the convergence of potential offenders and victims.

## H2b: The effects of either BCLs or in-app safety features on TNC crimes will be smaller when the other safety measure has been implemented.

Finally, we turn to community resources to propose moderating factors that could influence the effectiveness of the solutions by the government and TNC platforms. We denote the community resources that offset BCLs’ unintended adverse effects on property crimes as “exclusion inhibitors” and those that enhance in-app features’ impact on to reducing TNC crimes as “deterrence enablers”

Following RCT arguments, the unintended effect of BCL exclusion in increasing property crimes arises because it lowers the opportunity cost of committing crimes by reducing the expected income from legitimate activities. As a result, a financial buffer from alternative income sources to TNC for ex-convicts can temper the relationship between BCLs and property crimes (Bucknor & Barber, 2016). We consider three alternative income sources in the community for drivers who lose their gig jobs because of strict BCLs: (1) Reentry support and rehabilitation programs that provide career development as well as legal and health services for ex-convicts; (2) unemployment benefits; (3) availability of employment on other gig platforms, such as Postmates, Grubhub, and Doordash (Burtch et al., 2018). <sup>9</sup> It is worth noting that a significant portion of reentry programs and unemployment support initiatives are provided by nongovernmental entities, indicating their community-driven nature. In addition to these sources of income, the entry of other gig platforms into a community also expands job prospects for marginalized individuals. As such, these alternative income sources can offset the adverse economic incentives for marginalized individuals to commit property crimes due to exclusion from TNC jobs by BCLs. Given the large disparities in these alternative income sources across communities (Desilver, 2020, Forsythe & Yang, 2021), we expect communities with alternative income sources to see a lower increase in property crimes due to BCLs, leading to our hypothesis:

H3: The effects of BCLs in increasing property crimes will be smaller in communities with alternative income sources (i.e., reentry support, unemployment benefits, and other gig employment) for ex-convicts.

For deterrence enablers, we consider community resources that can complement in-app features to deter TNC crimes. A key guardianship measure for RAT empirical tests in communities is the local police workforce (Stahura & Sloan, 1988). We anticipate that the police workforce in a community will be particularly relevant in our context, as the crime deterrence effects of in-app safety features, such as Uber’s integration with RapidSOS,<sup>10</sup> depend on the reactions of the police force in a community (Levitt & Miles, 2006, Chalfin & McCrary, 2017). Therefore, we hypothesize:

## H4: The effects of in-app features in reducing TNC crimes will be larger in communities with a sufficient police workforce.

In sum, our research model and hypotheses add a novel twist to the current solution space of safe TNC rides. The government and platforms are not engaged in a zero-sum battle over platform regulation for safe rides. Instead, as illustrated in Figure 1, they target different aspects of a holistic safety solution: the government enforces BCLs to exclude dangerous drivers, and the platforms roll out digital safety technologies to deter crimes. These two initiatives, while distinct in approach, share a common objective of ridesharing safety and need to be considered jointly. Meanwhile, the community offers resources, such as support for ex-convicts and a police workforce, which interact with the exclusion and deterrence solutions. Reframing the problem from a solely governmental regulation of platforms through BCLs for ridesharing safety to a trilogy of guardians, collaborating to achieve both ridesharing safety and inclusion in gig employment for ex-offenders, opens a more holistic solution space.

## Context and Data

## US Legislation on Driver Background Checks and TNC Entries

We collected information on TNC background check regulations from US state legislation websites, focusing on milestone dates, such as decisions by the state house, senate, or council on related matters. By 2019, 43 states had passed BCLs. Notably, the date when the relevant bill was signed into law varied widely across states. For example, Colorado’s bill, passed in June 2014, was the earliest and Illinois’s bill, passed in May 2018, was the latest in our sample. The relevant bills were pending in five states and had been rejected in another three. Figure 2a displays the number of states with newly passed driver BCLs on TNC each month, from March 2014 to December 2018. Figure 2b shows the average duration between TNC BCL bill introduction and passage dates, ranging from 22 days in New Mexico to 579 days in California. Arguably, this duration is largely determined by state legislative procedures and the balance of seats between different parties in the senate or house, which makes the date that bills were signed into law somewhat exogenous to unobserved determinants of local crimes. We also gathered information on Uber and Lyft entry dates for all U.S. counties up to December 2017. The first city to offer Uber services was San Francisco in May 2010.

## TNC Crime Data Sources

We sourced the crime data from reports released by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2017, as compiled in Kaplan (2019). The reports covered data that police agencies voluntarily submitted to the FBI. The crime events included seven categories: murder, rape, robbery, assault, burglary, theft, and motor vehicle theft. As our analyses were at the county level, we aggregated these data from the police-agency level to the county-month level.

To better understand the impacts of BCLs on crimes directly associated with ridesharing, we collected data on the number of crimes associated with Uber and Lyft from two sources: news reports and legal dockets. For news reports, we relied on three prominent websites for such news reports: ridesafeworld.com,<sup>11</sup> vintagetaxiservice.com, and atchisontransport.com, which reported 872, 568, and 240 incidents in the U.S., respectively.

Given that these websites had varying data collection periods, constructing a full panel from late 2013 to 2020 was infeasible. Accordingly, we pooled the data from the three sites from 2014 to 2019—spanning a time frame covered by at least two of the sites—and, after removing duplicates, identified 1316 unique incidents. We validated our TNC-reported incidences by comparing the state-aggregated number of TNC crimes to total violent crimes from 2013 to 2018 finding a significant association between these two variables and no apparent outliers (Figure A2).

Lastly, we sourced TNC crime data from legal dockets via Trellis (trellis.law), a leading legal research platform. Considering cases filed against Uber and Lyft from 2013 through 2020, we selected TNC-relevant court records from 26 states where Uber and Lyft were named as defendants.<sup>12</sup> From these cases, we excluded those concerning auto negligence, motor vehicle mishaps, business torts, and labor disputes. The remaining predominantly spotlighted criminal incidents.

## Other County Covariates

We controlled for annual county-level demographic characteristics, socioeconomic factors, and geography-specific trends in crime. We obtained the county-year-level regional population, demographics and poverty rate data from the U.S. Census Bureau, and the GDP data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis. We also collected data on the size of the police force from the FBI website (FBI, 2020a) to examine the moderating factors.

## BCL Stringency

We defined a specific BCL regulation as high stringency if it excluded drivers for the following reasons: identity theft, dishonesty, or fleeing or evading police without further convictions for five years or more. The first two types of crimes are largely associated with fraudulence in seeking economic gains and are less relevant to crimes occurring in the ridesharing setting. We did not consider fleeing or evading police without further convictions as particularly dangerous behavior, as the fact that there were no subsequent convictions leaves the criminal nature of the evasion in doubt. If no actual convictions followed this violation after five years, it is likely that fleeing or evading police was not associated with an actual offense. There were three states that required a clean record of fleeing or evading police for five years or more. We provide examples of high-stringency and low-stringency BCLs in Table 4.

## In-App Safety Features

With the potentially unintended social cost of BCLs, primarily stringent ones, a subsequent question is whether these gig platforms could instead leverage technology. Such in-app safety features could enhance ridesharing safety without introducing job inequalities based on the ability to present a clean criminal record.

To empirically evaluate the role of platform-initiated in-app features in reducing TNC crimes, we pinpointed states with launches of in-app safety features during the sample period. These features include reminding riders to check the driver’s license plate, sharing the estimated arrival time or real-time locations with pre-specified users, the ability to dial 911 from within the app, and recognizing unexpected stops along the way using AI. There were significant geographical and temporal variations in the adoption time across states, which allowed us to compare the effectiveness of in-app features in reducing TNC crimes with that of the concurrent BCL. After a national launch, we considered all counties to have in-app features. An example is Lyft’s national launch of a feature on September 11, 2019, allowing users to directly contact 911 within the app. Given there had been very few local launches of in-app features by Lyft prior to the national launch, there was less regional variation in its in-app launch timeline and thus we mostly relied on legal dockets involving Uber as the defendant to gauge the effectiveness of in-app safety features. We summarize the descriptive statistics of key variables in our study in Table 5.

Our main identification concern is that BCLs are endogenous to property crimes. That is, states may pass laws regulating TNC drivers in response to concerns over abnormal changes in crimes. To address this issue, we conducted additional placebo tests and estimated a relative time model to evaluate the parallel trend assumptions crucial to our DiD design.

First, we constructed a placebo test exploiting the fact that it usually takes months or even years for a BCL bill to become law as it must go through multiple layers of legislative procedures. We created a “pseudo” BCL variable (BCL\_Introtosign) that took the value of 1 during the period between a bill’s proposal and its passage and examined the impact of this variable on property crimes. The key idea is that if the laws existed partially to address changes in trends of crime growth, we would be able to observe the difference in the change in crime between states with and without BCLs around the bill introduction dates rather than the bill passage dates. Thus, we constructed the placebo test as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {property crime} _ {c t} = \theta_ {1} B C L _ {s t} + \theta_ {2} B C L \_ I n t r o t o s i g n _ {s t} + \\ \gamma \text {controls} _ {c, y e a r (t)} + \alpha_ {c} + \beta_ {t} + \sigma_ {s} t + \epsilon_ {i t}, \end{array} \tag {2}
$$

where ??????\_???????????????? $i g n _ { s t }$ equals 1 if a BCL bill was introduced but not yet signed into law in state s at time t. We expected the estimated $\theta _ { 2 }$ to be insignificant if the BCLs were not endogenously introduced in states with different crime trajectories than the untreated ones.

## Estimating Impacts of BCLs and In-App Safety Features on TNC Crimes

Having established our primary finding on the exclusion solution by the government, we turned to another important guardian, the TNC platforms, and investigated the roles of BCLs (exclusion actions) and in-app features (deterrence actions) in reducing TNC crimes.

Given the count data nature of the outcome variables, which are highly skewed with sparse observations, we employed a high-dimensional fixed effect Poisson model that employed a quasi-maximum likelihood estimation procedure (Hausman et al., 1984, Guimarães & Portugal, 2009).

We tested H1a (BCLs reduce TNC crimes) and H2a (in-app features reduce TNC crimes) with the following estimation equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {l o g (E [ T N C _ {c t} | X _ {c t} ]) = \lambda_ {1} B C L _ {s t} + \lambda_ {2} i n - a p p _ {c t} +} \\ & {\pmb {\gamma c o n t r o l s} _ {c, y e a r (t)} + \beta_ {t} + \sigma_ {c} + \epsilon_ {c t}.} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

If H1a and H2a hold, we would expect both $\lambda _ { 1 }$ and $\lambda _ { 2 }$ to be negative. Following RAT, we also hypothesized (in H2b) that the joint (negative) effects of BCLs and in-app features on TNC crimes would be less than additive, which we tested with a variant of Equation (2):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log (E [ T N C _ {c t} | X _ {c t} ]) = \lambda_ {1} B C L _ {s t} + \lambda_ {2} i n - a p p _ {c t} + \lambda_ {3} B C L _ {s t} * \\ i n - a p p _ {c t} + \gamma c o n t r o l s _ {c, y e a r (t)} + \beta_ {t} + \sigma_ {c} + \epsilon_ {c t}. \end{array} \tag {5}
$$

H2b suggests that $\lambda _ { 3 }$ would be positive.

A particular concern of endogeneity is that the passage of BCLs by the government and the adoption of in-app safety features by TNC platforms respond to escalating concerns about ridesharing safety. However, these safety concerns might be correlated with unobserved trends in TNC crimes. As discussed in Estimating BCLs’ Impacts on Property Crimes section, the existence of BCLs could be a response to increasing crime trends in states. However, the significant variation in the time elapsed between state bills’ introductions and passage, reduces the conditional correlation between BCLs and such crime trends using state and time fixed effects. A similar logic would apply to the timing of in-app feature rollouts. Like the passage of BCLs, the rollouts of in-app safety features by platforms are driven by idiosyncratic factors that are not as likely to correlate with time trends in TNC crimes. This is corroborated by three additional pieces of evidence. First, although the unconditional correlation between BCL and in-app treatments is positive, it becomes negative and insignificant when conditional on the state and month fixed effects, which suggests that factors endogenous to both treatments will likely be absorbed by these fixed effects (Appendix Table A2). Second, a relative time model, examining the responses of TNC crimes from news sources to time dummies relative to BCL passage revealed no discernible difference in TNC crime trends prior to BCL passage across states with and without BCLs (Figure A4). Finally, we used a duration analysis to examine factors that determined the passage dates of BCLs across states (Table A3). We found that BCLs were introduced in response to macroeconomic trends, political climate, and changes in the police workforce, rather than recent TNC crimes. Macroeconomic factors (e.g., slower GDP growth, a greater population, lower unemployment rate, etc.), public security infrastructure (i.e., sworn officers per capita), and political orientations were significant predictors of early BCL passage, while TNC crimes, violent crimes, and property crimes had minimal correlation with the timing of the BCL passage.

## BCL Impacts on Property Crimes

We present the effects of BCLs on property crimes in Column 1 of Table 6. The estimated coefficient of BCL is positive and both statistically and economically significant, supporting H1b. BCLs contribute to a 0.02 standard deviation increase in property crimes, which translates into a 6.44% growth in property crime relative to the sample mean. This aligns with the financial channel suggested by RCT: marginalized individuals denied TNC jobs due to BCLs might be drawn to illicit economic activities, leading to an uptick in property crimes.

Using the same specifications, we assessed the impact of BCLs on violent crimes in Column 3. As per our conceptual framework, the impacts of BCLs on violent crimes are ambiguous. While LT suggests that BCLs may hinder exconvicts’ societal reintegration, potentially leading to more crimes, including both property and violent crimes, BCLs could also reduce TNC violent crimes. Our findings indicate that the effects of BCLs on violent crimes are not significant, suggesting that these two channels may neutralize each other. This null effect also aligns with RCT’s implications: the economic drivers bolstering BCLs’ significant influence on property crimes are less pertinent for violent crimes, which are, in theory, minimally influenced by financial motives.

The estimates from Equation (2) are reported in Columns 2 and 4 of Table 6. The coefficients associated with the indicator for the period between BCL introduction and passage are close to zero for both property and violent crimes, and the inclusion of this variable does not affect the main effects of BCLs. This evidence suggests that the crime changes are in response to the passage of a BCL rather than its introduction or discussion.

## Impacts of BCLs and In-App Safety Features on TNC Crimes

Next, we evaluate the effectiveness of BCLs, in-app safety features, and their interaction effects, as posited in H1a, H2a, and H2b. ## Contextual Factors

We further extended our analysis to examine two contextual factors capturing community resources that can make solutions (i.e., BCLs and platform-initiated digital safety technologies) less costly to society and more effective at promoting safe TNC rides. We differentiated between (1) exclusion inhibitors, which curb the unintended effects of BCLs on non-TNC crimes, and (2) deterrence enablers, which boost the effectiveness of in-app features in reducing TNC crimes.

## Exclusion Inhibitors: Economic Incentives of Ex-Offenders

According to RCT, if BCLs lead to more property crimes because they detrimentally impact the financial standing of ex-offenders, local financial relief or alternative income sources for marginalized people would be an important moderator of BCLs’ adverse side effects on property crimes. In states that provide a high level of support for marginalized people, especially ex-convicts, BCLs for TNCs would be less harmful to these individuals financially and BCLs detrimental effect on property crimes would thus be expected to be lower.

We considered three types of community resources: (1) reentry support and rehabilitation programs, (2) unemployment benefits, and (3) the presence of non-TNC gig platforms. We collected data on the number of reentry projects in each state from Help for Felons (helpforfelons.org),<sup>16</sup> which details statewide reentry programs for ex-convicts. Such reentry and rehabilitation initiatives relieve the financial duress of marginalized people by providing free or low-cost access to healthcare clinics and pharmacies, employment information, loans, emergency food, clothing, shelter, and more. We defined reentry as a dummy that switched to 1 if the number of local reentry projects is above the national median.

We used the measure of state-level unemployment benefits from Forbes (Rowan & Bailie, 2023), which estimates the average weekly unemployment benefits using data from the U.S. Department of Labor, Center on Budget and Priorities from 2020. For alternative platform jobs, we documented the entry month of three gig platforms: Postmates, Grubhub, and Doordash, and created a dummy that switched to 1 when at least one of the three alternative platforms was available in each state. We chose these platforms because they offer jobs similar to those on ridesharing platforms (Burtch et al., 2018) but typically require less stringent BCLs.

The results are reported in Table 11. In Columns 1 through 4, we report the coefficients on the interaction terms between BCL and reentry support, unemployment benefits, and the availability of alternative platforms pertaining to property crimes in separate regressions. All coefficients are negative. In Column 8, we add the three sets of interaction terms together in a single regression, in which all reported coefficients are negative and statistically significant, suggesting that in states bestowing higher levels of financial support for ex-convicts deprived of gig jobs due to BCLs, the impacts of BCLs on property crimes are smaller. In states with reentry support, any of the three gig platforms and mean unemployment benefits, the BCL joint effect is 0.0478- 0.0258-0.0218 = 0.0002, indicating that BCLs are no longer causing a significant surge in property crimes. The robust results identify the financial stress caused by BCLs as one of the key driving forces behind the increase in property crimes, consistent with predictions by the rational choice theory (RCT). As a policy implication, providing financial support to marginalized groups could help counteract the side effects of ridesharing BCLs, especially the more stringent ones. As falsification tests, we repeated the same analyses with violent crimes and found no significant impact of BCL and its interaction terms, as expected. ## Implications

Our study broadens the problem and solution spaces to manage the tension between the inclusion of marginalized individuals in TNC gig work and their exclusion from such jobs by BCLs for the ridesharing safety of the community. By drawing on LT, RAT, and RCT, we propose a trilogy of guardians to effectively manage this tension. In doing so, we expose the risks of a local and “partial equilibrium” by relying on the actions of the government as the sole guardian, and show the benefits of a more holistic, “general equilibrium,” by coordinating the interdependence in the actions of the government, the platform, and the community as three guardians. We summarize the implications in Table 13 and elaborate on them below.

Implication #1: By eliminating TNC jobs as a legal income source and stigmatizing individuals with the “ex-offender” label, stringent BCLs prompt the rational response of excluded drivers to turn toward illicit income sources.

We found that introducing inequality in job opportunities based on the ability to show a clean criminal background is counterproductive, according to economic incentives, as per RCT, and social stigma, as suggested by LT. Our results reveal that stringent BCLs significantly increase property crimes, likely driven by reduced income sources, without providing additional safety benefits. As we theorized, the mechanism of this social cost can arise due to incentives that promote economic crimes, as per RCT, and stigmatization, which reinforces a negative identity and cuts against economic reintegration for returned citizens, as per LT. A direct implication is that policymakers must evaluate their BCL interventions with respect to not only the need for safety in the ridesharing domain but also to the social and economic wellbeing of communities. The potential downside of social segregation and reduced income opportunities for marginalized groups, alongside the resulting criminal implications, need to be actively considered.

Our findings show that BCLs increase property crimes while reducing TNC crimes. This illustrates the downside of introducing stringent BCLs without considering their broader impacts on the financial standing of affected groups. From an LT perspective, socially and economically reinforcing an individual’s identity as “always an offender” through high-stringency BCLs can perpetuate criminal behaviors. The ex-offender label, coupled with economic exclusion that presumes continued criminality, risks reinforcing an individual’s identity as a criminal, both in self-identity and in the eyes of the community.

The findings suggest that BCLs for TNC drivers need to factor in broader and long-term social welfare beyond narrow and immediate policy effects. Background checks that exclude individuals from job opportunities on TNC platforms are not a shortcut to a safer community; they might instead promote social segregation and create an economic incentive for criminal activity in other domains such as property crimes. While an unregulated gig driver workforce may threaten public safety, simply banning individuals from participating in the TNC labor force isn’t without adverse consequences.

Implication #2: By leveraging platform-initiated digital inapp safety features to deter TNC crimes, the stringency of BCLs can be reduced, promoting TNC employment inclusivity for marginalized individuals.

Our study shows that by deploying in-app safety features in ridesharing markets, TNC platforms can detect or prevent TNC crimes without incurring the social costs of excluding marginalized citizens that come with BCL. Theoretically, such digital safety solutions can be used to orchestrate RAT’s key premise to reduce crime—i.e., breaking the space-time convergence of likely offenders and victims and increasing access to safety guardians (Cohen & Felson, 1979, Park et al., 2021).

From a policy perspective, with sophisticated digital infrastructures and access to large-scale, direct, and realtime data on rides, TNC platforms are positioned to contribute to public safety solutions in novel ways. They should be viewed not just as subjects of regulation but as partners with governments to curb TNC crimes while minimizing the exclusion of marginalized individuals from TNC jobs. Such partnering can be a path to explore solutions for safe, cost-efficient transportation and inclusion in the labor market—critical social goals that have been identified by the United Nations (2006). Highquality data on TNC rider safety, along with an understanding of the abilities of TNC firms to implement BCLs and deploy in-app safety features, can inform lawmakers on the appropriate stringency level of BCLs.

Implication # 3: The trilogy of guardians is a collaborative strategy to achieve public safety while providing TNC employment for marginalized individuals.

Our study indicates that the efficacy of BCLs and in-app safety features in curbing crime is intrinsically tied to community resources. Explaining the interdependence between the actions of the government, the platform, and the community draws on insights from LT, RAT and RCT. Through the RCT lens, alternative income sources in the community, such as reentry support programs and jobs on other gig platforms, should mitigate BCL-induced property crime by amplifying the opportunity costs associated with criminal activity. LT implies that social support for reintegration and reduced stigma are likely to mitigate the negative consequences of exclusion. RAT posits that punctual law enforcement reactions are crucial to amplifying the efficacy of in-app safety features aimed at disrupting the convergence of potential offenders and victims. The promptness and effectiveness of such law enforcement interventions hinge predominantly on the timely availability of local police resources.

The interdependence of BCLs and in-app safety features with community resources highlights the need to tailor context-specific solutions. This aligns with the broader principle that the design and deployment of information systems should resonate with the requirements of the context in which they are used (e.g., Sharma & Yetton, 2003, Hong et al., 2014, Venkatesh et al., 2016). Factors like BCL stringency, in-app safety features, community police resources, and community support for reentry and unemployment together create a varied landscape of resources that should be incorporated into a holistic solution for the dual goals of ridesharing safety and economic inclusion in the TNC labor market.

Underlying the “trilogy of guardians” perspective is a robust digital infrastructure and innovative digital technologies for the guardians to enact and coordinate their roles. These digital tools are crucial for the effective deployment of BCLs and in-app safety features. They are also essential to achieve real-time coordination with police authorities in response to an alert triggered by an in-app safety feature. Additionally, digital technologies facilitate the efficient identification and delivery of safety nets, mitigating the negative effects of BCLs. Governments can leverage data analytics and online platforms to quickly identify individuals in need, streamline the application process, and ensure secure and prompt benefit disbursement. Moreover, access to information on alternative income opportunities within communities helps governments tailor the design of BCL to the community.

The trilogy of guardians perspective rejects the assumption of a mere trade-off between two goals: public safety versus inclusion of marginalized individuals in the gig labor force.

Through collaboration among the guardians who utilize IT in their autonomous roles in screening, monitoring, connecting with safety authorities, and providing financial opportunities, we can simultaneously achieve both goals and ultimately work towards an improved equilibrium in terms of social welfare.

From a policy perspective, a primary takeaway from our study is that the level of BCL stringency should be contingent on TNC platforms’ deployment of in-app safety features, along with alternative income sources (reentry, unemployment benefits, alternative gig work) and policing resources in the community. We consider ridesharing safety strategies for three scenarios of community resources—i.e., police workforce and alternative income sources, which can respectively alter the effectiveness of in-app safety features and BCLs. In Scenario 1, where a community has a wellstaffed police force (increasing the effectiveness of in-app safety features) but limited alternative income sources (elevating the risk of stringent BCLs increasing crimes for economic gain), the community can rely on TNC platforms as the primary guardian of ridesharing safety, while employing minimal levels of BCLs (e.g., excluding sex offenders). In Scenario 2, where the police force is moderately staffed (reducing the effectiveness of in-app safety features relative to Scenario 1) and alternative income opportunities are moderate (reducing the risks of property crimes from BCLs relative to Scenario 1), BCLs can be adjusted upward to moderate stringency. Both in-app safety features and BCLs play important roles in ridesharing safety in this scenario. In Scenario 3, where police resources are scarce (further reducing the effectiveness of in-app safety features relative to Scenario 2) but alternative income sources are more abundant (reducing the risks of property crimes from BCLs relative to Scenario 2), scaling up BCLs to a stringent level becomes necessary and feasible. In this scenario, BCLs play a primary role in ridesharing safety, with in-app features playing a modest role.

## Concluding Remarks

Our research reveals an uptick in property crime as the unintended social cost of ridesharing BCLs that exclude marginalized citizens from the TNC labor market. Rather than viewing TNC platforms solely as regulation targets, we propose a holistic view of a trilogy of guardians to achieve both public safety and inclusive job opportunities. This approach capitalizes on the platform’s capabilities of digital technology for safety to offset the adverse effects of highstringency background checks and tailors the stringency of BCLs based on the resources in the community context.

Together, the trilogy of guardians creates a constructive and collaborative force for social justice, promoting job opportunities on gig platforms for marginalized individuals and public safety for the community. We are optimistic that our findings will inspire future work on digital platforms and technologies for social justice where digital platforms and technology are not framed solely as objects of regulation but are considered as agents that can contribute to nuanced, context-sensitive solutions.

## Details on Processing TNC Crimes from News and Dockets Data

To assign news reports of Uber/Lyft incidents to specific census counties, we employed a multistep process due to the nonstandardized reporting of incident locations. First, we utilized fuzzy name-matching techniques to match the reported locations with county names, taking into account potential variations or misspellings. However, to ensure accuracy, these matches underwent manual verification checks. Second, for the reports that did not find a county name match, we focused on those that included city names without county names. In such cases, w matched the city names with core-based statistical areas (CBSAs). Finally, for the CBSAs identified in the previous step, we assigned them to the most populous county within the respective CBSA, as population density often correlates with higher incident likelihood.

In processing docket data, the counties referred to in Uber/Lyft dockets are where the legal cases were filed, which may not necessarily correspond to the counties where the incidents occurred for two main reasons: (1) plaintiffs may have experienced the incidents while traveling but chose to file the cases in their home counties, and (2) plaintiffs may opt to file the cases at the headquarters of Uber/Lyft, located in San Francisco county. Our empirical analysis using Uber/Lyft docket data relies on the assumption that these two sources of measurement errors are orthogonal to the introduction of BCLs and in-app features.

To address potential biases resulting from (2), we repeated the analysis in Equation (5) excluding all Uber/Lyft dockets from San Francisco County. The results, presented in Table A5, consistently support hypotheses H1a and H2a, as indicated by the significant and negative coefficients of BCL and Uber in-app features in Columns 1 and 2. Furthermore, Column 2 provides evidence for H2b, suggesting that BCL and in-app safety features counterbalance each other in reducing TNC crimes.

Columns 3 and 4 reaffirm the effectiveness of BCL in reducing TNC crimes (H1a) using data from Lyft. However, due to uniform regional in-app launch timings with Lyft, we were unable to estimate the correlation between BCL and Lyft’s in-app launch. Consequently, the impact of Lyft’s in-app feature appears to be insignificant.
