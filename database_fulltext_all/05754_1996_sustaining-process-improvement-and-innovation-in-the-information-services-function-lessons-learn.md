## Abstract

Orchestrating programs of organizational transformation that result in sustained process improvement represents a difficult managerial challenge. Yet, ever-changing customer requirements, electronic partnerships, and increasingly complex intraorganizational arrangements are forcing many well-established firms to transform themselves from function-based forms of organization into process-based systems of managerial, task, and evaluative arrangements. Through a program of managed transformation, the Information Services (IS) function at Bose Corporation has realized dramatic improvements in the delivery of information products/services and is now "charting the course" for a sustained process management view that will define and measure business relationships well into the next century. In contrast to many well-publicized programs of change, the drive toward sustained process improvement and innovation by Bose IS resembles an evolutionary model of organizational learning and information sharing rather than a revolutionary model of immediate and drastic transformation. This study describes the defining stages, key events, and obstacles of the road traveled by Bose IS in transforming itself from a corporate utility into an enterprise-wide source of process innovation and improvement.

Key Words: IS service quality, process redesign, process innovation, organizational learning, change management

ISRL Categories: EI0206.03, DD06, DD0402, AF10

## Introduction

Organizational transformation philosophies such as Business Process Reengineering (BPR) and/or Total Quality Management (TQM) generate tremendous initial promise. Over time, however, these initiatives can lose momentum as managers grapple with the realities of limited organizational resources, slow pay-off, and diminished employee enthusiasm. In theory, process management provides a lens of analysis through which substantial benefits in business performance can be gained (Davenport, 1993). However, for many managers the question remains: How can process improvement philosophies and methods be perpetuated in a sustainable process management activity with both enterprise acceptance and impact?

This study chronicles the evolution, impact, and current state of process management activity within the Information Services (IS) function of Bose Corporation. In contrast to a drastic program of revolutionary change, gains in process improvement within Bose IS have been realized through an evolutionary and carefully managed program of learning, knowledge-sharing, experimentation, and cross-functional partnering. The definable stages, obstacles, as well as managerial interventions for successful navigation associated with this 10-year journey are presented through a five-phase transitory framework for IS executives seeking to understand potential paths toward a sustained process management philosophy. Emergent tools and methodologies adopted by Bose IS along with their measurable impacts are also highlighted as a means of understanding the nature and potential payoff of "improvement work" as well as its integration within larger managerial programs of organizational transformation.

## The Bose Corporation

The Bose Corporation was founded in 1964 by Dr. Amar Bose, a professor of engineering and computer science at MIT. With the help of Sherwin Greenblatt, president and chief operating officer (COO), a technical, results-oriented corporate culture was nurtured. Throughout its history Bose has consistently introduced acclaimed products in the high-end segment of the audio components market and is widely regarded as the world's leading manufacturer of premium audio speakers. These products are distributed through multiple channels including retail, direct marketing, and Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEM). Bose has thrived in an industry that emphasizes an innovation-driven ("product out") mentality and, until recently, has not emphasized customer-driven or process-based approaches to management.

## Total Quality and Process Management at Bose IS

Interestingly, the facts behind the drive toward process management within Bose IS seem at odds with many of the principles commonly associated with TQM and BPR (Hammer and Champy, 1993). While competitive events essentially forced the manufacturing function at Bose to dramatically improve production processes in the late 1980s, there was not a corresponding top-down directive for other functional areas to adopt Total Quality (TQ) philosophies, nor was there a strategy-driven management initiative to redesign the firm cross-functionally. In contrast to such a deliberate, senior-management-led program, process management within Bose IS has emanated from the entrepreneurial and partnering activities of a group of IS managers seeking to improve their area's performance. Also, contrary to many reported cases of process change, the experiences of Bose IS suggest that varying leadership roles, activities, and dialog should be adopted at critical points to sustain the momentum of process management activities.

Importantly, the ongoing journey toward process management at Bose IS has often included roadblocks and an unclear itinerary. However, a nurturing climate and timely managerial intervention have incubated this initiative from humble beginnings into a proven program of improvement activity. The progression of Bose IS can be framed as a series of five evolutionary stages: exploration, discovery, formalization, process-think, and process-link. As illustrated in Figure 1, key events associated with organizational learning and transfer of process management philosophies have enabled Bose IS to expand the reach of their improvement work over time. In the following sections, the defining characteristics and key events of each stage as well as potential pitfalls and managerial interventions between stages are highlighted.

## Exploration: 1985–1988

Upon the arrival of Bose's new IS director in October 1985, IS was not considered a "strategic" service. The Information Services Function (ISF) included both administrative services (mail room, copy centers, reprographics, courier services, in-house travel agency, electronic mail) and corporate information systems (data centers, application development, end-user computing). Beyond headquarters, the IS director shared responsibility for IS organizations around the world with national plant managers; in addition, manufacturing and engineering had their own IS groups. In essence, central IS was set up as an "a la carte" service organization. As noted by the IS director:

All I heard was: IS was not in touch; IS took too long; IS did not know what other departments wanted to accomplish and even if IS did, it could not provide it; and IS did not consider the customer in its total solution—rather it kind of just foisted its technical solution on you.

With the only directive from top management being "get the systems in order" and "better align them with the organization," the IS director was essentially autonomous with respect to resolving departmental problems. He began what at first seemed a personal crusade of "IS improvement" including: conducting "internal customer" surveys to identify major dissatisfiers; consolidating the IS organization; creating an in-house consulting organization; tracking and reporting IS responsiveness to the business; and creating a charge-back system to better align costs with benefits. However, despite these efforts, customer complaints were still prevalent at the end of 1988. This lack of progress caused the IS director to question the willingness of the IS staff to accept responsibility for problem resolution:

From the day I arrived, I was always taking charge. I identified the problems, and I was viewed as responsible for fixing them. People were glad to take an afternoon to go off site to talk, but when we got back, it was business as usual. A big mental breakthrough for me was when I realized that I did not have to be the only path for fixing everything. Prioritizing and handing out assignments and lobbying people to fix things, that should all be a part of the IS director's role, but we needed a systematic management approach that let them [IS staff] take control of problem solving on their own. That's when we encouraged IS managers and personnel to meet freely with staff of other functional areas to discuss common concerns and come up with common solutions. We started to see some buy in!

In essence, this initial stage of exploration became a set of “issue driven” activities in which a shared understanding of problems and likely solutions was slowly forged through informal networking. Interestingly, the IS director assumed the role of an investigator, collecting and reconciling problem-solving concepts as well as gathering other functional managers’ impressions of products and services delivered by the IS organization. By adopting this role, the IS manager gained a “first glimpse” of the customer’s view toward the IS function and, from this, identified an initial set of processes that seemed especially troubled. For example, these discussions revealed that the turnaround of help-desk requests was very lengthy and procedures for PC acquisition were tedious and time consuming.

As experienced by Bose IS, it is easy to stagnate within the phase of exploration. Given the pressures of day-to-day responsibilities, IS personnel did not initially step forward to take ownership for problems that were easier to pass on to users. Without a high degree of personal buy-in, there was an absence of a leadership coalition beyond the IS director and a lack of urgency among staff. A key event in transferring the initiative from words to action was the emergence of a coalition between IS personnel and peer managers from other functions. This “networking outside of the hierarchy" provided an additional channel of communication through which a shared assessment of both problems and opportunities could be developed. Gradually, through many informal discussions such as breakfast and lunch-time gatherings, a sufficient vocabulary was gathered, permitting the framing of problems through shared observations and common process-oriented language. This chain of events provided the foundation necessary for more formal forums of cross-functional skill building and TQ experimentation.

## Discovery: 1988–1990

To more aggressively identify customer concerns before they became crises, account teams for IS's largest internal customers were created. As the IS director recalls:

We had 18 people from the IS organization serving as account managers to clients. Teams of four-five people were going to the VP of manufacturing, sales, marketing, and other heads of business units, first to follow up on annual surveys and then to hold quarterly account meetings. These initial gripe sessions turned into issue resolution discussions.

In contrast to grappling with definitional aspects of problems, the discovery leg of the process improvement journey was driven by a common search by IS and its customers for "knowledge" regarding the best approaches for problem solving. As noted by the IS director:

The language definitely changed. Instead of rolling around in problems, we began to formally organize our networking activities within IS and across other functions. Our focus became learning and experimenting with quality concepts and reporting these experiences to others in a consistent and organized fashion.

As implied in this observation, the dialogue within IS as well as between IS and other functional areas within the discovery stage centered on formal TQ skill building and localized experimentation. For example, a self-help interest group dubbed the Process Improvement Networking Group (PING) was formed in October 1989 and included members from marketing, engineering, sales, finance, R&D, accounting, and IS. The mission of this voluntary, cross-functional improvement network was to develop the skills of each participant with respect to process improvement and management of change. The mission was supported through:

1. Sharing of ideas through group problem solving and discussion of process improvement.

2. Experimental use of TQ and process improvement practices within each function.

3. Benchmarking through visits to other companies.

4. Formal training in TQ practices.

Because the group lacked more than a general understanding of process improvement philosophies and sensed the need for synthesis among their efforts, a management consultant was retained as a tutor and moderator. As the IS director noted:

We were all operational and functional managers. We depended somewhat on each other, and we had to talk about process improvement. We knew we had to fix problems, but we did not know how. As our understanding of TQ grew through PING sharing, we recognized TQ as a different notion of empowerment. If I teach you tools to formulate and solve problems, then you don't have to tell me you are working on a problem. You can find and fix problems in your own area. That's a shift of power and responsibility.

Beyond PING, the director of IS began a self-education process—familiarizing himself with process and total quality concepts. In September of 1990 he helped organize a Society of Information Management Working Group (SIM, 1992) in which quality practices were shared from companies such as Xerox, Corning, Pratt-Witney, Eastman Chemical, and Milliken. Involvement in this working group heightened his awareness regarding core sets of TQ tools and concepts that could be readily applied in further developing the IS organization and in helping coordinate improvement activities between IS and other members of PING.

As a repository of “skills and experiences” was gathered through training and observation of quality practices at other firms, the compelling challenge became one of fitting the techniques to the idiosyncrasies of attitude, existing patterns of work, and reward systems within Bose. Since it was difficult to determine how the interjection of process improvement activity would interact with “mission critical” business systems, “managed experimentation” was instituted. This tactic consists of attacking a process problem that is well-understood, easily measured in terms of outputs, and is likely to be resolved successfully. For Bose IS, the process for acquiring PCS and software seemed well-suited for this initial improvement effort. Consistent with TQ practice, an improvement team was trained in quality concepts and assigned a theme, in this case, shortening the acquisition cycle. The improvement activities of such experimental TQ teams were tracked and reported back to the PING. As the collection of these localized experiments grew, more complex process experiments were undertaken until a general consensus of “best practice” regarding improvement activities within the cultural context of Bose emerged.

While establishing a set of proven process improvement techniques represented an important step, a major managerial challenge loomed in progressing beyond localized experimentation into more formal and wide-spread programs of process management. It became evident that wide-spread TQ implementation could not take place unless there was cultural readiness. To reinforce cultural commitment as well as build confidence for taking on greater improvement challenges, IS leaders determined that workers had to see immediate and tangible results from their TQ efforts. Without early success, confidence in newly acquired TQ skills might fade and momentum for improvement activities wane. The careful choice of initial improvement projects fostered a sense of accomplishment and provided a relatively low risk forum for refining improvement skills. Although IS progressed in addressing more complex process improvements, several other functional areas of Bose stagnated in the discovery phase primarily because of a lack of initial successes.

Another important factor in the shaping of culture was the practice of credible managerial communication. In essence, managers within Bose IS became living embodiments of process improvement through words and deeds. "Walking the talk" by incorporating process improvement practices into daily business meetings, performance appraisals, and routine question and answer sessions established high levels of credible communication, making employees believe that meaningful process improvements were possible and desired. Finally, given potential inconsistencies between widespread TQ and the prevailing "product-out" culture, top management support was solicited to reaffirm the need for change and to solidify sincere commitment to process initiatives.

## Formalization: 1991–1992

The activities of PING provided a useful framework for instituting a general set of TQ standards that, in turn, initiated the dialogue for the "policy driven" phase of formalization. The primary activities that supported this phase were codification of methods and results reporting, formal training of the rank and file, and diffusion of improvement work throughout IS. Within this phase, the director of IS found himself adopting the role of coach. Motivational aspects of leadership were required to impress upon rank and file the need for new methods and techniques, the usefulness of training programs, and the fact that this set of programs was more than the "latest fad" from management. As noted by the director of IS:

As a management team, we really had to rally the troops. We invested in formal TQ training for our employees and tied incentive systems to process improvement activities. Importantly, improvement activities do not necessarily connate successful outcomes. We have learned to "fail-forward." The goals are awareness, competence, and involvement.

Systems analysts, programmers, and operators began to think about and identify problem processes and to constructively diagnose root causes. To move beyond experimental projects to a more systematic process improvement method, Bose IS formally adopted the "improvement team" concept, and, in September 1991, 16 Bose Improvement Teams (BITs) were launched.

BITs may be designated by management or may be a volunteer group that forms itself. When a BIT has completed its improvement work, a standardized work process is put in place. The specific qualifications, roles, and responsibilities of BIT members and their supporting sponsors are defined as follows:

BIT Member — An individual who has the desire to improve the way daily work is done and who joins with others to effect positive change. BIT members receive training in Total Quality and learn methods for contributing their ideas.

BIT Leader — A BIT member chosen by the team to coordinate activities. BIT leaders receive additional training in leadership and facilitation skills.

BIT Advisor — An individual who is knowledgeable about Total Quality methods and who has been designated by management to serve as a coach to BITs. A BIT advisor is specifically assigned to each BIT team by their BIT Steering Committee member. It is best if the advisor is NOT part of the regular management or supervision of the team.

BIT Sponsor — A member of management who is interested in promoting the use of BITs and Total Quality methods to improve certain areas of the business, or to resolve specific problems.

BIT Steering Committee — A networking group that provides support for the corporation's ongoing Bose Improvement Team (BIT)

initiative. The BIT Steering Committee is composed of volunteers from each functional area.

Along with the formalization of roles and responsibilities, a structured process for initiating group formation and coordinating subsequent activities was instituted. The BIT cycle illustrated in Figure 2 is unique in that the BIT's task is conceptualized as ongoing—continuously attempting to improve problem processes and identify emerging process improvement opportunities.

A BIT begins with the discovery of a problem. This “improvement opportunity” may be detected by management or by employees who feel that the problem prevents them from doing the best job possible. A team is then formed by individuals who have a vested interest in solving the problem, and the process weakness is written out as a theme by the team. Support from management is then requested, which results in the assignment of an advisor to the team. Once a theme is created, improvement activities can follow one of two paths. If the improvement activity centers on a process that is known, seven-step reactive problem solving is utilized (Mizuno, 1988; Shiba, et al., 1993). If a process is not well-understood or needs to be designed, process discovery (a more proactive improvement technique developed by Bose) is utilized.

## Seven-Step Reactive Problem Solving: The Bisens

A lack of network reliability within the engineering function of Bose provided the impetus for the creation of a BIT dubbed the "Bisens." This name, which is an acronym for Bose Information Systems and Engineering Networkers, reflects the cross-functional team membership and shared process ownership between IS and engineering. Since the process and procedures associated with the administration of the network were understood, the seven-step reactive problem solving was employed. Figure 3 provides a graphical depiction of the activities and associated techniques utilized by this team in addressing this improvement opportunity.

Figure 2. Bose Improvement Teams: A Cycle of Improvement Activity

The initial task of the Bisens was to create a written theme to guide their improvement effort. After some debate, the team agreed that network downtime was the overriding issue that could be substantively addressed within a reasonable time period. This general theme was refined through the addition of a measurable goal (reduce downtime by 50 percent), a metric (downtime = hours in which at least one machine on the net is down), and a time frame for achieving the goal (three months). Next, the team collected data related to network activity. Network breakdowns were categorized by occurrence, length of breakdown, and nature of network failures. As shown in Figure 3, a Pareto chart of these events suggested that network downtime was indeed a significant problem (in some months machines were down 89 percent of the time) and that equipment failure was the largest contributor to network downtime. The identification of root causes for network downtime were then uncovered through cause and effect (fishbone) diagramming (Ishikawa, 1986). As shown in Figure 3, planning, wiring, equipment, and manpower formed the broad classes of causes associated with downtime. Specific root causes within these broad classes that seemed particularly problematic included incompatible cabling, lack of standards, lack of documentation, and a lack of diagnostic equipment.

The root causes identified through the exercise of cause and effect diagramming were used by the Bisens for planning and implementing improvements to the process of network administration. Compatible cabling schemes were identified and implemented as well as systems for documenting, labeling, and maintaining other components of the network. The impact of these effects was then evaluated through data collected on network activity after the implementation of process improvements. As shown in Figure 3, no hours of network downtime were observed subsequent to these improvements, therefore the solution was standardized. In the final step of this method, the Bisens reflected on the process for improving the process. In essence, the team felt that too much time was spent in the initial step of arriving at a theme. However, they also noted that the improvement process was beneficial in opening up critical lines of communication between IS and engineering. In summary, the activities of this BIT corrected a troubled process, which in turn resolved the unfavorable variance in network outcomes. Just as important, channels of communication were also created for further improvement opportunities.

While the formalization phase was critical for moving improvement work to a larger scale, Bose IS also learned that decentralized experimentation of process improvement efforts can easily dissolve into an array of confusing and disjointed projects, which can lead the organization in the wrong direction or in no direction at all. In order to realize gains associated with integrated process activity, it is essential that process improvement methods, tools, and activities be structured, focused on a common vision, and cast as ongoing. Within Bose IS, the process vision integrating BIT activities became the delight of both internal and external customers. In addition, improvement was woven into the fabric of business activity, creating a process mindset among employees in which improvement is not something accomplished in addition to everyday work—it is a part of everyday work.

## Process-think: 1992–1995

Initially, most improvement efforts within Bose IS were reactive in nature. BIT teams essentially responded to complaints or concerns levied by internal IS customers. However, as personnel became skilled, Bose IS began to negotiate the transition to an even higher level of process awareness. This post formalization phase of activity focuses on acting proactively rather than reactively in the identification of process opportunities and transferring process thinking to internal customers whose processes interface with those of IS. However, in contrast to earlier legs of the Bose IS process journey, movement beyond the stage of formalization has provided very different, and significantly more difficult, managerial challenges.

As an initial attempt to move beyond the use of only the reactive (seven-step) approach, a second, more proactive process improvement method was developed and honed over several BIT projects. This method is called process discovery and is used when a process is not well defined, is very dysfunctional, or is unstructured. In contrast to reactive approaches, this method requires the deliberate definition of process products, services, owners, customers, resources, and mapping. While still applied predominately in response to customer-identified problems, it represents a major move by Bose IS toward greater “process thinking” and a proactive focus.

## An Example of Proactive Improvement Through Process Discovery: The Installers

The improvement story of the "Installers" is representative of a BIT using the process discovery method. As illustrated in Figure 4, this improvement team used process discovery to design and implement a process to deploy application enhancements.

Upon creation of a theme, the Installers embarked into process discovery by identifying the products and services (deliverables) that would flow from the designed process. These outputs may be tangible products or informational products that are sent or communicated to customers outside of the group. As shown in Figure 4, the Installers identified a variety of products and services that constitute the "ends" of application enhancement activities. The next step of process discovery is the establishment of process ownership. The process owner is responsible for overall process performance and must coordinate all process functions to ensure cost-effective customer satisfaction. The systems development group was identified by the Installers as the logical locus of ownership for this process, and accordingly, roles and responsibilities of process participants and the process owner were formally defined. Next, the BIT focused on identifying customers for each product and service through a product by customer matrix. Together, these initial phases of process discovery provided the BIT with a general framework of process outputs, responsibilities, and customers from which a shared system of communication among team members could be formed.

In the final steps of process discovery, customer requirements are identified, resources needed to meet these needs are defined, and formal mapping of the process is undertaken. Process requirements are determined through formal survey of customers across dimensions of quality (how well does product or service meet customer needs?), cost (is the product or service worth the cost?), and delivery (was the product or service available when needed?). Through this activity, the Installers found that customers valued constant feedback with respect to development progress, on-time delivery, and equity in project costing practices. Utilizing these expectations, the Installers then identified the inputs and supporting resources necessary for fulfilling customer expectations. The inputs, outputs, and sequence derived from the earlier phases were then used by the Installers to create a process map. As shown in Figure 4, this map structures process activities across inputs and outputs, process checks, and interaction between the customer and process work. After a "walk through" by BIT members and formal testing, this process was then standardized and continuously monitored.

## Integrating Efforts Through Core Process Redesign

While BITs have shown great success in achieving smaller and more incremental process improvements, it has only been in the last few years that Bose IS has moved to tie these efforts together into a strategy-driven, cross-functional process view. In 1994, the IS organization in conjunction with other process-advanced functions adopted a strategy to break the enterprise-wide process think barrier. Leveraging greater buy-in from senior management earned from the success of the BITs efforts, a new top-down-driven focus on "core" process redesign was initiated. Core process redesign is defined as being opportunistic and should have high pay-off. A prerequisite for core process redesign is that principle process owners be significantly transgressed up the process improvement learning curve. Efforts have been made to equip the process owners with requisite process redesign skills necessary to conduct large-scale projects.

An initial foray into the “core business process design” method is the two-year-old “horizon’s project,” which seeks to redesign the majority of subprocesses within the defined “market to collection core process” of Bose’s component audio operations. The intent of the project is to empower the customer engagement personnel and unify order management-related activities with requirements planning. The selected business automation platform includes the installation of SAP R/3. This project utilizes a variation of the process discovery method and is heavily reliant on consulting and technical support from the IS department. A component of the approach includes the mapping of current process subclasses and the design and fit of a desired process architecture. The “time to market process” is the second targeted core area for process design and improvement. As a component of this project, the COO recently challenged management with the “stretch” goal of reducing the time to market core process from 12-37 months to nine months across all product lines by FY '97.

## Moving Laggards Up the Process Management Learning Curve

While many individuals and departments have made great strides toward process management, other personnel and departments have been much slower in their transgression. In July 1994, the COO, recognizing that the entire firm must progress up the learning curve if Bose is to meaningfully redesign all its core business processes, named the director of IS to the newly created position of director of corporate total quality. A major responsibility associated with this position is to move the entire firm toward integrated process thinking.

Instituted in 1995, the “micro process approach” is a strategy to gently push laggards up the learning curve by personalizing process improvement into small “doable” processes. The director of total quality states that the approach is basically a learning exercise to familiarize these individuals and functional areas with process thinking as well as its associated tools:

We have found from experience that training and actual hands-on application of process improvement techniques on micro or personal processes is a wonderful learning tool that we believe will be a strong behavioral migration path.

Experimenting with this approach, the director of total quality has placed a TQ advocate within the North American sales operations (viewed as a laggard in process thinking) and is working with several "insider" TQ champions in conducting TQ planning, process discovery, and BIT training. Upon completion of this training, all employees will select a small or personal process that requires improvement. They will identify the problem, identify customers, map it, measure it, make design changes, implement it, and track it. Initial successes seem to indicate that this behavioral-based approach is working, and plans are in place to implement it on a wide-scale basis.

Although process-think is a continuing challenge for Bose IS, two issues have emerged as important points of managerial intervention. First, it is important to establish a direct link between integrated process management activity and the competitive well-being of the enterprise. The sum of individual process successes within functional areas is not as great as the payoff from integrated process success across the entire enterprise. Integration has a direct impact on organizational adaptability and flexibility. Without an understanding of how process integration impacts corporate well being, sub-optimization within functional domains may inhibit the realization of greater competitive positioning.

It is also important to realize that process management is an endless journey that winds through the dynamics of task, process, function, and value chain. The vision of Bose management is to balance the portfolio of process skills and experiences across the functional areas such that significant improvement in core processes can be realized. Customer delight through continuous improvement provides the common theme for eliminating disparities in process skill between functional areas and keeping quality at the forefront of everyone's agenda. While such a vision can bind all employees and departments, it is also important to recognize that individuals and departments will vary in their learning breath and speed. These variations have required Bose to develop alternative but simultaneous routes to process thinking ranging from personal/micro behavioral strategies to large-scale core process redesign.

## Process linkage: 1996–beyond

The foreseen evolution of process management within Bose IS is “process linking.” In this yet-to-be-realized stage, business-based teams of internal and external partners share responsibility for managing the conglomeration of business process that constitute the value chain. The dialogue of this “market-driven” view of process management is the transfer of process improvement practice to external business partners. An initial move within the realm of process linkage has been in the “integrated supply chain core process.”

Based on their ability to meet TQ standards, Bose has empowered 10 of its major suppliers (plastics, printing materials, metal components, trucking, import/export services, corrugated packing, office supplies) to manage the Bose inventory—feeding corresponding production processes on a just-in-time basis. Of the 10 partnerships, only one has failed (from lack of volume). The direct benefits of process linkage include reduced inventory, lower transaction costs, faster response to problems, increased value engineering, and decreased procurement costs. Benefits to linked suppliers include increased volume (the plastics supplier's volume has increased three fold), lower production costs because of better coordination, and improved quality in operations.

An obstacle that seems to confront Bose in fully moving into the process linkage stage is difficulty in mobilizing a critical mass of trusted business partners that adhere to process management philosophies and standards. However, as Bose continues to mature in process management activities, it is anticipated that transfer of skills, regulated decision rights, and managerial systems to key members of their business alliance will lock in competitive and financial efficiencies, which will be extremely difficult to match by competitors who have just embarked on the journey toward integrated process management.

## Rewards of the Process Improvement Journey

While the journey toward process management has been costly in terms of managerial time, training, and financial resources for Bose IS, the adoption of quality philosophies and process management activities has significantly improved customer perception and key performance metrics associated with the creation, delivery, and support of information products and services. Over time, these improvement teams have grown in terms of the complexity and scope of their improvement efforts while decreasing the cycle time for project completion. Such efforts have been facilitated through training in quality-based structured problem solving (the seven-step method) and through the continuous refinement of methods for understanding and mapping process activities (process discovery). An important example of this phenomenon is the gain in productive deployment of human, machine, and financial resources through the redesign and creation of processes for systems development. From 1992–1994, the percent of systems development projects not completed on schedule fell from 20 percent to 8 percent and the percent of projects not completed on budget fell from 60 percent to 15 percent. In addition, the number of new systems implemented between

1992 and 1994 increased by 133 percent and the monthly average of outstanding project requests has fallen by 70 percent.

Overall, the efforts of these and other IS-based BITs have resulted in improved customer relations, greater cross-functional coordination, and subsequently a heightened role for IS within the enterprise. Importantly, personnel within IS are now equipped with a battery of process improvement techniques that efficiently channel their time and energy allowing the IS organization to achieve greater objectives with little increment in resources. This knowledge and improvement experience has also positioned IS to operate effectively within the process-based governance structure now envisioned by Bose's senior management. ## Improvement measured through the voice of the customer

Perhaps the most significant manifestation of gains within Bose IS are the perceptions of products and services delivered to its customers. On a yearly basis, the IS group surveys customers of its five functional divisions (Administrative Services, Technical Services, Information Systems, IS Consulting, and IS Administration) to measure both their reliance and satisfaction with products and services provided. As shown in Figure 5, integration of these metrics into an Improvement Resource Deployment Matrix (IRDM) allows IS management to assess their present ability to meet the needs of customers and accurately direct improvement efforts for better meeting needs. When satisfaction is significantly greater than reliance for a service, a large, positive performance gap is created, suggesting that some improvement resources should be redeployed to areas where satisfaction is low but reliance is high. Within the IRDM of Figure 5, this phenomenon would be represented by a transfer of resources from the “overextended” cell to the “major problem area” cell of the first grid. Such transfers of resources in the form of improvement efforts are designed to manage critical services into the upper right quadrant of the grid where IS is meeting or exceeding the needs of its customers.

As illustrated by the second and third grids of Figure 5, investment in TQ has significantly improved the positioning of the IS function's service portfolio. In particular, Technical Services and Corporate IS, both critical providers of equipment and software, have transgressed from "major problem areas" to the more favorable position of "meeting needs." In addition, Administrative Services and IS Consulting have shown favorable movement through the grid as a result of TQ activities. These votes of confidence from the customer in addition to the measurable outcomes of BIT activities have provided a sound affirmation within Bose for the time, expense, and effort devoted to quality practice. Importantly, the journey continues for IS as changing dynamics in product markets, technologies, and value-chain relationships necessitate improvement or redesign of processes within the business system.

## Implications and Conclusions

As discovered by Bose, the emergence of a "world class quality organization" that exists to delight customers is organized through seamless processes; encourages employees to be alert to new opportunities; and carries with it long-term growth and profitability may result from continuous organizational learning rather than radical reconfiguration. Such findings seem consistent with recent field studies that have examined organizational assimilation of process-based management (Shrednick, et al., 1992) and process reengineering (Caron, et al., 1994).

This study extends earlier works by providing structure to programs of process-based transformation in terms of definable stages and managerial interventions for sustaining organizational momentum. These characteristics of the Bose process improvement journey provide useful metrics for assessing specific levels of organizational learning and potential points of stagnation. This study also highlights specific techniques/methodologies utilized by Bose IS in orchestrating improvement work and their associated impacts. ## Launching a process management initiative

Often the launch of process improvement programs may begin at the phase of formalization (Harrington, 1991). In these instances, a grand strategy for business transformation, accompanied by new policies and operating procedures, is delivered by senior management to operational personnel for implementation. An implied and often fatal assumption of such top-down directives is that programs of process improvement can readily be generalized from context-to-context (Earl, et al., 1995). However, given the high percentage of BPR and TQ program failure reported in the popular press, this assumption may not hold. Within Bose IS, leaders recognized very early that process improvement techniques and existing organizational culture need to be synthesized into a pattern of work that leverages the strengths of both. The tools of this synthesis,

BITs (Figure 2), seven-step reactive process improvement (Figure 3), process discovery (Figure 4) and the improvement resource deployment matrix (Figure 5) were forged through an evolutionary and informal process of interfunctional information sharing and localized experimentation before formal diffusion through standard policy and procedure. These experiences have two closely related implications for other IS managers launching improvement efforts. First, improvement work need not begin with the grand launch of a new program. A more successful tact may be to incubate process improvement activities in key areas through interfunctional partnering and information sharing outside of the formal corporate hierarchy. Second, it seems important that managers craft improvement techniques and tools as well as existing patterns of work such that a supportive context for learning is created and sustained.

## Sustaining momentum for higher levels of improvement activity

Many senior IS managers may find that gains in process improvement activities have fallen short of expectations or even regressed over time. This state-of-affairs often leads managers and their constituents to conclude that marginal gains in improvement do not justify marginal increases in improvement work. In extreme cases, organizations may assume that the "war is won," thereby freeing resources for other pressing issues. As discovered within Bose IS, the phenomenon of diminishing return on improvement activity may be a symptom of lost momentum rather than a sign of over-achievement. These managers recognized that improvement efforts should be managed much like a portfolio of systems projects. In the initial stages of the improvement program, the portfolio should consist almost entirely of improvement projects that are well understood and bounded. Such a portfolio provides a context for training and early success among improvement workers. However, as a base of knowledge is gathered, it is important to balance the portfolio of improvement work with processes that are large in scope, complex, not well understood, or which have yet to be designed. Avoiding these types of projects presents a high cost to the organization in terms of lost opportunities. However, addressing complex projects without a foundation of incremental improvement activity may present improvement teams with work they are not equipped to handle. The implication for IS managers is that momentum for higher-levels of improvement work comes from carefully matching improvement opportunities with existing levels of skill and expertise. Improvement work should be orchestrated such that current projects provide the necessary staging for more complex and higher impact process activity.

## Integrating improvement efforts through a process vision

Perhaps the most important implication of the Bose experience for IS management is the need for a process vision to integrate improvement work and provide a basis of planning. Within Bose IS, the process vision is to exist solely for the satisfaction of internal and external customers. Along with improvement work through BITs, this vision implies a transfer of process thinking. For example, customers with development requests must specify the process the system will support, the process owners, and metrics of process performance. If the customer is unsure of how to generate these process specifications, Bose IS offers assistance and training in process mapping and measurement. This practice ensures that a process focus guides the design and development of new systems from both the analyst and user perspectives. On an even more ambitious level, Bose IS seeks to link development efforts between itself and partners in the Bose business network such that virtual information exchange can be created for improving service. Such networks promise greater accessibility by suppliers, retailers, and consumers to design product and manufacturing information. This accessibility will facilitate the gathering of customer input for improved product design and narrow the time required for getting products to market.

These initiatives are enabled by Bose's guiding vision. Without this vision, process-based improvement efforts cannot build upon one another causing the overall program of transformation to resemble a chaotic collection of projects rather than a program of managed learning directed at enhancing organizational and value-chain performance. Clearly, Bose has set lofty future goals, in which IS and process management play crucial roles. Whether Bose will reach universal process thinking, complete process linking, or achieve world-class quality is still a matter of conjecture. Ultimately, process management will be sustained at Bose if guiding philosophies are innate to existing culture and the value of improvement work demonstrated to all stakeholders. As noted by Dr. Bose:

We have already engaged the battle, but we have major challenges to meet if Bose is to be a billion dollar company by the end of the decade. When I think of what we want to do, I see it as a journey. I don't see us ever being done. We keep coming up with more ideas and ways in which we can improve and use these techniques. In that sense, it's not that we're going to "do" total quality, it's just that we're on an endless journey of improvement.
