# New insights into the problem of software piracy: The effects of neutralization, shame, and moral beliefs

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

Software piracy is a major economic concern for organizations. Previous research indicates that neutralization, a form of rationalization, can help explain software piracy intentions. However, a knowledge gap exists in our understanding of which neutralization techniques most influence software piracy intention. To address this gap, we developed a model that explains the effects of neutralization techniques on software piracy intention. We included different types of deterrents (formal sanctions, shame, and moral belief) in our model because individuals may use neutralization techniques to mitigate feelings of guilt and shame, which, subsequently, reduce the deterrent effect. Our empirical results (for 183 people surveyed) showed that appeal to higher loyalties and condemn the condemners strongly predict software piracy intentions. In addition, informal deterrents such as shame and moral beliefs are strong predictors. These findings suggest that anti-piracy efforts should involve educational intervention aimed at addressing these two neutralization techniques rather than relying on formal sanctions.

## 1. Introduction

Software piracy within organizations poses legal liability risks to companies, which several international standards seek to address via software asset management and audits (e.g., ISO 17799, 19770, 27001; ISO 2005a, 2005b; ISO 2006). It is perhaps no surprise, therefore, that this problem has received considerable attention, with researchers advancing a number of approaches to explain why individuals pirate software [4,6,16].

Previous research indicates that neutralization, a form of rationalizing, is an especially effective way of explaining software piracy [11]; it not only explains why people pirate software, but also why sanctions do not deter them from doing so. It also describes how people use neutralization techniques, or specific rationalizations, to justify breaking a law or social norm. However, previous research has not examined which neutralization techniques contribute most to software piracy. Those who argue for the need to understand what techniques are related to specific crimes consider this a serious deficiency [9].

To address this research gap, we tried to determine which neutralization techniques are most effective in explaining software piracy. We proposed a model that examined the effects of various neutralization techniques on intention to pirate software. Also, since deterrents, such as formal sanctions, shame, and moral beliefs, may lose their efficacy when neutralization techniques are used [17], we added a second research objective: to investigate whether these deterrents are more or less effective than neutralization techniques in the same model.

To test our model, we performed an empirical study using executive graduate students with work experience (a sample of 183 respondents).

## 2. Related work

Many studies have examined the influence of different factors on software piracy (see [18] for a review). These can be classified into four categories: (1) studies using TRA/TPB, (2) studies applying theories of ethical decision-making, (3) studies testing the influence of other factors on software piracy, and (4) studies based on neutralization theory.

Of these approaches, Morris and Higgins [13] applied neutralization theory from criminology to demonstrate that such techniques were particularly well-suited for explaining software piracy because they describe how people rationalize to justify breaking laws or societal norms and thus why sanctions do not deter software piracy. However, unfortunately, we see one deficiency in their work: they assessed the effect of six different neutralization techniques, but examined them as a single construct. This provided no insight into which techniques contribute most to software piracy. Only when this is known will it be possible to provide tailored intervention, e.g., by developing an education campaign.

## 3. Theoretical model development

Our model was based on two criminological theories: neutralization and deterrence.

## 3.1. Neutralization theory

Research has shown that offenders often rationalize to justify their criminal behavior. Such rationalization, technically known as neutralization techniques, help attenuate the feeling of guilt and shame that may inhibit criminal actions. Seven techniques have been studied: condemn the condemners, denial of injury, metaphor of the ledger, appeal to higher loyalties, the defense of necessity, denial of responsibility, and denial of the victim. These appear on the top bar of Fig. 1.

Condemn the condemners occurs when offenders move the focus of attention from their actions to actions of those who oppose such behavior. A criminal may, view the police as brutal, corrupt, or stupid. Thus, an offender is able to downplay the criminality of his or her actions. This is demonstrated in a study of bodybuilders and their illicit use of steroids. When asked: ‘‘What would you say to someone who claimed bodybuilders were drug abusers?’’ one respondent answered: ‘‘The people that normally think like that have a pint of beer in one hand, a big fat stomach, and a bag of French fries in the other hand.’’ Thus we hypothesized:

H1. ‘‘Condemn the condemners’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

The denial of injury technique involves delinquents rationalizing their behavior by diminishing the perceived harm of their actions. An offender may view auto theft as borrowing and vandalism as mischief. Evidence of this technique was found by Piquero et al.

[15], who evaluated the responses of MBA students on committing corporate crime; they were asked to fill out a questionnaire that contained a scenario based on a hypothetical drug. It stated that the drug had been banned by the Food and Drug Administration. The students were asked whether they would either inhibit or further the distribution of the drug. They were given a number of statements that reflected several neutralization techniques and were asked their views on the statements by responding on a Likert scale anchored at 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) [for example, a denial of injury, the statement read, ‘‘The government exaggerates the danger to consumers from most products.’’]. The researchers found that such a denial of injury significantly affected the students’ decisions to commit a corporate crime. Thus we hypothesized:

H2. ‘‘Denial of injury’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

With the metaphor of the ledger, an individual compares his or her criminal behavior (a debit) with his or her law-abiding behavior (a credit). Thus, the individual rationalizes the debit as insignificant and justified when compared with his or her overall honest activities, thus rationalizing deviant behavior. Thus we hypothesized:

H3. The ‘‘metaphor of the ledger’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

Appeal to higher loyalties occurs when a criminal is able to diminish the external and internal controls imposed by society by acquiescing to the demands of a smaller group. Evidence of this technique was provided by Cromwell and Thurman [2], who studied shoplifting. One respondent noted, ‘‘I never do it except when I’m with my friends. In order to be part of the group.’’ Thus we hypothesized:

H4. The ‘‘appeal to higher loyalties’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

The defense of necessity allows an individual to rationalize his or her criminal behavior as unavoidable. The white-collar criminal, for example, may argue that while certain business activities may be illegal (e.g., bribes), they are commonplace and necessary to remain competitive. Gray [7] provides an example of this technique in his observation of workplace health and safety at a factory in Canada. He noted that his supervisor was prepared to violate health and safety requirements on an assembly line. The supervisor justified these actions by claiming the company was under pressure and had to ship an important, but late, order from the factory. Thus we hypothesized:

H5. ‘‘Defense of necessity’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

The denial of responsibility involves the offender rationalizing his or her actions in a manner that absolves him or her of responsibility and thus avoids censure from others. Evans and Porsche [5] studied Medicare/Medicaid fraud by speech, occupational, and physical therapists; through a series of in-depth interviews, they found that three techniques were used to justify their actions. The second most commonly cited was denial of responsibility, which was used to justify the practice of cutting short a therapy session, but still charging for the whole. Some therapists would blame the patients for this behavior, thus shifting the responsibility onto the patient. As one respondent argued, ‘‘Sometimes my patients are sick and coughing so I cut the session short. Other times they are just uncooperative.’’ Thus we hypothesized:

H6. ‘‘Denial of responsibility’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

Finally, offenders may rationalize their actions through a denial of victim technique; in this, if the victim is ‘‘absent, unknown, or a vague abstraction,’’ the offender is able to marginalize his or her existence and thereby rationalize the crime as ‘‘victimless’’. A variation of this specific technique acknowledges that an offender may rationalize and justify his or her actions as rightful retaliation or punishment. Copes [1] interviewed 42 offenders who had stolen a vehicle. He noted that many of the offenders believed that their victims were ‘‘deserving’’ targets, who were stupid or careless. One offender explained, ‘‘He was stupid enough to leave the keys in the motorcycle. He wanted someone to steal it. So, I was doing him a favor, you know?’’ Thus we hypothesized:

H7. ‘‘Denial of the victim’’ positively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

## 3.2. Deterrence theory

While we expected the influence of neutralization techniques on software piracy would be substantial, we also realized that situational factors were likely to exert an additional influence on a person’s decision to commit software piracy. Deterrence theory is very influential in explaining norm-violating behavior [12]. Its influence has been noted not only in the field of criminology, but also in software piracy [14]. It explains individuals’ decisions to commit crimes as a computation of the benefits of performing a crime (utility) less the punishment or sanction from being caught (disutility). Although commonly applied to explain criminal behavior, such theories explain law-breaking or any behavior that violates social norms. Thus, an individual may commit a crime if the expected benefits outweigh the expected costs.

Although deterrence-theory-based models originally included severity and certainty of formal sanctions, less tangible sanctions, such as shame and moral beliefs, have recently been added. We include each of them in our model.

## 3.2.1. Shame

Shame is to a feeling of guilt or embarrassment induced if others know of one’s own socially undesirable actions. It can be regarded as a deterrent, because it is assumed to have effects similar to other sanctions. Thus, we hypothesized:

H8. Shame negatively affects the intention to commit software piracy.

## 3.2.2. Formal sanctions

Formal sanctions are explicit penalties imposed for certain forms of misconduct. The more forceful or effective the sanction, the greater the deterrence of undesirable behavior. Severity and certainty are important factors that determine the effectiveness of a sanction. D’Arcy et al. [3] found that the severity of formal sanctions had a significant negative effect on users’ intentions to commit computer abuse. We therefore hypothesized:

H9. Formal sanctions negatively affect the intention to commit software piracy.

## 3.2.3. Moral beliefs

Offenders may refrain from acting badly, not because they fear sanctions, but because they evaluate the offense as morally wrong. Recent research on deterrence theory has incorporated such moral beliefs [19]. Interestingly, Thus, we hypothesized:

H10. Moral beliefs negatively affect the intention to commit software piracy.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Scenario method

We used a hypothetical scenario method to examine software piracy empirically. Each short scenario presents respondents with a situation followed by a question asking whether they would behave in the same way under similar circumstances [10]. This method was chosen because software piracy, like other socially undesirable behavior, is difficult to measure directly due to the respondents’ tendency to conceal information or to respond to questions in socially desirable ways. Our hypothetical, third person approach provided an indirect means of measuring socially undesirable behavior, offering respondents a less intimidating means of reporting their true feelings.

We developed four scenarios (see Table A1 of Appendix A) they describe four different situations involving software piracy. Each participant randomly received one of the four scenarios.

## 4.2. Instrumentation

Where possible, items were drawn from previously validated studies. In cases where previous instruments used only a single indicator for an independent variable, additional items were derived to enable reliability testing. For the deterrence constructs, each severity of sanction measure was multiplied by its corresponding certainty of sanction measure. This yielded several composite sanction measures that reflected both the risk and cost of perceived sanctions. The dependent variable, intention to commit software piracy, was measured using a single item: ‘‘What is the chance that you would do what [the scenario character] did in the described scenario?’’ The response scale ranged from 0 (‘‘no chance at all’’) to 10 (‘‘100 percent chance’’).

## 4.3. Data collection

Data were collected from 183 graduate students in a large European business school. Students are a key demographic for software piracy. The required sample size to evaluate our model was 100 using the common ‘‘rule of ten’’ heuristic. However, as can be seen, we had nearly twice this number, which ensured sufficient reliability of the results.

## 5. Results

We analyzed our model using SmartPLS software. We chose PLS because of its usefulness in exploratory theory-building research and its ability to simultaneously evaluate the measurement and structural models. We tested our model for convergent and discriminantvalidityandreliability,andperformedtestsforcommon methods bias; the tests are shown in Appendix B. Our model met or exceeded the accepted thresholds for rigorous IS research.

In testing our hypotheses, we controlled for the effects of two variables in our PLS model. First, because each respondent received one of four different scenarios, we controlled for the effect of the scenario type on the dependent variable. Second, we found that the reported realism of the scenario (i.e., ‘‘How believable do you think the above scenario is?’’) correlated significantly with reported intention to commit software piracy (r = 0.25, p < 0.01). We therefore also controlled for the effect of perceived realism. In model analysis, only realism had a significant effect (path coefficient 0.14, p < 0.05).

## 5.1. Results of theoretical model testing

The results of our theoretical model testing are shown in Fig. 2. Because of the directional nature of each hypothesis, one-tailed tests were used to assess significance. Of the hypothesized neutralization techniques, condemn the condemners (0.20; p < 0.01) and appeal to higher loyalties were significant (0.19; p < 0.10). Both had a strong positive effect on intention to commit software piracy. However, the effects of the remaining neutralization techniques were not significant. Thus, of hypotheses H1 through H7, only H1 and H4 were supported. For the first two deterrence hypotheses involving sanctions, the effect of shame was significant (-0.32, p < 0.01), supporting H8. However, formal sanctions had no significant effect on intention to commit software piracy. Thus, H9 was not supported. Finally, found that moral beliefs had a significant negative effect on intention, with a path coefficient of -0.18 (p < 0.01). Thus, H10 was supported.

## 6. Discussion

One of the primary objectives of our research was to examine which neutralization techniques most strongly influence software piracy. Our empirical findings provide insight into this and can be summarized into six findings. First, the neutralization technique appeal to higher loyalties significantly increased the intention to pirate software. Second, the neutralization technique condemn the condemners significantly increased intention to pirate software; this is also a new predictor of software piracy. Third, the other components of neutralization did not have a significant effect on individuals’ intentions to pirate software. Fourth, the effect of moral beliefs on intention to commit software piracy was significant. Fifth, formal sanctions had no deterring effect on software piracy intention. Although this finding is consistent with studies on corporate crime, it differs from that of Higgins et al. [8]. Sixth, although these findings suggest that sanctions may not be a strong predictor of intent to pirate software, there may be other forms of sanctions that can deter software piracy. For example, the effect of shame on intention to commit piracy was significant.

## 6.1. Limitations

Our study was subject to several limitations. First, we were limited in terms of our use of intention as the dependent variable. Although one may question whether reported intention is indicative of actual behavior, studies in ethical and criminological research have found measures of projected behavior (or intentions) to be reliable predictors of future behavior. So, though we did not measure actual behavior, the use of scenario-based intention measure is possibly a reliable approximation and offers new insights into the phenomenon of software piracy.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Our results suggest that all sections of society should use shame as a deterrent to software piracy while attempting to convince people that software piracy is morally wrong.

First, in order to address the neutralization technique of condemn the condemners and appeal to higher loyalties educators and anti-piracy organizations must make a convincing case that arguments such as ‘‘it is acceptable to copy for a friend or someone who does not have money’’ are not tenable or ‘‘software agreements are unfair’’. Second, the fact that our results suggest that certainty and severity of shame have a significant deterrent effect on software piracy opens up an avenue for campaigns and education strategies that stress the likelihood and severity of shame. Finally, software development companies, educators, and anti-piracy organizations need to make a justified and convincing case that software piracy is not morally acceptable. This has been effective in several countries where the religious bodies have been active in denouncing the use of software piracy.

## 7. Conclusion

Software piracy continues to be a major economic concern. Previous studies have indicated that neutralization can help explain software piracy intention. However, they have not indicated the specific techniques that contribute most to software piracy intention. Without this information, it is difficult to design

## Appendix B. Model validation

To establish factorial validity and reliability for our model, we followed the PLS validation procedures. To test convergent validity, we performed a bootstrap with 500 resamples and then examined the t-values of the outer model loadings. Convergent validity was demonstrated because all indicators loaded significantly on their respective latent constructs. In our case, all indicators had loadings that were significant at the 0.001 level, denoting strong convergent validity.

To evaluate discriminant validity, two tests were performed. First, the cross-loadings of measurement items on latent constructs were examined. Discriminant validity was demonstrated when all items loaded more highly on its intended construct than on any other. The difference in loadings should be at least 0.10. In this test, all items showed excellent discriminant validity (see Table B1). Therefore, the model demonstrated high discriminant validity.

A second test of discriminant validity was to compare the AVE score for each construct. In the AVE test of discriminant validity, the square root of a given construct’s AVE should be larger than any correlation of the given construct with any other construct in the model. All the results of this test demonstrate strong discriminant validity.

Finally, to test the reliability of measurement items, SmartPLS was used to compute the composite reliability score, which is evaluated in the same way as Cronbach’s a. In this step, one item, Moral1, was dropped to improve reliability. The final results exhibited reliability scores above 0.80, well over the 0.60 threshold required in exploratory research. These statistics are shown in Table B2

In examining the construct correlations reported in Table B2, we note that constructs of the various neutralization techniques were highly correlated with each other. This is to be expected, since each of the techniques represents different forms of the same overall phenomenon. Nevertheless, to test for the existence of multicollinearity problems, we ran our model using regression to obtain variance inflation factor (VIF) scores. In our analysis, no VIF score exceeded 5.4 (a general rule is that the VIF should not exceed 10); therefore, we conclude tha multicollinearity was not an issue in our data.

Finally, we evaluated our model overall by calculating both the Stone-Geisser’s Q<sup>2</sup> and the Goodness of Fit (GoF) score. In evaluating the Stone-Geisser Q<sup>2</sup>, a value greater than zero indicates the model has good predictive validity; Q<sup>2</sup> for our model was 0.35. In addition, the GoF score for our model was 1.06. According to Wetzels et al. [20], the GoF cutoff value for a model with large effect sizes should be 0.36; our model well exceeded this. Both results indicated that our model had good model fit and strong predictive validity.
