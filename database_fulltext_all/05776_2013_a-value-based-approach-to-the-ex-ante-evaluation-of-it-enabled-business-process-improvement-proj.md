# A value-based approach to the ex-ante evaluation of IT enabled business process improvement projects

## A B S T R A C T

In this study, we propose a value-based management approach for assessing the potential for process improvements enabled by an information technology (IT) solution. Based on activity based management concepts, we perform a granular, process level analysis on workflow structure and associated resource consumption to provide quantifiable measures of potential improvement prior to system implementation. The proof of concept of this approach is illustrated in a southern United States county in need of integrating different government branches through an ERP implementation. Based on prior research, we first provide a catalog of non-value added (NVA) activities that can be used in management control and governance procedures for the systematic identification of process inefficiencies. Next, by breaking down processes to the level of atomic activities, we show that minimizing NVA activities provides a systematic means to mitigate process inefficiencies. A significant observation is that different NVA activities may impact process performance to varying degrees. Consequently, line items in the request for proposals (RFP) should be weighted accordingly during the vendor selection process, contrary to the common practice of treating all line items equally.

## 1. Introduction

Business process improvement (BPI) is gaining significant momentum as 21st century organizations continually seek to optimize their underlying processes to achieve higher quality at reduced cost and cycle time [82]. A recent survey reveals that 45% of organizations expect increased engagement in business process improvement projects. Indeed, BPI projects are one of the most important justifications for information technology spending [44].

The increasing number of BPI projects brings an imperative for managers. The potential performance improvement must be assessed before project initiation. While performance improvements from a BPI project may be observed along many dimensions such as reduced cost and/or time, better quality, increased productivity, etc., tracking performance along these metrics requires appropriate management control methods that enable precise measurement [56]. In other words, quantitative assessment of process performance is an integral component of BPI success.

While modern businesses increasingly focus on BPI, two of the top ten reasons for unsuccessful BPI initiatives concern the absence of proper measurements [27] – a lack of well-defined performance measures and a lack of monitoring. Clearly defined and measurable operational goals, such as percentage reductions in cost/time/ defects, are cited as prerequisites for success. However, precise estimates of such improvements prior to implementation often prove difficult. This is further complicated by the fact that BPI is highly intertwined with information technology (IT). For most businesses, IT is essential for their core and support processes [21]. Consequently, any process improvement is almost inevitably led by IT. ‘‘. . .it’s that you just can’t improve any process without IT being integrally involved. Most companies don’t set out to do an IT project. They set out to fix distribution or hiring or pricing, and they can’t move that needle without better IT’’ [61]. It naturally follows that assessing BPI initiatives is therefore inseparable from evaluating the impact of the proposed IT solution.

In this paper, we address management’s need for appropriate methodologies for ex-ante evaluations of IT-enabled business process improvement projects. Furthermore, we demonstrate how such pre-assessment methodologies can subsequently enhance the selection of the appropriate IT solution to achieve the desired process improvement. To this end, we adopt a ‘‘value based management’’ framework [35]. Our focus is on quantifying the precise value contribution of the different processes and subprocesses in an end-to-end process. To demonstrate the viability of our approach, we employ concepts from activity based management (ABM) – a practice within the umbrella of the value based management framework [35] – in the pre-evaluation phase of a BPI project for a case management process in a large country court system in the southern United States. To estimate the potential improvement, we first identify sub-processes and activities in the entire case management business process. Next, cost analysis is employed to apportion resource consumption to those specific sub-processes and activities. These activity costs are divided into two categories: value-added (VA) and non-value-added (NVA). Value-added activities are those that contribute directly to the desired process outcome, while non-value added activities are defined as those that contribute too little or no value to the desired outcome. The potential for process improvement is then evaluated based on how well the NVA activities can be mitigated through candidate IT solutions from different vendors. An important step in this vendor/solution selection process is the use of a nontraditional request for proposals (RFP) where functional line items are weighted according to their potential NVA reduction capabilities.

The contribution of our work is to analyze the potential application of a value-based technique using ABM as a standard methodology for process analysis to provide usable and useful information on resource consumption that can contribute to an exante quantitative assessment of an IT-based process improvement. As we illustrate in this paper, decomposing business processes into sub-processes and activities followed by the subsequent minimization of NVA activities in every possible sub-process provides a systematic and rigorous methodology. The NVA detection process is guided by a catalog of performance inhibitors developed through an extensive literature review. The different NVA categories identified ex-ante are also helpful in pinpointing weaknesses in the current process. This information may be utilized ex-post for selecting appropriate key performance indicators (KPIs). For example, the presence of Technological Silos – an NVA category – in a business process may be indicative of the need to monitor a productivity KPI, as the lack of technological support hinders productivity. Furthermore, a significant observation from our work is that different NVA activities may impact process performance to varying degrees. Consequently, line items in the RFP should be weighted accordingly during the IT solution/vendor selection process, in contrast to the common practice of treating all line items equally.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we provide a brief overview of the methods used for evaluating process improvements and IT investments. In Section 3, we discuss the suitability of the value-based technique using activity-based methodology for assessing process improvements and technological and organizational inhibitors that contribute to NVA, followed by the development of an ABM based methodology for measuring process improvements in Section 4. Section 5 illustrates the implementation of our approach. In Section 6, we conclude with implications for research and practice.

## 2. Literature review: business process, performance, and the role of IT

To be able to measure business process improvements led by IT, an understanding of the term ‘business process’ is deemed necessary along with a review of the methodological approaches to measure both process improvements and IT effectiveness as it relates to organizational performance. Our criteria for selecting a specific methodology from a set of possible candidates is guided by the requirements elicited from the academic and practitioner literature on process management. First, the metrics used should be definable, measureable, and preferably not subjective in nature [27,66]. Second, the methodology should inform us how the process improvement will occur, not only what to measure [1].

Finally, as the process improvement is enabled by IT, the methodology should shed light on the causal linkage between IT, business processes, and firm performance [5,19,50].

## 2.1. Business processes

Both academics and practitioners have defined business process with slightly different emphasis and focus. For example, Davenport [15] defines business process as ‘‘a structured, measured set of activities designed to produce a specific output for a particular customer or market. It implies a strong emphasis on how work is done within an organization, in contrast to a product focus’s emphasis on what’’ (pp. 5). According to Laguna and Marklund [43], a business process ‘‘describes how something is done in an organization’’ (pp. 3). The business process management common body of knowledge [2] of the association of business process management professionals (ABPMP) defines process as a ‘‘set of activities or behaviors performed by humans or machine to achieve one or more goal. . . . Processes are composed of a collection of interrelated tasks or activities which solve a particular issue. In the context of business process management, a business process is defined as end-to-end work which delivers value to customers’’ (pp. 23). While the definition of what constitutes a business process varies slightly across academics and professional organizations, each variation of the definition emphasize that ‘activities’ constitute an essential part of a business process. Inherently, a ‘Business Process’ can be conceptualized as a (set of) activities that firms engage in to accomplish some business purpose or objective [55,60].

## 2.2. Process performance/improvement

From the discussion of business processes in the preceding section, it is apparent that measuring process improvements essentially entails an analysis of how activities within an end-toend business process will be impacted by the proposed enhancement project. Those impacts are reflected in and may be captured through two types of performance – operational efficiency and operational effectiveness. Operational efficiency relates to measures such as cost, time, product/service quality, and customer satisfaction [16] and should be measured both before and after system implementation [66]. Operational effectiveness, however, relates to the benefits such as increased revenue and market-share that arise from implementing systems to support various activities [22]. In summary, metrics for both operational efficiency and effectiveness – the gauges to measure process improvement – are essentially quantitative in nature.

Several methodologies and frameworks exist for evaluating process performance from the perspective of process maturity. The foundation of process maturity models lies in the view that organizational capabilities evolve in a stage-by-stage manner along a logically anticipated path and that each of these stages can be clearly defined, managed, measured, and controlled [65]. The underlying hypothesis is that maturity leads to the institutionalization of policies, standards, and organizational structures [31] that ultimately leads to better control, more visibility into the future, and the ability to continually set higher goals [49]. Numerous maturity models have been developed by academics, practitioners, and international consortia [13,25,31,45,57,68]. Many of these models trace their origins to the capability maturity model (CMM) and its subsequent capability maturity mode integration (CMMI) developed by the Software Engineering Institute at Carnegie Mellon University [71]. The CMMI represents a five stage framework to assess an organization’s process maturity level and provide roadmaps for improvement [42].

In addition to maturity models, statistical methods such as sixsigma and statistical process control (SPC) rely on rigorously collecting and analyzing performance data to reduce variability in processes and thereby enhance process efficiency [48]. Value stream mapping (VSM) is a methodology that originates in lean manufacturing concepts and focuses on material and information flow from the beginning to the end of a product/service with the objective of reducing waste along the flow [2]. Activity-based costing (ABC) is an accounting methodology providing data on how costs are consumed by the activities within a process, along with before and after comparisons [4].

Among the methodologies discussed above, maturity models such as CMMI or the process maturity model [31] and statistical methods are not appropriate for the problem at hand. Statistical models are based on data collected during and after process execution and hence cannot be used for future projections. Maturity models, however, are applicable at the organizational level and are reflective in nature. They are suitable for post hoc analysis and charting a roadmap for improvement, as shown in various case studies [58,67]. However, they do not represent a quantifiable measure of specific improvements to a specific process, and hence are not suitable for our purpose. In this respect, both VSM and ABM apply to end-to-end business processes and provide precise measures of process improvement. The focus of VSM is the reduction of waste in material and time along the value chain, while ABM focuses on minimizing the total cost of production. We choose ABM for the purposes of our study. The rationale is elaborated in greater detail in Section 3.1.

## 2.3. IT evaluation decisions

BPI initiatives are nearly always enabled by IT solutions. Therefore, measuring an IT-enabled process improvement is closely related to the question of evaluating and selecting the appropriate IT solution (and vendor). In this respect, the literature on IT investment provides a rich theoretical base.

Large scale IT investment decisions are generally preceded by some ex-ante evaluation methods. Ex-ante evaluation focuses on estimating the future impact of an information technology investment. The evaluation may employ qualitative and/or quantitative techniques depending on the decision perspective used during appraisal [34]. As IT investments have traditionally been addressed within the realm of an organization’s capital budgeting process, quantitative measures stemming from the financial and accounting disciplines play a key role in the decision process [34,72].

Examples of the most commonly used financial measures include: cost benefit analysis (CBA), return on investment (ROI), net present value (NPV), discounted cash flow (DCF), and payback period method (PBP). CBA is a popular method in which the benefits of the project must outweigh the costs for the project to be undertaken. The CBA approach attempts to place a monetary value on each element contributing to the cost or benefit of the project [24]. Subsequent financial evaluation often follows CBA such as NPV, DCF, ROI and the payback method. NPV uses a discount rate to calculate the present value of all cash inflows and outflows from the IT project. One major drawback of NPV is determining an appropriate discount rate to apply to the IT project [54,69]. DCF is a method that attempts to provide the present worth of the future cash flows of a project. Finally, the payback period (PBP) method attempts to determine the earliest period in which the costs of the project are recovered [54].

## 2.4. Process oriented evaluation of IT investments

While the ex-ante financial and accounting measures discussed above are useful in deciding whether to acquire an IT system, many of the measures rely on subjective assessments rather than concrete ones [7]. More important, most of these techniques attempt to directly link an IT investment to firm performance. Subsequent research established that business processes are the intermediate link between IT investment and firm performance [19]. Consequently, a stream of research has been devoted to exploring the link between IT and business processes and its final effect on firm performance [5,19,50]. This stream of research argues that because IT impacts firm performance via intermediate business processes [19], it is appropriate to use process level variables as the dependent variable of interest [50,79]. Subsequently, numerous recent studies have focused on the value contribution of IT at the process level. Subramani [73] investigated the motivation of suppliers to invest in supply chain management (SCM) solutions. The results suggest that such investments are motivated by the desire to create relationship specific investments in business process and domain knowledge. The effect of process characteristics on the value of web-based business-to-business procurements is studied in Subramani et al. [74]. In context of the customer service process, it is shown that IT interacts with firm specific resources and capabilities (shared knowledge and flexible IT infrastructure) in complex ways, resulting in variations in process performance across organizations [64]. Another study by Elbashir et al. explores the causal linkages among IT, business process and organizational performance in the context of business intelligence systems [22]. Their study underscores the role of context in designing performance measures of IT-intensive systems. In investigating the interaction of IT and process redesign, Ramirez et al. [62] establish that IT is a necessary complement to process redesign efforts, and together they result in higher levels of production efficiency and market value.

It must be noted here that the focus of studies adopting process orientation to IT value is to establish a causal chain linking IT, business process performance and organizational performance. As such, most of these studies investigate final outcome variables such as production efficiency [62] or process characteristics [64,74]. Thus, they have enriched our understanding of IT and process performance by establishing (1) the conditions and contexts under which IT yields positive process performance [22,74] and (2) that process performance can ultimately be linked to organizational performance [5,64,73,76]. However, how and to what extent an IT solution impacts the elements within a process to yield higher levels of process performance remains an area worthy of further exploration. In this regard, the locus of value [38] is an important concept representing the primary level of analysis where the business value contribution of IT is measured. Research suggests that both the potential and realized value of IT should be considered [17] and that ‘‘Lower level (e.g., individual or business process level) measures facilitate clear and complete mappings to measures that capture the value of IT at its point of creation (the locus of value)’’ [18].

## 3. An activity-based framework for measuring IT-enabled business process improvement

From the discussion of the extant literature in the previous section, it is apparent that because business processes are the intermediate link between IT and business value (measured through organizational performance), assessing IT-enabled BPI is closely related to a process-oriented evaluation framework for IT investments. In this respect, the IT to business process and business process to firm performance links need to be explored at the ‘micro-level’. To this end, activity based management (ABM) provides a unique solution, as it focuses on the most granular level within a process – the activity. In the following, we briefly discuss ABM and its suitability for the purposes of our study.

## 3.1. Activity based management

ABM evolved from the accounting practice of activity based costing (ABC), a management accounting approach that focuses on ‘‘cost drivers’’. A cost driver is defined as an event associated with an activity resulting in the consumption of the firm’s resources [4]. For example, the resources required (both technology and staffing) to file a case. Activities are further divided into value added (VA) and non-value added (NVA) categories. For example, manually filling a form that is subsequently digitized would be considered NVA, whereas sending status reports to upstream and downstream stakeholders in the case management process is considered VA. ABM takes the knowledge gained from ABC one step further in identifying opportunities for improvement by eliminating or reducing NVA activities [30].

It has been proposed that activity based costing should be used as a guide to corporate strategy [12]. A subsequent study established that ABC is indeed associated with improved financial performance when it is used in conjunction with other strategic initiatives [8]. ABC can also be used as a complement to balanced scorecard performance measures [47,77]. In the area of operations management, Gupta and Galloway [30] illustrate how ABM may be integrated with total quality management (TQM) initiatives and call for ABM systems to be implemented across business functions. ABM is also shown to yield a better appreciation of the actual cost of quality, as it takes a process view of production [78].

That ABM enables useful insights into the most granular level of a process – the activity – makes it an excellent tool for quantitatively assessing the impact of IT on process performance. In other words, ABM provides a lens to explore how a process may be improved by a proposed IT solution. More important, this information can be available prior to implementing an IT solution as opposed to the traditional financial measures such as ROI, which provide information only after system implementation. In summary, ABM, as a methodological framework, nicely meets the criteria set forth at the beginning of Section 2.

## 3.1.1. ABM in IT evaluation research

ABC has been utilized as a method for justifying electronic data interchange (EDI) [32]. After comparing several investment methods, the authors conclude that ABC, in conjunction with simulation, is the most suitable. Guidelines to using ABM to evaluate IT investment are provided in Peacock and Tanniru [59]. They classify IT investments into four categories based on how focused the IT application is and the level of difficulty in measuring the impact of IT. They argue that the ABM framework is most suitable when the IT application is focused on specific activities and the level of complexity in measurement is low. Kim [40] applied an ABM framework to quantify the cost savings resulting from an ERP implementation by allocating the various cost components – hardware and software, consulting and training, maintenance and training – to different business processes.

Our approach, while based on the ABM framework, goes one step further in understanding ‘cost drivers’ as contributing value added and non-value added activities within a business process. This information is used to provide an assessment of the anticipated impact on project performance and identify areas for improvement. The appropriate IT solution is based on the projection of the degree of improvement while considering the costs of the project. In other words, the IT solution must minimize the amount of NVA to attain a reasonable return on investment. Section 4 describes the steps taken toward allocating ‘cost drivers with respect to the Case Management process in our study.

Our primary focus is on the non-value-added (NVA) activities in ABM. NVA activities are defined as those activities that generate a zero or negative return on the investment of resources and usually can be eliminated without impairing a process. To identify the sources of such NVA, we first conducted a comprehensive literature search using keywords such as process performance, business process risk, and business process reengineering success/ failure. ## 3.2.1. Organizational inhibitors

3.2.1.1. Strategy. Management’s lack of vision, oversight, and planning to align business processes with organizational goals give rise to unnecessary obstacles to process execution. In the context of higher education, leadership is cited as the foremost reason for BPI success [10]. A major reason for the high failure rates of business process reengineering was that the efforts had not been connected to corporate goals [3]. Strategic initiatives are cited as critical factors for successful ERP implementation [53]. In general, organizations need to have appropriate management structures that foster accountability [41] and risk management [36,41,75].

3.2.1.2. Resource. A lack of financial resources often results in inadequate systems and tedious manual processes, whereas the absence of appropriate human resources stymies the flow of information among different stakeholders. Large scale process improvement projects require significant capital investments, and the scarcity of such investments may lead to poor IT infrastructure [80]. However, interorganizational processes benefit from appropriate coordination, collaboration, and incentive structures among the human agents [53].

3.2.1.3. Training and education. This refers to the inability of an organization to equip its employees with appropriate instructional resources required in performing their day-to-day jobs [75].

3.2.1.4. Standardized policies and procedures. Business rules and governance frameworks are a means for organizations to achieve consistency in process execution and performance, the absence of which may lead to superfluous work, high degrees of variance in output, and the inability to monitor and track performance [11,29]. It has been noted that many organizations lack an understanding of their business processes, which is crucial for BPM success [23]. Public and private organizations that are well known for their IT performance leadership and management expertise not only implement comprehensive performance evaluation frameworks, they also ensure that such measurements are linked to appropriate levels of the organization [26].

3.2.1.5. Knowledge and information flow. Knowledge and information management is crucial for connecting isolated ‘‘pockets of innovation’’ [28] within an organization. IT led projects often fail to capture the business and human dimensions of processes [53] and require that systems (both organizational and technological) be in place to achieve a synergy among the business, human and IT dimensions of an organization [23,28]. The level of shared knowledge has been positively associated with process performance [37].

3.2.1.6. Culture. Organizational culture facilitates (or inhibits) an organization’s predisposition to change by influencing the organization’s ability to learn, share information, and make decisions [53]. Clemons et al. [11] refer to this as political risk- either because of resistance or a gradual loss of commitment, projects may face eventual collapse. Failure to anticipate resistance and politics, as well as a failure to properly communicate reasons for change, often contribute to inefficient and ineffective workflows [29].

## 3.2.2. Technological inhibitors

3.2.2.1. Technology. The lack of appropriate technological infrastructure and application systems are frequently cited as sources of inefficiencies in business processes. These include, but are not limited to, disparate systems that are unable to communicate with each other [33], insufficient/inappropriate software systems [23], rigid hardware and/or applications [10], limited telecommunications infrastructure, etc.

3.2.2.2. IT resources and capability. According to the resource based perspective, this refers to the ‘‘tacit, socially complex IT resources (such as shared knowledge)’’ [37] that result from the interaction of a firm’s IT assets (such as infrastructure and application portfolio) with non-IT resources (such as managerial capability). A firm’s IT capability refers to competencies built upon explicit IT resources but embedded in organizational know-how in such a way that it is valuable, rare, and costly to imitate [64]. It is these implicit, path dependent capabilities, not generic IT assets, that explain the variances in the relationship between IT and firm performance [64].

## 4.2. Allocate costs

Once the process workflow is mapped, identifiable labor and resource costs for the processes are allocated based on the output measure (e.g., the number of applications processed in a month) [52]. In consultation with process stakeholders, these costs are then categorized into VA vs. NVA.

## 4.3. Perform NVA analysis

In this step, the various NVA activities as identified above are labeled according to the categories in Table 1. Next, for each of the NVA categories within a process/subprocess, the potential reductions in NVA with the BPI project being considered are estimated. This step also requires consultation with process stakeholders. This helps to calculate an aggregate NVA impact score for each of the process/subprocesses.

For example, process X (manage customer information), may be identified as having 3 major NVA categories – technological, resource, and culture, that can be potentially reduced by 80%, 60%, and 20%, respectively, by implementing the IT project. This information is used in calculating a total NVA impact that is used in the subsequent step discussed in the next paragraph.

## 4.4. Evaluate request for proposals (RFP)

Finally, a weighted NVA impact is calculated to score each of the functional line items (as these correspond to the activities in the process workflow) in the RFP. Each vendor responds to the RFP by indicating whether its solution addresses the functionality. If a particular functional requirement was not part of the vendor’s solution, the vendor needs to provide additional cost information to fulfill this requirement. Utilizing the weighted NVA impact, the total amount of NVA reduction from each vendor’s solution and the subsequent potential benefit can be calculated using the following formula: potential benefit = (amount of total NVA  cost of IT project)/cost of IT project. The vendor with the highest potential benefit should be chosen.

## 5. Case study: ERP implementation for an integrated court system

## 5.1. Background

In this section, we demonstrate the applicability of the abovedescribed methodology in estimating potential process improvements through a case study. The study took place in a county in the southern United States that desired to improve its case management process. The county has eleven different court and law enforcement departments of the judicial system that are potentially involved in the case management process: Clerk of courts, District Attorney, Indigent Defense, Juvenile Court, Magistrate Court, Pre-Trial Services, Solicitor’s Office, Superior Court, Sheriff’s Office, Probate Court, and Drug/DUI Court.

The county experienced duplications of effort among the various departments within the court system. Manual or redundant efforts created by the inability to electronically transfer records among the departments caused delays throughout the court process. For example, one particular department retained a pool of employees for the specific purpose of retyping information when another department transferred a case file. Due to these inefficiencies and the need to utilize taxpayer resources more appropriately, the county recognized the need to effectively share information among their various courts and law enforcement branches and determined that all departments needed to integrate their disparate information systems and software applications using one system. The county’s main objective was to reduce inefficiencies in its case management process that begins at the initiation of a case and terminates in its trial.

A committee representing each of the eleven departments was formed to oversee vendor selection and project implementation. All committee representatives of their respective departments agreed that one vendor’s ERP solution would be selected and implemented throughout all departments to avoid any issues of integrating applications between different vendors. This way, the committee determined, the system would be simpler to implement, manage, and maintain while controlling costs.

Prior to using the ABM framework, the committee had narrowed their field of viable vendor candidates to three but failed in their RFP attempt to select one vendor for this project. ## 5.2. Using the ABM-based framework for the case management process

The county decided to use the value-based framework described previously to resolve the conflict among the three groups. This was achieved using a two-stage process consisting of the four steps outlined in the previous section and depicted in Fig. 1. The first stage uses the first three steps from our proposed framework to build a business case for the reduction of NVA activities. The second stage involves step 4, which uses the information from the first three steps and applies it to creating a weighted request for proposals (RFP) form that vendors must complete to compete for the bid.

## 5.2.1. NVA analysis of the case management process

Building the business case and applying the ABM framework involved the following three procedures. First, all activities for the case to trial process and their owners associated with the trial system were identified. For the judicial system, the activities were identified using standardized definitions provided by a consortium comprised by the Conference of State Court Administrators and the National Center for State Courts (NCSC). This consortium was tasked with developing guidelines to help state courts more effectively use their resources, both financial and human, for purposes of obtaining a state-of-the-art computer system (http:/ www.ncsconline.org/d\_tech/standards/). An extensive set of documents defines the steps involved in each type of case (e.g., traffic violation, DUI) as it goes from initiation to trial to closure. These documents were used to identify the processes, sub-processes, and associated activities.

The budget for this department is approximately 25% of the overall judicial system’s annual budget. The Clerk of Courts is considered the largest of all eleven departments. Second, an analysis was conducted to allocate costs to these activities within each subprocess. Employee surveys were used to document time allocation and perceptions regarding which activities are value-added vs. non-value-added. The cost allocation required the use of three methods: Salary trace, Headcount trace, and Direct trace. The Salary trace method allocates costs to an activity using a weighted average of the salaries of the individuals required to perform the activity. Headcount trace allocates the costs of certain items (e.g., stationary) used in an activity according to the number of individuals associated with the activity. Finally, the direct trace method allocates costs that are directly related to an activity (e.g., maintenance payment for a printer used to print pay-checks). Fig. 2 illustrates how the budget line items were allocated to the activities in the Clerk of Courts department.

Third, inhibitors of the activities that are the most costly for the department are identified. During facilitated sessions, participating employees were asked to identify barriers to success in performing their various activities. These barriers to success, or cost drivers, are typically the issues that prevent maximum efficiency and often cause rework and/or NVA activities to occur. Such barriers identified by the employees are grouped into one of the pre-specified categories provided in Table 1. For example, a lack of technology was a consistent NVA inhibitor for all of the eleven criminal justice departments. Seven of the departments shared a common technology platform, although many of the modules were not integrated across those departments. The other four departments used different software packages that did not communicate or integrate with those of any of the other departments. As a result, case files created in one department with one application and then transferred to another department using a different application caused the second department to recreate the file from scratch. In addition, information sharing and inter-departmental queries were impossible, causing excessive manual research and information retrieval. Fig. 3 provides a further breakdown of the activity costs identified in the previous step to identify the NVA costs associated with the activities within a department. The relative proportion of NVA vs. VA within that department is also shown in the figure.

As the primary objective is to reduce the cost of the non-valueadded activities, discussions were held with department leaders and employees, both from the technology and business side, regarding the extent to which NVA activities could be realistically reduced (e.g., 60–70%) through the implementation of an integrated ERP system. These discussions helped identify key functional requirements for the new system. These functional requirements were included to create a weighted RFP detailed in the next section.

5.2.2. Utilizing NVA analysis in the RFP and subsequent ERP vendor selection

The RFP lists the system functionalities required for each of the activities within a process. As illustrated in Fig. 4 (a partial view of the activities within the Clerk of Courts department), a five point Likert scale is used to identify the potential impact of the functional requirement on reducing the NVA activities, with 1 representing ‘‘little to no impact’’ and 5 representing ‘‘very high impact’’.

Each vendor responds to the RFP by indicating whether its solution addresses the functionality. If a particular functional requirement was not part of the vendor’s solution, the vendor needed to provide additional cost information to fulfill this requirement. As the NVA for each activity has already been calculated (refer to Fig. 3), we can now utilize the weighted NVA impact (Fig. 4) to compute the total amount of NVA reduction from each vendor’s solution. This is captured in the column ‘Annual NVA Addressed’ in Table 3.

The significance of this step is that unlike most RFPs, where functional line items are treated equally and vendors are scored on the number of requirements met, the functional line items in this RFP are weighted according to the potential impact of reduced NVA. As is evident from Fig. 4, not all requirements impact the buyer equally. In other words, requirements that more directly impact NVA reduction within the most pervasive and expensive activities outweigh requirements that have little or no impact on efficiency. By introducing our framework and its subsequent NVA calculations into a process, line items are weighted in proportion to their cost. As a result, the vendor that presents the most impressive list of features may not always have the best impact on the bottom line.

Finally, the ROI is calculated using the amount of NVA reduced over a seven-year time period in conjunction with the cost of implementing a new system. The total cost (column 4, Table 3) includes the software purchase (column 2, Table 3) plus the cost of implementation (Column 3, Table 3). The ROI represents the benefit (amount of NVA addressed over 7 years  total cost) divided by total cost. Based on the quantitative application of the ABM framework for evaluating the four vendors, a clear ‘winner’ emerged and the committee was able to unanimously agree on one vendor solution –Vendor D, which had the highest estimated ROI (see Table 3).

## 6. Concluding remarks and implications

The purpose of our work is to propose an ex-ante objective evaluation framework for IT-enabled business process improvement initiatives and to demonstrate how such a value-based framework can be further utilized in selecting an IT solution. This is performed via a rigorous analysis of the business process, the systematic identification of non-value-added activities within the (sub) processes, and comparing candidate IT solutions according to their ability to mitigate NVA activities in a real-life setting. The final outcome of the case management project represents the proof of concept for utilizing ABM and NVA analysis in providing a quantitative pre-assessment of IT-enabled BPI initiatives and the creation of a weighted RFP.

From our NVA analysis, two key points emerge that have implications for both research and practice. The first centers on the relationship between process performance and NVA categories and its implications for management control. The second relates to the value of NVA analysis in vendor selection and subsequent IT implementation success. These points are discussed in greater detail below.

## 6.1. Relationship between process performance and NVA inhibitors

The NVA categories developed and presented in Section 3.2 provide a starting point for understanding the impediments to process performance. We provide a comprehensive list that may be utilized for identifying inefficiencies and potential for improvement in similar BPI projects. The use of the NVA categories in different contexts would be helpful to determine empirically how and to what extent these inhibitors impede process performance. More important, it is possible that individual inhibitors impact different process performances measures such as time, cost, quality, and customer satisfaction to varying degrees. For example, a lack of training may have a higher impact on customer satisfaction, whereas technological silos impact quality the most. In other words, systematic relationship patterns between inhibitors and key performance indicators (KPIs) may exist. Knowledge of such relationships enables targeting specific process areas for improvement and is valuable for management control purposes. Finally, another important topic to address is the need to take this evaluation method to the next level: do the ex-ante predictions arrived at in this manner perform better at predicting actual outcomes than alternative methods?<sup>3</sup> We acknowledge that contrasting such outcomes may not be easy to implement in practice, especially because there may be a number of methods to contrast and many confounding factors. Nevertheless, we identify this as a potential opportunity to be explored in the future.

6.2. Impact of NVA analysis on IT implementation success and vendor selection

Our study also underscores the potential value of using the ABM-based framework during a request for proposals (RFP). In general, the list of requirements found in the standard contract language in RFPs stipulates that all functional line items be treated equally. However, as we illustrate in this paper through the use of the ABM framework, not all requirements are/should be created equal. Requirements that more directly influence large pools of NVA within the most pervasive and expensive activities create a higher functional priority than those that have little or no impact on the process. For example, NVA calculations for a procurement cycle RFP can weight the line items in proportion to the potential impact of cost savings in the process. As a result, the buyer can quantitatively evaluate the vendor by the value contributed rather than an evaluation based on the quality of a demonstration or list of features. While common wisdom in software development/ procurement emphasizes that an accurate analysis of requirements is crucial for successful implementation [e.g., [39,51]], our study illustrates that incorporating value expectations (e.g., reductions in NVA) in the contract language of the RFP can improve communication and clarify responsibilities between the contracting parties.

A subsequent aspect of system implementation is vendor selection, as the RFPs are intended to select both the vendor and the solution. There are several ‘‘soft’’ criteria that often guide the vendor selection process: vendor reputation, past relationships, and organizational politics, to name a few. While the NVA activity analysis provides an objective evaluation of the IT system (and by association the vendor), the trade-off between the subjective and objective measures is not always straightforward. Despite the recognition that IT investments are best evaluated at the business processes level, ‘‘process level analysis is still in its infancy, and there is a need for a systematic basis for identifying IT effects’’ [14] (p. 66). As the value-based framework proposed in this paper is applied closest to the primary ‘‘locus of value’’ (i.e., the business process level [18]), it can be used as a complement to traditional IT investment evaluation methods based on financial measures. Seen in a different way, the ABM-based IT evaluation framework may also be used as a lens for assessing the business value of BPI. In this respect, further (and preferably empirical) investigation is required to compare the traditional methods of value measurement with our proposed approach.

At this point, challenges in implementing the proposed approach should also be acknowledged. The literature on business process change/re-engineering [29] has cited the human factor as an important variable, and our experience confirms the signifi cance of this variable. Particularly. two points are worth mentioning. First, there was tremendous resistance from those actors whose tasks were labeled NVA activity (e.g., the manual reentry of case records). Accordingly, such BPI endeavors must be accompanied by a management plan to appropriately communicate the potential benefits and possibly also redefine employee roles and responsibilities. The second point concerns using the weighted RFP in comparison to the traditional method of exclusively using the feature sets in selecting the solution. As this was a step away from the conventional way of doing business, initially (somewhat) political groups had formed among the decision makers that required subsequent intervention by management.

In conclusion, we establish ABM as a standardized methodology that enables a quantitative assessment of how different activities may impact process performance and subsequently measure the potential for process improvement. The feasibility of this approach is explored via an actual BPI project evaluation of an integrated ERP solution for the various departments within a county’s court system. The study illustrates how a value-based framework can be utilized in process improvement initiatives by reducing the impact of specific non-value-added activities on performance measures. The NVA analysis also helps in weighting functional the line items required for evaluating vendor proposals. As process monitoring and evaluation is fundamental to management control and accounting, our work contributes to the stream of research that explores the links among IT, process improvement, and firm performance.
