# ROLEX: A NOVEL METHOD FOR INTERPRETABLE MACHINE LEARNING USING ROBUST LOCAL EXPLANATIONS<sup>1</sup>

Buomsoo (Raymond) Kim Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, Iowa State University Ames, IA, U.S.A. {rkim0927@iastate.edu}

Karthik Srinivasan School of Business, University of Kansas Lawrence, KS, U.S.A. {karthiks@ku.edu}

Recent developments in big data technologies are revolutionizing the field of healthcare predictive analytics (HPA), enabling researchers to explore challenging problems using complex prediction models. Nevertheless, healthcare practitioners are reluctant to adopt those models as they are less transparent and accountable due to their black-box structure. We believe that instance-level, or local, explanations enhance patient safety and foster trust by enabling patient-level interpretations and medical knowledge discovery. Therefore, we propose the RObust Local EXplanations (ROLEX) method to develop robust, instance-level explanations for HPA models in this study. ROLEX adapts state-of-the-art methods and ameliorates their shortcomings in explaining individual-level predictions made by black-box machine learning models. Our analysis with a large real-world dataset related to a prevalent medical condition called fragility fracture and two publicly available healthcare datasets reveals that ROLEX outperforms widely accepted benchmark methods in terms of local faithfulness of explanations. In addition, ROLEX is more robust since it does not rely on extensive hyperparameter tuning or heuristic algorithms. Explanations generated by ROLEX, along with the prototype user interface presented in this study, have the potential to promote personalized care and precision medicine by providing patient-level interpretations and novel insights. We discuss the theoretical implications of our study in healthcare, big data, and design science.

Keywords: Healthcare predictive analytics, explainable artificial intelligence, machine learning interpretability, healthcare information systems

## Introduction

With recent developments in big data technologies and the prevalence of large-scale datasets, the healthcare predictive analytics (HPA) field is witnessing a dramatic surge of interest in the information systems (IS) discipline. Existing IS studies focusing on HPA have proposed novel methods to optimally predict health outcomes such as readmission of patients (Bardhan et al., 2015) and abnormal inhaler usage (Son et al., 2020). Nevertheless, many HPA systems are not being put into practice due to a lack of trust (Ahmad et al., 2018; Ribeiro et al., 2016). One of the most important steps toward garnering trust in HPA systems is providing reliable explanations for its predictions. Such explanations play a critical role in supporting clinical decision-making, facilitating user engagement, and enhancing patient safety. Furthermore, it is highly risky to blindly accept and apply the results derived from black-box models. It could lead to undesirable consequences or life-threatening outcomes (Ahmad et al., 2018).

In this study, we propose a novel method to enhance the transparency and accountability of the HPA model by providing human-interpretable explanations of its predictions. Our proposed method, called RObust Local EXplanations (ROLEX), can be used to generate robust explanations at an instance level for any classification model. In other words, it can be used to generate model-agnostic, local explanations. Model agnosticity refers to the capability of explaining any supervised prediction model (Ribeiro et al., 2016). Existing methods in HPA attempt to explain specific prediction models such as neural networks or tree-based ensembles (e.g., Che et al. 2016; Choi et al. 2016). We claim that such a modeldependent approach to developing explanations is not aligned with recent trends in HPA, in which a wide variety of datasets and diverse types of prediction models are deployed. Thus, model-agnostic explanation methods enhance the applicability of HPA to a wide range of problems. Secondly, in HPA, it is common to encounter complex, nonlinear relationships between variables with heterogenous effects across observations. It is therefore insufficient to explain the model by providing only global, dataset-level explanations such as feature importance estimates. Local explanations refer to instance-level explanations that can be understood by humans (Ribeiro et al., 2016). In the context of HPA, local explanations can help to facilitate personalized care and advance precision medicine by providing patient-level interpretations. Our method is designed to produce more locally faithful explanations than existing local explanation methods through improved sampling and a novel optimization scheme. Finally, we develop a prototype user interface for ROLEX that comprises diverse modes of humaninterpretable, patient-level explanations.

We evaluate the proposed method using multiple HPA datasets—a cross-sectional, large-scale dataset from a cohort study and two public healthcare datasets. ROLEX consistently outperforms state-of-the-art explanation methods (Jia et al. 2019; Laugel et al. 2018b; Ribeiro et al. 2016) in terms of local faithfulness, which is defined as the ability to replicate the black-box prediction model in the vicinity of the instance of interest measured by a quantitative metric (Laugel et al. 2018b). Thus, ROLEX has the potential to enhance the transparency and accountability of various HPA systems, playing a crucial role in addressing “grand challenges and big questions” in HPA and IS research in general (Ram & Goes, 2021). We focus on a specific application of HPA, i.e., fragility fracture prediction, to explore the role of explanations in building trustworthy and accountable HPA systems in real-world problem-solving. Fragility fractures are defined as “fractures that result from mechanical forces that would not ordinarily result in fracture.” (National Clinical Guideline Centre, 2012). Sedentary lifestyles and increasing life expectancies in developed countries are driving the compelling need to develop HPA systems for the early diagnosis and prevention of fragility fractures. Fragility fracture prediction is representative of HPA problems that require local explanations since patients show highly heterogeneous patterns in terms of disease onset and contributing factors to the disease. We demonstrate the problem-solving capability of the proposed method in the fragility fracture prediction context, developing and interpreting robust local explanations in collaboration with healthcare experts. A user study with experienced healthcare experts shows that the prototype of our method has the potential to improve clinical decision-making and patient care and resolve information asymmetry, simultaneously benefiting diverse stakeholders.

## We summarize our key contributions as follows:

(1) We present the eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) framework to guide researchers and practitioners in designing interpretable systems for real-world HPA problems.

(2) We propose a novel method to explain individual predictions of any black-box machine learning model. Our method improves over state-of-the-art local model-agnostic explanation methods in terms of local faithfulness and interpretability of the local explanations.

(3) We also propose a new evaluation framework for local explanation models that not only accounts for the edge cases concerning local decision boundary (DB) but also provides a heuristic for choosing when using local explanations is suitable or not for each instance. The proposed Local DB-Aware (LDA) framework is applicable to any local explanation method, thereby providing general guidelines for instance-level interpretations.

(4) We demonstrate a prototype user interface based on our proposed XAI framework and design principles in interactive machine learning. A user study with healthcare experts reveals that the interface can fulfill various desiderata of XAI systems.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the following section, we discuss related work from relevant research streams. Then, we ground and motivate our study by providing a practical framework for XAI and reviewing related methods. Next, we present our proposed method, i.e., the ROLEX method, and its application to HPA. Finally, we discuss key findings and provide directions for future work.

## Related Work

HPA is a branch of data analytics that mainly focuses on forecasting the likelihood of a future health outcome based on patterns in historical data. With the increasing availability of large-scale datasets in the medical and healthcare fields—e.g., data from electronic health records (EHR), cohort studies, and healthcare apps—there has been a sheer growth of interest in HPA (Bardhan et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2020). Previous HPA studies in the IS discipline have focused on proposing novel methods to accurately predict diverse health outcomes, with an emphasis on chronic care. For example, Lin et al. (2017) proposed a Bayesian multitask learning framework for risk profiling in chronic care, and Meyer et. al., (2014) integrated machine learning and control theory to improve the prediction performance of chronic healthcare outcomes. More recent innovations in HPA include data-driven frameworks for risk profiling and management among asthma patients (Son et al., 2020; Zhang & Ram, 2020), longitudinal analysis of readmitted patients (Ben-Assuli & Padman, 2020), cost reduction in clinical decision-making (Fang et al., 2021), and social media analytics (X. Liu et al., 2020).

Given the widespread use of big data, healthcare is rife with applications warranting explanations and interpretations of automated model-based predictions (Ahmad et al., 2018; Choi et al., 2016). Model transparency is especially critical currently in healthcare since multidisciplinary teams consisting of professionals such as surgeons, hospitalists, and medical coders frequently collaborate to glean insights derived from HPA systems to make clinical decisions (Fichman et al., 2011). Moreover, blindly accepting the results from a black-box model may lead to grave consequences, possibly even life-threatening ones (Ahmad et al., 2018; Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2018). Finally, recent work in humanmachine connection shows that human experts and AI systems can collaborate to enhance the overall decision-making process (Jussupow et al., 2021; van den Broek et al., 2021). To facilitate such a collaboration process, AI systems should be easily understandable by human experts. Thus, explaining complex HPA systems can help enhance the synergies between AI and healthcare experts.

Recent studies have begun focusing on explaining complex prediction models in healthcare. For instance, Choi et al. (2016) utilized attention networks, which were initially used to align words in neural translation (Bahdanau et al., 2015), while Che et al. (2016) proposed a novel boosting-based framework for interpretable deep learning. However, their frameworks are focused on explaining only one type of model, i.e., deep learning-based models, and one type of data, i.e., EHR data. We argue that this approach is not consistent with recent trends, in which a wide variety of datasets and algorithms are utilized to advance precision medicine and personalized care. Therefore, there is a need for explainable prediction systems that can be generalized to different HPA problems and contexts. In this study, we present a novel method to explain machine learningbased HPA models. Our method is a local, model-agnostic explanation method, which can provide instance-level explanations for any supervised machine learning model. It has the potential to enhance patient care and advance precision medicine, going beyond analyzing the average treatment effects with conventional methods (Bardhan et al., 2020). In the following section, we review the core concepts of XAI and present an XAI framework for practical applications, which paves the way for developing our proposed ROLEX method.

## Theory and Motivation

## XAI Framework for Practical Applications

We are currently witnessing the golden age of AI applications (Gunning, 2017) in which cutting-edge computational technologies are playing an integral role in solving complicated real-world problems. However, state-of-the-art AI systems such as deep neural networks often have complex and opaque model structures, making it difficult for humans to interpret and explain their outputs (Gunning, 2017; Lipton, 2016). Consequently, XAI and related concepts such as machine learning interpretability are amassing significant attention among practitioners and researchers (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2018; Miller, 2019). Here, we emphasize three fundamental desiderata that should be thoroughly considered when building XAI systems.

First and foremost, the context for implementing the system should be fully understood and the design of the explanation mechanism should be meticulously aligned with it. Here, context refers to the business needs and requirements relevant to the practical utility of the system. In most real-world scenarios, the problem space is infinitely large such that there are innumerable factors determining the context (Simon, 1996). Therefore, only relevant factors that can afford insights into the problem of interest for a particular audience should be attended to (Murdoch et al., 2019).

Secondly, the ethics of implementing and deploying AI systems should be recognized throughout the whole process. The second principle of the Menlo Report (Kenneally & Dittrich, 2012), an ethical framework for information and communication technologies research, is beneficence. This principle refers to minimizing probable risks when conducting information and communication technology research. Notwithstanding the super-human capabilities of modern AI systems, these systems are often deemed to be incomplete, often exacerbated by their opaque architecture and limited scope for human interpretability. Therefore, the explanation mechanism should be designed to sufficiently communicate the potential shortcomings and incompleteness of the system in order to ensure safety and prevent unexpected mishaps.

Finally, the introduction of policies and regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016) has amplified the need for ensuring the human interpretability of prediction models. Furthermore, researchers should actively and proactively take up the challenge of raising current standards of transparency and accountability. It is often claimed that despite recent technological advances in the field of XAI, we do not have widely accepted, clear-cut guidelines for deploying them (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2018; Lipton, 2016). Therefore, more open discussions and theoretical developments are imperative for the practical applications of XAI, and interdisciplinary work with other fields such as law, public policy, organization science, and sociology will likely be fruitful.

This framework can be generalized beyond the HPA context, guiding researchers and practitioners in building reliable XAI systems for any practical application. Based on the discussion on XAI, we propose a novel method that takes into consideration all three features, thereby maximizing the problem-solving capability of the method.

## A Taxonomy of Existing XAI Methods

We use two distinct criteria that are widely accepted in the field, i.e., the scope of explanations and model specificity (Rai, 2020), to classify extant explanation methods. One criterion for classifying explanation methods is the scope of explanations. Global explanations attempt to explain patterns on a dataset level; this can be achieved by examining the model and the dataset itself. Coefficient estimates of dependent variables for a regression model and conditional rules developed from a single decision tree (DT) are classic examples of global explanations. In contrast, local explanations focus on a part of the dataset—i.e., certain data instances of interest. Interactions among different features and relationships between input and outcome variables are likely to be highly complex and nonlinear in many cases. Nonetheless, the relationship between the outcome(s) and inputs may be explained using a different linear function for each data record, or through instance-level explanations (Rai, 2020; Ribeiro et al., 2016). A promising method to design a complex artifact is to find “viable ways of decomposing it into semi-independent components corresponding to its many functional parts” (Simon, 1996). In a similar vein, local explanations for intelligent systems can be regarded as reverse designing a complex artifact to understand the phenomenon of interest more deeply.

The second criterion utilized to categorize explanation methods is model specificity. Explanation methods can either be considered model-specific or model-agnostic. Modelspecific methods apply to only certain types of models. In many cases, model-specific methods pertain to one of the inherent properties of the model. For instance, the attention mechanism (Bahdanau et al., 2015) would be a specific method only applicable to neural network models. In contrast, model-agnostic methods are flexible enough to be applied to different types of supervised learning models.

Combining the two criteria results in four types of explanation methods (Figure 1). First and most global, model-specific methods take advantage of inherently interpretable models such as logistic regression. Recently, models combining the predictive power of deep neural networks and interpretable models have been proposed (e.g., Yang et al. 2018). Also, attempts to visualize features of deep neural networks on a global level would fall into this category. The attention mechanism for neural networks is one of the most widely used examples of local, modelspecific methods. Attentional methods align input and output sequences and provide corresponding weights between the two, enabling explanations for output predictions (Bahdanau et al., 2015). For global, model-agnostic explanations, greedy function approximation algorithms are often used to estimate the partial function expressing the global relationship between variables (Friedman, 2001). Local surrogate methods are widely used for local, model-agnostic explanations and can be utilized within an interpretable model to approximate how the prediction model behaves on a local level. Methods such as perturbation sampling (Ribeiro et al., 2016) and feature attribution (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) have been proposed to approximate the global prediction model’s behavior at an instance level.

## Local and Model-Agnostic Explanations

We focus on local, model-agnostic methods in this study to develop an explanation method that is suitable for general HPA systems. Although researchers have argued that interpretable models can perform as well as their black-box counterparts, advocating the usage of inherently interpretable models instead of explaining complex black-box models after training (Rudin, 2019), we believe this is not fully aligned with recent trends in HPA. As real-world data in healthcare are becoming increasingly diverse, it is difficult to design and evaluate interpretable models for all data. Most machine learning algorithms operate based on the principle of inductive bias, making certain assumptions specific to the data (Mitchell, 1980). Algorithms to learn patterns from sequential data, e.g., recursive neural networks, assume temporal dependence between inputs, and convolutional neural networks are based on the intuition that objects in image-like data are localized in a few pixels. In the healthcare context, for instance, models such as hidden Markov models and recurrent neural networks can be more effective for clinical text data than other models. Thus, to maximize the applicability to any HPA problem promptly, a model-agnostic method would be a preferable choice for generating explanations.

A primary reason for focusing on local explanations stems from the complexity of problems in healthcare, as described previously. In such cases, high explanatory power on a global scale is insufficient to make practical inferences with actionable implications. For example, in a typical healthcare dataset, a physician or a patient is likely to be more interested in why the patient may be at a higher risk of acquiring a disease, rather than why patients in a certain age group are at a high risk of that disease. Figure 2 demonstrates how we can exploit local explanations to understand and solve the problem of interpretability in a “decomposable” manner. In this illustrative example, a machine learning model was trained to predict future fragility fracture risks among patients in a cohort study. For simplicity, only two standardized input variables from our dataset were used to fit the model. A detailed description of the dataset and the model is provided in the analysis section. The variable in the horizontal axis of Figure 2 indicates the BMD level of the lumbar spine, and one in the vertical axis indicates the overall BMD level of the entire body. Panel 1 in Figure 2 is a visualization of all sampled instances—marked with a blue “X” if an instance is classified by the model as negative, i.e., with low fracture risks, and marked with a red dot if it is classified as positive, i.e., with high fracture risks. At first glance, it appears that the interaction between the two variables is highly nonlinear and messy, making it difficult to interpret on a global scale. Panel 2 is a subspace of the feature space shown in Panel 1 and confines the x variable to the value ranges of [- 1.1, -0.92], and the y variable to the value ranges of [-0.2, -0.02]. Now, a clearer pattern recognizable by humans begins to emerge; most high-risk instances have x-values lying in the range of [-0.975, -0.950]. Lastly, from Panel 3, which is a subspace of Panel 2, we can derive some inductive rules. For instance, a green straight line in Panel 3 is a DB that separates high-risk and low-risk patients.

Specifically, we focus on perturbation sampling methods (Jia et al. 2019; Laugel et al. 2018b; Ribeiro et al. 2016) among many local, model-agnostic explanation techniques. In this family of methods, samples are generated around an instance to train local explanation models that can be directly interpreted by humans (e.g., linear regression). Perturbation sampling in the neighborhood of a data instance of interest is used to maximize the local faithfulness of local explanation models, defined as the capacity to replicate how the prediction model behaves in the vicinity of the instance (Ribeiro et al., 2016). The local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) method (Ribeiro et al., 2016) is a widely accepted local, model-agnostic method using perturbation sampling. For each data instance being explained, LIME generates synthetic samples in the entire input feature space and assigns weights to them in proportion to the distance from the data instance of interest. Finally, a local explanation model—i.e., a penalized regression model—is trained with weighted local samples.

However, this method has several shortcomings, including the lack of objective evaluative criteria and diluted local faithfulness of explanations. Accordingly, Laugel et al. (2018b) proposed a new quantitative evaluation criterion for local explanations and improvements in the sampling scheme for training instances to enhance local faithfulness with their local surrogate (LS) framework. LS starts by finding a data point that is close to the DB near the instance being explained, i.e., the “enemy” point. Then, it uniformly samples data points in the neighborhood of the data instance, which is defined as an ??-ball around the enemy point. The local embedding aided perturbation (LEAP) (Jia et al., 2019) locally samples training data in the submanifold of the neighborhood to improve the quality of data points. It is argued that the improved sampling procedures with an emphasis on locality enhance the local faithfulness of the ensuing explanations by feeding the explanation model with high-quality training samples.

Nonetheless, these approaches also have limitations. First, they do not consider the fact that instances in the same dataset can drastically differ in terms of their relationship to local DB. Moreover, explanations for instances that are too far from DB can reveal high fidelity scores even though they are not locally faithful. Secondly, existing methods assume that DB generated by a black-box model tends to be linear in the local scale, i.e., in the vicinity of the instance. However, our close examination of real-world data reveals that DB can be highly nonlinear in some cases, leading to several potential issues. First, the training samples can be highly imbalanced. In turn, highly imbalanced samples can lead to biases in the explanation model. Furthermore, using only a linear explanation model could lead to unfaithful interpretations in some cases. Finally, most existing methods lack a humaninterpretable user interface, which can be highly critical in real-world application scenarios. The proposed ROLEX method, which is described in detail in the following section, attempts to address these limitations.

## Proposed Method

Before explaining our proposed method, we briefly describe general notations and common procedures of local, modelagnostic explanation methods. First, a black-box prediction model $( { \hat { f } } { : } X \to Y )$ , e.g., deep neural networks, is trained in advance. Then, an interpretable local model $( s _ { x _ { i } } ) , \mathbf { e . g . }$ , logistic regression, is trained to explain the black-box model. Note that a desirable property of the local model is local faithfulness to the data instance being explained (?? ), instead of global faithfulness to the black-box model.

In training the local model, perturbation sampling is one of the most widely used techniques (Jia et al. 2019; Laugel et al. 2018b; Ribeiro et al. 2016). Perturbation sampling methods first generate synthetic samples around the instance $x _ { i } ( \{ x _ { i } ^ { ( i ) } \in R ^ { d } , j = 1 , 2 , \ldots , N \} )$ ). Then, a local explanation model $( s _ { x _ { i } } )$ that can be directly interpreted by humans, e.g., linear models and decision rules, are trained using the samples $( x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } ^ { , } \mathrm { s } )$ as input features and predicted labels by the black-box model $( \hat { f } ( x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } ) ^ { \ast } \mathrm { s } )$ as a target. Figure 3 shows a synthetic example demonstrating the process of generating local, model-agnostic explanations for a binary classifier ${ \breve { \hat { f } } } .$ In the figure, the blue region is where instances are negatively classified by ${ \hat { f } } ,$ whereas the orange region contains instances that are positively classified; one can see that DB is highly nonlinear on a global scale. The local explanation models $s _ { x _ { 1 } }$ and $s _ { x _ { 2 } }$ are locally faithful to the black-box model $\hat { f }$ around $x _ { 1 }$ and $x _ { 2 } ,$ , respectively. Furthermore, they are both simple models that can be easily interpreted.

In the context of HPA, each data instance corresponds to a patient with quantifiable clinical or nonclinical characteristics that are likely to be relevant in predicting future healthcare outcomes, e.g., fragility fracture risks and the likelihood of developing breast cancer. After predicting the fragility risk of patients with ${ \hat { f } } ,$ we aim to generate independent explanations for the classifier’s decision for patient-level prediction, e.g., high/low risks of fragility fracture. Finally, it should be emphasized that the local explanation models $( s _ { x _ { i } } \in S _ { \mathcal { X } } )$ are trained to primarily explain the black-box classifier, not to describe the phenomenon itself. Hence, regardless of the relationship between the black-box classifier and the phenomenon, the explanation model attempts to mimic the classifier. In some cases, the classifier may well approximate the phenomenon of interest—in such cases, the local explanation models can be used to represent both the classifier and the phenomenon. However, the classifier may be inefficient in mimicking the complex relationships between variables, i.e., an underfitted prediction model. For instance, the classifier may be inappropriately trained due to an insufficient number of training samples or a highly skewed training dataset. Nonetheless, the explanation model can still be valuable in such scenarios. For example, it can be used to point out where and why the classifier is underperforming. Such insights can be exploited to improve the classifier in the future by providing potential remedies for underfitting.

Hence, we propose the ROLEX method as an improvement over existing local, model-agnostic explanation methods (Jia et al. 2019; Laugel et al. 2018b; Ribeiro et al. 2016). The ROLEX method has three phases: (1) synthetic data generation, (2) local model fitting, and (3) patient-level explanations. We describe each procedure in detail.

## Synthetic Data Generation

The objective of this step is to generate synthetic data points $( \{ x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } , j = 1 , 2 , \dots , N _ { t r a i n } \} )$ that reflect DB close to $x _ { i }$ such that the explanation model $( s _ { x _ { i } } )$ is locally faithful. To this end, we employ LIME samples around the data instance $x _ { i }$ using a Gaussian perturbation sampling scheme (Ribeiro et al., 2016):

Nevertheless, the improved sampling schemes of LS and LEAP are not without drawbacks. First, although the growing spheres algorithm for LS is guaranteed to converge, the enemy point $( x _ { b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) } )$ discovered by the algorithm is not necessarily an optimal one that can maximize local faithfulness, $\mathrm { e . g . , } x _ { b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) }$ in Panel A of Figure 4. Moreover, a different enemy point $( x _ { b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) } )$ can be found for the same $x _ { i }$ in every iteration as it is based on stochastic sampling. Similarly, there is no guarantee that the local subspace learned by PCA fully reflects the local DB around $x _ { i } ,$ , ensuring high fidelity. Such issues are exacerbated when the training data are highly dimensional and complex and $\hat { f }$ is highly nonlinear, which is commonly observed in modern healthcare and medical datasets. Secondly, LS and LEAP apply the same sampling radius (??) to all data points being explained. This can be problematic in cases where the distance to the closest DB differs greatly across different $x _ { i } \mathrm { ^ { \prime } s } ,$ , which is also common in many real-world datasets. For instance, ?? in Panel B of Figure 4 is set to a small value so that the samples do not appropriately reflect the local DB.

To tackle the aforementioned issues, we take a distinct approach of directly optimizing the key parameters for the uniform sampling scheme. Specifically, we find $x _ { c e n t e r } ^ { ( i ) }$ and $\epsilon _ { i }$ while maximizing the local fidelity of $s _ { x _ { i } }$ trained with data points obtained with the sampling scheme defined as:

$$
P (x _ {i}, \eta) = x _ {c e n t e r} ^ {(i)} + \eta , \eta \sim U n i f (\epsilon_ {i})\tag{4}
$$

Here, $x _ { c e n t e r } ^ { ( i ) }$ is a data point in the feature space between $x _ { i }$ and the closest enemy point in the training set $( x _ { t r a i n \_ b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) } ) .$ , i.e., the closest training instance with the opposite predicted class:

$$
x _ {c e n t e r} ^ {(i)} = \beta_ {i} * x _ {t r a i n \_ b o r d e r} ^ {(i)} + (1 - \beta_ {i}) * x _ {i}, 0 <   \beta_ {i} <   1\tag{5}
$$

Note that unlike $x _ { b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) } , x _ { t r a i n \_ b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) }$ is a stationary point in the subset of the training data $( \{ x _ { j } | f ( x _ { j } ) \neq f ( x _ { i } ) \} )$ , which does not require a heuristic algorithm (e.g., growing spheres) to find. We can simply compute the distance between $x _ { i }$ and $x _ { j } \mathrm { ' s }$ (in $\{ x _ { j } | f ( x _ { j } ) \neq f ( x _ { i } ) \} ,$ ) and select one having the shortest distance to $x _ { i }$ . Thus, the final optimization objective is as follows:

$$
\underset {\beta_ {i}, \epsilon_ {i}} {\operatorname{argmax}} \mathcal {L} \left(s _ {x _ {i}} \Big | \left\{x _ {j} ^ {(i)}, j = 1, 2, \dots , N _ {t r a i n} \right\}\right),\tag{6}
$$

where $\mathcal { L } \left( s _ { x _ { i } } \middle | \left\{ x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } , j \ = \ 1 , 2 , \ldots , N _ { t r a i n } \right\} \right)$ is the local fidelity of the explanation model $( s _ { x _ { i } } )$ trained with data points $\left\{ x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } , j = 1 , 2 , \dots , N _ { t r a i n } \right\}$ generated with the sampling scheme in Equation 4.

Most off-the-shelf optimization schemes, e.g., Bayesian optimization (Snoek et al., 2012), can be used for solving Equation 6. By using a fixed enemy point in the training set $( x _ { t r a i n \_ b o r d e r } ^ { ( i ) } )$ instead of applying a stochastic update in every iteration as in LS, we obtain more stable explanations across different iterations, enhancing the robustness of explanations.

Furthermore, by directly optimizing the objective function rather than relying on heuristics, we can further improve local faithfulness. That is, several cases of suboptimal parameter tuning, which can lead to impaired quality of training samples (e.g., those demonstrated in Figure 4), can be circumvented by direct optimization. While developing local explanation models, existing methods often assume the local linearity of the DB. However, local DB can be nonlinear in real data (e.g., DB around $x _ { 3 }$ in Figure 3). This can lead to several issues in training such as an imbalance in the sampled data points and suboptimal explanation models which assume linearity. First and foremost, since decision boundaries tend to be highly nonlinear as mentioned, uniformly sampling in the vicinity of $x _ { i }$ can lead to imbalanced training data, i.e., having much more data points with one specific predicted label than others. This can be especially critical for instances far away from a DB. Hence, we use the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) (Chawla et al., 2002) to further improve the quality of synthetic data points. After uniformly sampling around $x _ { c e n t e r } ^ { ( i ) } .$ , we apply SMOTE to the minority class, reducing the likelihood of $s _ { x _ { i } }$ trained in a biased manner towards the majority class.

## Local Model Fitting

Using the synthetic data points and their predicted labels by the pre-trained black-box classifier, a local explanation model is trained. As described earlier, the synthetic data points $( \{ x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } , j = 1 , 2 , \ldots , N \} )$ ) are used as input features for an interpretable local model $( s _ { x _ { i } } )$ , with their predicted labels $( \{ \hat { f } ( x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } ) , j = 1 , 2 , \dots , N \} )$ as a target. Many existing methods use linear models, e.g., penalized linear regression, as a local model, assuming that local DB is approximately linear. However, as mentioned, cases may exist cases where local DB is nonlinear. In such cases, we argue that nonlinear interpretable algorithms such as DT and generalized additive models (GAM) could be effectively used. In determining the nonlinearity of local samples, and ultimately which algorithm could be used for local explanations, we propose the linear separability (LinSep) score:

$$
L i n S e p (x _ {i}) = b A c c _ {x _ {j} ^ {(i)} \in V _ {x _ {i}}} \Big (\hat {f} \left(x _ {j} ^ {(i)}\right), p _ {x _ {i}} (x _ {j} ^ {(i)}) \Big),\tag{7}
$$

where ???????? is balanced accuracy and $V _ { x _ { i } }$ is a neighborhood of $x _ { i } ,$ defined as an ??-ball around $x _ { i } ,$ i.e., $V _ { x _ { i } } = \{ x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } \in$ $R ^ { d }$ |???????????????? $( x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } , x _ { i } ) < \epsilon \} . p _ { x _ { i } }$ is a linear classifier, e.g., a single-layer perceptron, trained with sampled instances in $V _ { x _ { i } }$ . Local DB is completely linear, and all samples are linearly separably if the LinSep score is 1.0, however, in reality, such a case is uncommon. Thus, we determine that the local DB of an instance is approximately linear if the LinSep score is above a certain threshold, 0.8 in our case, and use a linear explanation model in that case. Otherwise, we use a nonlinear explanation model.

Nonetheless, it should also be noted that the complexity of explanations and faithfulness has a trade-off relationship in general (Poyiadzi et al., 2021). That is, a nonlinear explanation model could show a superior fidelity compared to a linear explanation model; however, it could be too complex to be human-interpretable. For example, a DT classifier with 100 leaf nodes is not easily interpreted by a human. Thus, in many cases, a nonlinear explanation model should be regularized or trimmed to strike a balance between faithfulness and complexity.

After the local model is trained, it is evaluated to assess the local faithfulness with respect to the original classifier. The local fidelity score (LocalFid) is a widely used metric to quantify the local faithfulness of local explanation models:

$$
L o c a l F i d \big (s _ {x _ {i}}, x _ {i} \big) = A c c _ {x _ {j} ^ {(i)} \in V _ {x _ {i}}} \Big (\hat {f} \left(x _ {j} ^ {(i)}\right), s _ {x _ {i}} (x _ {j} ^ {(i)}) \Big),\tag{8}
$$

where ?????? is a standard measure of accuracies such as classification accuracy and F-score. To approximate this, test data points are sampled in the neighborhood $V _ { x _ { i } }$ and accuracy measures are calculated using them. In short, the local fidelity score is an approximate measure that quantifies how well the local explanation model $( s _ { x _ { i } } )$ mimics the blackbox classifier $( \hat { f } )$ in the neighborhood of the data instance being explained $( x _ { i } )$

However, the neighborhood of instances with a greater distance from the DB tends to be homogeneous, i.e., with the same predicted label. Thus, test data points sampled in the neighborhood of such instances will tend to be homogeneous or highly biased, similar to the imbalance in synthetic data points in the data generation phase. Such homogeneity in test samples will render most standard predictive performance metrics used for local fidelity computation to become ineffective. To demonstrate, consider the case of the instance $x _ { 4 }$ in Figure 3 (Panel A in Figure 6). Let’s assume that we have trained two local explanation models for the instance $s _ { x _ { 4 } }$ and $r _ { x _ { 4 } }$ . It is clear that $s _ { x _ { 4 } }$ is more locally faithful to $r _ { x _ { 4 } }$ the local fidelity scores of the two models computed using samples nearby $x _ { 4 }$ (in the orange circle) would be identical if using standard metrics. For example, the accuracy score would be 1 for either model as both models trivially classify all the points within the orange circle as 1. In contrast, in the case of instance $x _ { 2 }$ (Panel B in Figure 6), which is close to the local DB, the local fidelity score will choose $r _ { x _ { 2 } }$ over $s _ { x _ { 2 } }$ since now we have both labels to be classified. Furthermore, local explanations for instances such as $x _ { 4 }$ are likely to be invalid and less informative. Note that although $s _ { x _ { 4 } }$ is relatively more locally faithful than $r _ { x _ { 4 } }$ , it is still considerably distant from $x _ { 4 }$ . In other words, although it approximates the closest local DB of $x _ { 4 }$ , it is likely to be not locally faithful enough to capture instance-specific patterns of $x _ { 4 }$ . When compared to $r _ { x _ { 2 } }$ , which is in the close vicinity of $x _ { 2 } ,$ , it is much less localized and thus less informative in terms of providing instance-level interpretations. In such cases, decision-makers should be cautious in interpreting a local explanation since it can be not as locally faithful as that for other instances. For example, we can consider comparing them to other modes of explanation, e.g., global explanations, or explanations for nearby instances having heterogeneous samples.

Therefore, we propose a novel local explanations framework that takes such edge cases into account. Figure 7 describes the decision procedure for the proposed LDA framework. First, instances $( x _ { i } )$ are tested whether the samples around them are homogeneous, i.e., having only a single type of predicted label. If they are homogeneous, the instance is excluded from fidelity evaluation and the ensuing local explanation warrants a special caution in interpreting it (Case 1). We describe the detailed procedure for this step and the resulting evaluation metric, i.e., local DB-aware (LDA) fidelity score in Algorithm 1. The LDA-fidelity score is a better approximation of the local faithfulness of explanation models, compared to the local fidelity score proposed by Laugel et al. (2018b). If the samples around the instance $( x _ { i } )$ are heterogeneous, the linearity of local DB should be tested using the LinSep score. If the LinSep score is over a certain threshold, i.e., if the local DB is approximately linear, a linear explanation model such as logistic regression should be trained (Case 2). If the LinSep score is below the threshold, a nonlinear local explanation model should be trained (e.g., DT or GAM) (Case 3). However, in this case, the complexity of ensuing explanations should be closely monitored to ensure human interpretability.

## Patient-Level Explanations

After the local explanation model is trained and assessed using fidelity scores, explanations are converted into humaninterpretable representations. Although most existing local, model-agnostic methods provide explanations in terms of feature attribution, e.g., coefficient estimates from linear models, we explore various modes of explanations, e.g., text justifications and visualizations, to enhance human interpretability and applicability. Further, it should be noted that using an “inherently interpretable” algorithm does not guarantee the human interpretability of ensuing explanations. Therefore, especially for highly dimensional data with complex relationships between variables, dimensionality reduction or feature selection methods should be effectively used to enhance the human interpretability of the local model and explanations. To facilitate human-interpretable explanations, we developed a design prototype of a user interface for ROLEX. We believe that it is imperative to develop a user-friendly interface that can effectively deliver essential information and promote the core values of XAI.

First of all, the prototype interface of the ROLEX method promotes the contextual relevance of XAI and the overall HPA system by intensifying the practical utility of the system. To solve a real-world problem, the problem must be concisely represented, enabling an effective and efficient search process to attain the desired ends (Hevner et al., 2004). In the context of HPA, this is equivalent to enhancing the comprehensibility of explanations for various end users such as physicians, caregivers, and patients. In the healthcare field, many human users face many cognitive challenges due to the complexity of HPA systems, which arises from the vastness of the problem space (Jussupow et al., 2021). Therefore, the interface has the potential to provide focus to the problem by reformulating it in a human-interpretable format. Secondly, the interface developed using ROLEX has the potential to enhance patient safety and ethics, minimizing the risks while maximizing the benefits. The ability to communicate incompleteness in the underlying automated algorithm is crucial in assuring the end user’s safety and ethics. When designed properly, we believe that the interface is capable of functioning as a tool to assess the deficiencies and uncertainties meticulously and systematically in the algorithm, enhancing the safety of the overall HPA system. It endorses an iterative process of reverse engineering the black-box model decisions in collaboration with key stakeholders such as domain experts. For instance, how the local explanation model $( s _ { x _ { i } } )$ approximates the “correct” relationship between constructs cannot be assessed with existing means of evaluation. What the local fidelity score assesses is how well $s _ { x _ { i } }$ mimics the black-box prediction model $( \hat { f } )$ , which approximates the reality in the vicinity of the instance being explained $( x _ { i } )$ . Hence, we argue that the “correctness” of the explanations, or their approximation to reality, should be assessed by meticulously examining finegrained details with existing medical knowledge, along with the local faithfulness of explanations. Moreover, in doing so, insights into the phenomenon and new medical knowledge can be obtained and $\hat { f }$ can be further improved by reverse engineering. Also, the interface can help users act on behalf of the public interest. By embellishing the transparency and accountability of the system, researchers and practitioners can be more actively involved in open discussions with various stakeholders such as end users and public policy/law experts.

Furthermore, developing a prototype system is a fundamental step in achieving both rigor and relevance in design science research (Hevner et al., 2004; Nunamaker et al., 1990, 2015). A prototype HPA system with a human-interpretable interface can facilitate active interaction between research and practice, healthcare experts and developers, and so on. The need for XAI arises from incompleteness in the underlying system and XAI is inherently a multidisciplinary science based upon knowledge in various fields and application domains (Miller, 2019). As the design science paradigm postulates that design is “both a process and a product” (Hevner et al., 2004), we believe that actively interacting with the experts and consolidating their feedback to the system can lead to actionable knowledge and practical insights. To achieve this, the comprehensibility, or human interpretability, of an XAI system should be guaranteed. An XAI system should be able to concisely represent the problem to human experts to involve them in the design and reverse-engineering process of the artifact. Nevertheless, how to design a humaninterpretable interface has rarely been addressed in XAI or design science research communities. Hence, we have developed a prototype system for ROLEX, consulting healthcare experts with in-depth experience in both medical research and clinical practice. We describe the prototype in the following section, along with application cases.

## Analysis

## Data

We used three healthcare datasets to assess the applicability of the ROLEX method for various HPA problems. Specifically, we employed two widely used public datasets and a largescale dataset from a cohort study related to fragility fracture. The first two are the Wisconsin breast cancer dataset<sup>2</sup> and the Cleveland heart disease dataset<sup>3</sup>, which are included in the

University of California Irvine (UCI) Machine Learning Repository. As both datasets are commonly used to assess the predictive performance of novel HPA models, we used them to assess the local faithfulness of explanations generated by ROLEX and ensure the generalizability of the method. The last dataset is from the Ansung cohort study, a prospective study started in 2001, supported by the National Genome Research Institute of the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Kim et al., 2017). Data were collected using a large community-based epidemiological survey of a patient population of Korean men and women (40 to 69 years old). 5,018 participants completed a preliminary examination and survey in 2001. For our study, we selected a subset of participants with valid dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) data indicating the presence or absence of a fracture 10 years after starting the cohort study, following a widely used guideline for fracture risk identification (Kanis et al., 2009). As mentioned, we used the cohort dataset to assess the applicability of the ROLEX method in building trustworthy and accountable HPA systems in the real world, developing a prototype user interface and local explanations in collaboration with experts.

Fragility fractures are becoming a severe medical and economic issue worldwide (Reginster & Burlet, 2006). In the United States, the occurrence of fractures is expected to increase from 1.9 million to 3.2 million between 2018 and 2040, with associated costs climbing from \$57 billion up to \$95 billion (Lewiecki et al., 2019). With growing concerns about the medical and economic burden of fracture-related diseases, the importance of early diagnosis and intervention before the first fracture is becoming critical (Reginster & Burlet, 2006). If patients with higher fracture risks can be identified in advance, effective measures can be taken to prevent them from developing one. For instance, it has been demonstrated that active medication for women with osteoporosis can significantly reduce their fracture risks (Black et al., 2000), while regular workouts and dietary interventions can reduce the risk of fracture in general. To enable the efficient and agile prediction of fragility fracture risks, a set of features relevant for fragility fracture prediction was shortlisted by an experienced physician from a collection of over 3,000 indicators. The resulting dataset for analysis comprised 29 attributes and 2,227 patients, 570 of which had been diagnosed with fragility fracture. The input attributes are classified into five categories, i.e., demographics, lifestyle, physical examination, medical records, and bone-quality scores (Table 2). As part of data preprocessing, records with null values in outcomes and clinically identified predictors were excluded, resulting in 2,074 patients. Finally, we split the datasets into training and test sets in an 80:20 ratio. The training set was used to tune hyperparameters and train the models and the test set was used to evaluate them.

ROLEX showed consistently higher local fidelity and LDAfidelity scores compared to established baselines in perturbation sampling-based local explanation methods. LS performed better than LIME and LEAP in most cases, indicating that sampling near the enemy points is more effective than sampling in the entire space and penalizing the samples having a larger distance to the instance. Interestingly, LEAP performed worse than LIME, which strongly indicates that the subspace learning procedure to learn the local intrinsic dimensionality was not effective in most cases. To mitigate this, we tried using other techniques than PCA such as kernel PCA and the tight local intrinsic dimensionality estimator (TLE) (Amsaleg et al., 2019), however, the results were similar. Future work could focus on improving the subspace learning procedure on real-world datasets with high dimensionality and complexity.

In addition, we observed that the local fidelity score was always higher than the LDA-fidelity score. A closer investigation regarding this observation revealed that the instances that were far from local DB, and thus had homogeneous test samples (Case 1 in Figure 7), always showed a local fidelity score of 1.0. In addition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing instances with homogeneous samples (Case 1) and the others (Case 2 and 3) (Table 6). For each instance $x _ { i }$ in the test set, we calculated the distance to all the other test instances to find the nearest $( x _ { i } ^ { N } )$ (i.e., with the minimum distance to $x _ { i } )$ and the farthest $( x _ { i } ^ { F } )$ (i.e., with the maximum distance to $x _ { i } )$ instances. Then, we calculated fidelity scores of local explanations for the nearest $( s ( x _ { i } ^ { N } ) )$ and farthest instances $( s ( x _ { i } ^ { F } ) )$ using samples near $x _ { i } .$ Based on the definition of local faithfulness (Laugel et al. 2018b; Ribeiro et al. 2016), we expected to find the fidelity scores using nearby explanations $( s ( \bar { x } _ { i } ^ { N } ) ^ { \bullet } \mathrm { s } )$ to be more locally faithful than those using explanations far away $( s ( x _ { i } ^ { F } ) ^ { \cdot } \mathrm { s } )$ since they are likely to better approximate the local DB of $x _ { i } \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } . }$ Such a pattern is commonly observed among instances with heterogeneous samples (Case 2 and 3) — $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot s ( x _ { i } ^ { F } ) ^ { * } { \mathbf { s } }$ show a significant performance decline compared to $s ( x _ { i } ^ { N } ) ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s } ,$ , ranging from 20% to 36%. However, instances with homogeneous samples showed a marginal performance decline when using $s ( x _ { i } ^ { F } )$ , which means that there is no sizable difference in local faithfulness between more localized $( s ( x _ { i } ^ { N } ) )$ and less localized explanations $( s ( x _ { i } ^ { F } ) )$ ). Hence, it could be said that the local fidelity score is a less reliable metric to determine which local explanations are more locally faithful than others for such instances. This finding supports our argument regarding the relationship between proximity to local DB and the local fidelity score, confirming the validity of our proposed LDA evaluation framework.

A highly likely reason for this finding is that black-box models using other algorithms showed an average LinSep score higher than 0.9, which implies that most instances have a linear local DB. In short, training samples for the local explanation models were likely imbalanced in many cases, which is supported by a significant contribution of the oversampling scheme. Furthermore, when the local DB is not linear, it is most effective to combine oversampling and nonlinear explanation models.

Since oversampling played the most critical role in improving the performance of our method, we conducted an additional ablation study by adding SMOTE to the baselines (i.e., LIME, LS, and LEAP) for the fragility fracture dataset. The results are reported in Appendix C. Adding SMOTE significantly improved the performance in terms of the local fidelity of LIME and LS, confirming the effectiveness of addressing the issue of imbalance with oversampling in other settings. However, despite the performance boost due to SMOTE, ROLEX still outperformed the added baselines, validating the effectiveness of other mechanisms, i.e., Bayesian optimization and nonlinear explanation models, in enhancing the local faithfulness of explanations.

As shown in the ablation study results, having nonlinear explanations can lead to improvement in local faithfulness when the local DB is nonlinear, e.g., in the case of explaining the XGB classifier. However, as mentioned, this can lead to hampered human interpretability of explanations since faithfulness and complexity have a trade-off relationship in general. Thus, we performed an additional experiment to show how regularization can help balance the two.

Specifically, we changed the maximum number of leaf nodes in the decision tree from 2 to 20 and tracked how the average local fidelity score changed (Figure 9). As expected, fidelity increased as the complexity of the model increased in general. However, after a certain point—around 10 leaf nodes in this case—the fidelity score plateaued and the gain from adding more leaf nodes became marginal. Thus, we set the maximum number of leaf nodes to 10 in this study since it provides a moderate level of human interpretability and a high fidelity score.

## Patient-Level Explanations and Local Interpretations

To develop patient-level explanations, top-k features for each instance were selected prior to the local model fitting procedure to ensure sparsity in explanations, which is an important precondition for human interpretability (Kim et al., 2020; Ribeiro et al., 2016). We used least angle regression (LARS) (Efron et al., 2004); however, other feature selection methods such as regularized trees can be used as well. Then, a local model was fitted for each instance and converted into human-interpretable formats, e.g., text justifications and visualizations. Finally, we generated a user interface by combining different modes of explanations, adopting guiding principles in developing interactive machine learning and intelligent user interface systems.

One of the principles of designing an explanatory system for interactive machine learning is to “be iterative,” supporting in situ learning by users while interacting (Kulesza et al., 2015). Thus, we designed an interactive interface that encourages a user to be actively involved in learning about the model and the phenomenon. The interface was designed as a web-based modular system to help users build a mental model while learning, providing small “bites” of information (Kulesza et al., 2015). The prototype interface was developed using the Plotly and Dash packages in Python.<sup>5</sup> For demonstration purposes, we implemented the interface<sup>6</sup> to explain a blackbox classifier (using the CB algorithm) for fragility fracture prediction.

However, it could be further developed and employed to explain other types of HPA models, including classifiers for heart disease and breast cancer prediction trained in the previous section. Now, we describe the prototype in detail (Figure 10).

1. Choosing a patient to explain: The user is instructed to choose a patient to explain. We chose to present explanations for one patient at a time instead of “overwhelming” (Kulesza et al., 2015) the user with too many explanations on a single screen.

2. Patient profile: After a patient to be explained is selected, the interface shows basic patient-level information. This includes age, height, weight, gender, true label (i.e., whether the patient develops a fracture during the next 10 years), and predicted probability of developing a fracture.

3. Text justifications for the classifier’s decision: The interface provides probable justifications for the black-box classifier’s decision on the selected patient. To do this, locally important features are extracted using LARS (k = 5) and observed values for those features are compared with dataset-level average values.

4. Local explanations for the selected patient: First, the user selects an explanation mode using a dropdown menu. Here, we presented largely three different modes of explanations, i.e., local coefficients (feature attribution), local decision rules, and visualizations (local histogram, box plot, and scatter plots) (Figure 11). However, it should be noted that other explanation modes, e.g., counterfactuals, could be included to cater to the specific needs of end users. Similar to the text justifications, the top five features are considered in order to maximize the comprehensibility of explanations. The explanations are interactive, e.g., users can select different variables to visualize and zoom into specific parts of a plot, in order to keep users engaged and facilitate their learning process.

Now we show how the explanations in the interface are interpreted for patient-level care and medical knowledge discovery. Note that we focus on the fragility fracture prediction problem here since the cohort dataset comprises fine-grained, patient-level information with the potential to generate meaningful local explanations and interpretations. Patient #283 developed a fracture within 10 years, but the black-box classifier predicted a fragility fracture with a 50.5% probability. The patient is a 68-year-old female who lacks physical stature. She is 136cm (around 4.45 feet) tall and weighed about 30kg (around 66 pounds) at the time of measurement. Table 8 shows the top five features and the local coefficients for Patient #283 obtained using ROLEX; Figure 12 presents a visualization of the coefficients. Both the table and the visualization are included in the interface. All variables appear to have positive coefficients, i.e., contributing to higher predicted fracture risks, except patient height. Such trends are consistent with the medical findings that lower-urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) (Liu et al., 2021) and higher levels of alcohol intake (Kanis et al., 2005) are significant risk factors for osteoporosis and fragility fractures. It has also been found that body fat mass/percentage (Wyshak, 2010) is a predictor of fragility fractures, especially among women. An inverse association between body fat and fracture risks has been reported (Wyshak, 2010). However, our analysis shows a positive relationship between the two.

Further interaction with experts suggested that a potential reason for such conflicting findings might be heterogeneity in patient characteristics. Specifically, it was suggested that although a positive association between body fat and fractures exists on average, some patient subgroups with different characteristics, e.g., physical stature, gender, and underlying conditions, may show different tendencies. Similarly, in previous work, it has also been argued that body fat percentage significantly interacts with patient-level characteristics such as age, weight, and height (Wyshak, 2010). Since Patient #283 considerably deviates from the average in terms of physical measurements, i.e., height and weight, a higher body fat mass/percentage might be associated with a lower fracture risk, contrary to the average treatment effects. Based on these discussions, future research could further assess the heterogeneity in the association between body fat and fractures. Such insights might not be obtained without patient-level local explanations.

The local coefficients can demonstrate how each feature contributes to the target outcome, i.e., fracture risks in our case, among a group of patients having certain characteristics. However, they have limitations in showing how the black-box classifier captures interactions between different features. Therefore, we also provided local decision rules, which are “if-then” statements having certain feature values or ranges as conditions and fracture risks as predictions. Figure 13 shows two salient decision rules for Patient #283. These rules show how the top five features interact with each other to assess fracture risks among patients with similar characteristics to Patient #283. We used repeated incremental pruning to produce error reduction (RIPPER) on synthetic samples $( \{ x _ { j } ^ { ( i ) } , j = 1 , 2 , \ldots , N \} )$ to generate salient rules locally applied to the patient.

Some experts also remarked that adding more data could help to show the complex pattern, enabling the model to better approximate the reality. In short, combining a variety of explanations has the potential to provide wider and broader perspectives on the problem, facilitating lateral thinking and creative solutions.

To assess how our method and the prototype can contribute to healthcare practice, we conducted unstructured interviews with four healthcare experts in South Korea, including three physicians and a CEO of a mobile healthcare company. All experts had at least five years of field experience in providing healthcare services to patients and preliminary knowledge in data analytics. We individually interviewed the experts for around 30 minutes. Specifically, we briefly explained the method and the interface and asked how they could be used in practice. Since the interviews were unstructured, we did not have specific questions or requirements for the interviewees in advance. Instead, we let the interviewees freely express their opinions and thoughts regarding their experience with the interface. After the interviews, we performed an inductive analysis on transcripts, i.e., codes were created from the raw data itself (Thomas, 2006). Then, we categorized the codes based on which desideratum in the XAI framework (Table 1) was the most related to each of them.

Table 9 presents a summary of the coding results. Overall, the experts believed that the proposed method and framework could improve various facets of clinical practice, fulfilling the three desiderata of XAI. In particular, the experts commented that the greatest benefits would be derived from improving communication with the general public because they do not have in-depth knowledge of healthcare; this would therefore serve the broader interests of different stakeholders. For instance, many problems in healthcare arise from information asymmetry between physicians and patients (Bloom et al., 2008). Furthermore, according to one expert, such problems seem to be paradoxically exacerbated by the recently increasing ease of access to online health information, e.g., the inundation of unmoderated health-related “fake news” on social media (Pulido et al., 2020). Therefore, it is important to explain complex decisions and their medical rationale in a comprehensible and objective manner, which would be the greatest value addition of the interface as per the experts. Explainable methods like ours can help avert potential conflicts in healthcare and can facilitate cooperation among various stakeholders.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this study, we propose the ROLEX method and demonstrate a real-world application case for explaining fragility fracture predictions. ROLEX shows superior performance in terms of local faithfulness compared to benchmark methods for the fragility fracture dataset as well as two public datasets. Moreover, we developed a user interface that provides human-interpretable, patient-level explanations. Thus, it has the potential to facilitate trustworthy explanations, promoting patient safety and user acceptance of complex HPA applications. Also, we propose guidelines for designing and implementing XAI systems in practice and present a taxonomy of extant XAI methods that are widely adopted in HPA. The explanations produced by ROLEX provide additional insights into global explanations such as coefficient estimates primarily due to the high level of heterogeneity across observations in health datasets. Particularly, for fragility fracture predictions, we found that global explanations were less effective in describing the underlying complex phenomenon of osteoporosis consisting of heterogeneous patterns across many patients, compared to instance-level explanations provided by local models.

## Practical Implications

Trust strongly influences the usability of IT artifacts, and reliable explanations play an important role in building trust (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999). Using our ROLEX method, physicians and patients can gain trust in HPA systems by interpreting complex black-box models and discerning their clinical relevance. Furthermore, researchers and practitioners can derive new insights into patient outcomes by exploring heterogeneous patterns across local explanation models using multiple approaches. Such insights can contribute to patient safety and well-being.

In general, explainable methods based on XAI guidelines (Table 1) enable the development of relevant, ethical, safe, and reliable AI systems to solve real-world problems. As a proof of concept, we demonstrated how an explanation method and user interface can be developed using our framework. We demonstrated that contextual relevance can be enhanced by applying ROLEX to the healthcare problem of fragility fracture risk profiling. Compared to existing methods in local, modelagnostic explanation (Jia et al. 2019; Laugel et al. 2018b; Ribeiro et al. 2016), our method enhances the local faithfulness of explanations, which enables more fine-grained interpretations and applications. In turn, this can improve personalized treatment and care and advance precision medicine, addressing grand challenges in healthcare. Moreover, local explanations generated by ROLEX can improve the overall HPA system by supporting the reverse-engineering process. For example, since local explanations enable individualized interpretations, they can help experts discover patterns beyond average treatment effects. Experts can examine the heterogeneous patterns among various patient groups and identify different predictors that are salient for different groups. Then, machine learning models for different patient groups having more parsimonious and relevant features can be trained.

Second, ROLEX can contribute to real-world problem-solving in light of ethics and safety by enhancing the ability to detect and expose the incompleteness of the system. Decisions made by healthcare practitioners can have a considerable effect on the quality of multiple lives and occasionally determine the life expectancy of patients. Our evaluation framework helps comprehensively navigate the local explanations procedure. That is, it not only helps compare local explainable methods but also informs as to when local explanations can be more (or less) effective. Furthermore, we emphasize the human-centric elements of an XAI system, i.e., human interpretability and a user-friendly interface, which can help practitioners better articulate the incompleteness of the overall system. It has the potential to bolster the transparency and accountability of the black-box prediction models in HPA systems. This can reduce conflicts between stakeholders in the healthcare field, e.g., between physicians and patients, most of which arise from information asymmetry (Bloom et al., 2008).

## Contributions to IS Knowledge Base

Lin et al. (2017) discussed how solving healthcare problems with an HPA system is relevant and can contribute to the IS discipline, especially in design science research. Extending the discussion, we claim that our ROLEX method provides the ability to explain results from such systems and therefore is relevant for multiple research streams in IS, including healthcare IS, big data and predictive analytics, and design science. Our study sheds light on patient safety and user acceptance, which are highly critical in the healthcare context (Ahmad et al., 2018). It has been established that trust plays a critical role in the user’s acceptance of technology in various contexts, and we believe that it plays a more important role in the context of healthcare because users are generally more reluctant to change (Fichman et al., 2011). Furthermore, enhanced transparency can encourage conscientious decisionmaking on behalf of the greater public interest.

Explanations with the help of domain experts and the reversedesigning process can support the discovery of new knowledge regarding a phenomenon of interest (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2018). In the context of big data analytics, this can be one way to exploit the synergistic relationship between big data and theory (Rai, 2016). Moreover, we discussed how such human-interpretable, lucid explanations promote information diffusion across diverse stakeholders and may reduce conflict among them. Finally, we pay attention to guidelines for designing XAI systems for real-world problem solving. We propose that XAI systems are design artifacts with features that are distinct from conventional AI systems. Based on the features, we suggest guidelines for designing XAI systems in practice that can help researchers examine their XAI methods and set a theoretical foundation for their studies. We also attempt to advance current standards of XAI methods with theoretical discussions and in-depth investigation of the problem-solving process in healthcare.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has a few limitations and considerations. The optimization process of finding optimal parameters $( \beta _ { i } , \epsilon _ { i } )$ can be slightly more time-consuming, especially when the dataset is complex and highly nonlinear. Nevertheless, directly optimizing for the local fidelity metric eliminates the need to fine-tune hyperparameters (e.g., the initial value of the sampling radius) or apply other heuristic algorithms (e.g., PCA for LEAP and growing spheres for LS), thus compensating for the optimal parameter search cost. Furthermore, it considerably improves the faithfulness of explanations measured with both local fidelity and LDA fidelity scores. Future work can improve the optimization process, e.g., explore optimization methods other than Bayesian optimization. Moreover, since the evaluation results can differ based on how we set $r _ { t e s t } .$ , future research could focus on the relationship between learned parameters $( \mathbf { e . g . } , \ \beta _ { i } , \epsilon _ { i } )$ and $r _ { t e s t }$ and their impact on the local faithfulness of explanations.

Another limitation of our method is commonly shared by local explanation models for producing multifaceted interpretation mechanisms. Global linear models have a single set of coefficients for generating interpretations. Although each data point has its own set of models and coefficients in local explanations, this requires the end user to inspect each data point, possibly inducing mental overload. In contrast, although it is less common in healthcare, there could be edge cases in which explanations for many instances are homogeneous. That is, global and local explanations become essentially equivalent in a certain region of the feature space. In either case, it might be helpful to assess midlevel explanations, e.g., explanations for patient subgroups. Future work could focus on the systematic exploration of local boundaries to extricate homogeneous subgroups and identify ways to generate trustworthy and reliable midlevel explanations. Similarly, generating robust and reliable explanations for constantly changing data, i.e., data shifts, can be a nontrivial issue. For instance, if the distribution of new data differs significantly from existing data for which explanations are generated, current explanations might be less valuable. Determining how to utilize local explanation methods to generalize in dynamic environments in which data shifts are prevalent could be an interesting research direction.

Although our primary focus in this study was on explaining HPA systems, we believe that our method can be generalized to other domains having similar characteristics. Future work could investigate the applicability of local explanation methods such as ROLEX across different datasets and domains. Furthermore, while we focused on explaining black-box binary classifiers in this study, there is ample scope for extending ROLEX to explain multiclass/continuous outcome prediction as well as other facets of machine learning, such as unsupervised learning and reinforcement learning. Moreover, although the local explanations generated by ROLEX are consistent with prior medical findings and were validated by experienced healthcare experts, post hoc explanation methods have inherent limitations, e.g., susceptibility to adversarial attacks (Slack et al., 2020). In addition, perturbation sampling methods have limitations in explaining distances that are far from the local DB, as explained in our evaluation framework. Since this is a nontrivial issue in real-world applications, users should be aware of such limitations, especially in disciplines with high stakes such as healthcare and criminal justice. There are also recently developed methods that focus on faithfulness to ground truth (e.g., Guidotti 2021). Although the focal aim of our study was to enhance the faithfulness of local explanations for a blackbox model (??<sup>̂</sup>), this could be also of critical importance in some real-world cases. Future studies could seek to improve the explanation model’s ability to approximate ground truth and evaluate it. Finally, we plan to further develop the proposed prototype of the user interface described earlier. We hope to collaborate with other domain experts in healthcare and with experienced practitioners in user experience/interface to enhance the practical value of our framework and interface.

## Conclusion

In this study, we introduce a theoretical framework for designing and deploying XAI systems in practice and use it to develop a method for generating local explanations for safe and user-centric healthcare predictive analytics. We demonstrate the applicability of our method by developing a prototype HPA system for predicting and explaining fragility fracture risk. Our study contributes to the IS knowledge base, particularly in healthcare IS, big data analytics, and design science by introducing a new design artifact for explaining predictions, which sheds light on trustworthy and user-centric IS.
