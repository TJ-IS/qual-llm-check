# Examining the effects of malfunctioning personalized services on online users' distrust and behaviors

## a b s t r a c t

Merchants adopt web personalization technologies to offer product recommendations in the hope of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing online users' decision making in a shopping process. Although there is a large body of research on the favorable effects of web personalization on in<sup>fl</sup>uencing online users' decision making, it is often assumed that web personalization functions well. Only scant research examines the adverse outcomes of web personalization on online users' perceptions and behavior when the personalized services malfunction. This research aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine malfunctioning personalized services that produce irrelevant and biased product recommendations in online shopping. Irrelevant recommendations are offerings not matched to online users preferences, whereas biased recommendations are offerings generated for the merchant's interests. When online users encounter such malfunctioning personalized services, they may distrust the personalization agent, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences their interactions with the agent. We drew on distrust theories to develop six hypotheses. To test the hypotheses, 245 participants were recruited for a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in which they were tasked to download free music tracks from a personalized music download website. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that both irrelevant and biased recommendations lead to high distrust in a personalization agent's competence and integrity. Competence distrust, but not integrity distrust, in a personalization agent negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences online users' interactions with the agent.

## 1. Introduction

The adoption of web personalization in electronic commerce is growing at a phenomenal rate. According to a survey by Unica [42], 73% of merchants considered using web personalization to facilitate communication with their customers. A report by KPMG reveals that investment in web personalization is a high priority in business [31]. Web personalization enables a merchant to identify each individual user, acquire information about users' preferences by having them provide information to the merchant either directly or through tracking devices on the websites, and provide special treatments in the form of individualized content and layout matched to their preferences [21,27–29]. The ultimate objective of a merchant adopting web personalization is to generate more business opportunities by providing online users with highly focused and relevant product recommendations [14,39,40]. This technology enabler is referred to as a “personalization agent,” a collection of software modules that deploys tools to collect and analyze the browsing behavior and transactions of online users [1]; and the individualized content is referred to as “personalized recommendations.”

The proliferation of web personalization makes it a desirable area of study for information systems (IS) researchers. Prior work often suggests that web personalization favorably in<sup>fl</sup>uences an online user's perceptions and behavior. For instance, one group of studies examines various personalization strategies and their effects on capturing the attention of online users and biasing their purchase decisions. Tam and Ho [39] conducted <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments using a ring-tone download website to show that preference matching is an important factor affecting the acceptance of the offer for personalization agents. In another study, Tam and Ho [40] investigated the effects of content relevance and self relevance on users' cognitive processing and decision making when they use personalization agents. Ho et al. [16] looked at how the timing of web personalization in<sup>fl</sup>uences a user's decision to take a recommendation. As the personalization agent works as a virtual sales agent, some IS researchers conceptualize it as a human being who develops an interpersonal relationship with an online user. Thus, they use the trust theories to explain how online users build (human) trust in the personalization agent.

Trust is particularly relevant to web personalization research because online users are involved in risks, such as privacy infringement or monetary losses [14,35]. Prior research has examined how the performance of a personalization agent in<sup>fl</sup>uences online users' trust in the agent. Wang and Benbasat [45] found that perceived ease of use of a personalization agent<sup>1</sup> in<sup>fl</sup>uences a user's trust in the agent, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences the perceived usefulness of the agent and a user's intention to use. Komiak and Benbasat [17] examined how the extent of preference matching in<sup>fl</sup>uenced online users' cognitive trust and integrity trust in a personalization agent, and consequently, their decision to adopt the agent. Buczak et al. [5] looked at the personalization algorithm for generating a recommendation and conducted an experiment to demonstrate that users built trust in a personalization agent only when the agent adopted an algorithm that produced recommendations that were highly relevant to their preferences. In the context of mobile personalization, Ho and Bull [15] found that the personalization agent's capability to detect a mobile user's location in<sup>fl</sup>uenced his or her competence trust and integrity trust in personalized mobile services.

However, the research design employed in prior studies might be biased toward <sup>fi</sup>ndings that are favorable to the effectiveness of web personalization for two reasons. First, prior studies (e.g., [8,39,40,44,45]) investigated self-developed personalization agents for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c purpose of experiments for data collection, in which participants worked on the same task. Thus, it was easy for their agents to understand the participants' preferences precisely. However, in practice, merchants have to make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investment in technologies, such as data mining and click stream analysis tools, to detect online users' preferences so accurately [1]. The issue is further complicated because a person's preferences are sometimes constructed spontaneously, especially in situations where he or she has little product knowledge or is not familiar with the online shopping environment [46]. Therefore, in practice, the prediction performance of recommendations made by primitive personalization methods that solely use registration pro<sup>fi</sup>les and past transaction records should be poor [28,30]. Consequently, personalization agents in commercial websites are unlikely to perform highly in servicing online customers.

Second, the objective function of personalization agents tested in prior research (e.g., [14,15,17]) is relatively simple – to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of online users in a shopping process – and the agent simply presents personalized recommendations that attempt to satisfy experimental participants as much as possible. However, in practice, a merchant has to strike a balance of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts among multiple parties, including online users, product vendors, and the merchant itself. The merchant wants to attract and retain online users with personalized recommendations that ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their shopping requirements and help them complete their shopping process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. Further, the merchant wants to assist product vendors to promote their products, which may be less popular but of a high pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin, to online users. As the merchant's revenue hinges on both the sales to online users and commissions from product vendors, the merchant is unlikely to use only one criterion – maximizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of online users – for the generation of personalized recommendations, and has incentives to provide recommendations that are less preference-matched (but of a higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin) to online users for bene<sup>fi</sup>ting product vendors as well as the merchant.

The objective of this research is to examine how the above two “problems” of web personalization in<sup>fl</sup>uence online users' perceptions and behavior toward the personalization agent. We refer to these problems as irrelevant recommendations and biased recommendations. Irrelevant recommendations are incorrect recommendations that do not match online users' preferences caused by a merchant's lack of full understanding of the preferences. Biased recommendations are a merchant's product recommendations that are not generated based on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of online users. We collectively refer to these problems as malfunctioning personalized services. Apart from online users' privacy concerns regarding the personalization agent's use of their information for purposes other than providing recommendations without seeking their prior consent (such as selling the information to marketers or using it for other promotional activities organized by the agent), this paper examines the following research questions.

1. How do irrelevance and bias of recommendations in<sup>fl</sup>uence online users' distrust in a personalization agent?

2. How does online users' distrust in a personalization agent affect their interactions with the agent?

In this study, we conducted an online experiment for data collection. We collaborated with one of the largest digital content providers in Paci<sup>fi</sup>c Asia to set up a personalized music website. Unlike the experiments in prior research, this website purposely generated irrelevant or biased recommendations to online users. We invited 245 participants to sample and download digital music from our website multiple times. After each round of download, they completed a questionnaire to report their experience with the personalization agent.

The reminder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on distrust and prior research on personalization agents. Section 3 outlines the theoretical framework of this study and develops the hypotheses. Section 4 outlines the methodology and Section 5 presents the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The paper concludes with a discussion of the study's theoretical contributions and practical implications, as well as future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Distrust: A psychological state

Trust has drawn much attention from IS researchers. Prior research (e.g., [11,38]) has examined why and how trust in a technology in<sup>fl</sup>uences an online user's behavior toward the technology. It also investigates the multidimensionality of trust (competence, integrity, and benevolence) [22,23]. According to the literature on trust, trust and distrust are two distinct constructs [3,19,20] and not two opposite ends of the same construct. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while trust brings people a feeling of safety and security, distrust brings people a feeling of worry, suspicion and fear [24,41]. The two groups of feelings (i.e., safety and security; and worry, suspicion and fear) can co-exist—trust and distrust are two distinct constructs that can co-exist. Findings from prior neuroeconomics studies suggest that trust and distrust are not two opposite ends of the same concept—people secrete different types of hormones [48] and have different kinds of brain activities [9] depending on whether they are in a trusting or distrusting state (not a higher or lower amount of the same kind of hormones or brain activities). The current research takes the perspective that distrust is distinct from trust, and anticipates that the adverse effects of irrelevant recommendations and biased recommendations on an online user's shopping behavior are more than just a reduction of trust in the personalization agent. It uses distrust as a mediating mechanism to explain the adverse effects of malfunctioning personalized services that produce irrelevant and biased recommendations on an online user's interactions with the personalization agent.

There is scant IS research on the effect that distrust in a technology has on user behavior. One exception is McKnight et al., who developed a conceptual model of distrust [23,24] and instruments to measure the constructs in the model [25], and empirically tested the model in the context of Internet expert advice websites [26]. In particular, McKnight et al. synthesized prior research from various social science disciplines to form a conceptual model that links three aspects of distrust—disposition to distrust, institutional-based distrust, and interpersonal distrust. Disposition to distrust captures a person's general tendency, or traits, to distrust other people in general, which includes his or her distrusting stance and suspicion of humanity [23,26]. Institutional-based distrust consists of two components. The <sup>fi</sup>rst component, (negative) structural assurance, explains the distrust of a person in an institution, such as companies and websites [23,24]. The second component, (negative) situational normality, captures a person's distrust in a situation that takes place in the institution, such as conducting business transactions with a company or on a website [23]. Interpersonal distrust captures a person's distrusting beliefs and distrusting behavior in response to his or her distrust in a particular person or company [25].

## 2.2. Competence distrust and integrity distrust

In studying trust, prior research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three dimensions of trust—competence trust, integrity trust and benevolence trust. Although distrust is conceptually distinct from trust, it may also be examined with these three dimensions, as both trust and distrust look at the interpersonal relationships among humans, or between a human and a technology. In addition, similar to a trusting relationship, a distrusting relationship always involves two parties: a trustor and a trustee.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension of distrust is competence distrust. Competence is the ability of the trustee to do what it says it will do. Competence distrust is the trustor's belief that the trustee does not have the ability to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the duties and perform the obligations for the trustor [23,24]. The trustor may form such a distrusting belief based on his or her observations and experiences with the trustee. For instance, if a salesperson (a trustee) is incapable of giving relevant product suggestions to customers (trustors) to assist their shopping, or if the customers are recommended to purchase over-expensive or useless products, the customers are likely to suspect, or even be cautious about, the competence of the salesperson. Consequently, the customers form competence distrust in the salesperson.

The second dimension of distrust is integrity distrust. Integrity is the honesty of the trustee to behave in the trustor's interests. Integrity distrust is a trustor's belief that the trustee fails to make good faith agreements such as the trustee's dishonesty to hide information from, or even to cheat, the trustor to gain an advantage [23,24]. The trustor distrusts in the integrity of the trustee based on the trustor's experiences in dealing with the trustee. For instance, a salesperson (a trustee) dishonestly recommends a customer to purchase products from a particular product vendor because the salesperson receives a higher commission from that vendor than from other vendors. Hence, even though there exists many cheaper, or more preference-matched, alternatives from other product vendors, the salesperson does not show them to the customer and limits all recommendations to one particular vendor. In such a situation, the customer may be cautious about the integrity of the salesperson (even though he or she may consider the product suggestions to be relevant to his or her preferences). Consequently, the customer forms integrity distrust in the salesperson.

The third dimension of distrust is benevolence distrust. Benevolence refers to the trustee's act of, or a general inclination toward, kindness and sincerity. Benevolence distrust is the trustor's belief that the trustee does not care about, and is not motivated to act in, the trustor's interests [23,24]. One of the most common approaches for the trustee to demonstrate his or her benevolence to the trustor is by showing kindness and sincerity to the trustor through close interactions [24,44]. As the current research examines the role of a personalization agent that displays recommendations on a website, the interaction process between the agent and online users is relatively limited—the agent presents product recommendations, and online users click on the recommendations and decide to buy or not. As it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for online users to precisely judge the benevolence of an agent based on these simple interactions, the current research does not investigate benevolence distrust in a personalization agent; rather, it focuses solely on how irrelevant recommendations and biased recommendations lead to the formation of competence distrust and integrity distrust in a personalization agent. This approach was adopted by prior research on web personalization—for example, Komiak and Benbasat [17] suggest that only trust in the integrity and competence of the agent in<sup>fl</sup>uences web users' interactions with the agent.

Distrust of a trustor in a trustee may alter his or her behavior with the trustee. When a trustor distrusts in the competence and/or the integrity of the trustee, the trustor tends to respond carefully because the trustor is worried or even cautious about the possible negative consequences from interactions with the trustee [23]. The trustor may try to protect himself or herself by minimizing his or her reliance on the trustee. Therefore, in the above examples, when a customer distrusts the competence and/or the integrity of a salesperson, he or she is likely to refuse the salesperson's recommendations. If an alternative is available, the customer may want to switch to a new salesperson and terminate the relationship with the current salesperson. This possible change in behavior is particularly crucial in the web personalization context because a reduction in interactions with the personalization agent may lead to a decrease in a merchant's revenue, such as revenue from clicks on advertised products.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

## 3.1. Malfunctioning personalized services and competence distrust

This research examines the effects of irrelevant recommendations and biased recommendations on two dimensions of distrust of online users – competence distrust and integrity distrust – in a personalization agent, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence their interactions with the agent. We <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the irrelevance of personalized recommendations. Irrelevant recommendations are personalized offerings that do not match online users' preferences. In the process of personalization, the personalization agent often explicitly asks users their preferences during membership registration or through regular consumer surveys, and/or implicitly collects their past transactions, and then analyses the data to produce recommendations [40]. If the recommendations are irrelevant, online users are likely to think that some procedures in the generation of personalized recommendations have gone wrong. Hence, they suspect or are even cautious about the ability of the agent to generate good recommendations to help them simplify the selection process for a suitable product in a web environment. In other words, the agent is perceived to be incompetent, and users may need to invest their own personal time to screen out recommendations irrelevant to their actual preferences. In the worst case, if online users (by mistake) select a recommended product not matched to their preferences as their <sup>fi</sup>nal choice, they may suffer a monetary loss. As a result, if an agent fails to generate relevant recommendations, users will be worried about or even fear the negative consequences caused by the malfunctioning personalization services. Consequently, they form competence distrust in the personalization agent that produces recommendations irrelevant to their preferences. Therefore, we posit the following hypothesis:

H1. The extent of irrelevance of personalized recommendations will increase online users' competence distrust in the agent.

Next, we examine the possible effects of biased recommendations on online users' competence distrust in the personalization agent. Biased recommendations are generated based on business criteria that strike a balance between the gains of product vendors and the shopping experience of online users. Thus, in an attempt to maximize their own bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, merchants ignore online users' bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and their personalization agent intentionally does not recommend the products that best match the users' preferences. Instead, the merchants base their recommendations on business criteria, such as product stock levels in the warehouse, commission rates from product vendors, and even promotion strategies of product vendors [45] to generate recommendations. In the study by Komiak and Benbasat [18], the extent of bias is regarded as being the inverse of the choice ranges of product vendors recommended to online users. In other words, recommendations that come from a single vendor are biased because they are favorable to one particular vendor. Recommendations that come from many different vendors are unbiased because they represent a fair range of choices from which online users can select. A personalization agent may have ignored online users' indicated preferences and limited their choices in the hope of (unfairly) biasing their <sup>fi</sup>nal choice, as users generally expect an agent to generate product recommendations from a full database of products and conduct a fair screening to provide recommendations for customers. Receiving biased recommendations from one particular product vendor may lead users to think that the agent is incompetent because the agent does not keep track of all products from all vendors, fails to carefully analyze customer transactions for generating better recommendations, or uses poorly designed personalization algorithms. Recommendations biased toward one particular product vendor increase online users' fear and worry about the personalization agent's competence in recommending suitable products for their purchase consideration. Consequently, they form distrust in the competence of an agent that produces biased recommendations. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. The extent of biases of personalized recommendations will increase online users' competence distrust in the agent.

## 3.2. Malfunctioning personalized services and integrity distrust

The personalization agent gives an implicit promise to online users – to provide good recommendations for the customers' bene<sup>fi</sup>t – in exchange for accessing the users' pro<sup>fi</sup>les and transactional data. In this relationship, when online users agree to provide their preferences to the personalization agent, they expect the agent to keep this promise to generate personalized recommendations for their bene<sup>fi</sup>t. Prior research suggests that when the personalization agent keeps this promise, online users trust the integrity of the agent [17,45], but when the agent breaks this promise, the users may distrust the integrity of the agent.

When a personalization agent generates recommendations that are not relevant to the online users' preferences, users may view the agent as obtaining data from customers but not providing the corresponding services. Therefore, they may suspect the agent's purpose in collecting the data or even worry about how the agent actually uses their data. Hence, they may consider that the agent has broken the promise and is dishonest. They may even see that the agent deliberately provides a list of irrelevant products as personalized recommendations in order to mislead them to purchase these products for a high pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin. This leads them to fear or even be cautious about the agent's integrity. In other words, they form integrity distrust in the personalization agent. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis regarding irrelevant recommendations:

H3. The extent of irrelevance of personalized recommendations will increase online users' integrity distrust in the agent.

When a personalization agent generates recommendations biased to a particular product vendor, online users may feel that the agent is not providing a fair and reasonable set of recommended products for them to choose from; thus they feel that the agent dishonestly biases (or even misleads) them to purchase a product for the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of a product vendor and/or merchant, and not in the users' interests [18]. Online users may even worry or be anxious about a merchant's attempt to sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for other sources of revenue, such as to earn a high commission from a particular product vendor. Hence, they may consider that the personalization agent fails to adhere to its principle to serve customers honestly. Consequently, the users form a feeling of fear and worry about the dishonesty of the agent, and are likely to form integrity distrust in the agent. Hence, we propose the following:

H4. The extent of biases in personalized recommendations will increase online users' integrity distrust in the agent.

## 3.3. Effects of distrusting beliefs on user behavior

When online users distrust the competence of a personalization agent, they worry, or even fear, that the outcomes of interacting with the agent may be harmful to them. If they are uncertain about the outcomes of using the agent in the shopping process, they may try to reduce interaction or even avoid interacting with the agent. Research by McKnight and Choudhury [24] demonstrates that when a person distrusts the competence of a technology, he or she is unlikely to use it to complete a task (in their context, not web personalization but the use of an online legal consultancy website for making a decision in a legal case). Therefore, we hypothesize that when online users distrust the competence of a personalization agent, they are likely to reduce their interactions with the agent:

H5. Online users' competence distrust in the personalization agent will reduce their interactions with the agent.

We next examine the effect of integrity distrust in a personalization agent on user behavior. When online users distrust the integrity of a personalization agent, the negative emotions of fear and worry urge them to be alert to the agent's possible dishonesty and the associated risks [23]; thus, they are likely to reduce or avoid interacting with the agent to minimize the risk of being cheated and incurring potential harm. Therefore, we anticipate that online users who distrust a personalization agent's integrity are likely to reduce their activities to explore the agent's recommendations. This is aligned with the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of McKnight and Choudhury [24] that a person's integrity distrust in an online legal consultancy website reduced his or her interactions with it. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

H6. Online users' integrity distrust in the personalization agent will reduce their interactions with the agent.

To test the six hypotheses, we collaborated with a digital content provider to set up a personalized website that delivered malfunctioning personalized services – offering irrelevant recommendations and biased recommendations – and used this website to conduct a <sup>fi</sup>eld study, which is detailed in Section 4.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. The experimental context

The experimental context was an online shopping environment for digital music. We chose digital music because it is experience goods, such that online users often click on digital music to listen to songs before purchasing them, which makes interactions with digital music recommendations by a personalization agent particularly meaningful. To prepare a personalized digital music website, we cooperated with a digital music content provider who provided us with 5451 digital music tracks from 120 popular artists in China. We also obtained information on the tracks such as the release date, the name of the music company, and a popularity index (based on album sales and track ranking from billboards). The digital music content provider also generated popularity indices based on the sampling and downloading of records by its real-life customers. To eliminate the possibility that participants' prior experience with the provider's of<sup>fi</sup>cial website would in<sup>fl</sup>uence their perceptions of the web personalization, we used the digital music tracks, track information, and popularity indices to develop a new personalized music website for the purpose of this experiment. Participants were asked to download music from our website in six rounds,<sup>2</sup> and we divided the 5451 digital songs into six pools. In each pool, there were approximately 900 digital songs by 20 different artists.

We used a different pool of songs for different rounds of visits to ensure that participants encountered a new set of recommendations on each visit. This eliminated the possibility of participants building distrust in the personalization agent due to the boredom and tedium of seeing repetitive personalized recommendations. A general category of artists was available at the top of the website, which allowed participants to browse all the artists, albums, and songs. This provided another means for them to download songs they liked if they refused to explore the personalized recommendations.

## 4.2. Design and manipulation

We adopted a 2 (irrelevant vs. relevant) × 2 (biased vs. unbiased) full-factorial design and used the following process to generate personalized recommendations to the participants in each of the six rounds. Since all the tracks were pop music and all participants were young people, we considered recently published, popular songs by their favorite artists to be relevant to their preferences. Thus, we used participants' artist preferences, as well as the publication dates and the popularity indices of the songs to generate relevant vs. irrelevant recommendations. We manipulated biased vs. unbiased recommendations in the same way as described in Komiak and Benbasat [18]—biased recommendations came from one particular product vendor, whereas unbiased recommendations came from multiple product vendors. In our experiment, biased recommendations came from only one favorite artist of the participants, whereas unbiased recommendations came from all of their six favorite artists.

The process to generate a personalized recommendation involved two steps. First, in each round of download, before the participants started the website navigation, they were asked to choose and rank their top six favorite artists among the 20 different artists for that round. We provided a different artist list in each round to prevent the participants from seeing identical personalized recommendations. Then, depending on whether the participants received relevant or irrelevant recommendations, we used song popularity indices and the publication date to generate six popular and recent music tracks (for the group receiving relevant recommendations) or six less popular and old music tracks (for the group receiving irrelevant recommendations). Further, depending on whether participants received biased or unbiased recommendations, they received six personalized recommendations from the sixth favorite artist (for the group receiving biased recommendations) or from all of the six different favorite artists (for the group receiving unbiased recommendations). Each participant remained in the same treatment group throughout the six rounds. For all groups, personalized recommendations were presented at the top of the website.

## 4.3. Participants

We recruited university students through open enrolment on a university campus, posting ads on public notice boards on campus. The ads contained a hyperlink for online registration, where participants could start at any time and place during the four-week recruiting period. During the registration, participants were asked to report their demographics. They could then logon to our personalized music website to browse the music category and listen to preview versions of music tracks at any time and place. However, after they downloaded one track, they had to wait for 18 h before the next download because we wanted to clear the participants' memories of prior experience with our personalization agent and prevent them from using their memories to answer the post-task questionnaires. Thus, in the following logon session, the participants should re-assess the personalization agent to complete the questionnaire rather than recall and report their experience from the previous logon. As a token of appreciation, we offered each participant six free music tracks (one for each round) and an entry into the draw for one of two 32G iPod touches. For this study, we successfully recruited 245 participants (150 females and 95 males, average age = 22). They were all Internet users, who spent 4.5 h per day on average web browsing; 98.4% of them had music download experience and 90.6% had online shopping experience.

## 4.4. Study procedures

During the registration, participants were asked to <sup>fi</sup>ll in a pre-task questionnaire about their demographic information. They were also asked to respond to items that measured their need for cognition, institutional-based distrust, and disposition to distrust. These constructs served as control variables in our study. The questionnaire items for these constructs are shown in Appendix A. At the beginning of every logon session, participants chose a list of artists and were then directed to the personalized digital music website. On the website, participants would <sup>fi</sup>nd six personalized recommendations presented at the top of the window and a list of general items at the bottom of the window (see Fig. 2).

There was a [More Details] label next to the album image of each music track. This label was a hyperlink, which participants could click on to browse the details of the music track (see Fig. 3). The details of the track included artist information, track release date, and track duration. On the same page, two link options were provided with one labeled “Listen,” and the other “Add to Basket.” By clicking on the “Listen” link, participants could listen to a 30-second preview track, and then add any number of tracks to their shopping baskets. We used the number of recommended songs that they listened to for previewing as a proxy to measure the amount of interactions with the personalization agent, which was our dependent variable. When the participants clicked on the “Basket” button on the top menu, items inside the basket were displayed (see Fig. 4). At the end of each logon session, participants completed a post-task questionnaire, in which we asked them to report their perceptions of personalized recommendations (i.e., the extent of irrelevance and the extent of bias).<sup>3</sup> Then, we asked participants to respond to items that measured their competence distrust and integrity distrust in the personalization agent. After a participant downloaded a music track, he or she waited for at least 18 h for the next download. The entire experiment involved six rounds of download, and participants took about a week to complete it.

A pilot test with 13 participants was used to test the performance of the personalized music website. Participants completed a music download session within 10 min. They all agreed that the navigation process and the digital music selection task were smooth.

## 4.5. Control variables

Apart from the independent and dependent variables speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Fig. 1, in our data collection, we collected <sup>fi</sup>ve control variables: gender, age, disposition to distrust, institutional-based distrust, and need for cognition. We included gender and age as control variables, as prior studies indicated that they in<sup>fl</sup>uenced online users' intentions to adopt or use a system (e.g., [37,43]). We included institutional-based distrust because, as suggested by McKnight et al. [23,24], it in<sup>fl</sup>uences competence distrust and integrity distrust. We included disposition to distrust, as it was found to have an effect on competence distrust and integrity distrust, as well as institutional-based distrust [4,23,25,26]. Finally, we included the need for cognition as a control variable, as Tam and Ho [39] showed that it in<sup>fl</sup>uences the amount of online users' interactions with a personalization agent.

## 5.2. Structural model results

The data analysis results are depicted in $\mathrm { F i g } . 5 . ^ { 4 }$ Our model gave a reasonably good prediction—the predictors used in this study explained 42.3% of the variance in users' competence distrust in the personalization agent, 42.2% of the variance in users' integrity distrust in the agent, and 17.0% of the variance in the interactions with the agent.

H1 and H2 examined the effects of two types of malfunctioning personalized services on a person's distrust in the competence of a personalization agent. Our result shows that irrelevant recommendations generated by an agent had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on online users' competence distrust $( \mathrm { t } = 6 . 1 2 3 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient was positive $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 7 )$ , which supported H1 because it indicated that users would have a higher level of competence distrust in the agent when they perceived recommendations to be irrelevant than when they perceived them to be relevant. H2, which examined the effects of biased recommendations on online users' distrust in the competence of a personalization agent, was also supported. We found that users were likely to form a higher level of competence distrust in the agent when they perceived recommendations to be biased than when they perceived them to be unbiased. This positive relationship was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the positive coef<sup>fi</sup>cient $( \mathrm { \beta } = 0 . 3 5 8 )$ . According to the PLS result, this relationship was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathrm { t } = 5 . 7 4 3 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ ).

H3 and H4 examined the effects of the two types of malfunctioning personalized services on a person's distrust in the integrity of a personalization agent. H3 was concerned with whether irrelevant recommendations generated by a personalization agent had a positive impact to online users' distrust in the agent's integrity. Our result shows that when users perceived personalized recommendations to be irrelevant to their preferences, they were likely to distrust the agent's integrity. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient was positive $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 5 )$ , indicating that the extent of perceived irrelevance exerted a positive effect on their integrity distrust in the agent. Therefore, H3 was supported $( \mathrm { t } = 4 . 5 1 1 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ . H4 examined the effects of biased recommendations on online users' distrust in the integrity of a personalization agent. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that when users perceived recommendations to be biased, they were likely to form distrust in the agent's competence. This relationship was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the positive coef<sup>fi</sup>cient $( \beta = 0 . 4 2 1 )$ ) for the relationship between biased recommendations and an online user's integrity distrust in the personalization agent. The effect of biased recommendations on integrity distrust was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathrm { t } = 6 . 7 1 7$ $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Thus, H4 was supported.

Our last two hypotheses, H5 and H6, focused on the effects of online users' competence distrust and integrity distrust in a personalization agent on their interactions with the agent. H5 was concerned with competence distrust. We found that the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the relationship between online users' competence distrust in the personalization agent and their amount of sampling of personalized recommendations was negative $( \beta = - 0 . 3 2 4 )$ . This negative coef<sup>fi</sup>cient indicates that users with high distrust in the agent's competence were unlikely to interact with the agent. This con<sup>fi</sup>rmed H5 $( \mathrm { t } = 2 . 6 2 0 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). H6 examined the effect of integrity distrust on online users' interactions with the personalization agent. It hypothesized that users' integrity distrust in the agent would have a negative impact on user interactions with the agent; that is, online users with high integrity distrust in a personalization agent were predicted to be unlikely to explore the recommendations generated by the agent. Our PLS result indicates that the relationship between users' integrity distrust and their interactions with the agent was positive $( \mathrm { \beta } = 0 . 2 9 5 )$ ), which was opposite to what we had predicted. Thus, H6 was not supported. Interestingly, this positive effect of integrity distrust on an online user's interactions with the personalization agent was found to be statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathrm { t } = 2 . 2 6 6 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). We discuss this unexpected result in Section 6.1.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key findings

Table 3 presents a summary of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The objectives of this research were two-fold: <sup>fi</sup>rst, to investigate if and how malfunctioning personalized services that produce irrelevant and/or biased recommendations affect online users' distrust in the competence and integrity of the personalization agent; and second, to examine how their competence distrust and integrity distrust in the agent in<sup>fl</sup>uence the amount of interactions with the agent. Our results show that when online users perceive the recommendations provided by a personalization agent to be irrelevant to their preferences, they are likely to distrust the competence (H1) and integrity (H3) of the agent. When users perceive the recommendations provided by the agent to be biased to a particular product vendor (not to their interests), they are also likely to distrust the competence (H2) and integrity (H4) of the agent. Competence distrust (H5) also reduces online users' interactions with the personalization agent.

One possible reason for this is the context of our study, which was digital music shopping. The extent of how much an individual likes a song depends not only on his or her liking of the artist, but also on the popularity of the song. Generally, popular songs are often those strongly promoted by the music companies; thus, the individual may still sample those song recommendations that are generated from the agent based on criteria other than his or her artist preferences. Another possibility is that online users were young people who liked web sur<sup>fi</sup>ng to explore interesting new things.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

This research makes two theoretical contributions. First, our work contributes to the web personalization literature by examining the “dark” side of web personalization—malfunctioning personalized services in the form of irrelevant and biased recommendations. The extant research has mainly examined how (good) personalized recommendations attract online users' attention, in<sup>fl</sup>uence their clickstream, and assist their decision making (e.g., [15,16,39,40]). The current research anticipates (and has empirically proven) that malfunctioning personalized services lead to online users' distrust in the personalization agent, with consequences that are more severe than a reduction of trust. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings align with those of Yin et al. [47], who proposed that online users were more sensitive to negative messages (in their case, negative online reviews) than to positive messages. Adding to Yin et al. [47], the current research introduced the concept of distrust to examine the negative consequences when online users encounter such “bad” things on the Internet. We conceptualized malfunctioning personalized services with irrelevance and bias of recommendations. The current research shows that both irrelevant and biased recommendations lead online users to

## 6.3. Practical implications

This research has two practical implications for practitioners. First, our results indicate the importance of minimizing (or even removing) the chance for a personalization agent to produce irrelevant and biased recommendations because they reduce an online user's interactions with the personalization agent (through the mediation of competence distrust). As the generation of irrelevant recommendations is likely to be caused by a lack of understanding of an online user's needs, merchants should invest more effort in enhancing the prediction accuracy of their personalization agents. For instance, they could collect more data from online users and/or make an investment in technologies such as data mining, text mining, click stream analysis, and customer relationship management applications to create more complete preference pro<sup>fi</sup>les to generate relevant recommendations. Although the investment can be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, it is justi<sup>fi</sup>able since these technologies eliminate the production of irrelevant recommendations that hinder online users' interactions with personalization agent, which is a kind of advertising revenue and relates to sales opportunities.

Compared with the problems that originate from irrelevant recommendations, the problems that originate from biased recommendations are more challenging for merchants. Merchants attempt to provide “biased” recommendations to online users because of their promotion strategies and/or for gaining a high pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin from the product vendors. They understand the cause of biases but may be reluctant to eliminate the biases. One possible way to resolve this issue is the method used by search engines like Google and Yahoo! of providing search results that are generated based on a user's search query in one region of the website, and their sponsors' recommendations in another region [36]. To borrow their idea for our web personalization context, we suggest that merchants could produce two lists—one containing personalized recommendations generated based on an online user's preferences, and the other labeled “sponsored recommendations” containing recommendations that are promotions from product vendors. This arrangement can strike a balance between minimizing the adverse effects of biased recommendations on online users' distrust in the competence and integrity of a personalization agent and maximizing merchants' opportunities to introduce high pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin promotion items to their customers.

Moreover, our results show the adverse effects of competence distrust on an online user's interactions with a personalization agent. As competence distrust reduces the interactions between users and the agent, merchants should be aware that users care about the competence of agents in generating useful, preference-matched recommendations. If online users perceive the personalization agent to lack competence in generating useful recommendations, they will avoid interacting with the agent. To minimize the adverse effects of competence distrust on users' interactions with the agent, merchants may consider soliciting feedback from online users who did not use their recommendations to ask why they did not sample the personalized recommendations. Such information would help merchants discover the problems of their personalization agent and their recommendation methods. In addition, this arrangement lets online users understand that the merchant cares about the quality of its services. This also helps reduce users' fear and worry about using (malfunctioning) personalization services.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

This research has three limitations. First, our experimental participants were university students. As they are frequent online shoppers and generally have reasonably strong knowledge in information technology, they may react to malfunctioning personalized services in a different way from those who rarely shop online and those with little knowledge information technology. Second, in our experimental context, we did not ask participants to use their own money to purchase the music tracks they chose. Thus, an incorrect decision costs nothing for the participants. Even though they did not suffer any monetary loss from selecting an irrelevant and/or biased recommendation, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings still demonstrate that irrelevance and bias of recommendation could lead to online users' distrust in the competence and integrity of a personalization agent. This means that the adverse effects of irrelevant and biased recommendations are salient. However, our experimental setting might not be able to induce the full fear and worry of the participants about the malfunctioning personalization services, and thus, they probably under-reacted toward the services. Hence, future research may consider using a setting in which there would be a higher stake involved in the participants' decisions and or behavior and examine how adverse the effects of distrust in the personalization agent can be. Third, to generate relevant vs. irrelevant recommendations, we used participants' artist preferences, as well as the publication dates and the popularity indices of the songs as the personalization criteria, because we assumed that young people liked new, popular songs. We do acknowledge that some young people may have “special” tastes. For instance, some may have been in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their parents, and so they prefer old to new songs. Future research may consider using an experiment setting with a product type that is less affected by fashions and trends to examine malfunctioning personalized services.

A possible future research topic in this area is to develop a study that links the rewards to be paid to the participants with the results of the experiment (for example, the participants need to use the tokens they earn in the experiments to purchase the products that the agent recommends, which is a popular method used in experimental economics). This arrangement simulates the (adverse) impact of a malfunctioning personalization agent on the purchase decision, which makes the participants reveal their real (distrusting) behavior when they need to make a purchase decision based on the irrelevant and/or biased recommendations generated from the agent. This arrangement could help us gain a better understanding of how distrust interacts with distrusting behaviors.

## 7. Conclusion

This research is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to examine the adverse effects of malfunctioning personalization services on online users' distrust in, and interactions with, a personalization agent. We investigated how the two types of malfunctioning personalized services – irrelevant recommendations and biased recommendations – lead to the formation of competence distrust and integrity distrust in the personalization agent. This research demonstrates that irrelevant and biased recommendations generated from a personalization agent increased online users' competence distrust and integrity distrust in the agent. Competence distrust, but not integrity distrust, in the personalization agent reduced online users' interactions with the agent. Our results provide IS researchers with a model that investigates how a malfunctioning system in<sup>fl</sup>uences a user's distrust, which in turn, in<sup>fl</sup>uences his or her behavior toward the system. Our research also provides IS researchers with a set of survey items that measures competence distrust and integrity distrust in a personalization agent. Our results offer practitioners with guidelines for eliminating irrelevant recommendations and incorporating biased recommendations into their personalized recommendations.
