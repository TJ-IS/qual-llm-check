## Abstract

We attempt to use general systems theory (GST) to understand why the resources of Texaco's corporate information technology function consistently did not match its task during its 40-year lifetime. Our interpretation uses mechanistic, organic, and colonial systems metaphors, each with three components. The first is an analysis of a management action system made up of organizational indicators such as Texaco's revenues, profits, employee numbers, IT budgets, and IT personnel numbers. The second is a narrative of performance versus resource needs, which shows a gap between the resources and expanding responsibilities of Texaco's IT function. The third is a management perception system, which offers reasons why top management continually misinterpreted IT's performance as inferior. Our results show that the mechanistic, organic, and colonial interpretations converge. In addition, our GST-based interpretations show how top management might have remedied the situation.

Keywords: Systems theory, mechanistic systems, organic systems, colonial systems, historical research, interpretive research, IT function success factors, IT function failure, longitudinal study, punctuated equilibrium, radical change, organizational change, organizational alignment

## Introduction

Chevron acquired the remnants of Texaco's corporate information technology function (Texaco IT or IT) in 2001. Top management had always looked down on the function. After rounds of outsourcing and downsizing, it was relegated to maintaining legacy systems and managing third-party contracts. Measured by its effectiveness, however, Texaco's IT function was a success. It helped the company eliminate over 50,000 employees. In this regard, its contributions were often measured in thousands of jobs eliminated per software application (Hodges 1999). $^{2}$

The story of Texaco's IT function is typical of many centralized IT organizations (Willcocks and Lacity 1998) that have lost their status and had their functions transferred to business units, third-party enterprise solutions, and outsourcing firms. This erosive process motivates many questions—for instance, what factors lead to the erosion, and what outcomes arise from the erosion (Carr 2003; Earl and Feeny 1994; Enns and Huff 2000; Harvard Business Review 2000).

We view the history of Texaco IT through three general systems theory (GST) metaphors: mechanistic, organic, and colonial. Each results in a distinct interpretation. Each metaphor includes three systems constructed from historical evidence. The first is a management action system (Macts), which models management actions as measured by public indicators such as revenues, profits, employee numbers, IT budget, and IT personnel numbers. The second is a Texaco IT performance versus resource needs narrative (PvsNn), which provides a narrative performance record for Texaco IT. The third is a management perception system (Mpers), which bridges the apparent disconnect between Texaco IT's performance and the lack of resources provided by top management.

Texaco IT failed because top management consistently misinterpreted its performance as poor. This conclusion holds across the mechanistic, organic, and colonial interpretations. The Macts, PvsNn, and Mpers systems, in turn, allow us to explain why an effective IT function was slowly dismantled.

## General Systems Theory as an Interpretive Lens ■

GST enables holistic study of history. It serves as a framework for understanding how the related management action systems (Mactses), IT function performance versus resource needs narratives (PvsNns), and management perception systems (Mperses) evolved in concert over the lifetime of Texaco IT.

GST allows for multiple, simultaneous paradigms (Porra 1999), each with its own system metaphors (Klir 1985). While mechanistic (see Gray and Rizzo 1973; Yates 1987) and organic system paradigms (Bailey 1994; Giddens 1984; Luhmann 1982a, 1982b; von Bertalanffy 1968; von Foerster 1960) are dominant, a colonial paradigm is emerging (Porra 1999). These paradigms capture three discontinuous perspectives of Texaco IT's past. A more reliable interpretation of Texaco IT's history emerges from the convergence of these paradigms than from a single paradigm.

GST provides methodological rigor (see Bailey 1994). System typologies, including the three system metaphors in this paper, are well documented (e.g., Bailey 1994; Boulding 1956; Kilr 1985; Miller 1978; Porra 1999). Thus, mechanistic, organic, and colonial systems act as historical-construct inventories (Bailey 1994) against which readers can judge research quality. These features of GST make it possible to model the disconnection between top management and Texaco's IT function.

GST offers hard and soft philosophical underpinnings (Checkland 1999). Hard GST, also known as systems engineering, is used to design practical systems for given objectives. Soft GST, also known as theoretical history, is used to study the past in terms of hypothetical (but logical) systems (von Bertalanffy 1968).

We found evidence to support all three metaphors. We used an aspect of GST that allows modeling by analogy where evidence fell short (Rosen 1974). We bridged evidence gaps by constructing hypothetical models of the past, relying on central mechanistic, organic, and colonial system characteristics.

## Searching for a Lens

Arriving at GST as an interpretive lens grew out of a 15-year search for an explanation for the failure of Texaco IT. Many lenses were explored. Only one, based on the productivity paradox (see Solow 2002), was rejected as inapplicable, because IT's value did not appear in Texaco's productivity statistics. The remaining lenses were applicable and showed Texaco IT both as a success and a failure. The conflicting lenses reveal that Texaco IT's failure was a complex phenomenon.

## Lenses Suggesting Texaco IT Was a Success

Several lenses suggested that Texaco IT was successful. One such lens was efficiency (see Strassmann 1985, 1990). Comparisons with peer organizations by the American Petroleum Institute (API) showed that Texaco IT was consistently among the industry's most efficient performers (McDonald 1996). Its personnel never exceeded 3 percent of the firm's personnel establishment, and its annual budget never exceeded 1.1 percent of the firm's revenue. The Gartner Group ranked Texaco IT ahead of oil companies such as SoCal, Gulf, Exxon, Shell, and Mobil in cost efficiency (Texaco 1983). It spent the least on computing power and had the lowest computer lease expenses.

Based on its billing rate, Texaco IT was efficient relative to the IT outsourcing firms Texaco used (firms such as EDS, AVG, GEISCO, and IBM). In batch-processing and interactive services, Texaco IT's rates were less than one-half of EDS's, one-eighth of AVG's, and about one-ninth of GEISCO's (Texaco 1988). In interactive computing, it charged less than one-half of EDS's, one-quarter of AVG's and Geisco's, and one-fifth of IBM's costs.

According to the IT relationship lens, IT should partner with business units (Earl and Feeny 2000; Feeny and Willcocks 1999; Peppard and Ward 1999; Venkatraman and Loh 1994). Joint projects were the norm at Texaco IT until the 1990s. Top management was involved in all major automation efforts (e.g., accounting functions and plant operations). Hodges, the IT leader with the longest tenure, confirmed the partnership's goal: "I was in the business of building Texaco, not in the business of building an IT department" (Hodges 1998).

The IT leadership lens suggests that successful IT functions should have nontechnical CIOs (Palmlund 1997; Romanczuk and Pemberton 1997). All five Texaco IT leaders (K. Ward Savage Jr., Les Hodges, Jim Metzger, Don Bennett, Les Amidei, and Rick Diaz) were nontechnical. Each was a graduate of Texaco's tradition of circulating managers through many functions.

Finally, the IT organizational-form lens states that IT should be distributed to the business units (Pastore 1997; Strassmann 1995) and outsourced (Lacity and Hirschheim 1993; Willcocks and Lacity 1998). Texaco IT was distributed to business units in 1989 and began outsourcing in 1990 (Pilgreen 1998).

## Lenses Suggesting Texaco IT Was a Failure

Other IT function research results suggested Texaco IT failed. For example, the IT alignment lens suggests that IT should align its strategy and structure with those of the firm (see Sabherwal et al. 2001). At Texaco, this alignment did not begin until the mid-1980s when Texaco's strategy changed to "low cost." Of the five strategic thrusts presented by Rackoff et al. (1985), Texaco IT's strategy was low cost throughout its lifetime, and its structure (Pilgreen 1999; Texaco 1988) was mechanistic throughout its lifetime (see Burns and Stalker 1961). The firm focused on producing a standard product. The roles of various business units remained clearly defined and relatively stable. IT had a clear role in the firm's hierarchy until 1989 when it was distributed to the business units. IT then shared its authority with business-unit managers. Since 1989, IT's structure has been misaligned with that of the firm. IT's strategy and structure were both aligned with those of the firm only for a short period from the mid-1980s through 1989.

An organizational evolution lens also suggests Texaco IT failed. According to this body of research, successful organizational units evolve independently (see Sabherwal et al. 2001; Tushman and Romanelli 1985). Dramatic environmental changes constantly repositioned Texaco. The subsequent changes in the firm contributed to the diminution of the IT function because it had no means of evolving separately from the greater firm (see Carroll and Delacroix 1982; Meyer et al. 1993).

A technology lens suggests that technology is inherently inflexible (Land 1982; Land and Somogyi 1986) because information systems are costly and financial estimates inaccurate. IT forecasts are particularly inaccurate during times of rapid technological, political, organizational, and economic change. Texaco IT failed because top management did not make allowances for IT during turbulent times.

Finally, a power lens yields an evolutionary account of why Texaco IT lost power negotiations with top management (Hirschheim et al. 2003). This account shows how a perception gap of IT's performance developed between IT and top management.

## Systems to Examine

The array of conflicting results from interpretive lenses seems to preclude a single conclusion.

Texaco IT was either a success or failure depending on the lens and the duration of observation. It was not possible to identify a chain of proximate factors that explained satisfactorily why the unit failed. GST provides a holistic perspective of Texaco IT's simultaneous success and failure because it allows interpretation of both visible and invisible factors through several discontinuous lenses. We identified three central systems for examination.

## Management Action Systems (Mactses)

The Texaco Mactses assume public organizational indicators are a direct result of management action. Thus, Mactses show Texaco management's output over time. $^{3}$ Mactses are considered real in the sense that organizational indicators are largely considered a representation of actual events.

Mactses, however, are also a matter of interpretation. We consider organizational indicators to be management's in situ interpretations of how they acted to produce specific outcomes. For example, Texaco's annual reports reflect management's interpretation of operational and accounting data affected by their actions. We produced Figure 1 from Texaco's annual reports $^{4}$ and Figure 2 from Texaco's annual reports and the five-year strategic business plans of Texaco IT, $^{5}$ the Getty IT merging plan, and IT reorganization studies.

## Performance Versus Resource Needs Narratives (PvsNns)

For the Texaco IT function's PvsNns, we extracted sections from a history of Texaco IT (Porra et al. 2006). Thus, the PvsNns are based on substantial evidence including the IT function's strategic and business plans, third-party statistics, press reports, memos, personal notes, research reports, interview transcripts, and a posteriori recollections at the industry, firm, and organizational levels. Because every narrative is an interpretation, we applied GST, which may have been outside the interviewees' horizons of meanings. This two-way connection between ordinary and scientific language is known as the double hermeneutic (Bleicher 1982). Although the original author has interpreted the evidence already, we interpreted it as an indication of Texaco IT being a real system adapting to management actions and environmental forces.

## Management Perception Systems (Mperses)

For Texaco Mperses, we created a model of perception systems that could have coevolved with the Mactses and that could shed light on the IT function's struggle. Mperses bridge the mechanistic, organic, and colonial interpretations of Mactses and the corresponding PvsNns.

GST has served as a lens for studying perception systems in the past. Perception systems are described as largely unconscious systems that leave behind little evidence (von Bertalanffy 1968). They are founded on experience, values, culture, and the environment. Perception systems are real in the sense that observers can describe them as serving a purpose (Checkland 1999). Measured by their effect, they are akin to hard systems. Perception systems mediate action systems, which eliminate a perceived real-world problem, but which may not conform to reality.

## System Alignment

Our mechanistic, organic, and colonial interpretations of Mactses, PvsNns, and Mperses are based on the premise that they should be aligned within each system metaphor. Thus, a mechanistic Macts, PvsNn, and Mpers should remain stable during mechanistic periods, change gradually during organic periods, and shift radically during colonial times. Change is persuasive when these radical shifts occur across several indicators within a single year.

The mechanistic, organic, and colonial change periods are clearly different. Observing them in Texaco's public figures provides a basis for interpreting Texaco Mactses. ## A Mechanistic Systems Interpretation

Mechanistic systems are isolated from their environment. They lack directiveness or telos (von Bertalanffy 1968). Only an outside force can change their structure. Thus, they have static teleology or fitness. Even though social systems are rarely mechanistic (Bailey 1994), system participants may interpret the world through mechanistic concepts. While their interpretation may be fluid, their mechanistic aspects operate toward an end according to the original blueprint.

Mechanistic interpretations are based on formal, unchanging rules (Ashby 1956). Action is focused on refining the rules without changing their premise. Thus, mechanistic systems engage in statistically predictable processes. The system's final state can be determined from the initial conditions (Checkland 1999).

## Mechanistic Management Actions (Macts)

Figure 3 suggests that Texaco never experienced a purely mechanistic period. Only two of the eight indicators, net income and IT user base, show relative stability from 1970 to 1983 and from 1970 to 1981. During this period, net income grew by \$0.4 billion to a total of \$1.2 billion (Figure 1), and the IT user base grew by less than 1,000 users (Figure 2). Similarly, IT personnel remained relatively stable until 1978, diminishing in size from 700 to 658. Judging from the growing IT budget and the stagnant IT personnel, top management grew IT's resources in proportion with the function's responsibilities only in areas outside of personnel.

Top management may have missed signals of growing IT needs, because IT's impact is delayed (see Dedrick et al. 2003). Texaco started eliminating personnel, including IT staff, in 1984 (Figure 2), 15 years after the IT function was founded. Whatever caused the postponement, the early stability in IT's size turned into a blueprint for later decisions. IT grew slowly, with personnel increasing only from 700 in 1970 to 880 in 1981 (Figure 2). The all-time high of 1,200 IT employees was merely the temporary result of acquiring Getty's IT staff. Texaco IT swiftly consolidated the two functions (Texaco 1988) and started a slow decline to 510 employees in 1999 (Figure 2).

During the 1980s, Texaco IT's user-base grew from about 1,000 to approximately 14,000 (Figure 2). Over the decade, the function was given \$32 million and 230 additional employees to deal with the new users (Figure 2). Within the next decade, IT helped 6,000 more users, lost \$2 million from its budget, and shrunk by 500 employees (Figure 2).

The presence of just one stable period across only two indicators, net income and IT user base, suggests that top management has primarily interpreted organizational change through other lenses since the early 1980s. That top management interpreted personnel needs at the overall firm differently from IT personnel needs suggests IT staffing was considered differently. The stable period in net income indicates a top-management strategy portraying a stable financial picture for the shareholders.

## A Mechanistic IT Performance Versus Resource Needs Narrative (PvsNn)

The automation effort at Texaco in the 1980s was massive and international in scope. It consisted of three automation waves: plant and office consolidation in the 1970s, financial accounting from exploration to retail operations, and the desktop revolution (Hodges 1999). Texaco management was fully involved in all three waves. Each new wave called for new technologies, which in turn increased Texaco IT's resource needs.

IT communicated its resource needs to top management in detailed plans, reports, memos, and personal conferences. IT leaders believed close contact with top management was key to the function's success (Pilgreen 1999). They reported to senior vice presidents until 1987. This reporting relationship gave access to top executives on a first-name basis (Hodges 1998).

Unfortunately, personal contact and extensive communication did not save IT from increasingly difficult resource shortage. The early stages of the charge-back systems were implemented by 1979 (Hodges 1999). These additional usage-based overhead charges to the large user departments allowed IT to justify its expenses (Porra et al. 2006).

The biggest pressure I felt was in mid '80s. McKinsey and Company did an added value analysis of the corporation. They'd say, "We can eliminate this and this. Don't ask for extra people." They had good recommendations, but 70 percent of the benefits derived from that study took IT over two years to do. It was a transfer of work from the business units to IT. We reduced their expenses, but IT's expenses were going up! I felt like a 40-year-old man being circumcised! The pain of it was terrible. (Hodges 1999)

Top management's plan did not realize that IT facilitated the cost cutting.

We had 150 accounting offices around the U.S. People took orders to get product. Each office had 20 to 40 people in it. A service station could call in an order. They would then call the trucker, who would get the product, bring it to the service station and prepare the invoice manually. And all that cost was operating expenses. It was called “transportation costs.” I automated all that. I put in five computers around the country. Now I have 10 computer-controlled terminals. Tandem machines operate 24/7. I had to put a network to every service station. It took six, seven years as big as we were. I was in a meeting where a vice president was talking to the chairman: “Man, I have cut my expenses by \$50 million. I saved 5,000 people, but this overhead is eating me up.” We did the same function, but now it was called overhead. I think American businesses have had a bad eye about overhead being so high. One of my theories about overhead is that we’ve transferred work to it. (Hodges 1999)

As a result of McKinsey's added value analysis (AVA) in 1985-86, business units effectively outsourced their functions to IT. The information necessary for calculating IT's share in this partnership was available. Hodges provided annual memos to business-unit leaders including a detailed account of IT's quarterly contributions to business unit performance in terms of cost savings from completed applications. Nonetheless, "by 1986 business units openly questioned the size of the 'overhead' that IT was on their budgets" (Porra et al. 2006). Due to the charge-back systems, the business unit accounting proxies reflected the cost of using the IT department. No mechanisms existed to attribute benefits back to the function that created them.

The mechanistic Texaco IT PvsNn suggests IT was top management's essential partner. It shows that IT leaders clearly communicated their contributions and resource needs to top management. In contrast, the narrative shows that top management ignored the partnership. We created a mechanistic Mpers to explain why.

## Mechanistic Management Perceptions (Mpers)

For modeling mechanistic management perceptions (Mpers), we identified mechanistic system concepts in evidence using a template (Table 2) created from the central mechanistic ideas discussed above. Six signs were recorded.

## Signs of a Mpers Isolated from its Environment

The fact that management ignored IT's contributions and resource needs signals a Mpers isolated from the reality of the function. Even though top management was fully aware of the costs and benefits of automating the firm, their resource allocations consistently failed to reflect this awareness.

## Signs of a Mpers with No Telos

IT could have assumed more responsibility when it was distributed to the Texaco business units in 1989. It had accumulated three decades of knowledge about business processes. IT had learned how to support oil exploration, drilling, production, distribution, and marketing during the three waves. However, the reorganization only erased IT's local control and officially transferred ownership of the function's contributions to the business units. IT's role remained unchanged (Pilgreen 1998). Business units were in charge of what, when, and where and IT of how (Texaco 1988).

Because mechanistic interpretations do not recognize holism, top management could rank corporate units by perceived importance. At the bottom were functions like IT that owned their labor but not their product. Its cumulative contributions to the firm's performance remained beyond mechanistic perceptual horizons. From this standpoint, IT leaders' principle about being "in the business of building Texaco, not an IT department" (Hodges 1998) was a mistake. In a mechanistic perception environment, organizational units seek top-management recognition of local success.

## Signs of a Mpers in Need of Change by an Outside Force

The massive reorganization of Texaco in the 1980s was a sign of a Mpers in need of change by an outside force. According to Business Week and The Wall Street Journal, management missed signs of the firm's inefficiency and ineffectiveness (Sullivan 1991; Vogel and Bremner 1991). The firm was ranked last of the eight major oil companies in oil production. Texaco had also failed to take advantage of advances in information technology (Vogel and Bremner 1991). Even though IT reported savings per project to business-unit leaders annually, the leaders did not realize these savings until shareholders forced the firm to restructure.

## Signs of a Mpers Useful for a Purpose

Top management's resistance to recognizing the contributions of IT signals a Mpers that is "useful for a purpose." Every new IT contribution increased business unit efficiency. The fixed mechanistic perceptions of IT thus showed increasingly successful business units.

## Signs of Formal Rules of Control Supporting a Mpers

Texaco's formal accounting standards supported the practice of reporting IT-enabled performance improvement in the context of individual business units and left IT to be measured solely by perception. Until the history of Texaco IT was written (Porra et al. 2006), no official record of IT's performance existed.

## Signs of a Mpers Including Unidirectional Causal Trains

IT's exclusion from formal accounting effectively resulted in unidirectional causal processes that made its contributions to business-unit performance seem increasingly less important. Business units had less need for IT's day-to-day labor with every new automated process.

## An Organic Systems Interpretation

Organic systems are open systems that use feedback to maintain a balance with their surroundings (von Bertalanffy 1968). This homeostasis is a basis for teleology or purposive behavior (Wiener 1948) allowing organic systems to adapt to environmental change through fixed arrangements (von Bertalanffy 1968). Because of these arrangements, organic systems pass from undifferentiated wholeness to differentiation of parts. They grow inflexible over time. This equi-potentiality is the basis of a lifecycle. In organic terms, progress means incremental change toward the end of the system (von Bertalanffy 1968).

Organic systems are also emergent. They cannot be explained by analyzing parts. Because the complex is new at each level of analysis (von Bertalanffy 1968), the behavior of each part seems different when analyzed in isolation. For example, a management action system cannot be constructed by studying the behavior of an individual manager. Studying the behavior of many managers over time, however, can contribute to a holistic understanding of a management perception system.

In the following sections, we interpret an organic Macts using Figure 3. We then contrast this interpretation with the organic Texaco PvsNn and produce an interpretation of organic Texaco Mpers based on the organic Macts and PvsNn.

## Organic Management Actions (Macts)

Texaco experienced two organic periods across a total of five indicators. The first took place in the 1970s. By 1979, Texaco's personnel numbers had fallen from 74,000 to 66,000. By 1981, the firm's revenues, assets, and IT budgets increased organically from \$6.5 billion to \$59.4 billion, \$9.4 billion to \$27.5 billion, and \$70 million to \$92 million, respectively. The second organic era took place in the 1990s. Between 1988 and 1997, IT's personnel numbers fell from 1,050 to 440. The firm's overall personnel also fell from 40,000 to 29,000 between 1991 and 1995.

The organic lens at Texaco was clearly more pervasive than the mechanistic one. Top management perceived organic change when they controlled Texaco's personnel, revenues, assets, and IT budgets and personnel. Management's organic actions sought to gradually increase or decrease these resources according to a multi-year strategic plan.

Two factors suggest why top management consistently increased IT's budgets throughout the 1970s. First, the IT function was new, and few college-educated information systems professionals existed (Hirschheim et al. 2003). The educational and employment background of the IT staff remained similar to that of business-unit personnel throughout the decade (Hodges 1999). Top management and IT shared an understanding of the benefits and costs of information systems (Porra et al. 2006).

Second, IT's first general manager, Savage, came from top management. He reported directly to the chairman of the board and so had direct access to resources (Hirschheim et al. 2003). Savage's legacy apparently helped the second general manager, Hodges, who still enjoyed a close relationship with top management but, like future IT leaders, reported to a senior vice president instead.

During the second organic period in the 1990s, the rate of IT personnel cuts exceeded those of the overall firm by 25.6 percent. While the overall personnel diminished by 32.6 percent, IT staff was cut by 58.1 percent during the same period. Significant cuts in the IT staff can be attributed to Bennett, the IT leader of the time, who was believed to have a mandate from top management to cut IT consistently with overall cuts in the firm (Pilgreen 1999).

Organic management actions occur across several organizational indicators simultaneously. Therefore, it is likely that Texaco management interpreted organizational change through an organic lens. Organic top management IT resource considerations were rare. IT budgets grew during the first organic era because top management was directly involved in IT leadership. IT staff was never a target of positive organic growth considerations by top management. This suggests management believed that advanced technologies compensated for human power in IT.

## An Organic IT Function Performance Versus Resource Needs Narrative (PvsNn)

IT's reports were often candid about its challenges and prospects. The 1983 IT strategic plan states, "One of our major weaknesses has been in extending the power of the computer and access to data to the individual" (Texaco 1983, p. 4). The 1988 IT business plan predicted, "The next three to five years will be a period of great change and upheaval" (Texaco 1988, p. 2). IT's reports mostly consisted of technical and financial facts associated with information system projects. This style of reporting stands in contrast to management portrayals of information system successes of the firm overall.

Several Texaco annual reports made reference to an information system backing business unit success. Nonetheless, even though IT assisted business units with information systems, annual reports mentioned technologies but not the function itself (Table 3). Top management instead assigned credit for implementing information systems to the unit the system was designed to assist. Thus, IT had no public presence outside its own publications. Being frank about its circumstances in the unit's central historical record may have hurt IT's reputation in the eyes of top management. Differences in reporting style do not, however, explain why IT was excluded categorically from annual reports. We created an organic model of Mpers to understand why the function was not included in the public Texaco success story.

## A Colonial Systems Interpretation

Colonial systems are based on mobile animal colonies:

A colony is a voluntary collection of individuals with the shared characteristics of a common evolutionary social history (phylogeny); common methods for realizing stability and radical change (species-level evolution); and a common local context (Porra 1999, p. 39).

The collective identity or “humanness” (Porra 1999) $^{6}$ of the colony is a result of members spending long time periods together and sharing what they learn about the colony with others. Colonial systems are recognized at the social level of analysis.

Although colonies have unique histories due to the circumstances of their evolutionary pasts, they share an evolutionary pattern, which conforms to Eldredge and Gould's (1972) punctuated equilibrium model. This model describes alternating stages of stasis and punctuations. Punctuations break up stasis and occur when a colony radically transforms to survive an environmental shift (Porra 1999).

Punctuations may lead to perpetual instability and death (Porra 1999). In such a case, the colony's evolutionary pattern portrays random oscillation between periods of prosperity and struggle until the colony dies. Colonies become extinct suddenly, but not necessarily uniformly (Porra 1999). Ailing colonies may contain offspring in the form of subcolonies, each with its own distinct identity. Each subcolony embodies its own professional knowledge, customs, and values that may outlive the parent colony.

Subcolonies initially form when groups become separated from the parent colony. They develop in isolation according to their own needs, effectively creating prototypes of a new generation that may be more fit for the changing environment. This mechanism is called punctuated prototyping (Porra 1999).

In the following sections, we interpret a colonial Macts using Figure 3 and contrast it with the colonial Texaco PvsNn. We then produce an interpretation of colonial Texaco Mpers based on the colonial Macts and PvsNn.

## Colonial Management Actions (Macts)

Texaco experienced five colonial eras, which appear as five punctuations across seven organizational indicators. The first two punctuations were oil crises: the 1973-1975 oil embargo in the United States, and the 1978-1980 doubling of OPEC oil prices following the Iran-Iraq war. The third punctuation was due to a computer user base explosion between 1981 and 1985. The fourth occurred because of the Getty acquisition in 1983 and $1984^{7}$ and the fifth because of the subsequent bankruptcy between 1986 and 1988.

During the first colonial era, Texaco's revenues and assets grew from \$11.8 billion to \$25.1 billion and from \$13 billion to \$17.9 billion (Figure 1). During the second colonial period, revenues and assets grew from \$29.1 billion to \$50.8 billion and from \$20.2 billion to \$26.4 billion. During the third colonial era, the IT user base grew from more than 1,000 to 9,000 (Figure 2). During the fourth colonial period, several organizational indicators also increased. Texaco's total assets grew from \$27.2 billion to \$37.7 billion, personnel from 55,000 to 68,000, IT employees from 920 to 1,200, and IT budgets from \$117 million to \$136 million (Figures 1 and 2). During the last colonial era, Texaco's net income and IT budgets (Figures 1 and 2) fell sharply from \$0.7 billion to negative \$4.4 billion and from \$159 million to \$125 million, respectively. A characteristic of this punctuation is that Texaco's net income swung back to positive \$1.3 billion during the last year of the shift.

Two of the four forces behind punctuations were exogenous. Figure 3 shows how Texaco revenues and assets directly reflected the sudden increase in oil price from the oil crisis. Figure 3 also shows the effect on the IT user base of the suddenly increased availability of personal computers and intelligent terminals. Texaco initiated the last two punctuations itself. First, top management sought to acquire Getty. Second, this aggressive move resulted in bankruptcy. Even though the last two punctuations can be attributed to a shortage of oil reserves, no environmental force calling for radical action can be identified. The evidence of five colonial periods over seven organizational indicators suggests that Texaco's top management usually saw change as radical. The five punctuations were followed by a decade of oscillation in revenues, assets, net income, and IT budgets. This indicates that management did not consider that the firm might have difficulty stabilizing after radical actions. Moreover, top management missed the impact of the desktop revolution. This indicates that information technology changes were not monitored properly.

## A Colonial IT Function Performance Versus Resource Needs Narrative (PvsNn)

The first two punctuations—the oil crises—did not directly affect the IT function. It maintained personnel and budget stability. $^{8}$ The third punctuation—the computer user base explosion—was the first environmental shift directly targeting Texaco IT. Hodges, the IT leader at the time, was optimistic that end-users would relieve IT's workload taking care of most programming tasks using easy-to-use applications and fourth-generation languages. He believed IT could focus on the development of complex, large-scale transaction processing systems while users took care of their own information needs (Porra et al. 2006).

Hodges was wrong. Putting PCs on users' desks actually generated more work for the department as measured by increasing end-user contact hours (Texaco 1984). For example, IT delivered more than 50,000 training hours to nearly 85 percent of the firm's personnel in 1982. This alone increased each IT employee's workload by an average of 55 hours for the year. Interactive computing was the primary environmental force propelling the department into an untenable position of exploding user demands.

The last two punctuations—the Getty acquisition and subsequent bankruptcy—were the first environmental shifts clearly affecting both Texaco and IT. Texaco IT was charged with absorbing Getty IT's functions and personnel and helped consolidate the information systems of both firms. A total of 8,000 jobs were eliminated from Getty, but Texaco IT grew from 910 in 1982 to 1,050 by 1986 (Figure 3). That number began to decline, however, following the bankruptcy, and the degree of automation was forced to increase.

The Texaco IT PvsNn shows that the last three punctuations increased IT's workload. After 1980, the unit provided automation for all radical shifts affecting the firm. The narrative also suggests that top management expected IT to deliver the same results under an increasingly difficult resource shortage. We created a colonial model of a Mpers to explain why management did not acknowledge that radical action requires matching resources for the supporting infrastructure.

## Signs of a Mpers that Indicate an IT Colony

Top management may have seen IT as a colony because the unit had a professional identity since the late 1970s, when the first college graduates were trained in the field (Hirschheim et al. 2003). Moreover, a group of individuals at Texaco embody the history of IT's distinct identity. This group of veteran employees exhibited a self-assumed responsibility to store historical evidence and spread the IT story.

The IT colony included Sam Baty, Les Hodges, Ed McDonald, and Carolyn Pilgreen, who lent their historical artifacts and stories for this study. While Hodges retired and Baty left, most moved to Chevron after the acquisition. The fact that the offices of the core individuals remained in close proximity at Texaco for years supports our conclusion. IT survived because it had its own identity to preserve and the close contact necessary for sharing it.

CEO Kinnear's 1988 total quality management initiative shows how the colony used its past. Most IT employees refused to participate because “it was one more thing to do” in an already difficult labor shortage (Pilgreen 1998). Because stories like this were transmitted through the unit’s oral traditions, friendly disobedience became a template for behavior for years to come.

## Signs of a Mpers that Indicate a Distinct IT Identity

When Amidei became the new general manager of IT in 1996, the unit repeated its defiant behavior: "During [Amidei's] 18 months he introduced a matrix organization to replace the traditional hierarchy. Although the process included many good ideas, IT received it with cool resignation" (Pilgreen 1999). The IT colony rejected Amidei, and he had practically no impact on the function. The unit returned to its former hierarchy after his departure (Pilgreen 1999).

The history of the function's name changes also reflects IT's resistance to imposed change. The department's official mission changed several times. It was named the Computer Services Department in 1967, the Computer and Information Services Department in 1979, IT Department in 1987, and has been Global Information Services since 1997. Judging by IT's reports, the name changes mirrored popular trends and failed to describe its actual tasks (see Texaco 1983, 1984, 1987, 1988, 1993). Under all of its titles, IT continued to market its labor; develop, maintain, and operate information systems; and train users as if its purposes were internally driven.

## Signs of a Mpers Indicating that IT had a Distinct Evolutionary History

Texaco IT's distinct evolutionary history became apparent during the desktop punctuation. Top management missed the most significant environmental force targeting IT in the unit's history. Management did not realize that end-user numbers grew from about 1000 to 10,000 (Figure 2) in the first five years of personal computing. Management did not give IT the means to fulfill its responsibilities. The IT subcolony felt ignored by its parent and morale was “at its lowest ever” (Pilgreen 1999).

## Signs of a Mpers Indicating That IT Relied on Punctuated Prototyping

The mandate of putting a computer on every desk without the necessary support to achieve that goal turned IT's parent colony into an inhospitable environment. IT, however, had begun to apply punctuated prototyping much earlier. It had established boundaries since its beginning in 1967 and immediately set off on its own direction (Hirschheim et al. 2003). IT's isolation from its parent became apparent before 1978 as top management started ignoring IT's resource requests. The final step of IT colony members leaving Texaco was interrupted by the 2001 Chevron acquisition.

According to the organic interpretation, Texaco's top management interpreted organizational change as organic on several occasions. The consequent Macts resulted in two organic periods across five organizational indicators. Top management gradually increased Texaco's personnel, revenues, assets, and IT budgets. Macts in the late 1980s led to the reduction of IT personnel and to the reduction of all personnel in the 1990s. While top management was comfortable with an organic lens, IT was not a main target of their actions. The organic IT PvsNn confirms that top management routinely interpreted business unit performance through an organic lens; annual reports portrayed the firm as steadily progressing. The reports also confirm that the IT function was consistently excluded.

Finally, the colonial Macts interpretation shows that Texaco was comfortable with interpreting change through a colonial lens. On occasion, top management made radical moves. The Getty acquisition in the early 1980s shifted Texaco's total assets, personnel, and IT employees and budgets. The subsequent bankruptcy later influenced net income and IT budgets. The colonial interpretation also shows that top management did not necessarily use the same lens to scan for significant environmental forces affecting IT prospects. The colonial IT function PvsNn confirms that the lack of top-management support during the desktop revolution resulted in a resource squeeze for IT.

The mechanistic, organic, and colonial interpretations of Mactses and IT PvsNns all converge to confirm Texaco IT's failure. However, each interpretation shows both distinct causes for the failure and a consistent disconnection between top management and IT. Our constructions of management perceptions suggest some reasons behind this disconnect.

According to the mechanistic interpretation of a Mpers, IT was a utility and a commodity. From management's perspective, trying to get more out of IT for less showed effective management. The organic Mpers interpretation echoes this finding. As a utility, IT was not a significant participant in the firm's organic growth objectives, nor was it important enough to be part of the firm's progressive public image. The colonial Mpers also best describes how IT evolved away from the overall firm. It grew boundaries and developed its own identity, history, professional knowledge, organizational preferences, and oral tradition. The unit established and maintained its own purposes and practices and communicated its own standards of success to top management.

In summary, Texaco's top management seemed to favor different lenses for different purposes. A mechanistic lens was for IT management, an organic lens was for Texaco's public image, and a colonial lens was for organizational transformation. Top management seemed to shift among lenses according to the purpose of the interpretation—not necessarily according to environmental cues.

All three management perception systems suggest that Texaco's top management was unfamiliar with IT. It did not recognize IT's value to the organization, its identity, performance, needs, or related environmental forces. In this context, perceptions became more influential than performance. Thus, from the mechanistic perspective IT was an inferior provider; from the organic perspective, IT had consistent performance growth problems; from a colonial perspective, IT was an alien entity. Top management could justify ignoring IT's performance and resource needs by selectively retreating into the most convenient perspective. This practice explains why top management preferred making decisions based on user complaints, inadequate accounting procedures, and external consultants and ignoring information provided by IT.

## Limitations

GST allows constructing models of the past, but historical models are uncertain. System metaphors can only provide hypothetical stories and cannot indicate probability. A limitation of any historical interpretation is that it is removed from the actual events.

Moreover, our interpretation of the history of Texaco's IT function has three levels and becomes more uncertain at each. Actors made first-level interpretations at the time of the events. We inferred second-level interpretations of management actions from organizational indicators. We also based the second-level Texaco IT narrative on historical evidence. Management perception systems are third-level interpretations. They model the kinds of perceptions that could explain the disconnection between top management and IT. Our results are based on these portrayals rather than actual Texaco management perceptions.

Our interpretation suggests that Texaco's top management used mechanistic, organic, and colonial lenses to interpret change and build templates for their actions. Our interpretation is partial because other lenses exist in GST and elsewhere. A related limitation is that we did not interview Texaco's top management for verification of our interpretation.

Historical evidence has data vacuums and system concepts carry over these vacuums with uncertainty. They effectively allow us to bridge actions, events, and perceptions with logically fitting passages that cannot be traced.

Finally, it is unclear how well public corporate data represents management action. In light of continuously changing financial accounting practices, organizational indicators may lack reliability.

## Conclusions

We have interpreted 40 years of Texaco's IT function to show that top management perceptions of IT performance may not accurately reflect how the unit actually performed. It is essential to continue similar long-term interpretations. Our interpretation calls for a recognition of the nature of phylogenetic change in technology. This may be a difficult paradigmatic shift, because only limited recognition of long-term-change effects and their causes exists, even within the IT profession. Only a few longitudinal studies exist (e.g., McKenney et al. 1997) with the necessary historical perspective to examine phylogenetic issues such as we have studied here. A clearer historical research agenda might help large firms recognize the value of their IT functions.

We have also shown that GST can be a useful interpretive lens. It offers several distinct perspectives on the past, provides tools and rigor for interpreting regular and pervasive change, and allows modeling phenomena invisible to many other lenses. As a result, GST can complement research in areas such as IT function success and failure, where previous research results provide a conflicting backdrop for new research.

We sought to identify some ultimate causes for Texaco IT's failure. We found that it failed because top management consistently misinterpreted the unit's performance. These perceptions led top management to reduce the unit's resources. Texaco IT's consistent failure to convince top management of its needs and contributions indicates several areas for improvement.

First, there is an essential need to develop formal IT function standards. Some progress has already been made in this area. Recent accounting procedural changes help firms capitalize on internal information system development costs, providing alternatives for the overhead rationale (AICPA 1998). Improvements have also been made in the relationship between IT and top management. Methods of forming strategic alliances seem particularly promising in this area (see Benko and McFarland 2003).

Second, IT leaders should familiarize themselves with top-management perceptions of their performance and resource needs. They should learn about the lenses being used and about the consistency of the resulting perceptions. Equally importantly, however, IT leaders should be aware of their own interpretive lenses and how their own perceptions of IT differ from those of top management.

Third, IT leaders should independently scan the environment for influential forces. This scanning should include forces that affect both IT and the overall firm. Reevaluating IT's purpose, status, and resources during environmental shifts may help realign the function with the firm in the post-punctuation environment.

Many areas for future research remain. These include replicating this study in other IT organizations that succeeded (or failed) during the desktop revolution, expanding this research to include Texaco's top management's perceptions of IT, and replicating the study in firms with different views on the success of their IT unit.

We have also argued for historical research and for the use of GST as an interpretive lens. To this end, we interpreted the Texaco IT case to explain its failure in the context of the unit's lifetime. We conclude that long-term success of IT is largely a matter of IT leaders understanding and influencing top management perceptions of IT's performance.

## About the Authors

Jaana Porra is an assistant professor at the Department of Decision and Information Sciences in the C. T. Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. She is currently a Bauer Faculty Fellow in recognition of her research. She has previously served on the faculties of the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Southern California, and the University of Texas at Austin. She has published in journals such as Information Systems Research, Journal of the AIS, Communications of the ACM, Information Systems Frontiers, Decision Support Systems, and Database. Jaana has 20 years of work experience in the IT field and with firms such as Volvo, Nokia, The Gap, Bank of America, McKesson, and Texaco. Her current research interests include systems theory as an interpretive lens, colonial systems, CIO and IT function success, and historical research.

Rudy Hirschheim is the Ourso Family Distinguished Professor of Information Systems in the Information Systems and Decision Sciences Department of Louisiana State University. He previously was the Tenneco/Chase International Professor of Information Systems at the University of Houston. He has held visiting professorships at Monash University (Melbourne, Australia), Curtin University (Perth, Australia), University of New South Wales (Sydney, Australia), and the University of Bayreuth, (Bayreuth, Germany). Rudy has been on the faculties of Templeton College, Oxford, and the London School of Economics. He has also worked as a senior consultant with the National Computing Centre in Manchester, England. He is co-consulting editor of the John Wiley Series in Information Systems. He is on the editorial boards of Information and Organization, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Journal of the AIS. Rudy is past vice president of publications for the Association for Information Systems.

Michael S. Parks is an associate professor of Decision and Information Sciences in the C. T. Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. He has published in journals such as Management Science, Journal of the AIS, Operations Research Quarterly, International Journal of Production Research, Kybernetes, Cybernetica, and Journal of MIS. His current areas of interest include client-server methodologies and 3D graphics.
