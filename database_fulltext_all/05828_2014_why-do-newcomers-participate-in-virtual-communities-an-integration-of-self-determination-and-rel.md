# Why do newcomers participate in virtual communities? An integration of self-determination and relationship management theories

Hsien-Tung Tsai <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Peiyu Pai <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Taipei University, Taiwan

<sup>b</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Chengchi University, Taiwan

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 3 October 2012 Received in revised form 31 July 2013 Accepted 4 September 2013 Available online 13 September 2013

Keywords: Psychological needs Participation behavior Relationship satisfaction Social identity Virtual community

## a b s t r a c t

This study proposes and tests a model that outlines the antecedents of newcomers' participation behavior in virtual communities and incorporates both mediating and moderating effects. According to self-reported data from two periods and objective behavioral data, the combined ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of needs for autonomy, relatedness, and competence determines newcomers' cognitive social identity, which then in<sup>fl</sup>uences participation behavior through affective commitment and collective self-esteem. Of the three needs, autonomy exerts the strongest effect on cognitive social identity. Moreover, relationship satisfaction moderates the relationship between awareness of group membership and affective commitment, which helps explain why newcomers with high awareness of their group membership might not cohere with a particular social group. Overall , by combining self-determination theory with relationship management theories, this study establishes an explanatory platform for newcomers' participation behavior. The authors conclude with a discussion of the managerial and research implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 1. Introduction

The emergence of new information and communication technologies has initiated radical transformations in social interactions, which in turn have important implications for the formation of virtual communities [58,59]. To many commentators, virtual communities hold great promise as management tools, because they can offer valuable insights into product innovation [45], facilitate deep and enduring bonds with consumers [11], and reduce customer service costs by enabling peerto-peer problem solving [49,59]. Moreover, virtual communities re<sup>fl</sup>ect the increasingly popular “consumer empowerment” movement, which encourages companies to view their customers as proactive cocreators rather than as passive audiences, and co-opt customer competencies for value creation [12,49].

Recent studies of virtual communities provide some support for this promise (e.g., [34,45,59]), but extant theoretical or empirical research does not offer a detailed understanding of why newcomers to a virtual community might contribute time and information for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of other members. This issue is important, because the long-term viability of virtual communities requires not only discretionary efforts by existing members to enact positive community development but also newcomers' voluntary contributions [50,59]. Indeed, according to virtual community research, newcomers' participation can introduce new knowledge, perspectives, and energy, and thereby cultivate a more vibrant, self-sustaining virtual community [50].

Among the theories usually employed to explain member participation behavior in virtual groups, the social capital framework [20,44,58,59] offers unique variables and processes that are relevant to certain aspects of social behavior. Yet social capital-based factors cannot fully explain member participation behavior. For example, as a core construct of social capital theory, reciprocity offers only limited explanations for why members proactively participate in virtual communities [58,59]; this is especially true in the case of newcomers' participation, as these recent members have had only limited interactions with other members. Most social capital-based mechanisms similarly require extended periods of time, or at least repeated interactions, to exert an effect. In addition, existing virtual community studies focus primarily on identifying factors associated with member participation (e.g., [20,58]); little research investigates mediating processes or moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uences on newcomers' participation decisions. As such, the present study seeks to advance our understanding of newcomer behavior in virtual communities by proposing and testing a model that outlines the antecedents of newcomers' participation behavior and incorporates mediating and moderating mechanisms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we attempt to contribute to virtual community literature in three ways.

First, drawing on self-determination theory [25,52] and social identity theory [15,55], we offer viable explanations for some variance in newcomer participation behavior. Self-determination theory explicates the nature of member–group relationships on the basis of three psychological needs: autonomy, relatedness, and competence. To the extent that newcomers experience ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of these needs through participation processes, they become more likely to adopt the values of the social group [26,52]. This internalization process creates a closer psychological attachment to the community and motivates newcomers to help achieve the community's goals. Their decision to participate proactively in the virtual community in turn re<sup>fl</sup>ects a manifestation of their voluntary effort to achieve these goals [1,55]. Second, we add to the virtual community literature by investigating mediating mechanisms that underlie the relationship between newcomers' psychological needs and participation behavior. This explicit focus on mediating mechanisms better speci<sup>fi</sup>es the theoretical relationships among the independent and dependent variables and helps clarify how the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of essential psychological needs in<sup>fl</sup>uences newcomers' participation behavior [10]. Third, we investigate the moderating effects of relationship satisfaction on participation decisions and, which helps to explain prior research <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicating that not all members who are aware of their group membership cohere with the social group [30,50].

We structure the remainder of this paper as follows: In the next section, we present our conceptual framework and research hypotheses. We then describe our research methodology and present the empirical evidence, after which we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and managerial implications. We conclude by indicating some limitations of our research and future research avenues.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

We present our research framework in Fig. 1. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we begin with the antecedents of awareness of group membership: needs for autonomy, relatedness, and competence. Awareness of group membership should produce two forms of social identity, affective and evaluative, that prompt newcomers' participation intentions. We also consider how relationship satisfaction moderates the links from cognitive social identity to affective commitment and collective selfesteem.

## 2.1. Social identity

Social identity is “that part of an individual's self-concept which derives from his knowledge of his membership of a social group (or groups) together with the value and emotional signi<sup>fi</sup>cance attached to that membership” [54, p. 63]. Several studies from different <sup>fi</sup>elds suggest that social identity consists of three related but distinct components: awareness of group membership, affective commitment to the group, and evaluative signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of group membership [9,15,29]. Awareness of group membership is a cognitive sense of the self as a representative of a particular social entity, such as the virtual community in our study. This perception develops through cognitive categorization processes, in which the person forms self-categories of community membership and similarities to other members, as well as dissimilarities to nonmembers [1,9]. These classi<sup>fi</sup>cations re<sup>fl</sup>ect the notion of consciousness-of-kind for social groups [4]. That is, self-awareness or self-knowledge of belonging to a virtual community is one way that a member can achieve a social identity.

Affective social identity can be characterized as affective commitment to the focal group (e.g., [15,29]). In organizational settings, affective social identity represents “a partisan, affective attachment to the goals and values of an organization, to one's role in relation to the goals and values, and to the organization for its own sake, apart from its purely instrumental worth” [18, p. 533]. In brand communities, researchers have characterized this idea as “kinship between members” which also encourages relationships between consumers and brands [4].

The evaluative component of social identity refers to the positive or negative value connotation that a member attaches to group membership; it arises from evaluations of self-worth that might be derived from membership [29,48]. Other terms that refer to the same aspect of social identity include collective self-esteem [40] and group-based self-esteem [15]. These forms differ from global self-esteem, in that they are situation speci<sup>fi</sup>c and thus responsive to proximal factors [15,48]. Group-based self-esteem re<sup>fl</sup>ects a person's perception of personal value because of his or her role as an active member of a virtual community. Members with higher group-based self-esteem thus should perceive themselves as more important, meaningful, and worthwhile within their respective communities [47]. Because the three components of social identity differ in nature and have unique antecedents and consequences [15,29], we treat them as three distinct constructs rather than components of a higher-order latent variable.

## 2.2. The impact of psychological need fulfillment on cognitive social identity

Self-determination theory primarily serves to explicate the psychological processes that facilitate social relationship development [25,52]. From an organismic perspective, this theory assumes that humans are active agents who engage their surroundings in an attempt not only to master and assimilate aspects of their social environments, but also to accommodate interesting, important aspects of those environments [25,26]. In social group settings, Ryan and Deci [52] further propose that when the group helps ful<sup>fi</sup>ll people's needs for autonomy, relatedness, and competence, those people become more likely to adopt the values of the group. Social identity theory, as a complement to selfdetermination theory, further suggests that newcomers likely identify with a virtual community that helps them satisfy one or more of their key, self-de<sup>fi</sup>nitional needs [1,15,55]. Therefore, need ful<sup>fi</sup>llment relates to social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation not only because members obtain self-relevant, easily accessible information from their community identities, but also because those identities enable them to maintain and authentically express their sense of who they are (i.e., their traits and values; [55]). Below, we discuss this process for each psychological need.

First, self-determination theory regards autonomy as a form of self-government, or the extent to which members feel self-directed in their actions [25,39]. A person whose need for autonomy has been met should report stronger feelings of “volition, agency, and initiative” [38, p. 368], whereas less autonomous members cannot use their personal skills, lack such strong feelings, and may express less awareness of their group membership. As Blais et al. [17] demonstrate, a social relationship that fails to provide a sense of autonomy produces lower enjoyment and well-being ratings. In this sense, autonomy is similar to what Austin and Vancouver [8, p. 357] call “self-determination,” or the sense of freedom to make choices and avoid constraint or coercion. The degree to which a relationship partner helps ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a need for autonomy thus enhances the development of the relationship [39,56]. In a virtual community setting, an atmosphere that encourages autonomy signals the community's own member-centric orientation. Newcomers then should experience their participation as an expression of their selves, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences their cognitive connection with the virtual community [12,57].

Second, relatedness refers to a person's need to feel a sense of closeness with others [26]. Leak and Cooney [39] argue that a relationship partner that ful<sup>fi</sup>lls this need is the object of greater positive affect. The experience of relatedness also can produce intimacy [25,51]; Reis and Patrick [51] even de<sup>fi</sup>ne intimacy as feelings of being understood and cared for. To the extent that newcomers experience such connective features and a sense of being cared for through their community participation, they are more likely to adopt the values of the community [39,56]. From a social identity perspective, feelings of relatedness also make a virtual community-based identity more attractive, thereby strengthening newcomers' community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [1,54].

Third, the need for competence re<sup>fl</sup>ects the need to feel effective in one's efforts and capable of achieving desired outcomes [51]. This notion is similar to what previous researchers have termed “personal mastery,” de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a person's active avoidance of mediocrity or poor performance [8,56]. Substantial research con<sup>fi</sup>rms the importance of a persistent sense of competence for psychological wellbeing and positive relationship functioning. For example, Patrick et al. [46] show that believing that one is effectively making progress toward one's goals is psychologically bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. From a review of self-determination studies, Ryan and Deci [52] conclude that support for competence leads people to transform the group's values and encourages more ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious relationships. Bandura's [14] work on self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy also reveals that the belief in one's own ability to bring about desired outcomes largely determines psychological well-being, which in turn facilitates the development of community-related cognitive social identity.

Hypothesis 1. (a) Autonomy, (b) relatedness, and (c) competence exert positive impacts on a newcomer's cognitive social identity in a virtual community setting.

2.3. Links of cognitive social identity with affective and evaluative social identity

Categorizations of in- and out-groups accentuate perceptions of pro totypical similarity among people in the same group and prototypical differences across people in different groups [31,55]. The selfcategorization process involves a cognitive assimilation of the self with a particular in-group, which likely prompts the person to align his or her self-perceptions and behavioral decisions with those of contextually relevant in-group prototypes [31]. The process also can reduce new members' uncertainty, producing additional positive ingroup attitudes, cohesion, and altruism, as “a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of depersonalized, prototype-based inter-individual attitudes” [31, p. 126]. As social psychology literature reveals, self-categorization enhances the affective component of social identity in interpersonal relationships (e.g., [29,32]).

In line with this argument, Bergami and Bagozzi [15] posit that selfcategorization provides a cognitive basis for participation decisions. Furthermore, affective commitment and group self-esteem motivate and facilitate the transformation of this self-categorization into behavioral intentions. Newcomers who perceive a high degree of similarity with members of an in-group are more predisposed to view their virtual community participation through a positive lens. Such “bright side” experiences encourage active, responsible involvement in community self-governance [1,16]. Responsible members also keep themselves informed about issues affecting the community, make more speci<sup>fi</sup>c relationship investments in other members, and encourage others to do likewise.

Similar to social attraction mechanisms, self-categorization also can lead members to express more trust in group members, and in turn focus on the long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of their relationship [15,16,54]. Moreover, due to social attraction, members prefer to engage in and consume community-related rites, rituals, and routines, such that they feel more integrated into the community. Over time, they develop group-based self-esteem, because these integrated members perceive themselves as important and valuable community members [29,47]. In group settings, Bergami and Bagozzi [15] <sup>fi</sup>nd that cognitive self-categorization has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive effects on both affective commitment and group self-esteem.

Hypothesis 2. Cognitive social identity has a positive impact on (a) affective social identity and (b) evaluative social identity in virtual community settings.

2.4. Links of affective and evaluative social identity with participation intentions

We also expect affective commitment and group-based self-esteem to predict members' intentions to participate in virtual communities. Affective commitment, as derived from a positive emotional attachment, relates positively to members' participation [4,5]. For example, Meyer and Allen [42] <sup>fi</sup>nd that members' commitment to the social group leads them to perceive their duty to help fellow members, which triggers their prosocial intentions. Similarly, Bhattacharya and Sen [16] observe that when affective commitment increases, people feel more connected to and interdependent with others, so they give something of themselves to promote the group's well-being. For virtual communities, Wiertz and de Ruyter [59] suggest that greater affective commitment causes members to feel a sense of responsibility to assist others in the collective by contributing information more frequently and providing more helpful answers to questions.

Members with higher group-based self-esteem believe they personify greater value in the community and enjoy relatively high social status; thus, they assume they have the necessary resources to contribute effectively to the community [15,22,29,48]. Bergami and Bagozzi [15] further suggest that collective self-esteem motivates members to promote the community and undertake extra effort for the common good. The rationale for this argument comes from a social selfenhancement view: People with high collective self-esteem strive to engage in group-related behaviors that bene<sup>fi</sup>t their in-group, because this process reinforces collective self-esteem [22,47]. In other words, members with high group-based self-esteem have a vested interest in the community's success (i.e., they want to communicate their positive af<sup>fi</sup>liation to relevant audiences). Their decision to participate proactively in the community thus represents a manifestation of their voluntary effort to help it achieve success [55]. Community success that results from these efforts should not only reinforce their high groupbased self-esteem but also help them maintain cognitive consistency [15,47].

Hypothesis 3. (a) Affective social identity and (b) evaluative social identity have positive impacts on a newcomer's intention to participate in the virtual community.

## 2.5. The impact of participation intentions on behavior

The intention construct is central to the theory of planned behavior [3]; it refers to “a person's motivation in the sense of his or her conscious plan to exert effort to carry out a behavior” [28, p. 168]. Intentions provide “indications of how hard people are willing to try, of how much of an effort they are planning to exert, in order to perform the behavior” [3, p. 181]. Conner and Armitage [21] suggest that intentions direct the motivation to act in appropriate ways, as well as the initiation of planning and related mental and physical activities to implement goal-directed behaviors. A suf<sup>fi</sup>cient degree of actual control over the relevant behavior should cause members to carry out their intentions when they encounter the intended opportunity. A growing body of empirical work supports the fundamental logic that behavioral intentions positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavior (for a review, see [21]). Thus:

Hypothesis 4. Participation intentions have a positive impact on participation behavior.

## 2.6. Moderating role of relationship satisfaction

In addition to direct effects, it may be useful to consider potential moderators of the links from cognitive social identity to both affective commitment and collective self-esteem. That is, we conceive of cognitive social identity as a “cold” form of awareness of group membership, whereas both affective commitment and collective self-esteem are “hot” motivations that induce intentions to act (see also [15,29]). How, then, does awareness of group membership transform into affective commitment and collective self-esteem? This issue is especially important in virtual communities, where many newcomers simply “lurk,” regardless of their high group membership awareness [11,50]. Therefore, we turn to the customer satisfaction literature to investigate how newcomers' evaluations of their participation experiences might in<sup>fl</sup>uence the conversion of their group membership awareness into affective commitment and collective self-esteem.

Relationship satisfaction, as a positive affective state, results from the member's appraisal of various aspects of her or his relationship with the virtual community. The marketing literature offers two general conceptualizations of satisfaction: transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and overall or cumulative [23]. The former provides speci<sup>fi</sup>c diagnostic information about a particular community encounter; the latter accrues across multiple discrete encounters and interactions with other community members over time [19,23]. Therefore, cumulative satisfaction provides a more fundamental indicator of the person's long-term relationship orientation. In this study, we focus on how appraisals of cumulative participation experiences moderate the link from cognitive social identity to affective and evaluative forms of social identity.

When newcomers are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their relationship with the virtual community, they should sense a stronger psychological bond with it that seems unique or nonsubstitutable. Particularly in virtual communities, in which tangible elements tend to be less salient, relationship satisfaction can be a critical “push factor” that encourages newcomers to become insiders, embedded in the community. Cheung and Lee [19] also suggest that positive exchange experiences over time produce relational exchange norms that govern members' interactions. Thus members who experience greater relationship satisfaction should feel motivated to reciprocate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they receive by making more relationship investments in the community [4,24]. In a virtual community setting, we predict that these satis<sup>fi</sup>ed members also exhibit relatively strong links between their cognitive social identity and their affective commitment and collective self-esteem. In contrast, members who experience low relationship satisfaction likely suffer from higher uncertainty, such that they may disengage through psychological or physical withdrawal, to avoid further identity threats [31]. In turn, the links from their cognitive social identity to affective commitment and collective self-esteem should be relatively weaker.

Hypothesis 5. The impact of cognitive social identity on (a) affective social identity and (b) evaluative social identity is stronger for members who experience high satisfaction than for members who experience low satisfaction with the virtual community.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Participants and questionnaire

For the main data collection, we conducted a two-stage, Web-based survey of newcomers to virtual communities that were hosted by a large, Taiwanese Internet community platform. The communities' topics of interest included restaurants, sports, traveling, and culinary events. The platform offers user-friendly, unique designs and features, such as a virtual gift-giving system that allows members to design and send virtual gifts (e.g., <sup>fl</sup>owers, cakes, dolls, greeting cards). Furthermore, members can easily access information that other community members have shared, using the platform's powerful search engine. This platform maintains a database of each member's daily participation activities (e.g., visit times, uploads, shared articles, gifts sent).

Using data we gathered from this platform, together with selfreported survey data, we empirically tested our proposed model using a longitudinal design. This approach is less susceptible to common method bias and offers greater potential for causal inferences [10]. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase (T1), we used questionnaire items to measure respondents' psychological needs and relationship satisfaction with the community. In the second phase (T2), one month later, we employed a second questionnaire to assess the three components of social identity and participation intentions. Two months after the completion of this two-stage survey, we collected data about newcomers' actual participation during those two months.

To de<sup>fi</sup>ne which members were newcomers, we considered both prior research and real-world input from the platform provider. Ashforth and Saks [7] test their model of newcomers' organizational behavior by de<sup>fi</sup>ning “newcomers” as those who had started a job with a new company within the previous ten months. Morrison [43] instead uses nine months as a threshold. Without any speci<sup>fi</sup>c consensus or clear theory about what constitutes a “newcomer,” especially in a virtual community context, we turned to the community platform provider and its of<sup>fi</sup>cers. These sources noted that the virtual environment gives members ready opportunities to communicate and

## 4. Results

Following Anderson and Gerbing [6], we adopted a two-step approach to test the models. First, we conducted con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the measurement properties of the re<sup>fl</sup>ective latent constructs. Second, we performed structural equation analysis to test the research hypotheses. We ran all the models using the LISREL 8.80 program [36]. We assessed the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t using chi-square tests, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), nonnormed <sup>fi</sup>t index (NNFI), and comparative <sup>fi</sup>t index (CFI) [33,41]. Satisfactory model <sup>fi</sup>t required nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant chi-square tests, RMSEA values less than or equal to .08, and NNFI and CFI greater than or equal to .90 [13].

## 4.1. Measurement model evaluation

## 4.1.1. Internal consistency

We used two measures to evaluate the internal consistency of the constructs: composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). As Table 2 shows, the CRs range from .72 to .85, and the AVEs range from .56 to .74, above the recommended cutoff levels of .60 and .50, respectively [13]. Therefore, all constructs exhibit good internal consistency.

## 4.1.2. Discriminant validity

We assessed the discriminant validity of the measures using three approaches. First, we built a CFA model with 11 latent constructs and 20 measures. The results showed that the model <sup>fi</sup>t the data well. ## 4.4. Moderating influences of relationship satisfaction

The proposed moderator, relationship satisfaction, is a continuous variable, so we conducted ordinary least squares regression analyses to test whether relationship satisfaction strengthens the associations between (1) cognitive social identity and affective social identity and (2) cognitive social identity and evaluative social identity. We measured relationship satisfaction with a two-item scale adapted from de Wulf et al. [24]: (1) As a member, I believe I have a high-quality relationship with this virtual community, and (2) Based on all my participation experience, I am satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with this virtual community. We followed the moderated regression procedures recommended by Aiken and West [2], such that we entered relationship satisfaction and cognitive social identity <sup>fi</sup>rst, and then added their interaction term. Furthermore, we centered the predictor and moderator, and then computed their interaction term by taking the product of the mean-centered variables to reduce possible multicollinearity. Both relationship satisfaction and cognitive social identity exerted signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on affective social identity in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage. In the second stage, the partialled product of relationship satisfaction and cognitive social identity provided a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive predictor of affective social identity (β = .18, p b .01), in support of H5a. We used the same procedures to test H5b, but in this case, the partialled product of relationship satisfaction and cognitive social identity did not significantly predict evaluative social identity. Thus, H5b did not receive support.

## 5. Discussion and implications

This study attempts to enhance understanding of newcomers' participation behaviors in virtual community settings. Using a longitudinal research design and multiple measurement sources, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that need ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, with regard to autonomy, relatedness, and competence, helps trigger newcomers' cognitive social identity, which then elicits their affective and evaluative social identities. These various identities then create an intention to participate proactively in the virtual community. The results not only clarify the theoretical relationship between distal antecedents and participation behavior but also improve understanding of how different factors jointly explain newcomers' participation decisions.

## 5.1. Antecedents of cognitive social identity

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that newcomers in virtual communities have inherent needs to experience their participation behavior as freely chosen and volitional (i.e., autonomy), to feel cared for by other members (i.e., relatedness), and to feel effective and skillful in the community activities they undertake $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ competence). This <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that newcomers gravitate toward virtual communities and experience well-being during their interactions when those communities provide opportunities for ful<sup>fi</sup>lling their basic needs. Dholakia et al. [27] instead reveal that virtual community members' needs for information, self-discovery, entertainment, social enhancement, and interpersonal interconnectivity do not strongly affect social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. These researchers call for research that explores different, essential psychological needs and their relation to member participation decisions. We have answered this call by investigating three essential psychological needs and demonstrating that cognitive social identity channels their effects into affective and evaluative social identities, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence participation decisions. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that the three innate psychological needs of autonomy, relatedness, and competence are essential for the constructive development of relationships between newcomers and virtual communities. In relation to Dholakia et al.'s [27] <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the link between psychological needs and the higher-order social identity, our results suggest that the cognitive, affective, and evaluative components of social identity play unique roles in newcomers' participation decisions. Further research on newcomer behavior should treat social identity as three distinct constructs rather than as a higher-order factor with three subcomponents.

In addition, this study clari<sup>fi</sup>es the roles of autonomy and relatedness in members' group participation decision processes. Our results, from a Taiwanese context, show that to enable the progression of newcomers' relationships with the community, the ful-<sup>fi</sup>llment of autonomy needs plays a more important role than the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of relatedness needs. Support for autonomy traditionally has been more highly valued in Western societies, characterized by an individualist culture, whereas a sense of being connected with and cared for by other members has been more highly valued in Chinese societies, characterized by a collectivist culture [35]. However, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the present study contrast with this viewpoint and suggest that virtual community cultivators or managers should emphasize an environment marked by support for members' autonomy, rather than control, to foster a positive participation atmosphere.

5.2. Relationships of cognitive, affective, and evaluative social identities with participation

Newcomers' cognitive social identity in<sup>fl</sup>uences participation decisions through affective commitment and collective selfesteem. Most prior studies of virtual communities have focused on identifying factors that can explain member participation (e.g., [58,59]); we take a different approach. In our more comprehensive model of participation decision making, we include both distal (cognitive social identity and its antecedents) and proximal (affective commitment and collective self-esteem) determinants of newcomers' participation intentions. Distal determinants in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision to engage in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c behavior, whereas proximal variables determine a person's persistent effort [9]. These results demonstrate how awareness of group membership in<sup>fl</sup>uences member participation behavior, through the dual control of mediating variables, which extends work by Cheung and Lee [19]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also provide some support for Bergami and Bagozzi's [15] argument that the cognitive component of social identity had an indirect effect and both affective and evaluative components have a direct effect on group members' voluntary decisions.

## 5.3. Moderating role of satisfaction with the community

Satisfaction strengthens the relationship between self-awareness of membership and affective commitment. In other words, in the absence of positive appraisals of participation experiences, cognitive socia identity may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to enhance affective commitment.

This moderating effect helps explain why newcomers with high self-awareness still might not become embedded in a social group [1,30]. Our study also extends Bergami and Bagozzi's [15] <sup>fi</sup>ndings by identifying an important boundary condition for the prediction of affective commitment based on cognitive social identity. In general, newcomers with a high degree of prototypical similarity to the in-group are more likely to seek interaction opportunities when they join the group. Thus, high satisfaction with community participation, combined with greater perceptions of cognitive social identity, lead to greater affective commitment.

Finally, the results indicate that satisfaction does not moderate the effect of cognitive social identity on evaluative social identity. In a post hoc analysis using quantile regression [37], we found that the effects of satisfaction on evaluative social identity are asymmetric: Satisfaction exerts a greater impact on members in the bottom and upper quantiles but a lesser effect in the median quantiles. This asymmetric effect may neutralize the stronger moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of satisfaction on the relationship between cognitive social identity and evaluative social identity. This important issue marks a research challenge that merits further investigation.

## 6. Limitations and directions for further research

Although our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are encouraging and useful, their applicability must be considered in light of some of the study's limitations. First, it is unclear whether our <sup>fi</sup>ndings generalize to all types of virtual communities. We study members from the same community platform, which features only a few types of communities. The strength of the various relationships we test may differ in other types of communities. Richer insights might derive from additional studies conducted in different types of virtual communities across multiple community platforms.

Second, our investigation of participation factors includes a limited number of constructs that may be important in newcomers' participation decisions. Further studies should investigate whether and how other factors might in<sup>fl</sup>uence community participation. For example, community leader support may motivate members by fostering a social climate of active participation in the virtual community. We also have focused on the roles of three psychological needs and a mediating process in which their ful<sup>fi</sup>llment in<sup>fl</sup>uences newcomers' participation decisions. More research is needed to investigate multidimensional factors that also could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of these psychological needs. For example, virtual community characteristics (e.g., member responsiveness, interpersonal support) and community platform attributes (e.g., perceived control, perceived ease of use, enjoyment) may in<sup>fl</sup>uence newcomers' sense of need ful<sup>fi</sup>llment [27,57]. These effects merit further investigation.

Third, the self-concept likely comprises three fundamental selfrepresentations: the individual self, the relational self, and the collective self [53]. Therefore, people may seek to achieve selfde<sup>fi</sup>nition and self-interpretation (1) in terms of their unique traits, (2) in dyadic relationships, or (3) through group membership [53]. Research that incorporates all three levels of the selfconcept (i.e., individual, relational, and collective) might be able to offer clearer explanations of members' participation behavior in virtual community settings.<sup>2</sup>
