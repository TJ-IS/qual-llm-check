# A nomological network of customers’ privacy perceptions: linking artifact design to shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency

Hartmut Hoehle<sup>a</sup>, John A. Aloysius<sup>b</sup>, Soheil Goodarzi<sup>c</sup> and Viswanath Venkatesh<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Chair of Enterprise Systems, University of Mannheim, Mannheim, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Department of Supply Chain Management, Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR, United States; <sup>c</sup>Department of Information Systems, Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR, United States

## ABSTRACT

The explosive growth of mobile devices and their widespread acceptance by customers along with the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of autoID technologies have prompted retailers to consider adoption of emerging technologies. Their motives are to enhance in-store customer shopping experience and to acquire an advantage in the competitive retail environment. Two interrelated issues nevertheless have been a hindrance: mobile shopping application usability and customers’ privacy concerns. Drawing on a recently developed conceptualisation of mobile application usability and the multidimensional developmental theory of privacy, we tackle these two issues. We theorise about the impact of artifact design on mobile application usability and the consequent impact of usability on customers’ privacy concerns and shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. We tested our hypotheses in two retail store laboratory studies in which the participants were assigned to two di<sup>f</sup>erent shopping tasks: general browsing (n = 194) and goal-directed shopping (n = 190). We found that adhering to mobile application usability principles could mitigate privacy concerns and consequently, improve shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest new avenues to alleviate customers’ privacy concerns using artifact design, thus complementing conventional approaches that focus on preventive measures to deal with the issue of privacy concerns.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid spread of mobile devices has led us to an application-driven society wherein retailers aim to integrate mobile applications into their omnichannel strategies. For example, 44% of all Americans have reported that their mobile devices in<sup>fl</sup>uence at least 75% of their purchases in retail environments (RIS Research, 2015). This widespread use of mobile applications by customers in addition to the capability of autoID technologies to automatically identify products and capture data have encouraged retailers to combine both technologies (Aloysius & Venkatesh, 2013). AutoID technologies refer to devices, such as barcodes, RFID, magnetic identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, biometric technologies, and optical character readers, that can automatically capture data. These technologies are used to improve data accuracy and to reduce the amount of time and labour needed to input data manually (Violino, 2005). Using autoID technologies can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for retailers (e.g., mass merchandisers, department stores) because it helps them to pursue omnichannel strategies in which they can integrate important information for customers (e.g., product reviews) in brick-and-mortar environments. Customers could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from autoID-based mobile applications because they provide superior, timely, convenient information and lead to a more e<sup>fi</sup>cient shopping experience (Venkatesh, Aloysius, Hoehle, & Burton, 2017).

In the past, customers have, however, rejected autoID-based shopping applications due to privacy concerns. For example, many customers protested against <sup>fi</sup>rms, such as the apparel manufacturer Benetton in Italy (Ohkubo, Suzuki, & Kinoshita, 2005), Tesco PLC in the U.K. (Ohkubo et al., 2005) and Metro Group Future Store in Germany (Günther & Spiekermann, 2005), because these <sup>fi</sup>rms had announced or started using autoID technologies in their retail stores. There are two major privacy threats associated with the adoption of autoID mobile applications to retrieve information in retail stores: (1) purchasing anonymity and pro<sup>fi</sup>ling; and (2) mobile phone vulnerabilities and information disclosure.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst threat is related to purchasing anonymity and pro<sup>fi</sup>ling. The application of autoID technologies in retail stores for data retrieval purposes has been suspected to compromise shopping anonymity and threaten civil liberties (Gar<sup>fi</sup>nkel, Juels, & Pappu, 2005). When customers use autoID technologies to retrieve product information during their shopping trips, the information about their browsing and shopping behaviour could be collected by the retailer. Even more alarming, if a customer pays for a scanned/read item with his or her credit card (or store loyalty card), the item’s unique barcode/RFID number could be linked with the customer’s identity and other information (Clarke & Flaherty, 2008). Clearly, monitoring shopping behaviour of customers and associating them with speci<sup>fi</sup>c items are particularly intrusive, especially given that most customers are averse to being tracked and pro<sup>fi</sup>led (Clarke & Flaherty, 2008; Spiekermann & Evdokimov, 2009). For instance, they may not want their personal identity to be linked with certain products (e.g., medication they take to treat impairments), or they may be unwilling to be pro<sup>fi</sup>led and viewed as, say, frugal customers who purchase cheap products (Spiekermann, 2009). It also should be noted that customers’ privacy concerns over their purchasing anonymity may even be heightened in case of RFID readers. This is because barcode scanning is done deliberately by a shopper and thus the information regarding the shopper’s interest in a product is captured with his or her knowledge. However, mere proximity of an RFID reader to a tag is su<sup>fi</sup>cient for reading and capturing data. Hence, shoppers who use an RFID reader may be more worried that, without their intent, an automatic read could be initiated as they walk through an aisle (Venkatesh et al., 2017).

The second threat is related to mobile phone vulnerabilities and information disclosure. Although barcode scanners appear to be more favourably viewed due to the voluntary nature of barcode scanning, anecdotal evidence suggests other privacy issues speci<sup>fi</sup>c to information retrievals via barcode and quick response (QR) code scanners. For one, in 2011, Kaspersky Lab found a QR code that could install an application on a user’s phone. The application would then send text messages at \$1 per message on the user’s tab without his or her knowledge (Shannon, 2011). Compromising marketing <sup>fi</sup>rms that generate barcode and QR code for products and services, hackers could hijack the code and link it to a virus, botnet, or malicious code (CNET, 2012; GEER, 2013). This malware can then perform harmful activities such as obtaining information on a mobile device without its user’s awareness. Also, some barcode and QR scanning applications that even may come from legitimate companies have intrusive permissions to collect users’ data. For example, they are often able to geolocate the users or track their web browsing and applications history (Miliefsky, 2015).

Beyond the abovementioned concerns, customers may worry that the information they share with retailers is susceptible to unauthorised disclosure. Especially, recent data breach incidents in retail stores, such as Target, which has led to compromise of personal and <sup>fi</sup>nancial information of millions of customers (McGrath, 2014), may have ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed customers’ privacy concerns about collection of their information through autoID technologies.

Anecdotal evidence suggests that customers’ privacy concerns related to autoID-based mobile shopping applications could be reduced by designing better applications and improving usability (see Aloysius & Venkatesh, 2013). Thus, to design good mobile shopping applications that successfully integrate autoID technologies, retailers need to understand underlying factors of mobile application usability, how these factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ privacy concerns, and how mobile application usability and customers’ privacy concerns a<sup>f</sup>ect customers’ shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Our literature review suggests a few related scienti<sup>fi</sup>c gaps corresponding to these issues. First, prior research studying the e<sup>f</sup>ect of usability on privacy-related constructs, such as risk and trust, is mostly limited to semiotic features of technology artifact usability (e.g., Cyr, Head, & Ivanov, 2006; Kim & Moon, 1998; Li & Yeh, 2010; Resnick & Montania, 2003). However, recent work has provided valid and reliable instru ments that provide a more comprehensive under standing of usability (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). Hence, considering a holistic usability conceptualisa tion and investigating the e<sup>f</sup>ect of usability features, beyond the semiotic design, could complement prior work. Moreover, despite acknowledging that usability is necessary for IS success (e.g., Fisher, 1999), extant research sheds little light on how outcomes, such as customers’ privacy concerns and shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by mobile application usability. Finally, as noted by Bélanger and Crossler (2011), in spite of a reasonable amount of work on individuals’ privacy concerns related to IS and autoID technologies, most work in this area has focused on causal explanation (e.g., Thiesse, 2007), classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (e.g., Razzouk, Seitz, & Nicolaou, 2008), or prediction (e.g., Cazier, Jensen, & Dave, 2008), but not on action. This appears to be the Achilles’ heel of privacy research in IS (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011). Therefore, there is a growing need in IS research to develop well-designed and implementable tools that can alleviate customers’ privacy concerns and provide customers with superior shopping experiences, thus improving retailer performance (Matook, 2013; Te’eni, 2001; Te’eni & Feldman, 2001).

Against this backdrop, we draw on existing work on mobile application usability (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015) and privacy (Laufer & Wolfe, 1977) to develop a model that links autoID technology artifact design, combined with content design, to shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency via mobile application usability and privacy concerns. We argue that a higher degree of usability can lead to privacy-friendlier mobile applications that o<sup>f</sup>set customers’ privacy concerns (Provost, Martens, & Murray, 2015). To test our model, we conducted two studies in which participants evaluated autoID-based mobile applications tailored for retail environments.

We expect to make two key contributions. First, we augment the privacy literature by building a nomological network around privacy concerns. We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally connect upstream privacy concerns to design characteristics of an emerging technology and downstream to shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Second, we present implications for practitioners about how they can enhance the likelihood of success of such emerging mobile technologies that otherwise cause privacy concerns. Given the multidimensional conceptualisations of both usability and privacy, we can see speci<sup>fi</sup>c usability attributes that link to speci<sup>fi</sup>c privacy aspects, thus providing actionable guidance.

## 2. Theoretical background

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst outline advances in the literature on mobile application usability in particular and human-computer interaction (HCI) in general, as this informs our artifact design and helps us to address our main research objective. Second, we summarise the existing research on how usability impacts individuals’ privacy-related perceptions, such as risk and trusting beliefs. Third, we discuss individuals’ privacy concerns in the context of HCI. Finally, we explain our outcome variables related to shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and their importance to the current work.

## 2.1. Mobile application usability and HCI

As the outcome of technology design, usability has been acknowledged to be an important metric for evaluating the quality of information systems (Fink, Zeevi, & Te’eni, 2008; Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006). Mobile application usability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which a mobile application can be used by users to attain their goals with e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and satisfaction (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). By adapting Apple (2011) user experience guidelines for mobile applications, Hoehle and Venkatesh (2015) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed six major factors representing mobile usability––i.e., application design, application utility, interface input, interface output, interface graphics, and interface structure––that predict continued intention to use mobile applications. We adapt Hoehle and Venkatesh’s (2015) work by examining the factors they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and clustering the constructs into conceptually similar groups suited to the context of autoID-based mobile shopping applications. This step is undertaken for two major reasons. First, measurement theory suggests that constructs sharing a common theme and similar characteristics can be viewed as theoretically akin and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as such (Mackenzie, Podsako<sup>f</sup>, & Podsako<sup>f</sup>, 2011). That is, multiple constructs that represent distinguishable facets of a single conceptual domain and refer to the same general type of property may be uni<sup>fi</sup>ed to articulate relationships more succinctly. Such grouping is commonly used in organisational research. For instance, research on employees’ turnover intentions in organisations has often grouped individuals’ tenure (representing the relationship between an individual and company) and age (representing other variables such as maturity and family responsibilities) (Van Breukelen, Van Der, & Steensma, 2004). Also, Serva, Fuller, and Mayer (2005) have considered three constructs (i.e., ability, benevolence, and integrity) constituting trustworthiness of a team. Therefore, grouping enables us to develop more meaningful hypotheses as our groups have been selected to conform to the context of autoID-based mobile shopping applications. To identify the groups, we examined previously identi<sup>fi</sup>ed constructs for conceptual similarities and reviewed the existing literature on each construct. Finally, it should be noted that having a reduced set of constructs is important to keep the theoretical scope of this paper manageable.

## 2.2. Usability and privacy-related perceptions

Prior work has found that usability attributes––such as aesthetic graphics––of IT artifacts can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ risk and trusting beliefs. For example, in the context of websites and electronic commerce, visual design factors of the user interface have been found to impact customers’ feeling of trustworthiness (Cyr, 2008; Kim & Moon, 1998). Also, greater degree of perceived usability of a website has been noted to improve customers’ trust levels (Flavian, Guinaliu, & Gurrea, 2006). In the context of mobile technology, quality elements of the artifact have been shown to positively a<sup>f</sup>ect customers’ trust toward the vendor (Zhou, 2011). Moreover, higher degree of mobile device usability has been argued to lead to lower degree of customers’ perceived risk that in turn results in higher extent of adoption intentions (Li & Bai, 2010). Finally, design aesthetics of a mobile website have been found to contribute to its key characteristics, such as usefulness, ease of use, and customisation, that in turn positively a<sup>f</sup>ect customers’ trust in mobile commerce (Li & Yeh, 2010).

As brie<sup>fl</sup>y mentioned earlier, despite the abovementioned body of literature, the existing research in this area does not take a comprehensive view of technology artifact usability, when investigating its e<sup>f</sup>ect on users’ beliefs and perceptions. That is, recent advances in usability have indicated that usability attributes are not limited to visual design aesthetics but include some nuanced communicative aspects such as instant start, e<sup>f</sup>ort minimisation, and content relevance (see Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). Besides, the existing literature lacks studies investigating the e<sup>f</sup>ect of usability on privacy perceptions that are the very nature of risk and trust. In other words, individuals’ risk and trust perceptions are consequences of their overriding privacy concerns (Fortes & Rita, 2016; Hong & Thong, 2013). As also noted by Malhotra, Kim, and Agarwal (2004, p. 341), “users with a high degree of information privacy concerns are likely to be low on trusting beliefs and high on risk beliefs.” Therefore, considering a multi-faceted usability conceptualisation, our work complements the extant literature on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of artifact usability on users’ privacy perceptions beyond the partial semiotic e<sup>f</sup>ects on users’ risk and trusting beliefs.

## 2.3. Individuals’ privacy concerns in the context of HCI

Privacy concerns refer to “an individual’s subjective views of fairness within the context of information privacy” (Malhotra et al., 2004, p. 337), and it represents an individual’s control over his or her personal information in terms of the amount of data being collected, errors that might be committed in the information, improper access of unauthorised entities to the information, and secondary uses of the information (Smith, Milberg, & Burke, 1996). Prior work on HCI and IS studying individuals’ privacy concerns has commonly used a multidimensional scale (e.g., Smith et al., 1996; Stewart & Segars, 2002) to assess an individual’s privacy concerns. The dimensions are unauthorised access, errors, secondary use, and collection (Table 1 lists the construct de<sup>fi</sup>nitions).

We view privacy as a commodity that can be traded when enough incentives (e.g., convenience) are presented (Dinev, 2014). We further draw on Laufer and Wolfe (1977) who articulate a multidimensional developmental theory (MDT) “which helps us to understand individual perceptions of privacy” (Laufer & Wolfe, 1977, p. 22). Of relevance to our work, MDT of privacy suggests that the interpersonal dimension of privacy (i.e., applicable to individuals’ use of IS and mobile applications) has two elements: information management and interaction management (Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Given the importance of grounding studies in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts (see Johns, 2006), these two components of interpersonal interaction––i.e., information management and interaction management––are central to our work because they place privacy concerns into a context (Hong & Thong, 2013).

The information management component of privacy concerns “describes how an individual manage his or her personal information” (Hong & Thong, 2013, p. 277). The crucial element of information management is the “calculus of behaviour” in which one tries to predict consequences of engaging in a given behaviour (Laufer, Proshansky, & Wolfe, 1974). MDT of privacy argues that there are situations in which individuals are willing to reveal their information because the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts outweigh potential negative consequences (Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Prior work in IS and HCI suggests that users are willing to provide personal information to organisations as long as they believe that the organisations make every e<sup>f</sup>ort to protect the acquired personal information (Culnan & Williams, 2009; Smith et al., 1996).

## 2.4. Shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency

Past research has argued that a shopping decision in complex retail environments is typically made through a two-stage process: product screening and product evaluation (Payne, Bettman, & Johnson, 1988). Zhang, Agarwal, & Lucas (2011) have pointed out that at the product screening stage, customers locate and screen a set of products and after a cursory evaluation of them, they choose a consideration subset of the most favourable alternatives. Next, at the product evaluation stage, customers examine the alternatives in the consideration subset in detail and make their purchase decisions. AutoID technologies could be advantageous to both product screening and evaluation activities. That is, at the product screening stage, autoID technologies could save customers time and information search and screening costs when they perform their preliminary evaluations to come up with the consideration subset. This allows customers to evaluate more products, come up with a larger consideration subset, and thus improve their decision-making quality. Further, at the product evaluation stage, quick provision of product information and reviews will allow customers to perform detailed comparisons and more in-depth evaluations of alternatives, and therefore achieve better decision-making quality and shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, along with agility across multiple channels, is an important requirement of omnichannel retailing. Prior research on technologyenabled shopping has acknowledged shopping e<sup>fi</sup>- ciency to be a key variable (Zhang et al., 2011) because it is a major driver of consumer store loyalty (Ratchford, 2001). Further, because shopping is a joint e<sup>f</sup>ort that involves both customers and retailers, shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency is a critical outcome for customers and it declines when customers switch from one retail store to another (Zhang et al., 2011). Shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency consists of the costs (i.e., product brokering) and the value (i.e., quality of the purchase decision) of technology-mediated shopping by customers (Zhang et al., 2011).

## 3. AutoID-based mobile shopping applications

The autoID-based mobile application in which we were interested allows shoppers to scan tagged products in a retail environment (either via barcode or RFID technology). The mobile application runs on smartphones, such as Apple’s iPhone. We also aimed to understand practitioners’ views and conducted an exploratory investigation studying practitioners’ perceptions toward the proposed shopping assistance artifacts (see Rosemann & Vessey, 2008). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we conducted several discussion rounds with four <sup>fi</sup>rms that supply scanning technologies for retail stores and with representatives from ten retailers.

The interviews con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the reports and views in the trade and academic press that leading retailers were interested and engaged in e<sup>f</sup>orts to introduce mobile scanning technology for their customers. The latest advancements in mobile devices, the popularity of mobile applications, and the increasing sophistication of customers (younger customers in particular)

have led to retailers’ interest in mobile applications in the retail store (RIS Research, 2014). Among others, process optimisation, customer convenience and shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and labour savings are the main motives to evaluate business opportunities for integrating mobile applications into retail stores. Retailers desire to move to an omnichannel strategy in which the customer experience would be seamless whether they were online, using a mobile phone, or using a traditional channel in the store (RIS Research, 2014).

Such a strategy requires customers to be able to seamlessly access product information as they can in online channels. In particular, our review of trade press articles suggests that retailers are currently exploring mobile scanning applications for customers to provide additional product-speci<sup>fi</sup>c content during the traditional in-store shopping experience. Because most shoppers own smartphones, retailers could create mobile shopping applications that customers could use during shopping trips. One survey found that 51% of customers who used a mobile phone in the store for a shopping-related task looked up product reviews, whereas 45% used a QR code or barcode to look up product information (Smith, 2012). The idea of leveraging the information provided on the retailers’ website by mirroring it to mobile applications seems to be an intriguing opportunity for retailers.

Based on trade press (e.g., Clark, 2012; RIS Research, 2012), academic research (e.g., Bose, Ngai, Teo, & Spiekermann, 2009; Thiesse, 2007), and the above-indicated applicability check with practitioners, we used two hardware designs that involved a smartphone barcode scanner and a smartphone RFID reader.

Regarding the content design, prior research on technology adoption, e-commerce, and usability has found application content to be a key attribute determining the success of an e-commerce or m-commerce application (e.g., Venkatesh & Agarwal, 2006). Further, the extant literature on e-commerce and mobile applications has recognised product information and product reviews as key content playing important roles in customers’ purchase behavior (Forman, Ghose, & Wiesenfeld, 2008).

Product information consists of any information about a given product in which a shopper is interested. Product information has been argued to be critical for online shoppers because it helps them in making purchase decisions (Gu, Park, & Konana, 2012). Product information has also been acknowledged as an important determinant of customers technology adoption in the context of mobile applications (Kowatsch & Maass, 2010), as customers appreciate additional product information on the spot when shopping in brick-and-mortar stores. Nonetheless, contrary to e-commerce shoppers, customers using mobile applications in retail stores may not appreciate lengthy and detailed product information. This is due to the fact that the more information provided on a mobile application, the more challenging the navigation of the mobile application. This can lead to users’ cognitive overload that negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences their perceptions of ease of use of a given mobile application (Adipat, Zhang, & Zhou, 2011; Shaft & Vessey, 2006).

Product reviews include independent evaluations of a given product provided by previous purchasers. Product reviews have also become well-accepted features of e-commerce applications because customers value reviews from their peers as independent and impartial evaluations of a product (Zhou, Lu, & Wang, 2010). Additionally, product reviews have also been found to be valued by in-store customers employing mobile shopping applications (Kowatsch & Maass, 2010). Extensive product reviews, however, have been argued to lead to less user-friendly applications and increase cognitive overload on customers (Beck, Pahlke, & Seebach, 2014; Zhou et al., 2010).

Thus, we included product information and product reviews as our content designs. The two con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of our hardware and content designs are shown in Table 2.

## 4. Model and hypothesis development

Figure 1 presents our research model. Design science research has two goals: the development of technological artifacts and the production of knowledge (Baskerville, Kaul, & Storey, 2015). There is a recognition that a single design science study requires di<sup>f</sup>ering criteria to justify the knowledge produced at di<sup>f</sup>ering stages of the study when pursuing these two goals (Baskerville et al., 2015). In our research, we build hardware and application content and then hypothesise how di<sup>f</sup>erent con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of hardware and application content may in<sup>fl</sup>uence constructs relating to dimensions of usability, privacy concerns, and shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Thus, the model links hardware and content design of the autoID-based mobile applications to shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency via mobile application usability and privacy concerns. Taken together, our model suggests that, through favourable usability perceptions, shoppers’ privacy concerns can be mitigated and that in turn can lead to greater shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Research Model.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis focuses on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of hardware design and content design on mobile application usability. As described earlier, we designed two alternative autoID-based hardware designs, namely a barcode scanner and an RFID reader. Based on prior work on mobile application usability (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015; Hoehle, Zhang, & Venkatesh, 2015) and autoID-based research (Aloysius & Venkatesh, 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2017), we hypothesise that hardware design will have an e<sup>f</sup>ect on how users perceive autoID-based mobile application usability. For example, prior work suggests that RFID readers enable product data capture without necessarily having optical line-of-sight to read a product tag. The requirement of optical line-of-sight adds a step for customers to obtain product information because they are required to physically position the mobile device or the product being scanned. Such additional e<sup>f</sup>ort will negatively a<sup>f</sup>ect users’ usability perceptions that are particularly salient when considering mobile shopping applications (Aloysius & Venkatesh, 2013).

As explained earlier, we also designed three content designs that can be displayed on autoID-based mobile shopping applications, namely product information, product reviews, and a combination of product information and product reviews. Prior work on autoID-based mobile shopping applications suggests that customers welcome having access to product information and reviews on the spot (Venkatesh et al., 2017). However, additional content can be challenging to integrate in mobile applications and display on the small screens of smartphones. When such information is presented on a smartphone screen, users will be required to scroll through content and potentially even su<sup>f</sup>er from information overload due to the amount of information displayed. Consequently, an interface that includes both product information and reviews will be perceived to be more challenging in terms of usability. Thus, we hypothesise that

Hypothesis 1a: Customers provided with an RFID reader will have more favourable usability perceptions than customers provided with a barcode scanner.

Hypothesis 1b: Customers provided with product information in combination with product reviews will have the least favourable mobile application usability perceptions in comparison to customers provided with product information or product reviews.

Our next three hypotheses focus on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of application usability on privacy concerns. Although an individual’s overall privacy concern could be viewed as an attitude, such general concerns (e.g., attitudes) are mutable due to speci<sup>fi</sup>c perceptions of a particular retail store, device, or software. Related to this, the psychology literature provides evidence about how attitude change occurs (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2006). Application content is the degree to which shoppers see a value proposition in using the autoID-based mobile shopping application because its content is useful and well-designed (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). The two constructs associated with the application content are application design and application utility (Hoehle et al., 2015). Well-designed mobile shopping applications should be intuitive to use and shoppers can use them during their shopping trips. Likewise, if customers view that the mobile shopping application serves its purpose well and allows them to obtain useful information (e.g., product information or customer reviews), they will see value in the mobile application and perceive it to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial when using a shopping assistant (Hess, Fuller, & Campbelld, 2009).

As discussed earlier, the calculus of behaviour is a crucial determinant of information management (Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Individuals’ privacy concerns rise if they believe that their personal information is threatened by unauthorised access and errors triggered by an organisation’s inadequate information management (Hong & Thong, 2013). When managing their personal information, individuals tend to make tradeo<sup>f</sup>s between costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of revealing their personal information (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Laufer et al., 1974; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977; Smith, Dinev, & Xu, 2011). In other words, individuals may give up some privacy for some economic or social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in return (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977; Matook, Cummings, & Bala, 2015) and perceive situations in which they reveal personal information to be less intrusive if they clearly <sup>fi</sup>nd bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Laufer et al., 1974).

Applied to the context of our work, shoppers may feel that they scarcely have any control over what will occur to their personal information after it is collected (Hong & Thong, 2013) because they do not perceive that the information management practices adopted by a retailer or its associated mobile application are in their control. Thus, they will resort to making an assessment and surmising whether it is worth revealing their information. Here, we suggest that the more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts individuals derive from mobile application design and mobile application utility for in-store shopping, the less their privacy concerns regarding their information management (Dinev & Hart, 2006). This logic has been widely used in the context of online shopping to elucidate customers’ privacy concerns and their consequent shopping behaviour (Flavian et al., 2006). For example, if the mobile shopping application is well-designed and intuitive to use, users will see the e<sup>f</sup>ort the retailer made to design the application as a signal that suggests that the retailer would probably make an e<sup>f</sup>ort to protect customers’ personal information (i.e., avoiding the occurrence of unauthorised access and errors to their information). Likewise, if shoppers see high utility in the mobile shopping application and <sup>fi</sup>nd its content useful, they will value the extra information when shopping. When users value the mobile shopping application and their attention is on the salient extra information, they will be less likely to mind the potential downside of managing their information (i.e., unauthorised access and errors) and thus perceive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the application to outweigh the costs. Thus, we hypothesise that

Hypothesis 2: Usability perceptions relating to application content will negatively influence customers’ privacy concerns relating to information management.

Our third hypothesis focuses on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of user interaction on interaction management. User interaction describes how customers interact with an autoID-based mobile shopping application and how customers exchange data and information with the application. Prior work on mobile application usability suggests that user interaction includes interface input (e.g., scanning) and interface output (e.g., concise language for product information) (Hoehle et al., 2015). An autoID-based mobile application with a high degree of user interaction will allow users to easily input their data and display the desired output during shopping trips. For example, easy input of information and automated data entry support, such as autoID-based scanning features, reduce the e<sup>f</sup>ort customers have to expend during their shopping trips. Similarly, e<sup>f</sup>ective output of product information or product reviews reduces confusion and helps shoppers to gain a better idea of the information being transmitted (Middleton & Cukier, 2006). Such easy input and e<sup>f</sup>ective output of data will decrease the cognitive impediments of using the autoID-based mobile application and make the exchange of information more accessible to customers. This will enhance perceptions of control in the interaction with the mobile application and help customers to actively manage their interaction with the retailer. This reasoning has also been applied in the context of online shopping. For example, Pavlou and Fygenson (2006) argued that the ease of getting information from a website positively a<sup>f</sup>ects customers’ perceptions of controllability over their interaction with the website.

Interaction management explains how customers manage their interaction with the retailer o<sup>f</sup>ering additional product information and product reviews via an autoID-based mobile application. Prior work has associated secondary use and collection with interaction management (Hong & Thong, 2013) and found that when individuals believe they can exert more control over a given interaction, they are less likely to perceive their privacy to be invaded over the course of the interaction (Culnan, 1993; Culnan & Williams, 2009; Laufer et al., 1974; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). This could be due to their perception that they actively manage the interaction and can stop it at any point. This rationale has been also used in the context of online shopping (e.g., Hwang & Kim, 2007; Xu, Crossler, & Bélanger, 2012). For example, Hwang and Kim (2007) found that customers’ privacy concerns are low as long as they have a feeling of control over a given ecommerce transaction. Thus, we hypothesise that

Hypothesis 3: Usability perceptions relating to user interaction will negatively influence customers’ privacy concerns relating to interaction management.

Our fourth hypothesis focuses on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of interface presentation on interaction management. Interface presentation describes how the autoIDbased mobile shopping application presents the content that customers view during their shopping trips (Wells, Valacich, & Hess, 2011). Interface presentation of an autoID-based mobile application is an important component of its usability and also a typical focal point to improve customer acceptance of the application. Interface presentation includes interface graphics (e.g., bar charts representing customer reviews) and interface structure (e.g., top-to-bottom structure). An autoID-based mobile application that is e<sup>f</sup>ectively designed and has well-structured content, such as product information and reviews, will be perceived to have superior interface presentation (Hong, Thong, & Tam, 2004; Wells et al., 2011).

When users can extrapolate from their existing cognitive maps to interact with an IT artifact, the interaction in question will be easier for them (Hess et al., 2009; Wells et al., 2011). As a result, they will be more likely to perceive their interaction to be under their control. To illustrate, a well-designed interface graphics represents a consistent approach of presenting important information to shoppers, thus reducing an element of ambiguity in using the autoID-based mobile applications. Likewise, a well-structured representation of information, such as product reviews being ordered by the rating of helpfulness, will lead to a more certain shopping experience. Thus, considering the MDT’s argument about the key role of individuals’ control over their interaction management, we assert that interface presentation could reduce privacy concerns in terms of interaction management. This is consistent with prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings that limitations in interface presentation directly a<sup>f</sup>ect cognitive processing (Lowry, Twyman, Pickard, & Jenkins, 2014) and can adversely in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ privacy concerns due to ambiguity and uncertainty (Kim & Moon, 1998; Resnick & Montania, 2003). For example, well-designed user interface graphics will lead to a better user experience (Hong et al., 2004; Lim, Benbasat, & Ward, 2000) and the consistent use of various graphical techniques, such as realistic icons or images and aesthetic graphics, represents a structured approach of presenting important information to customers. Customers will likely value appropriate graphics and logical paths to enforce structures due to improvement of their control over their interaction and facilitation of shopping completion at the same time. Thus, we hypothesise that

Hypothesis 4: Usability perceptions relating to user interface presentation will negatively influence customers’ privacy concerns relating to interaction management.

In retail stores, customers make an e<sup>f</sup>ort to screen and evaluate various items and then make their purchase decisions. Shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency is a function of the costs and the value of technology-mediated shopping (Zhang et al., 2011) for in-store customers. Customers’ worries about their privacy will have a positive impact on the costs of technology-mediated shopping (i.e., product screening and evaluation cost) and a negative impact on the value of technologymediated shopping (i.e., decision-making quality).

Prior research shows that in-store customers’ privacy concerns associated with mobile shopping negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence shopping outcomes (e.g., Aloysius, Hoehle, Goodarzi, & Venkatesh, 2016) because privacy concerns can increase the sense of unease customers may experience during their mobile shopping. For example, Venkatesh et al. (2017) found that in-store shoppers express higher levels of security concerns, including privacy perceptions, when using autoID-based mobile shopping applications. Because higher levels of privacy concerns make mobile shopping to be an apprehensive activity, concerned individuals incur more cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort during their shopping. Further, the extra cognitive resources being used by customers with greater privacy concerns could have been expended in their product screening and evaluation, and could lead to a more e<sup>fi</sup>cient shopping experience. Moreover, when using autoID technologies in retail stores, customers who are concerned about their privacy are less likely to be willing to perform an exhaustive product screening and evaluation to <sup>fi</sup>nd items that best suit their needs. This will lessen their chance of making high-quality purchase decisions. Thus, we hypothesise that

Hypothesis 5: Privacy concerns will negatively influence customers’ perceptions of shopping eficiency.

## 5. Method

To test our model, we conducted two studies in a retail store laboratory, with di<sup>f</sup>erent types of customer tasks: (1) general (exploratory) browsing in which participants browsed around the retail store in a leisurely way and they were not asked to choose a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product (2) goal-directed shopping where the objective of the participants was to choose a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product. We varied the hardware and content design, as discussed earlier.

## 5.1. Participants and measurement

We recruited participants from a mailing list maintained by a retail laboratory. A targeted email was sent to individuals who had speci<sup>fi</sup>cally consented to be on the mailing list to receive advertisements and promotions from retail stores. In order for the list to be composed of a diverse population, the laboratory checked and organised the list to represent di<sup>f</sup>erent demographic characteristics (i.e., gender, age, ethnicity, education, income, and technology experience). The email invited individuals to participate in a study examining a new store design and to state their opinions and feedback. The participants committed 60 to 90 minutes for the study to visit the new store and complete a 30-minute questionnaire about their experience. Overall, we had 194 eligible participants in study 1 (general browsing) and 190 eligible participants in study 2 (goal-directed shopping). There were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences in demographic characteristics of participants across the two studies, and the participants were split almost equally between men and women, with an average age of 38.8 and a standard deviation of 8.4 in study 1 and an average age of 38.1 and a standard deviation of 8.2 in study 2. To operationalise the constructs in our study, we used existing scales–the items and sources are shown in Appendix A.

## 5.2. Setting, artifacts, tasks, and data collection procedure

The design of the retail store laboratory was intended to resemble a retail store in its layout. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the retail store laboratory was arranged as a mock storefront for “Acme Products.” Four shopping aisles with about 400 di<sup>f</sup>erent products falling into 100 product categories––such as electronics, GPS navigation systems, CDs, DVDs, books, and cleaning supplies–– were located on both sides of the aisles. To create a realistic store look, speci<sup>fi</sup>c arrangements––such as aisles, products on shelves, and end caps––were made and all areas were signposted to guide the participants along the aisles. These measures were taken to increase the external validity of our studies.

To provide the individuals with comprehensive instructions on how to participate, once entering the retail environment, each participant met with a retail store laboratory attendant, who was not directly involved in the research. Each participant was given 500 “Acme Dollars” to spend and was told to feel free to browse around the store as they would in their actual retail shopping trips. Each participant was given an iPhone 5 and instructed how to shop with their assigned applications–i.e., either a barcode scanner or an RFID reader. As an illustration, for the RFID reader, participants were told that the application “would allow you to obtain product content (i.e., product information, product reviews) if you place the phone within 6 inches of the product,” and to feel free to “use this application to aid you in your browsing and shopping.” Similar instructions were given to participants who received iPhones with barcode scanning functionality. They further were given a demonstration of using the artifacts.

As stated above, attempting to establish the generalisability of our theory, we performed two di<sup>f</sup>erent types of shopping studies (i.e., general browsing shopping vs. goal-directed shopping). Although we created a uniform study design and identical retail store laboratory setting across both studies, the context of the two studies was di<sup>f</sup>erent in terms of the shopping tasks being assigned to the participants. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst study (general browsing shopping), the participants were allowed to browse around the retail store environment without a pre-determined goal to purchase a particular product (Bloch, Sherrell, & Ridgway, 1986). Therefore, in accordance with the literature on retail browsing and customer shopping motivations (see Bloch, Ridgway, & Sherrell, 1989), by specifying no product categories, we provided a search context where participants could use the technology to gather information about various products irrespective of their need to buy them. These participants, nonetheless, were instructed to choose at least <sup>fi</sup>ve products that they were interested in buying. In the second study, participants were assigned a more focused task being goal-directed shopping (Van Osselaer & Janiszewski, 2012) in which shoppers should carry out brand evaluations and make choices within product categories that have been previously speci<sup>fi</sup>ed––i.e., a shopper visits the retailer to buy a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product. Thus, in this study, we asked participants to choose from alternatives in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c predetermined category–i.e., a portable GPS system––in which there were multiple brands with distinct features, being placed in a designated area in the retail store laboratory.

As mentioned previously, each participant in our studies was exposed to one of the two di<sup>f</sup>erent hardware designs––i.e., a barcode scanner or an RFID reader––and one of three di<sup>f</sup>erent content designs––i.e., product information, product reviews, or both product information and product reviews. Our hardware designs were consistent with the emerging technologies being explored by retailers, such as Walmart (Angrisani, 2013; Banjo, 2012) and Coop (Scandit, 2015), who have been examining mobile phone barcode scanners to enhance their customers in-store experience. RFID-enabled smartphones are another such emerging technology (Clark, 2012). With regard to our content design, actual product information and/or product reviews were transmitted to the device from a major retailer’s online store site.

Those participants who used the iPhones with barcode functionality needed to align the device with the barcode on the product packaging to receive the product content (i.e., product information and product reviews) on the iPhone’s screen because a barcode scanner requires optical line-of-sight to the barcode to capture its information. However, in the RFID reader condition, participants just needed to hold the device within close proximity (about 6 inches) of the product. All participants were randomly assigned to either barcode or RFID-enabled iPhones and the use of the shopping application was voluntary. The participants were given 45 minutes and 30 minutes to complete the shopping task in study 1 and study 2, respectively. After <sup>fi</sup>nishing with the shopping task, they <sup>fi</sup>lled out a 30-minute survey about their perceptions of the entire shopping experience.

We did not conduct manipulation checks commonly used in studies because the barcode and RFID use in our studies were concrete manipulations (see Perdue & Summers, 1986). In other words, there were no latent or higher-order constructs manipulated in our studies.

## 6. Results

Before discussing the results related to the measurement and structural models, we discuss the patterns of participants’ behaviour in our studies. On average, participants spent 34 minutes to complete their shopping tasks in our studies. We found that those using the RFID reader spent more time compared to participants using the barcode scanner. Participants using the barcode scanner scanned an average of 12 products and participants using the RFID readers scanned an average of 23 products. The average time for completing the survey and additional questions that served as <sup>fi</sup>llers (from the perspective of this research) was 25 minutes.

## 6.1. Measurement model

To estimate the model, we used partial least squares (PLS), a component-based structural equation modeling technique focusing on maximising the variance explained in estimating the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed model. We used the software package Smart-PLS 2.0 (Ringle, Wende, & Becker, 2005) and started our analysis by assessing whether pooling of the datasets (study 1 and study 2) was possible following the procedure outlined by Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, and Davis (2003). Venkatesh et al. (2003) pooled data across organisations by demonstrating that there was structural equivalence across data collected from di<sup>f</sup>erent organisations (i.e., there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant beta di<sup>f</sup>erences). Similarly, we pooled data across studies as we found structural equivalence of all relevant relationships. Our results suggested that it was appropriate to pool the datasets for our analysis.

We assessed the psychometric properties of the scales by examining the item loadings, internal consistency reliabilities (ICRs), and average variance extracted (AVE). Results obtained from the PLS measurement model indicated internal consistency and discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). All items loaded highest on each anticipated factor and above .70. Further, the loadings were greater than the cross-loadings (Hair, Anderson, Tatham, & Black, 1998). Reliabilities, descriptive statistics, correlations, and the square root of AVEs for study 1 and study 2 are shown in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. All ICRs were greater than .70, demonstrating internal consistency. Moreover, the results indicated further evidence of discriminant validity because no inter-construct correlations were greater than the square root of the AVEs

## 6.2. Hypothesis tests

The results of the structural model testing are shown in Table 5, with a summary in Figure 2. Hypothesis 1a suggested more favourable usability perceptions for participants using RFID readers than participants using barcode scanners. For this hypothesis, we found all path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant except for user interface graphics. Thus, hypothesis 1a was partially supported. Hypothesis 1b regarding the e<sup>f</sup>ect of content design on mobile application usability was fully supported in both our studies. Hypothesis 2 suggested a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of application content (i.e., application design and application utility) on information management (i.e., unauthorised access and errors). We found that that all of those path coe<sup>fi</sup>- cients in both studies were negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Thus, hypothesis 2 was fully supported. Hypothesis 3 suggested that user interaction (i.e., user interface input and user interface output) will have a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on interaction management (i.e., secondary use and collection). These negative relationships were fully supported in study 2. However, the negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of user interface input on collection and the negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of user interface output on secondary use were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in our <sup>fi</sup>rst study. Thus, we found partial support for hypothesis 3. Hypothesis 4 suggested that interface presentation (i.e., user interface graphics and user interface structure) will have a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on interaction management. However, the path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients related to this hypothesis were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant apart from interface structure on collection in study 1. Thus, hypothesis 4 was not supported. With regard to the e<sup>f</sup>ect of individuals privacy concerns on their shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency (i.e., product evaluation cost, product screening cost, and decision-making quality), both studies fully supported the proposed e<sup>f</sup>ects of information management on product screening and evaluation costs and decision-making quality. However, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of interaction management on shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency was not fully supported in either of our studies. Thus, hypothesis 5 was partially supported.

## 6.3. Tests for bias and robustness

We ran a series of tests to assess the possibility of bias and verify the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. A potential threat to the testing of our hypotheses was common method bias because we used survey data and our constructs in the model were measured using a similar method, and data were collected at the same time from the same individual. To minimise the threat of common method bias, we followed prior work (e.g., Carlson & Kacmar, 2000; Carlson & Perrewé, 1999) and created a latent variable (i.e., the method factor). This variable included all indicators in the model and we included the method factor as a predictor of all endogenous variables in the structural model, namely unauthorised access, errors, secondary use, collection, product evaluation cost, product screening cost, and decision-making quality. Unlike covariance-based SEM applications, such as LISREL, PLS does not produce goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t indexes for the model. Thus, following Subramani (2004), we performed a pseudo F-test to check whether the adding the method factor signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved the R-square. We used the method factor variable as a predictor of the usability variables, privacy concerns’ variables, and shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency variables. In each case, the increase in R-square was negligible: less than 3%. The concomitant pseudo F-statistic was always below 1 and non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The results suggested that none of the pseudo F-tests were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.05 level. We also found that all of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant paths in our model remained signi<sup>fi</sup>cant after controlling for this e<sup>f</sup>ect. Thus, we concluded that the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates in the model were not biased from common method variance.

## 7. Discussion

Recent research on privacy issues has refuted the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of preventive approaches to reducing individuals’ privacy concerns (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Xu & Bélanger, 2013) and cautioned privacy researchers against ad hoc approaches currently hampering the design of privacy enhancing tools (Xu et al., 2012).

However, little empirical work has taken a systematic approach to tackle this issue. Here, we used autoIDbased mobile shopping applications in two studies and sought to respond to recent calls for research on the privacy paradox (e.g., Dinev, 2014). We developed a model of rational cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis to better understand how rational customers disclose their information in exchange for expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from a well-designed mobile application. We designed and built autoIDbased mobile shopping applications and developed a framework that links mobile application usability to privacy concerns that in turn a<sup>f</sup>ect customers’ shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. To evaluate the mobile shopping applications and test our model, we conducted two studies and found empirical support for most of our hypotheses. Primarily, we hypothesised that high levels of application content reduce individuals’ concerns regarding information management in the context of autoID-based mobile shopping applications. Our results strongly supported this hypothesis suggesting that application content is an important driver for individuals’ information management concerns in the context of our work. We further hypothesised that the better the user interaction with the autoID-based mobile application, the less concerned customers would be in terms of their interaction management. Although this hypothesis was fully supported in study 2 (goal-directed), the results of study 1 (general browsing) partially supported it.

Search or shopping behaviour of customers in retail environments has been dichotomised into goal-directed and general browsing (exploratory), both of which can potentially result in a purchase (Janiszewski, 1998; Moe, 2003). Goal-directed shopping refers to customers shopping behaviour when they have a planned or particular product in their mind. Therefore, their search patterns are focused and directed toward making a shopping decision (Moe, 2003). General browsing (exploratory), in contrast, refers to customers’ shopping behaviour when they are less deliberate and possibly not even considering a purchase (Bloch et al., 1986; Moe, 2003). Hence, general browsing tends to be stimulusdirected rather than goal-directed (Moe, 2003). It has been noted that customers tend to have a utilitarian approach and higher levels of interactivity, involvement, and attention during goal-directed shopping (Novak, Ho<sup>f</sup>man, & Duhachek, 2003). In contrast, during general browsing, customers tend to have a hedonic approach and are less likely to be interactive, involved, and focused (Moe, 2003; Novak et al., 2003). Therefore, a relative lack of interactivity, involvement, and deliberation of users during general browsing can make the e<sup>f</sup>ect of user interaction on interaction management somewhat inconsequential. This could potentially explain the reason why the negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of user interaction on privacy concerns was partially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in study 1.

Contrary to our expectation, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of interface presentation on the interaction management component of privacy concerns (H4) was non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. In other words, neither of our studies produced evidence that e<sup>f</sup>ective presentation of graphics and overall organisation of an application in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ concerns over their interaction management. This can potentially be due to non-interactive nature of mobile applications’ interface presentation. For instance, subtleties of the animation (interface graphics) or top-to-bottom arrangements (interface structure) of a mobile application are not typically subject to change in reaction to users’ responses (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). Thus, such a lack of interactivity in mobile applications’ interface presentation may make it ine<sup>f</sup>ective in managing the interactional aspect of users’ privacy concerns.

We <sup>fi</sup>nally found that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of privacy concerns on shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency (H5) was partially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. That was due to the nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect of one of our privacy concerns constructs (i.e., collection) on shopping e<sup>fi</sup>- ciency. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in our studies, shoppers’ concerns about the amount of their information possessed by the retailer did not a<sup>f</sup>ect their product screening and evaluation costs and decision-making quality. A possible explanation for this non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship is that collection of users’ information, in and of itself, does not a<sup>f</sup>ect the potential sense of unease that individuals may be experiencing during their technology-mediated shopping. That is, the potential subsequent misuse of users information is the main source of the additional costs imposed on shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. For example, it has been argued that if users are aware of the retailer’s intended use of collecting their information, they will perceive the collection to be fair (Malhotra et al., 2004).

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

Our work makes several theoretical contributions. First, we add to the literature on privacy as we evaluated autoID-based mobile shopping applications and empirically studied customers’ privacy concerns toward these technologies in a retail context. To our knowledge, ours is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies that o<sup>f</sup>ered an alternative approach, here using artifact design, to overcoming customers’ privacy concerns. Leveraging a recent conceptualisation of mobile application usability, our work illustrates how autoID-based artifacts can be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to proactively reduce individuals’ privacy concerns in retail environments. There have been recent calls in the IS literature for “design and action research on privacy. As design science becomes an increasingly important area of research, IS researchers should consider the development of more (and easier to use) privacy protection tools for individuals, groups, organisations, and society” (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011, p. 1035). Our work responds to such calls and our proposed solutions can be used to design an actual privacy-enhancing artifact. Our solution is distinctive because it focuses on design, in contrast to the majority of prior privacy studies having o<sup>f</sup>ered to take preventive and precautionary measures, such as withholding information disclosure or restricting information access, as solutions to privacy concerns (Xu & Bélanger, 2013).

Second, and related to our <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution, we advance the extant body of research on privacy by developing and validating a model to elucidate the mechanism through which mobile application usabil ity principles could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce privacy concerns and increase shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The granularity of the mobile application usability conceptualisation provides insights in how technology design could a<sup>f</sup>ect individuals’ privacy concerns. For example, we found that application content and user interaction can alleviate the severity of individuals’ privacy concerns over their information management and interaction management, respectively. A reasonable explanation for this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that, simply stated, superior content and interactivity of a mobile application should reduce confusion and more e<sup>f</sup>ectively display product information and product reviews.

Third, we incorporate mobile application usability conceptualisation into the retail environment and build upon its applicability to the newest retail innovation. Hence, our study also extends the overall nomological network of IT-enabled retail stores. We built on Hoehle and Venkatesh (2015) work and examined the generalisability of the concept and instrument of mobile application usability by testing its e<sup>f</sup>ect on individuals privacy concerns. We found that mobile application usability is an important antecedent of individuals privacy concerns. Advances in theory building emphasise the importance of examining the generalisability of newly established models in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts as it provides a richer understanding of the context and more accurate interpretation of the theory (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Johns, 2006). This could be due to the fact that existing theories may break down, and such breakdowns are an opportunity for creating new knowledge (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007).

## 7.2. Practical implications

Several practical implications follow from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. From a design perspective, our work demonstrated that individuals’ privacy concerns can be reduced by e<sup>f</sup>ectively designing mobile applications following well-established usability principles. From the viewpoint of retailers, our results o<sup>f</sup>er signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value to pioneering retail stores exploring the autoID-based technologies to enhance customers’ shopping experiences. In the past, retailers have been struggling to propose a solution for their customers’ privacy concerns about autoID technologies. As mentioned earlier, mass protest campaigns against <sup>fi</sup>rms, such as Benetton, Tesco PLC, and Metro Group are examples of such customers’ dissatisfaction when the aforementioned retailers had announced or started using autoID technologies in their sales process. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated that mobile application usability can be used to decrease customers’ privacy concerns, and consequently, improve customers’ shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Another practical implication of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for retailers is that a customer’s shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency is not determined solely by a well-organised store set-up, a wide selection of products, or a quick checkout process in retail stores. But, customers’ privacy concerns incur signi<sup>fi</sup>cant costs to their product brokering activity and produce a harmful e<sup>f</sup>ect on the quality of their purchase decisions. Therefore, retailers are advised to take innovative privacy protection measures--e.g., pursuing mobility initiatives with high degree of usability--to improve their customers’ shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

## 7.3. Limitations and future research

Despite the above implications, our work has some limitations that can potentially serve as directions for future research. Controllability is crucial to experiments because they enable us to create precise conditions developed from our theory while holding other factors constant and thus, enhance the validity of our conclusions (Calder, Phillips, & Tybout, 1981). To maintain experimental control and consistency in our studies, we preinstalled product information and reviews in mobile applications and had participants use the mobile devices provided, rather than their own mobile phones. Although the participants were instructed to presume that the mobile phones issued to them were their own devices containing their data, it can be argued that the second privacy threat (i.e., personal information disclosure) that we discussed earlier would not be relevant to the participants in our experiments. However, it should be noted that such scenario-based experiments are common and considered reasonable in marketing research (e.g., Kim & Jang, 2014). As a trade-o<sup>f</sup>, we underscore that we achieved a high degree of internal validity using experiments and by manipulating hardware and content design.

We collected data from two sequential experiments that lacked random assignment of the participants to the two di<sup>f</sup>erent shopping conditions. As a result, we were unable to directly examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of shopping contexts/tasks on shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. However, prior HCI research has emphasised the importance of considering the nature of the task when evaluating individuals’ reactions to technology. Therefore, a potential avenue for future research is to enrich the literature by taking into account the interplay among various task characteristics, shopping outcomes, and manipulations of technology artifacts.

By linking artifact design to shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, we developed a nomological network around customers’ privacy concerns. There could be more nuanced interrelationships among privacy concerns and related constructs, such as trust and risk (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Smith et al., 2011), for which we did not account. These potential interrelationships, however, may enhance the proposed nomological network. Future research could also investigate and account for potential moderators that could play a role in an expanded nomological network--for example, cultural backgrounds (Hoehle et al., 2015) and technology adoption constructs (Venkatesh, Thong, & Xu, 2016)--linking design to shopping outcomes. Particularly, individuals’ attributes, such as self-e<sup>fi</sup>- cacy, mobile phone familiarity, and prior experience with the iOS, are variables that should be controlled, especially in future non-experimental studies. In addition, our research objectives focused on discovering customers’ reactions toward two technologies, namely barcode scanners and RFID readers. In other words, we intended to conduct a design science study comparing the technological artifacts in an experimental setting and we did not ask participants to evaluate artifacts regarding their speci<sup>fi</sup>c technological characteristics, such as the ease-of-scanning ability or potential information overload. Future research could extend our work and measure these features because measuring such technological characteristics could provide a more nuanced view and stronger <sup>fi</sup>ndings on these issues.

Given our research design, we additionally note some limitations in terms of the generalisability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For example, the RFID equipment was used in a retail scenario that did not involve many metal surfaces and the participants did not encounter major issues related to scanning. If retailers were to implement RFID tags in stores, customers could potentially pick up signals from multiple tags, which could negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceived usability of the mobile shopping applications. It would also be interesting to consider broadening the applicability of our artifacts to products such as fresh and processed food where safety is of utmost concern for consumers. For example, our results could help retailers present the origins of products to customers in retail stores. Thus, further research is required to establish the generalisability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in alternative environments. Details on the robustness of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings will help retailers to implement the system and ensure that their speci<sup>fi</sup>c store environment and product assortment allow the anticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to be realised. Future research could also evaluate if hierarchies or di<sup>f</sup>erent screen layouts could be used to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence usability perceptions. This could potentially lead to situations in which product information in combination with product reviews are e<sup>f</sup>ectively presented, thereby positively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing usability perceptions. The outcome of such studies could be an interface design that displays product information coupled with product reviews without increasing cognitive load (e.g., by using graphics, summaries, collapsed regions).

Finally, one crucial aspect of research where new technologies are introduced is the potential for expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation and how it may impact the outcomes. As we examined initial perceptions related to the technologies after exposure, additional work could be based on measuring perceptions both before and after exposure to the technology or, in a longitudinal design, measure expectations and experiences (here, the constructs of interest could be usability and privacy)--these could then be used together to predict outcomes (see Brown, Venkatesh, & Goyal, 2012, 2014; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010).

## 8. Conclusions

Drawing on e multidimensional developmental theory of privacy (Laufer & Wolfe, 1977) and conducting two retail store laboratory studies, we theorised and studied the e<sup>f</sup>ect of mobile application usability (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015) on in-store customers privacy concerns of autoID-based mobile shopping assistance artifacts. To test our hypotheses, we conducted two studies to better understand individuals perceptions of autoID technologies. We observed mitigated levels of privacy concerns and consequently elevated levels of shopping e<sup>fi</sup>ciency resulting from well-designed autoID-based mobile shopping applications. We complement prior work, that has heavily focused on o<sup>f</sup>ering preventive measures to tackle the issue of privacy concerns, by examining the role of usability in this context.
