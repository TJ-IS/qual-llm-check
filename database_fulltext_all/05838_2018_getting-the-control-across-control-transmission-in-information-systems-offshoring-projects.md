## Abstract

Offshoring of information systems (IS) projects has become a widespread global practice. While prior research suggests that controlling, and communicating with, offshore vendors represent key managerial challenges, the topic of how control is communicated, or transmitted, from client to vendor has been widely neglected. Our study focuses on control transmission in the critical relationship between client and offshore vendor managers, and its impact on the performance of IS offshoring projects. Drawing on a matched‐pair survey with 172 client and vendor managers from 86 projects, our results provide several new insights to the IS literature: First, we find that both behaviour and outcome control have the capacity to be transmitted consistently in lateral IS offshore project relationships. Second, our results show that consistent transmission translates into a performance effect only for outcome control. Third, we find that high‐performing offshore projects are characterized by both greater control transmission consistency and greater use of outcome control compared to low‐performing projects. In sum, our study extends theory on IS project control by highlighting that effective control of IS offshoring projects is not only about selecting proper controls but also about ensuring that, as a controller, you get the control across to the controllee.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Since the early 2000s, outsourcing information systems (IS) projects to offshore vendors (ie, IS offshoring) has become an established practice for improving productivity and competitiveness in client organizations (King & Torkzadeh, 2008). In 2013, the IS offshoring market exceeded USD 100 billion in revenues (Oshri, Kotlarsky, & Willcocks, 2015) with continued yearly growth estimated between 8% and 12% (Willcocks, Lacity, & Craig, 2015). However, despite the many potential benefits of IS offshoring, including significantly lower labour costs and access to a highly qualified labour pool in countries such as India (eg, Rottman & Lacity, 2004), client firms have often reported failures and disappointments (Ranganathan & Balaji, 2007; Remus & Wiener, 2009). Prior studies estimate that as many as 50% of all IS offshoring projects fail to realize expected benefits (Fabriek, van den Brand, Brinkkemper Harmsen, & Helms, 2008; Nakatsu & Iacovou, 2009; Philip, Schwabe, & Wende, 2010). In addition to contractua arrangements, relational trust, and collaborative and problem‐solving practices (King & Torkzadeh, 2008; Rai, Maruping, & Venkatesh, 2009), existing research highlights the importance of the client's ability to effectively control, and communicate with, the offshore vendor for the success of IS offshoring projects (Hanisch & Corbitt, 2007; Iacovou & Nakatsu, 2008; Ranganathan & Balaji, 2007; Remus & Wiener, 2009).

By exercising control, the client (controller) attempts to influence the behaviours of the vendor (controllee) to ensure goal alignment (Kirsch, Sambamurthy, Ko, & Purvis, 2002; Ouchi, 1980). Earlier research has accumulated theoretically important and practically relevant knowledge on control portfolios and their antecedents, effects, and dynamics for (offshore) outsourced IS projects (eg, Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2010; Tiwana & Keil, 2009). Research also points to the critical importance of the relationship between client and offshore vendor managers, acting as boundary spanners and channelling control from client to vendor (Mahnke, Wareham, & Bjorn‐Andersen, 2008; Rai et al., 2009; Zimmermann, Raab, & Zanotelli, 2013). While we know that this relationship is of particular importance and that some control modes are more likely to be effective than others in off shore project contexts (eg, Gopal & Gosain, 2010), we still do not know much about the communication of controls from client to vendor managers. This shortcoming stems from the fact that IS project control research has historicall focused on structural control arrangements, and only recently broadened its scope to address how IS project control is exercised (Heumann, Wiener, Remus, & Mähring, 2015; Narayanaswamy, Grover, & Henry, 2013; Wiener, Mähring, Remus, & Saunders, 2016). Contributing to this “turn” in IS project control research, we draw on the concept of con trol transmission (Ouchi, 1978) to study the consistency with which controls are communicated, or transmitted, from client managers (controllers) to offshore vendor managers (controllees), and the relationship between consistent con trol transmission and IS offshoring project performance.

While the complexity inherent in IS offshoring projects seems to make them particularly prone to control transmission challenges, this topic has so far only been studied in the context of hierarchical superior‐subordinate relationships (Ouchi, 1978). In IS offshore project settings, control has to be transmitted across organizational boundaries (Tiwana & Keil, 2009), and overcome cultural, geographic, language, and time zone differences (Dibbern, Winkler, & Heinzl, 2008; Keil, Im, & Mähring, 2007), increasing the likelihood for miscommunication and misunderstanding between client and vendor managers. Our study therefore addresses the following research questions:

## 1. How well do different control modes transmit in lateral IS offshore project relationships?

## 2. How is consistent control transmission related to the performance of IS offshoring projects?

We argue that understanding control transmission and its performance implications is highly important for key stakeholders of IS offshoring projects and for client managers in particular. Prior literature suggests that client managers change controls and/or add new controls when experiencing project performance problems (eg, Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gregory, Beck, & Keil, 2013; Remus & Wiener, 2012). However, the actual problem may not be the existing controls but rather the inconsistent transmission of these controls. Therefore, our current understanding of IS project control can, for example, wrongfully lead client managers to increase the amount of control, while they instead should ensure that existing controls are consistently received by the controllee.

We pursue our research questions through quantitative analyses of matched‐pair data encompassing 172 respondents from 86 IS offshoring projects. Our data capture formal control transmission between client manager (control ler) and vendor manager (controllee), brokering the relationship between the 2 organizations (Mahnke et al., 2008) and thus playing a critical role in the management and success of IS offshoring projects (Rai et al., 2009). Our focus is on transmission of formal control, ie, behaviour and outcome control (Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch, 1996; Ouchi, 1979 Rustagi, King, & Kirsch, 2008; Tiwana, 2008). Formal controls tend to dominate the control portfolio in offshore outsourced projects (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Tiwana & Keil, 2009), and informal controls are not necessarily transmitted through control dyads in the same way as formal controls (Ouchi, 1978; Wiener, Remus, Heumann, & Mähring, 2015).

The article is structured as follows: We first review control theory and earlier literature on controlling IS offshoring projects. We then introduce the concept of control transmission and develop theoretical arguments concerning the transmission of formal control and its impact on project performance. Next, we describe the research methodology, present the survey results, conduct follow‐up analyses, and account for study limitations. Finally, we discuss key implications for theory and practice in light of prior literature.

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | IS project control

Consistent with existing research on IS project control (eg, Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 1996; Rustagi et al., 2008; Tiwana, 2010), our study views control as any attempt by an individual to influence the actions and behaviours of another individual to attain organizational objectives (Cardinal, 2001; Flamholtz, Das, & Tsui, 1985; Ouchi, 1979). Control is often seen as dyadic in the sense that there is a controller (eg, the client manager for an IS offshoring project) and a controllee (eg, the offshore vendor manager) (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 2004). The controller uses a set of control mechanisms to regulate or adjust the behaviours of the controllee (Das & Teng, 1998; Kirsch, 1996; Ouchi, 1979). These mechanisms constitute a portfolio of controls (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 1997) and are typically categorized into formal and informal controls. Mechanisms used to exercise formal control are explicit in nature (eg, project plans and job descriptions), whereas informal control mechanisms (eg, peer pressure) represent implicit determinants of behaviour (Jaworski, 1988). As noted above, the focus of this study is o formal control, which is commonly divided into 2 modes: behaviour and outcome control (Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch 1996; Ouchi, 1979).

When exercising behaviour control, the controller prescribes specific rules, methods, and processes (eg, software development methodology and reporting processes) and rewards the controllee based on the extent to which she fol lows these prescriptions (Kirsch, 1997). Behaviour control can be effectively used when work procedures leading to desired outcomes are known and observable (Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch, 1996). Outcome control is exercised when the controller specifies interim and final outputs (eg, project milestones and budgets), regardless of the process b which these outputs are achieved (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996). To effectively implement outcome control, the controller needs to be in the position to define targeted project outcomes and compare them to actual outcomes (Kirsch, 1997). The controllee is then rewarded (or sanctioned) based on how well she meets prespecified targets. Thus, outcome control is concerned with what to accomplish, while behaviour control is concerned with how to con duct project tasks (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch et al., 2002)

Early studies focused almost exclusively on control choices in internal IS development projects (eg, Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002), while recent research directs more attention to the performance effects of such choices in IS outsourcing and offshoring projects (eg, Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2008, 2010; Tiwana & Keil, 2009). Adding to this, there is currently an emergent interest in how IS project control is enacted through the interaction between controller and controllee (eg, Gregory & Keil, 2014; Narayanaswamy et al., 2013; Wiener et al., 2016). Through its focus on control transmission, our study follows and extends this recent “turn.”

## 2.2 | Control in IS offshoring projects

On an overview level, IS offshoring research primarily concerns the offshoring decision and the management of off shore projects (Dibbern, Goles, Hirschheim, & Jayatilaka, 2004; Wiener, Vogel, & Amberg, 2010). Over the last 2 decades, the focus has shifted from the decision stage to the management stage (ibid). Core research topics regarding the management of IS offshoring projects include the governance and control of offshore vendors (eg, Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Gregory et al., 2013), the management of offshore relationships (eg, Levina & Vaast, 2008; Zimmermann et al., 2013), and the transfer of knowledge between client and vendor (eg, Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Williams, 2011; Zimmermann & Ravishankar, 2014). Common for these studies is that the quality of the relationship and how it is conducted are viewed as essential aspects of offshoring as an organizational activity. Relat edly, research on the success and failure of IS offshore projects identifies the client's capability to effectively contro and communicate with the vendor as critical success factors (eg, Iacovou & Nakatsu, 2008; King & Torkzadeh, 2008; Ranganathan & Balaji, 2007; Remus & Wiener, 2009).

Against this backdrop, our study addresses a key aspect of effectively managing IS offshoring projects by focusing on the communication, or transmission, of formal controls in lateral client‐vendor relationships. Relevant to the focus of our study, prior IS project control research shows inconclusive results regarding the effectiveness of forma control in offshore‐outsourced projects. For example, Srivastava and Teo (2012) find no significant influence of formal control (referred to as “mechanistic governance”) on the performance of IS offshoring projects. Tiwana (2008) finds that both behaviour and outcome control independently enhance project performance, whereas anothe study by Tiwana (2010) suggests that only behaviour control, but not outcome control, enhances project ambidexterity (which captures project performance in an “alignment” dimension and combines it with an “adaptiveness” dimension). In contrast, Gopal and Gosain (2010) find only limited support for the positive effect of behaviour control on IS offshore project performance; rather, their analysis indicates the relative strength of outcome control over behaviour control in ensuring project performance. Since these studies have not included control transmission, it is possible that differences in how well control is transmitted could be an unknown factor behind inconclusive results concerning control effectiveness.

Tiwana and Keil (2009) take a step towards exploring this idea in response to finding that, for their dataset, control increases performance in internal projects but not in outsourced projects, in spite of controllers reporting greater use of control in outsourced settings. To explain this anomaly, Tiwana and Keil introduce the conceptual distinction between attempted and realized control: attempted control consists of “the control mechanisms that the controlle implements in a given project, independent of whether or how they are exercised,” whereas realized control refers to “the extent to which the controller is able to successfully exercise a given control mechanism” (Tiwana & Keil, 2009, p. 13). While they do not empirically test for the existence and consequences of differences between attempted and realized control, their conceptual argument supports the idea that control transmission could be a potential facto behind the inconsistent results in prior studies.

Interestingly, up until now, the concept of control transmission (ie, control given compared to control received) and in particular its consistency, has been neglected in studies in the context of IS offshoring projects. This may b explained by the implicit assumption in the current literature that control mechanisms given by a client manager are received the same way by the vendor counterpart, as well as by the single‐informant and controller‐focused approach most commonly used in collecting data on controls (eg, Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2008, 2010; Tiwana & Keil, 2007).

In the next section, we elaborate on the control transmission concept and develop hypotheses regarding th transmission of behaviour and outcome control, and its performance impact, in IS offshoring projects

## 3 | THEORETICAL DEVELOPMENT

## 3.1 | Control transmission

As described above, control transmission builds on the distinction between control given and control received (Ouchi, 1978). In line with Ouchi, we define control given as the controller's (client manager) exercised control, and contro received as the controllee's (vendor manager) perception of exercised control. Therefore, both the client manager's and the vendor manager's control understandings relate to the same set of control actions, namely, those exercised b the client manager.

A key difference between Tiwana and Keil's (2009) concepts of attempted and realized control on the one hand, and the concept of control transmission on the other, is Ouchi's clear distinction between control given and received (Ouchi, 1978). WhileTiwana and Keil (2009) focus the controller as the sole actor, and use the somewhat overlapping terms “implement” to describe attempted control and “exercise” to describe realized control, Ouchi's approach allows for a communicational view of control transmission where the goal is for control to reach the controllee, which is a fundamental prerequisite for control to be effective (Wiener et al., 2016). Ouchi's (1978) view also allows us to approach the crucial difference between what controllers give and what controllees receive.<sup>1</sup> With this view, the relationship between control given and control received determines the consistency in control transmission, as indicated by a positive and significant relationship between control given and received (Ouchi, 1978).

Evans (1975) and Ouchi (1978) suggest that formal control gets distorted as it is transmitted between controller and controllee in a hierarchical setting. However, so far, it is still unclear how the problem of control transmission translates to lateral IS offshore relationships, where the controller has no formal position of authority over th controllee (Kirsch et al., 2002), and control has to be transmitted across firm boundaries (Tiwana & Keil, 2009) and over distance (Dibbern et al., 2008).

## 3.2 | Control transmission in IS offshoring projects

Although behaviour and outcome control follow the same basic steps (Eisenhardt, 1985; Kirsch, 1996, 2004), they differ in their antecedent conditions and characteristics (see Table 1). Taking into account the mode‐specific conditions and characteristics as well as the specific attributes of the lateral control relationship between the client manager and the offshore vendor manager, we argue that both behaviour and outcome control have the capacity to transmit consistently in such relationships.

Behaviour control is considered to be more flexible (or adjustable) than outcome control. For instance, when specifying work processes, the controller can usually choose from a variety of alternative means for achieving desired outcomes. Thus, depending on the specific control context, she can select the most suitable process. Behaviour control is also “richer” in the sense that it is able to capture a wide range of activities. In a hierarchical setting, Ouchi (1978) finds poor interlevel transmission consistency for behavioural control, which he explains with the argument that one “cannot expect the [senior management] to be aware of the subtleties of behavior which are valued” on an operationa level (ibid, p. 175). In lateral client‐vendor control relationships, however, the client manager typically represents the client's IT department and works closely together with the vendor manager on the same project task and at the same hierarchical level. The client manager can thus be expected to possess the knowledge required to tailor behaviour control mechanisms to local differences and needs (Kirsch et al., 2002; Ouchi, 1978). The close interaction between the client and the vendor manager is likely to allow for harnessing the flexibility and richness of behavioura controls and to facilitate their consistent transmission to the controllee

Outcome control is often quantifiable and thus more comparable across hierarchical levels and organizational units (Ouchi, 1978). The quantifiability of targeted outcomes not only facilitates the controller's ability to measure these outcomes but also facilitates her ability to communicate controls. Consequently, the consistent transmission of out come control seems to be less dependent on the organizational context, ie, whether outcome controls are transmitte in hierarchical superior‐subordinate or lateral client‐vendor relationships (cf Tiwana & Keil, 2009)

In addition, controllers and controllees operating at the client‐vendor interface of an IS offshoring project often develop a close working relationship (Levina & Vaast, 2005). In line with the relational governance view, social interaction between the client and the vendor manager facilitate the development of a shared understanding, shared norms and values, and mutual trust (Fischer, Huber, & Dibbern, 2011). Such interaction, of course, also includes the transmission of formal control mechanisms, and therefore the development of a consistent understanding of the behaviour and outcome controls in use. Moreover, trust along with shared norms and values promote joint problem solving (Uzzi, 1997) as well as agreement and mutual adjustment of applied control mechanisms (Goo, Kishore, Rao, & Nam, 2009; Poppo & Zenger, 2002), thereby fostering a shared understanding of the controls being used. ## 3.3 | Control transmission and IS offshoring project performance

Earlier literature is inconclusive regarding the performance implications of control transmission consistency. Whil previous studies make the implicit assumption that formal controls must be transmitted consistently from controlle to controllee to enhance performance, they either do not test this assumption empirically (eg, Tiwana & Keil, 2007, 2009) or find no empirical support for it (Ouchi, 1978). The following hypothesis therefore focuses on the relationship between the consistent transmission of formal control modes and the performance of IS offshoring projects, which is expected to provide us with a more nuanced understanding of the effectiveness of behaviour and outcome control i lateral IS offshore project relationships.

While behaviour control is often found effective in in‐house IS contexts (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Tiwana & Keil, 2009), its effectiveness in the specific context of IS offshoring projects is disputed. Because of offshore distance challenges (Dibbern et al., 2008) as well as the relative absence of preexisting information channels and processes (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003), it can be difficult and costly for the client manager to monitor and evaluate th behaviour of the vendor manager and thus to effectively exercise behaviour control in offshore project settings. Put differently, even if the offshore vendor manager receives the behaviour controls given by the client manager (ie, if behaviour control is transmitted consistently), it will still be difficult for the client manager to assess whether her vendor counterpart acts upon the given controls. This is consistent with earlier empirical studies that do not find a significant effect of behaviour control on IS offshore proiect performance (eg, Gopal & Gosain, 2010)

On the other hand, the effective use of outcome control seems to be largely independent on whether the project is embedded in an in‐house or an outsourced/offshored context (Tiwana & Keil, 2009). Specifically, the measurability o desired project outcomes—the key antecedent condition of outcome control (see Table 1 above)—does not require visibility of distant vendor behaviours (Tiwana & Keil, 2009), making it easier for the client manager to effectivel enact outcome controls by specifying and evaluating vendor deliverables. We therefore expect that, if outcome controls are transmitted consistently from the client manager to the vendor manager, they will result in a performanceenhancing effect. This is in line with the observation in earlier literature that control portfolios in offshore outsourced IS projects are dominated by outcome controls (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). Moreover, in contract‐based contro relationships, vendor managers are likely to pay particular attention to adhering to client‐specified outcomes, such as delivery dates and performance levels, given that nonadherence to these outcome targets is often associated with contractual penalties (Tiwana & Keil, 2009). Adding to this, previous studies offer considerable empirical evidence that outcome control has a significant and positive effect on the performance of IS offshoring projects (Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2008). On this basis, we expect that the consistent transmission of outcome control, but not of behaviour control, shows a positive relationship to project performance. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H3. Consistent transmission of outcome control between the client and vendor manager is positively asso ciated with the performance of IS offshore projects.

In the following, we describe the applied research methodology and then test the hypotheses, first through our main analysis, then through a post hoc analysis eliciting further results.

## 4 | METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 | Data collection

We developed matched‐pair survey instruments to test our hypotheses. Client managers (controllers) were asked about the extent to which they give behaviour and outcome control within the examined projects and evaluated project performance. In our study, the client is seen as the assessor of project outcomes. This is because the client is the party who accepts and approves the results of a project (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). The client is also close to the context where the delivered artefact is used and therefore in a better position to assess project performance (Tiwana & Keil, 2009). Vendor managers (controllees) were surveyed on the extent to which they receive behaviour and outcome control from their client counterpart. Client and vendor managers supplied information about their professional background. Moreover, project executives provided us with general project information. This multiinformant approach reduces the threat of common rater bias, a major source of common method biases (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). To host the survey instruments, a website was launched. We also prepared paper‐based questionnaires to eliminate coverage error (Schaefer & Dillman, 1998).

We used a convenience sample to collect the survey data. To identify appropriate IS offshoring projects and respondents, we contacted management executives of client and offshore vendor firms by e‐mail and phone. The executives were professional acquaintances of one of the authors. The use of this known‐sponsor approach (Patton, 1990) enhanced our ability to ensure the appropriateness of the ultimate respondents and resulted in immediate legitimacy and credibility of the research team and study (Rustagi et al., 2008). If an executive agreed to participate, she or he was asked to nominate suitable projects and respondents. To ensure the quality of the survey data, projects and respondents had to satisfy 3 criteria for inclusion in the sample: First, offshore projects either had to have been completed within no more than 12 months or had to be underway for at least 3 months and already reached at least one critical milestone. Second, projects had to allow access to both the client manager and her/his vendor counterpart. Third, client and vendor members of a dyad must have had operated in their roles for at least 2 months. These criteria ensured that projects included had progressed to a reasonable maturity and increased the reliability of the participants' responses.

As an a priori strategy to minimize nonresponse error, we used Dillman's (2007) Tailored Design Method. Wit this method, multiple contacts with the target population are made to maximize response. The executives contacted invited a total of 210 client and vendor representatives to fill in the questionnaire. In all, 189 respondents (92 clients and 97 vendors) participated in our study, resulting in a response rate of 90%. To form one data record for eac matched pair, the matching client and vendor data records were joined through a unique ID. Nonpaired data records were dropped from the analysis, resulting in a final sample size of 86 dyads. Follow‐up communications with non participants did not reveal any trends or reasons that would point towards a non‐response bias

## 4.2 | Construct measures

All latent variables were measured reflectively with multiple items, following the approach of previous studies (eg Kirsch et al., 2002). All items were operationalized at the unit of analysis, the client‐vendor dyad representing one IS offshoring project, and rated on 5‐point Likert scales using “strongly agree” and “strongly disagree” anchors. To measure formal control given and received, we adopted Kirsch et al. (2002) items for behaviour and outcome control, Sim ilar items were also used in other key IS project control studies (Tiwana, 2008, 2010; Tiwana & Keil, 2007, 2009). Project performance was measured using 4 items adapted from prior IS research (Banker & Kemerer, 1992; Kirsch, 1996; Kumar & Bjorn‐Andersen, 1990). We conceptualized project performance as consisting of efficiency and quality aspects (Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Högl & Gemünden, 2001; Wallace, Keil, & Rai, 2004). While the efficiency dimension addresses whether a project is on budget and time, ie, process performance (eg, Nidumolu & Subramani, 2003), the quality dimension covers the degree to which a project meets client requirements and adheres to IS standards (Kirsch, 1996). In line with related studies (eg, Tiwana, 2008; Tiwana & Keil, 2009), we combined efficiency and quality measures into one single construct, enabling us to analyse performance effects on an aggregated level. We also checked for one‐dimensional control effects on project efficiency and quality (Gopal & Gosain, 2010). As noted, we follow Ouchi's (1978) approach to measure consistency in formal control transmission. With this approach consistent control transmission is indicated by a positive and significant relationship between control given and control received. Correspondingly, we use the interaction effect of control given and received on IS offshoring project performance to model and measure the link between control transmission consistency and project performance Using interaction terms is a common approach to analysing assessment congruence and differences between individ uals (eg, Deluga, 1998; Furnham & Stringfield, 1994).

Construct measures were pretested and refined by 5 offshore (project) managers and 6 experienced researchers. In addition, we conducted a pilot study with 8 client and 3 vendor respondents working in a long‐term IS offshoring arrangement between a multinational client and an Indian vendor. The pilot resulted in minor adaptations in the word ing of some items. Respondents in the pilot study were not included in the main sample. An overview of the construc items is shown in Table A1 in Appendix A.

To control for the influence of the “manager's expertise” (Ouchi, 1978) on the use and reception of formal control modes, we included the client's offshore experience as a control variable in our research model. Two additional contro variables theoretically related to project performance were also included in the model to account for alternative explanations: project size (Pressman, 2001) and prior interactions (Ethiraj, Kale, Krishnan, & Singh, 2005). Both variables were estimated by asking client executives to classify the personnel expenditure of the project (proxy for project size and the client firm's experience with the offshore vendor (proxy for prior interactions).

To assess the reliability and validity of the measurement model, we used the partial least squares (PLS) pathweighting scheme with 1000 iterations. We transformed our model into 2 submodels: model A and model B, focusing on the transmission and effectiveness of outcome control and of behaviour control, respectively. While the mode split did not affect the hypothesis test results, it helped lower sample size requirements and boost predictive power (Cohen, 1992). According to Hair, Hult, Ringle, and Sarstedt (2014), a PLS model with 5 predictors, a minimum R<sup>2</sup> of 25%, and predictive power of 80% would require a minimum of 70 observations. We also conducted a power analysis with G\*Power (Faul, Erdfelder, Buchner, & Lang, 2009), which confirmed that our data sample of 86 (paired) observations meets the minimum sample‐size requirements.

For both models, item reliability was verified through an analysis of item loadings. Following Hair et al. (2014), we only deleted items with outer loadings between 0.4 and 0.7 when this led to an increase of composite reliability or average variance extracted (AVE) above the suggested thresholds of 0.6 and 0.5, respectively. As a result, we had to remove 2 outcome control items from model A and one project performance item from model B (see Table A1). All other items can be considered practically significant (Hair, Tatham, Anderson, & Black, 1998). The measurement models also showed adequate construct reliability, which is indicated by composite‐reliability measures ranging from 0.713 (behaviour control given) to 0.854 (project performance) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) Table B1 (in Appendix B) displays the correlations between the main constructs. The boldface diagonal cells are the square root of the AVE, which is a measure of the variance shared between a construct and its items. Each construct has an AVE of greater than 0.5, indicating convergent validity (ibid). Moreover, each construct shares more variance with its assigned items than with any other construct (see off‐diagonal cells in Table B1) and cross‐loadings are lower than within‐construct loadings, establishing discriminant validity (Hulland, 1999). We also assessed the heterotrait‐monotrait ratio of correlations for both submodels (Henseler et al., 2015). All heterotraitmonotrait values were lower than the most conservative cut‐off value of 0.85 (see Table B2 in Appendix B), confirming discriminant validity.

Finally, pairwise correlations did not reveal any exceptionally correlated variables (Bagozzi, Yi, & Phillips, 1991) indicating low risk of common method bias. T tests on all main constructs comparing early (first 30) and late (last 30) respondents showed no significant differences (see Table B3 in Appendix B), providing assurance against nonresponse bias (Armstrong & Overton, 1977).

## 4.3 | Descriptive statistics

In total, we collected data from 86 IS offshoring projects and subprojects of large‐scale offshore arrangements in 17 client organizations, representing multiple industries (eg, aerospace, energy, health care, IT, and manufactur ing). All client firms operated from German‐speaking countries (12, 4, and 1 from Germany, Switzerland, and Austria, respectively). Fourteen firms were large for‐profit organizations; the other 3 firms were medium‐sized enterprises.

## 5 | ANALYSES AND RESULTS

We transformed our research model into a structural equation model using SmartPLS 3 (Ringle, Wende, & Becker, 2015) with a bootstrap size of 5000 samples. Partial least squares path modelling is an appropriate choice when the research focus is on predicting key target constructs (eg, Gefen, Rigdon, & Straub, 2011; Hair, Ringle, & Sarstedt 2011; Ringle, Sarstedt, & Straub, 2012). This fits the focus of our study, where the key target construct is IS off shore project performance. Furthermore, the PLS estimation method is “remarkably stable even at low sample sizes” (Gefen et al., 2011, p. A3; cf Hair et al., 2011) and “provides precise estimates [even] in situations with extremel non‐normal data” (Ringle et al., 2012, p. viii). For our dataset, this suggests that PLS is more reliable than covariance‐based structured equation modelling (using software such as Amos and LISREL) or traditional regression analysis.

As shown in Table 3, behaviour control given is positively and significantly associated with behaviour control received (ß = 0.256, P value <.05), providing support for H2. However, the analysis results did not show a significant path between outcome control given and outcome control received (ß = 0.154, ns). (This will be further explored in our post hoc analysis.) Interestingly, the analysis also revealed that outcome control received (ß = 0.347, P value <.01) is significantly and positively related to the performance of IS offshore projects, whereas behaviour control received (ß = −0.034, ns) is not. ## 6 | DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

The study's objectives were to examine how formal control modes transmit in lateral IS offshore project relationships, and how their consistent transmission is related to project performance in this context. In our theory development, we postulated that both behaviour and outcome control have the capacity to transmit consistentl in offshore client‐vendor relationships but that only the consistent transmission of outcome control positively affects the performance of IS offshoring projects. Using dyadic data from 86 offshore projects, we were able to find support for our hypotheses, as specified in Table 6.

Before discussing the study results, three limitations of our study need to be mentioned: First, while our datase covers a large variety of IS offshoring projects and client industries, including aerospace, energy, health care, IT, and manufacturing, the geographical range is limited. Specifically, our data encompass client firms operating from Germanspeaking countries only (all clients were based in Germany, Switzerland, or Austria) and vendor firms predominantly operating from India (about 80% of the vendors<sup>3</sup>). Second, drawing on Ouchi's (1978) seminal work, we use the interaction term between control given and control received to measure control transmission consistency, which is a common approach to analysing congruence between individuals (Deluga, 1998; Furnham & Stringfield, 1994). An alternative approach would have been to compute the difference scores between control given and control received. However, prior literature points to several analytical and conceptual issues related to creating a new construct from difference scores, including ambiguous score interpretation, proper treatment of the direction of difference scores, and reduced construct reliability (eg, Johns, 1981). We therefore decided to stay true to Ouchi's approach. Third, our research model explains approximately 25% (R<sup>2</sup> = 24.94%) of the variance in offshore project performance. Prior studies have identified several factors besides formal control that impact the performance of IS offshore projects, such as client participation and differences in work practices (Rai et al., 2009). The focus of our study, however, was not to fully explain variance in project per formance but rather to explore the role of control transmission consistency in lateral client‐vendor relationships

## 6.1 | Contributions and implications for research

The findings of our study represent three contributions with considerable implications for research. Our first contribution lies in the discovery of how formal control transmits in lateral, client‐vendor relationships and how transmission consistency is related to project performance. This topic has not been addressed in prior studies. Ouchi's (1978) study of control in hierarchical control relationships finds that outcome control is less susceptible to transmission “loss” while behaviour control exhibits almost no consistency in the transmission from controller to controllee. In contrast, our study shows that in lateral control relationships between client and vendor managers, both behaviour control and outcome control have the capacity to transmit consistently. The analysis results thus support our theoriz ing about the higher propensity for controllers in lateral relationships to understand and adapt to controllees and their local context (cf Goo et al., 2009; Levina & Vaast, 2005; Uzzi, 1997). However, our analysis also revealed that contro given and received might not always be strongly related because of inconsistent control transmission in lowperforming projects. Therefore, caution has to be exercised in using only controller‐reported data on control (given)

Furthermore, following control theory, Ouchi's (1978) study carries a strong assumption that control transmission inconsistency invites agency problems and negatively affects organizational performance. Similarly, Tiwana and Kei (2009) assume that differences between attempted and realized control should be minimized in order for the contro relationship to be effective. Our study, by finding that formal controls transmit well in high‐performing projects but not in low‐performing projects, provides first empirical evidence for the previously missing link between control transmission consistency and performance. This represents a novel finding in relation to both organizational control theory and the IS project control literature.

Our second contribution concerns formal control use and effectiveness patterns in IS offshoring projects. We find no performance impact of behaviour control in our study, Given the inconclusive results of earlier empirical studies a to whether behaviour control has a positive impact (Tiwana, 2008, 2010) or no impact (Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Srivastava & Teo, 2012; Tiwana & Keil, 2007, 2009)<sup>4</sup> on the performance of (offshore) outsourced IS projects, the results of our study strengthen the verisimilitude of the earlier studies that found behaviour control ineffective in offshore contexts. This is also in line with related studies that caution against client intervention in vendor processes (Levina & Ross, 2003). Due to economies of scope, vendors usually possess best practices and standard processes that are most suited to the offshore project context. Hence, forcing the vendor to change these well‐established proce dures might be detrimental to proiect performance. We further find a positive relationship between outcome contro and offshore project performance. This supports findings from several studies on the effectiveness of outcome con trol in IS outsourcing and offshoring projects (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2008). Also, our findings strengthen findings from earlier studies that have found outcome control to be more effective than behaviour control in offshore project settings (Gopal & Gosain, 2010).

By extending our analysis, we were also able to reveal important findings about control usage patterns across low‐ and high‐performing projects. Specifically, we find that client managers in high‐performing projects rely to a significantly greater extent on outcome control than do managers in low‐performing projects. Furthermore, we find that client managers use significantly more outcome control than behaviour control in high‐performing projects, which is not the case for low‐performing projects. While earlier studies have reported a preference for outcome control among (offshore) outsourcing clients (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Tiwana & Keil, 2009), our study explicitly connects thi pattern to high‐performing projects.

Our third contribution integrates the control transmission phenomenon with previously studied aspects of con trol, ie, the antecedents and effects of control choices (Wiener et al., 2016), by highlighting that superior project performance requires both appropriate control selection and consistent control transmission. Put differently, as shown b our extended analysis, neither control selection nor control transmission by itself promotes project performance. For example, we find that behaviour control transmits well but does not impact performance, and we also find that low performing projects still use relatively high levels of outcome control (albeit less so than high‐performing projects) while suffering from inferior control transmission.

These findings have important implications for research on IS project control. Our study shows that control transmission problems occur not only in hierarchical intraorganizational control settings, as observed by Ouchi (1978) but also in lateral, interorganizational settings. Therefore, scholars researching IS project control should consider not only which controls are used and combined by the controller, which has been the focus of most IS studies so far (eg, Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al., 2002; Rustagi et al., 2008), but also how control is transmitted between controller and controllee. In other words, we need to pay closer attention to how control is enacted (Chua, Lim, Soh, & Sia, 2012; Wiener et al., 2016), and how control changes as it travels through a control system (Ouchi, 1978). As shown in ou literature review, our current body of knowledge on IS project control contains evidence that is partly contradictory and somewhat inconclusive on how control modes influence performance. The insights offered by our study suggest that con trol transmission may represent a key piece to the puzzle of IS project control

## 6.2 | Implications for practice

The results of our study serve as a reminder to managers that it is not only what you do that matters but also how you do it. That is, effective control of IS offshoring projects depends on both the careful use of appropriate control mechanisms and the consistent transmission of these mechanisms from client to vendor. This provides the basis for three important implications for practice: First, rather than adding more control when experiencing performance problems, clients should pay attention to how well control given is actually received by the vendor. By not only paying attention to the variety and intensity of controls but also to the challenge of consistent control transmission, offshore clients may be able to configure and implement better control solutions. Second, and consequently, since our study shows that high project performance is linked to consistency in control transmission, client and vendor managers should consider investing in monitoring and evaluating not only the project as such but also the quality of the control relationship. By paying attention to how well control is passed from the client to the vendor and investing in ensuring consistency in control transmission, both parties contribute to lowering project‐related risks and improving the chances for success. Third, the study results imply that clients should carefully assess whether it is possible to effectively enact behaviour control. Even though behaviour control transmits well, our study indicates that behaviour control received does not impact project performance in offshore IS projects. Rather, our results show that offshore clients are better off applying outcome control. An important managerial implication is thus to focus on using outcome control mechanisms instead of investing in information channels that facilitate behaviour control.

## 6.3 | Directions for future research

By providing empirical evidence for the importance of formal control transmission, our paper opens up several interesting directions for further research. First, since our study establishes the relevance of formal control transmission in relation to the client‐vendor control dyad, it becomes interesting for future research to examine how control is transmitted in other control dyads. For example, we can expect that client managers working at the vendor interface are in turn controlled b their internal clients as well as by higher‐level managers (eg, Kirsch, 1997). Similarly, vendor managers need to exercise con trol over vendor project teams, while also being controlled by their superiors. By studying other control dyads as well as b expanding the scope to cover a larger set of control relationships, new and interesting patterns of control transmission ma be observed. Future studies might also take a micro turn and study control transmission consistency on the control‐mech anism level to better understand exactly where and how control transmission is particularly difficult and challenging.<sup>5</sup>

Second, while prior literature is inconclusive on the effectiveness of behaviour control in (offshore) outsourced IS pro jects, the results from our study tip the scale towards the position that behaviour control does not have a performance impact in IS offshore project contexts. Nevertheless, our results also show that client managers in high‐performing pro jects invest in selecting and transmitting behaviour control, which cannot be explained by the extant body of knowledge One possibility is that there is something we do not quite understand about the interplay between behaviour and outcome control. For instance, it is known from research on project reporting that (vendor) project managers tend to have an optimistic bias in status reporting (eg, Snow, Keil, & Wallace, 2007). Hence, it may well be that controllers use behaviour control in part to collect “triangulating” information for assessing vendor adherence to outcome control. For example offshore clients may monitor the use of specific procedures and methods (behaviour control) not necessarily only to ensure systematic use of these procedures and methods as such, but also as a way to compare their observations with project status and progress reports (outcome control) to better determine their veracity. More studies on the interpla between control modes, as well as more in‐depth field studies on IS project control, would be fruitful in helping us under stand why clients use control modes that do not seem to impact performance.

Third, prior research has studied how and why the configuration of control portfolios changes over the course of an IS project (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gregory et al., 2013; Kirsch, 2004). The theoretical arguments put for ward in this study suggest that the transmission of formal control from controller to controllee will also vary over time A study that captures the dynamics of control transmission would therefore be a promising path for future research

We believe that by providing empirical evidence for the importance of formal control transmission and its link to project performance, this study can contribute to a scholarly discussion that brings theory development on IS project control into new territory.
