# VALUE OF LOCAL SHOWROOMS TO ONLINE COMPETITORS<sup>1</sup>

Jayarajan Samuel Information Systems and Operations Management, College of Business, University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, TX 76019 U.S.A. {jayarajan.samuel@uta.edu}

Zhiqiang (Eric) Zheng Information Systems and Operations Management, Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {ericz@utdallas.edu}

Ying Xie Marketing, Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {ying.xie@utdallas.edu}

In this study, we quantify the value of physical showrooms to online competitors by investigating how the presence and absence of local showrooms impact customers’ search and purchase behaviors with online competitors. By using an exogenous event of a large retailer’s offline market exit and a unique dataset tha captures customers’ online browsing and purchasing activities before and after the event, we empirically examine the changes in online search and sales made by customers who lived within neighborhoods where local showrooms closed. We devise several search-oriented measures to complement the conventional salesbased measures in order to quantify the value of a showroom more completely. Using a difference-indifference approach, we do not find a significant impact on online competitors’ sales when a neighboring showroom closes. However, we observe a significant increase in customers’ online search intensity as well as an increase of direct navigations to Amazon in the absence of local showrooms. We discuss a wide range of implications both for online and offline retailers based on our results.

Keywords: Multichannel shopping, cross-channel competition, showrooming, consumer search

## Introduction

Traditional retail stores have been downsizing or shutting down due to intensified competition from large online retailers such as Amazon in the last decade (La Monica 2017). This wave of retail stores shutdown, termed as the great retail apocalypse, has been ever increasing since 2008 and reached record levels in 2019.<sup>2</sup> The Limited, a popular women’s clothing retailer, announced the closure of all its physical stores in January 2017 and moved all its sales to the online channel (Wattles 2017). Macy’s, Kmart, and Sears stores have also recently announced large-scale store closures (Franson 2017; Romo 2018). By mid-2019, popular retail outlets Payless, Gymboree, Charlotte Russe, and Shopko announced a combined closure of 5,864 stores (Coresite Research 2019). The tenant space of these mega stores in malls are being filled by, among others, showrooms to augment online channels (Gustafson 2015). Amid the recent business practice of opening and closing brick-and-mortar stores, we wonder what the consequences are. What value do these local showrooms bring to consumers in the current multichannel retail environment? What are the impacts on competing online retailers? Specifically, how would a retailer closing or opening a physical store impact its customers’ shopping experience and their resultant purchasing behaviors with an online competitor? Which types of retailers would benefit (or get hurt) most? These fundamental questions concerning retailers and their customers in the competition of new online-offline multichannel retailing motivate our research quest in this paper.

Online and offline channels are distinctive in the types of product information and consumer experience they provide. Customers may prefer the direct assistance of store staff as well as the convenience of physically evaluating a product in a local store, but at the same time favor the ease of search (e.g., for comparing prices or other product attributes) in an online channel. Many customers take advantage of the presence of both channels by visiting a local store to experience the product first and then purchasing the product from an online retailer (that may or may not be the same retailer as the local store) or conversely price shopping for a product online and having it matched at a physical store. As local showrooms constitute such an important part of many customers shopping journey, it is essential for retail business owners to understand the value of offline showrooms to online retailers from the perspective of the customer shopping experience. The answer to this question will help address the concern of the possible collapse of offline retailing businesses occurring now (La Monica 2017).

Although the assistance role of a brick-mortar store to competing online retailers, dubbed as the showrooming phenomenon, has long been recognized by the industry, there is a lack of empirical research to systematically measure and quantify the value of a local store to cross-channel competitors. This is partly due to the difficulty of observing exogenous showroom opening or closing events for researchers. This difficulty is further exacerbated by the lack of detailed information regarding how customers’ shopping behaviors change as a result of the presence (or absence) of a local store. Some notable exceptions exist (Avery et al. 2012; Bell et al. 2017; Brynjolfsson et al. 2009; Rappa et al. 2015; Venkatesan et al. 2007). However, these studies focus on multichannel shopping within the same retailer and do not address the more concerning question with regard to online competitors such as Amazon: What value does a local store (and its showroom) provide to an online competitor and how does it affect local customers’ shopping behaviors with the online competitor? How should an online retailer align its marketing strategy in the presence and absence of a competing retailer’s local showroom? These questions become increasingly more acute due to the large-scale shutdown of traditional retailers (Franson 2017). We address this research gap by empirically examining the impact of local store closure on its online competitors in a multichannel retailing environment.

From a methodological standpoint, the existing empirical literature predominately relies on sales as the single metric to quantify the value of a local store (Avery et al. 2012; Bell et al. 2017), while leaving customers’ search behavior, an indispensable part of customer multichannel shopping journey, untapped.<sup>3</sup> We maintain that measuring how local stores facilitate customers’ online searches during the pre-purchase period is not only as important, but also a more direct barometer for quantifying the value of a local showroom. E-commerce consulting and research firms such as Deloitte have long been promoting consumers’ online search patterns as frontal evidence for showrooming effects (Mason 2013b), adding support for our approach. Toward this end, in the current study, we develop new metrics to quantify the value of a local showroom by examining how it alters customer search behaviors with online competitors. Our approach enriches the literature that predominantly focuses on the sales perspective.

We address the above questions by taking advantage of an exogenous event that occurred during November 2008 which caused the closedown of 155 Circuit City stores, a leading consumer electronics retailer at that time. In this industry, we consider Circuit City and other brick-and-mortar retailers as showroom “providers” while competing online retailers such as Amazon as showroom “beneficiaries.”

When a Circuit City store closes, we could expect some local customers within the neighborhood to migrate from the offline channel to the online channel (sales substitution) or drop out of the purchase process due to high product search and evaluation costs (sales dampening). These two effects move in opposite directions, and as a result, their relative magnitudes determine whether the net sales effect (due to the closure of a local Circuit City store) would be positive or negative. Since we cannot disentangle these two sales effects without having the proprietary offline sales data across all competing retailers, we devise new metrics to measure the value of a showroom to online competitors by examining how customers change their online search behaviors in reaction to a local store closure. In fact, as demonstrated in Huang et al. (2009), search is a necessary antecedent to sales. Accordingly we expect an increase in consumers’ online search intensity as a whole, in the absence of local stores, which previously served customers’ needs in product information search (called search effects), echoing Jing (2018). Also, we expect a change in the use of online search engines and other search aids after a local showroom closes (called referring domain effect). Taken together, we enrich the literature on the showrooming effect by examining the value of a physical showroom to the competitor’s online channel with respect to both sales and consumer online search.

We tracked the zip codes of 155 focal Circuit City store locations. A third party provided a large click-stream dataset that records detailed customer search and purchase behavior at the individual level during each month of 2008 and 2009. We used a difference-in-difference (DID) approach to tease out the changes in customers’ online product search and purchase patterns after the abrupt closedown of local Circuit City stores within a given zip code for the product categories of consumer electronics. We then measured the impact of depriving a physical showroom on competing online retailers such as Amazon and bestbuy.com. The results related to sales are statistically insignificant for both Amazon and bestbuy.com. However, our analysis in consumer online search reveals interesting patterns. We find that Amazon customers indeed intensified their searches once the local Circuit City store closed. This increase in search intensity weakened in the presence of a nearby Best Buy store. Interestingly, we did not find a significant change in customers’ online search behaviors at bestbuy.com. This contrast clearly shows that Amazon customers seek additional product information from online channels before making a purchase when a neighborhood store closes.

Our results on search engine referrals (e.g., Google and Yahoo) are also revealing. We find that across online retailers, more customers are referred by a search engine following a showroom closure. However, fewer shoppers land at Amazon via a search engine following a showroom closure than before. Instead, buying customers tend to go directly to Amazon, making Amazon itself the default product search engine. These effects are weakened in the presence of a nearby Best Buy store.

Based on our findings using the proposed showroom metrics, we examine the gainers and losers of physical showrooms and provide viable business strategies for both offline and online retailers. Our results show that the loss of a local store increases consumers’ product information search cost online and, therefore, online competitors should be prepared to react to the entry and exit of local showrooms differently to better facilitate consumers’ online shopping.

## Related Research

The value of showrooms has been previously studied in the context of multichannel shopping (e.g., catalog, internet, store front) within the same retailer. Venkatesan et al. (2007), by analyzing purchase data from offline stores and an online channel (of the same retailer), find that retailers have the opportunity to grow profits by encouraging customers to shop in multiple channels. Avery et al. (2012) study the impact of opening physical stores on the internet sales of a high-end retailer of apparel, accessories and home furnishings, and document synergistic benefits that physical showrooms bring to online sales (for the same retailer). Bell et al. (2017) investigate the cross-channel migration of customers for an eyeglass retailer (WarbyParker.com). They show that (for this retailer) the introduction of a display-only showroom increases the overall demand and helps customers migrate toward their online channel. Wang and Goldfarb (2017) find a complementarity effect between offline and online channels for the same seller, which is driven by the offline store playing a marketing communications role for the brand. Gu and Tayi (2017) investigate how the strategic placement of products between offline and online channels for the same retailer increases profit and consumer surplus. The operations management literature has also tackled the coordination between online and offline channels and has found that adding an online channel by a manufacturer could benefit both channels (Tsay and Agarwal 2004).

With regard to the value of physical showrooms to online competitors, the literature is thin and lacks empirical evidence, partly due to the difficulty of obtaining data across channels and across competitors. The extant studies have mainly attempted to examine this issue using an analytical approach. Mehra et al. (2017), for example, build a gametheoretical model to show that profits of retailers with showrooms could be hurt by online competition. Balakrishnan et al. (2014) study the pricing effect of showrooms on both online and offline channels using a model that accounts for uncertainty and heterogeneity in customer product valuation. Kuksov and Liao (2018) demonstrate, through a theoretical model, that consumers’ engagement in showrooming may lead to increased profitability of offline retailers when factoring in the manufacturer’s decisions.

Although both industry and popular press highlight the importance of consumer search in the context of showrooming (Mason 2013b; Quint et al. 2013), few existing studies have examined the phenomenon from the lens of consumer search. Extant studies have almost entirely focused on the sales impact. For example, Tang et al. (2016) study the competition effect of a showroom’s entry on online sales for a specific product category (shoes). Forman et al. (2009) document how a local store reduces online sales for a product category in nearby neighborhoods. Bell et al. (2017) and Wang and Goldfarb (2017) find that market communication from the offline store aids sales in the online channel of the same firm. Kumar et al. (2019) report that the offline store increases customer engagement for online customers thereby increasing online sales. Hence, we add to this important and growing body of literature on the value of showrooms by defining search-oriented metrics (in addition to the traditional sales-based metric) and measuring the impact of a showroom exit on online competitors’ businesses from the lens of consumer search.

Figure A1 in Appendix A summarizes the extant literature on the value of an offline channel (showroom). We group the related papers along two dimensions: whether the analysis is empirical or analytical in nature, and whether the study addresses the impact on competing retailer(s) or not. Our paper fills an important gap in this literature by empirically evaluating the change in customers’ online purchase and search behaviors (intensity and referral) at competing online retailers before and after a large multiproduct neighborhood store closes.

## Research Context and Design

A key challenge in studying the cross-channel effects of a local showroom closure is the difficulty of directly observing or experimentally manipulating the counterfactual effect: firms rarely open or close showrooms for the sole purpose of assessing their relative benefits. We must also control for other factors that may confound the cross-channel effect extraction when such a store is closed in a given neighborhood.

An ideal setting would be if stores were closed due to some exogenous event such as operation and management inefficiencies, unrelated to the makeup of the markets they serve. We could then isolate and extract the effects of such an exogenous store closing event on competing online retailers. Below we identify such an event.

## Research Context

In November 2008, Circuit City, the second largest electronics retailer in North America at that time, announced that it would close 155 stores across the United States. Circuit City was forced to file for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in November 2008 due to intense competition (e.g., Best Buy and Costco) as well as a series of bad management decisions such as excessive store expansions, ill-fated cost saving measures, loss of key personnel (due to changes in wage policies), and executive leadership churn.<sup>4</sup> This event could be considered exogenous for the purpose of this study for two reasons. First, our main interest is in estimating the impact of local store closure on the search and purchase behaviors of local customers with regard to online competitors. It is possible that Circuit City chose to close a store at a certain location because of its low local sales, but it is hard to imagine that it chose to close the store merely for the sake of thwarting the customers’ showrooming behavior with respect to online competitors such as Amazon.com. Second, this event is an exogenous shock to the market since even as late as April 2008 Blockbuster publicly announced a takeover bid of U.S. \$1 billion for Circuit City and subsequently withdrew its offer in July 2008 citing market conditions, indicating that there was little notice regarding bankruptcy protection filings. Only days before the bankruptcy filing, Circuit City announced the closure of 155 stores across the United States. This event of a single large retail chain going out of business and closing all retail stores in an abrupt manner was bound to ripple through other online and offline retailers. This provides an ideal backdrop for assessing the value of a local showroom due to the exogenous nature of the store closure event.

For the purposes of this study, we consider all the product groups sold by Circuit City.<sup>5</sup> Since the presence of a local store provides customers an opportunity to evaluate and purchase products in a physical showroom, we expect that impacts of the local showroom closing will manifest through product information searches and purchases for these goods. We accordingly restrict our attention to the product categories sold at Circuit City.

Circuit City had several online competitors. Online retailers are known to offer a more competitive price and wider variety of goods than their brick-and-mortar counterparts (Ghose and Yao 2011, Granados et al. 2012). However, the downside to an online retailer is the lack of touch-and-feel evaluation that consumers can experience in a local store before making a purchase decision. We consider the two leading online retailers, Amazon and bestbuy.com, as a pair of contrasting top online retailers in the consumer electronics segment: the former is primarily an online retailer while the latter’s parent company, Best Buy, is very similar to Circuit City with omnichannel (offline and online) outlets.

In 2008, Amazon was the number one online retailer by sales revenue and its consumer electronics market share alone was reported at 10% (Brohan 2013). A study of Amazon customers’ online activity patterns before and after Circuit City’s store closings provides an ideal research setting for examining the value of local showrooms to cross-channel competitors, operating with online-channel only. We also consider bestbuy.com because it is the online channel of Best Buy, a direct and main competitor to Circuit City until 2008. According to a report by Harkness Consulting (Camacho et al. 2007), Best Buy also had the largest market share in this industry with 25% followed by Walmart (15%) and Circuit City (9%). A closer examination of customers’ online channel activities at bestbuy.com before and after the Circuit City showroom closures would provide further insights on the impact of a local showroom on cross-channel competitors, operating with omni-channels.

## Research Design

In order to study the value of an offline showroom to online competition, we consider how customers search for product information before making the purchase. As prescribed by the theory of planned behavior (TPB), a customer’s purchase intention is driven by her attitude toward this behavior and further strengthened by others’ opinions of the product (Ajzen 1991). Pavlou and Fygenson (2006) further show that intentions to purchase products online drive intentions to search for information online, which in turn influences the ultimate purchase. Kumar et al. (2019) use TBP and prospect theory to theorize why customers need physical inspection to gain product information. Extending these studies, we prescribe how customers change their online search and purchase behaviors when a physical store in their neighborhood closes.

In the presence of a local store, a potential customer can gain product knowledge by visiting the store. This gain in product information and knowledge increases a potential customer’s favorable view toward the product, which in turn enhances the chance of her eventually making the purchase. Accordingly, when a local store is absent, customers are deprived of this choice and hence will need to seek product information elsewhere.

In order to unravel the mechanism behind the various options available to customers when a physical store closes, we first consider a showroom that is operating in a neighborhood within the same zip code with two kinds of visitors, buyers and showroomers.<sup>6</sup> The buyers are the in-store buying customers and the showroomers visit the store but make their purchase at a competing online retailer.

When an offline showroom closes, some buyers may migrate to other channels such as online stores (Kumar et al., 2019). We refer to this migration of buyers to the online channel as the sales substitution effect. Previous literature has examined this substitution effect between offline and online as a result of a store entry (e.g., Forman et al. 2009). At the same time, it is also possible that there are customers who may not have had a particular purchase target in mind. Such customers would have used the physical store to evaluate and purchase items that caught their attention. Such purchases could have been made instantly at the physical store (buyers) or at a later point in time at an online store (showroomers). These purchases might not happen after the local store closes because these customers could be deterred from the purchase process due to higher search effort, decreased shopping convenience, and loyalty to the closed store etc.,<sup>7</sup> as summarized in Neslin et al. (2006). We refer to this loss of online sales due to various scenarios laid out above as the sales dampening (complementarity) effect. Such sales complementarity effects have been observed by Avery et al. (2012), Bell et al. (2017), and Wang and Goldfarb (2017) when a retailer opens a local store. In our context, we do not observe offline purchases of the local customers (buyers or showroomers). This makes it impossible to separate the sales dampening effect from the sales substitution effect in our empirical context. We instead, can only measure the net sales impact online (called the net sales effect) as a result of the aggregation of these two sales effects which operate in opposite directions.

Similarly, the customers (buyers or showroomers) face severa choices that affect their online search intensity after an offline store closes. The showroomers who continue to shop online and the buyers who come into the online buying process, now have the choice of obtaining product information at the online store instead. Since these online buyers now are deprived of an offline store (which they had access to earlier) to facilitate their search for product information and product evaluation, we expect their online search effort to increase in order to compensate for the closure of an offline store. In the presence of an additional offline competitor (Best Buy stores, in our case), we expect the increase in online search intensity to be weakened due to the availability of alternative offline channel for product information and evaluation.

In the context of online searching (Bronnenburg et al. 2016; Moe 2003), the number of webpages viewed can be perceived as a proxy measure for the amount of information acquired, termed breadth of search (Huang et al. 2009). The number of minutes spent (toward a sale) corresponds to the effort of processing this acquired information, termed depth of search (Huang et al. 2009). These two metrics are also common measures in web analytics (for example, used in Google Analytics) to characterize the amount of user activities on a website.

We accordingly use the breadth and depth of an online search to measure search intensity. Since customers are likely to intensify their online searches for more expensive items (Zhang, et al. 2006), we attach an economic value to the traditional measures of search intensity by calculating the ratios of the search measure against the dollar amounts spent.<sup>8</sup> Specifically, we measure the breadth of a given search by the number of pages viewed per dollar of purchase and the search depth by the number of minutes expended per dollar spent. A high value for the pages viewed (minutes spent) per dollar of purchase indicates that a high amount (breadth and depth) of information is extracted from the site for every dollar spent. In our empirical context, we expect that a higher amount of information must be extracted from online counterparts, which should translate to a higher number of pages viewed (and more minutes) per dollar spent due to the closure of a local showroom within the neighborhood. We collectively call these changes in customer search patterns the online search effect.

Additionally, we expect changes in the way customers reach online sellers via their use of search engines after a store closes in their neighborhood. In the United States, search engines such as Google, Yahoo, and Bing are the primary portals used to locate websites on the Internet. In the presence of a local showroom, customers may take advantage of the showroom in order to evaluate product fit and quality while using search engines primarily for product price information. When the option of a local showroom no longer exists, consumers may be forced to rely on search engines entirely or use large online stores such as Amazon (e.g., through product reviews posted on its website) to locate product fit and quality information before a purchase. As Pavlou and Fygenson (2006) argue, purchase intentions are strengthened by others’ attitudes toward the intended product (subjective norms). In our context, others’ attitudes can be represented by product reviews available through online vendors such as Amazon. Hence we expect an increase in direct navigation to online sellers such as Amazon that provide comprehensive product information, if customers are relying on these large online sellers to be their de facto product information portals in the absence of a physical showroom. We use the referring domain effect to label this change in the navigation path to online sellers after the closure of an offline showroom.

Figure A2 in Appendix A elaborates the three components we have defined to measure the showrooming effect. We posit that if an offline showroom matters to the online competitor, then the showrooming effect would be exhibited in customers shopping behaviors with regard to the online competitor, for those customers within the vicinity of a Circuit City store which subsequently closed. This showrooming effect would be weakened in the presence of a local Best Buy store since there would still be an alternative showroom to absorb the effects of Circuit City’s store closure. Additionally, we expect the showrooming effect to diminish in size for online transactions made by customers who live farther away from the closing stores and to be more pronounced for product types that lend themselves to touch-and-feel, such as experience goods (Wang et al. 2017).

## Data

Our data come from a major click stream data vendor who tracked online activities of a representative panel of more than 100,000 customers in the United States Corresponding to Circuit City’s store closures in November 2008, we chose to examine a two-year period of data from January 2008 to December 2009. The data provided the following information for each customer: the time stamp for any online search and purchase activity, the domain name of the site visited, the referring domain name (left blank if the user went directly to the site), the number of pages viewed, the duration in minutes spent by the user on the website, information on whether or not a purchase was made, and if yes, a description of the product, the total dollar amount spent on the purchase, and the zip code of the user’s location. We excluded browsing sessions that did not result in a purchase because browsing-only sessions do not specify the contents viewed; therefore, we are unable to determine whether or not the information sought in a browsing-only session is related to a Circuit City product category.<sup>9</sup> For every browsing session that results in a purchase, we also capture the referring domain by which the customer arrived at this website. The referring domain could be a search engine such as Google or Yahoo, or it could be blank, indicating that the customer went to the website directly. We do not consider referring domains other than search engines or blank since the data for other specific referring domains is very thin.

## Econometric Analysis

The abrupt closure of Circuit City stores and data on online shopping behaviors of customers before and after this event provides us the opportunity to apply a standard DID method to evaluate the cross-competitor cross-channel impact of a physical showroom.

## Characteristics of Control Versus Treated Groups

In order to understand the characteristics of these two groups, we gauge the trends and patterns in online consumer purchases and search behaviors under each zip code. The important metrics we have justified and chosen are related to online sales, page views, duration of time spent online per transaction, and the referring domain. In Table 3 we report the model-free average DID values for our outcome variables.

From Table 3 we observe that the Amazon sales for the treated group do not change significantly as compared to the control group. However, the search metrics for Amazon do increase significantly in the zip codes where a Circuit City store closed (treated) as compared to the control group.<sup>14</sup> We see no statistically significant changes for bestbuy.com nevertheless.

## Endogeneity Concerns

First of all, we argue that the event of a Circuit City store closure is exogenous to the showrooming effect (i.e., it is unlikely that Circuit City decided which stores to close as a response to perceived benefits to online competitors). However, there might still be a concern that not all zip codes are the same. The characteristics of consumers within a zip code where Circuit City never existed may be very different from those where it was present. In fact, these differences could be the very reasons a Circuit City store was opened in that zip code in the first place. This heterogeneity in the zip code characteristics may introduce endogeneity. We address this endogeneity concern in the following ways.

First, we use the information concerning whether or not a competitor to Circuit City existed within the control group zip code. If so, we can say that such a control group zip code has similar characteristics to the one where a Circuit City store existed and closed. For a comparable competitor to Circuit City, we choose the Best Buy chain of stores. During 2008 and 2009, Best Buy was the number one electronic retail store and accordingly an equal competitor to Circuit City. This comparison of the control group with only zip codes where a Best Buy store but not a Circuit City store exists ensures that the zip codes in both the control and treated groups are similar with respect to their demographics and reasons for opening large retail electronic stores. As a falsification test, our analysis will take this information of whether a Best Buy store existed or not into account, effectively examining the third difference with regard to the presence or absence of Best Buy stores.

Second, we address endogeneity by conducting the econometric analysis using fixed effects for the location (zip code) and time (month-year). Zip code-fixed effects would account for any time-invariant residual characteristics within each zip code. Similarly, the month-year fixed effects would account for any seasonal characteristics that are unique to a particular month-year.

## Online Search Effect

In this section, we first present the regression model on search intensity, assessing how search breadth and depth change as a result of store closure. We then examine how frequently consumers rely on search engines to conduct online searches before and after a store closure.

## Search Breadth and Depth

We expect that if a customer does not have the luxury of evaluating a product at a physical store, she would incur additional costs by viewing more online pages and spending more time for a given transaction. The AmazonPagesPerDollar<sub>i</sub> and BestBuyPagesPerDollar variables capture the number of pages viewed for every dollar worth of products on amazon.com and bestbuy.com respectively. Similarly, AmazonMinsPerDollar and BestBuyMinsPerDollar capture the minutes spent at the website for every purchase dollar. We, therefore, run the following DID model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log (\text {AmazonPagesPerDollar}, \\ \text {AmazonMinsPerDollar, BestBuyPagesPerDollar}, \\ \text {BestBuyMinsPerDollar}) _ {i t} = \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \beta_ {1} \\ \text {CCStorePresent} _ {i} \times \text {AfterStoreClosing} _ {t} + \beta_ {2} \\ \text {CCStorePresent} _ {i} \times \text {AfterStoreClosing} _ {t} \times \\ \text {BBStorePresent} _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Again the main coefficient of interest is $\beta _ { I } ,$ measuring the impact of the exit of local showrooms on consumer online search intensity. The coefficient $\beta _ { 2 }$ captures the moderating effect of Best Buy Stores as an alternative local showroom.

## Referring Domain Effect

In the absence of a showroom, we expect consumers’ navigation to online sellers to change. This effect can be measured as the change in the number of consumer online purchases (transactions) referred by a search engine or direct navigation to the online seller. Alternatively, the variables AmazonNoReferringDomain , and BestBuyNo-ReferringDomain , denote whether or not the online transactions completed were navigated directly (i.e., no referring channel was used for the transaction).

## Robustness Checks

Although our main results point toward an increase in customers’ online search intensity with online competitors after a physical showroom closes, several alternative explanations may exist. In this section, we conduct various robustness checks to rule out certain alternative explanations.

## Alternative Measure of Search Intensity

In order to further investigate if the increase in search intensity manifests itself independent of sales amount, we define a more traditional online search measure called search per transaction (Huang et al. 2009). The average number of pages browsed per transaction for zip code i in month-year t is given by PagesPerTransaction , un-normalized by the dollar amount. Similarly we define the average number of minutes per transaction for zip code i in month-year t as MinsPer-Transaction . In addition, for completeness, we also define the average dollar sales amount per transaction for zip code i in month-year t as SalesPerTransaction . Using these alternate measures as dependent variables, we perform the same DID analysis for Amazon and bestbuy.com.

The results presented in Table 10 show that the store closure did not have a significant effect on average sales per transaction for either Amazon or bestbuy.com. However, we find that after store closure, the online customers of Amazon residing in those zip codes significantly increase the average number of pages that they view per transaction by 20%. This increase is dampened by 15% when a physical Best Buy store exists in these zip codes. Similarly, these Amazon customers increase the minutes spent per transaction by 17%. All results for bestbuy.com are statistically insignificant. These results further validate our main findings, which state that search intensities increase for Amazon customers when a physical store closes.

## Self-Selection

It is possible that those zip codes with Circuit City stores may be systematically different from the zip codes without stores. We have partially dealt with this issue by purging the timeinvariant residual characteristics using zip code fixed effects. However, there might still be a concern that the control group of zip codes are not a representative counterfactual for the treated group because Circuit City stores may have been operating in the treated zip code regions precisely due to some time-varying differences (e.g., changing customer population). To further investigate if the increase in search intensity has a causal link to the Circuit City store closures (and not due to other endogenous reasons), we matched each zip code from the treatment group with an equivalent zip code from the control group using zip code level demographics from our data. More specifically, we used a coarsened exact matching algorithm implemented by Iacus et al. (2009) to match locations in the treatment group with locations in the control group which have similar demographics such as the average household age, average income and average household size. The matching results left us with 55 zip codes in each group. Using the data from the combined 110 zip codes, we ran the models for sales and search (TotalSales, PagesPerDollar, and MinsPerDollar) and presented the results in Table 11.

The results in Table 11 show that the net sales effect is insignificant for either Amazon or bestbuy.com. The search breadth and depth values for Amazon increase significantly by 10% and these increases are dampened in the presence of a Best Buy store by 8% significantly. We do not see any statistical significance in the search results for bestbuy.com. These results collectively are similar to our main results, thereby lending credibility to the Circuit City store closing causing the increase in online search.

## Heterogeneity Within a Zip Code

Although we have controlled for location specific characteristics by using zip code fixed effects, there could be heterogeneity within the geographic zip code area which may be unaccounted for. Hence, we add location specific demographics in the regression equations as interaction terms with our DID term (corresponding to â ). Specifically, we used the zip code specific average household age, average income and average household size for each location where online sales originated.

The results in Table 12 show that the net sales effect is statistically insignificant for either Amazon or bestbuy.com. However the search breadth and search depth for Amazon increase by a statistically significant 27% and 25% respectively. ## Serial Correlation

Due to the longitudinal nature of our data, it is likely that serial correlation may be present and the standard errors of the coefficients may be understated (Bertrand et al. 2004). In order to address this concern we implement two techniques suggested by Bertrand et al. that are independent of the structure of the error term and are applicable to our data with a large number of states (zip codes).

## Ignoring Time Series

The first approach proposed by Bertrand et al. is to ignore the time series. Following this, we averaged the outcome variable for each zip code before and after the Circuit City store closure date for the 110 matched zip codes. Such a technique is applicable in our research since the treatment (closure of the store) was implemented simultaneously across all the affected zip codes. We then performed the same DID analysis as before but without time fixed effects.

The results in Table 13 clearly show that our primary conclusions hold. We do not see any statistically significant change in sales neither for Amazon nor for bestbuy.com in the neighborhoods where the Circuit City Store closed. However, we do see increases in search intensities (search breadth and depth) for Amazon. Consistent with our main results, we also do not see any change in search metrics for bestbuy.com after the Circuit City stores closed.

## Variance-Covariance Matrix Corrections

Another method suggested by Bertrand et al. is to use a White-like estimator to calculate the variance-covariance matrix of the error term. This estimator is consistent for any correlation (including serial correlation). We implemented this estimation procedure to recalibrate our main model using the 110 matched zip codes.

Table 14 shows that this analysis yields consistent results with the main results of the paper that search intensities increase for Amazon but not for bestbuy.com after accounting for serial correlation in our data.

## Discussion

Many online retailers have been grappling with the decision of whether or not to seek an offline presence, some with unsuccessful experiments. Some of them have recently announced opening physical storefronts (Maloney 2018) after successfully catering to internet shoppers. For example, WarbyParker.com, the fashion eye wear retailer in the United States, has seen a boost in its online sales after introducing display-only physical showrooms in 2010 (Groth 2012). Recently, Amazon.com, an exclusively online retailer, opened its offline store in Seattle (Alicea 2018). Such moves by online retailers to open offline stores require a clear understanding of the value of local showrooms to online retailers. In this paper, we aim to provide such insights by empirically investigating the impact of local showroom exits on consumer online shopping behaviors in a competitive market. We examine the changes in customers’ online product information search and purchase patterns with respect to online competitors before and after the closedown of local Circuit City stores using a DID approach. In so doing, we have quantified the value of having a physical showroom to online competitors such as Amazon and bestbuy.com.

Our results show that even in the absence of change in online sales, the online search intensities increase for online-only retailers such as Amazon when a local showroom operated by a competitive retailer closes. We interpret these results to suggest that there exist customers who are moving their searches from a physical showroom to the online channel of a competitor when deprived of a local store. At a finer level, this “substitution” (showrooming) effect of obtaining product information is more evident for experience goods and is in line with results observed by Wang et al. (2017). We also find that this showrooming effect is more intense from customers who reside geographically very close to the showroom. The decay of the increased search activity as one moves away from the closed store is similar to the changes in sales documented by Forman et al. (2009). Interestingly, the showrooming effects do not exist for an online competitor that also has a physical store presence. All results for bestbuy.com are statistically insignificant. It appears that shoppers to omni-channel retailers are unaffected by a competitor’s store closing.

## Contributions

This paper represents the first attempt to empirically quantify the value of a local showroom on competitors’ online business, thereby adding to the larger literature on the substitution effects (Brynjolfsson et al. 2009; Forman et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2014) and complementarity effects (Avery et al. 2012; Bell et al. 2017; Wang and Goldfarb 2017) of multichannel retailing. Our tackling of this question from a cross-channel, crosscompetitor perspective constitutes the primary contribution of this paper.

This study also makes a secondary methodological contribution. In view of the inadequacy of using the sales measure in the absence of both the offline and online sales data across competitors, we develop new metrics from the lens of consumer online searches, constructed feasibly from search intensity (search breadth and depth) and referral domain.

On the substantive end, this paper yields several unique findings. We do not find that Amazon’s online sales change significantly upon a local store closure. However, Amazon customers increased their search intensities. Taken together, this demonstrates that one may underestimate the true value of a local showroom if limiting her attention to sales only. How local showrooms influence customers’ search behavior before reaching a purchase decision is equally, if not more, important. This finding overcomes the oversight of the literature. We also find that Amazon customers are less reliant on search engine referral and more of them go directly to Amazon when a local showroom closes. This non-intuitive result points to the fact that Amazon acts as an alternative showroom online in the absence of a physical showroom. A PowerReviews (2016) survey shows that 38% of customers use Amazon as the starting point for product search, beating Google’s share of 35%. In the current wave of the collapse of the mega stores such as Macy’s, our finding suggest that the monopoly power of Amazon will be further strengthened, in terms of serving as the de-facto product search engine.

## Managerial Implications

Our results yield several managerial implications to both the beneficiaries (competing online retailers) and providers of showrooms (brick-and-mortar stores).

First of all, our research demonstrates the value of physical showrooms in customers’ online search and purchase processes. Our findings endorse the recent moves by large online retailers such as Amazon to open physical kiosks in an endeavor to provide tangible product information to customers. Several specialized online retailers such as NastyGal, ModCloth, and Bonobos have also seen success in operating with omni-channels. The notion of virtual showrooms also highlights the importance of physical showrooms. For example, a mobile shopping app Divalicious<sup>20</sup> allows users to “try on” apparel on virtual mannequins. Shoppers upload their own images and then view how these avatars appear “wearing” the selected products. Such uses of IT to provide additional product information is in line with the suggestions of our findings.

Second, our results reveal that once a showroom closes, the online marketplace anticipates more traffic from additional customers. This group of customers will seek product information more intensely. Large retailers such as Amazon should, therefore, cater to such customers by reducing their search costs, for example, by providing better search algorithms and easy-to-search product catalogs. Online retailers can also strengthen their review and recommendation systems to help ease customers’ unfamiliarity and uncertainty with products due to a lack of local showrooms.

Third, our results show that more customers accessed Amazon directly following local store closure. In other words, more buying customers access Amazon via a search engine (Google or Yahoo) when a showroom exists within the neighborhood. This result has subtle implications for online advertising. For example, Amazon could adjust its online advertising budget (e.g., using Google’s Adwords) depending on whether or not the online searcher comes from a neighborhood with a local showroom. Our results lend support to the fact that the value of a search engine referral is higher for Amazon in neighborhoods with a physical showroom. Amazon should allocate greater advertising budgets for such neighborhoods and vice versa.

Fourth, on the provider side, cross-channel (online) competition poses a real threat for brick-and-mortar stores. Search intensities on online stores are lower in the presence of a showroom, suggesting that there exist potential customers who visit stores but do not purchase. Store managers must, therefore, probe why certain customers are subject to the showrooming effect and develop instruments to mitigate such effects. For example, if lower prices online are the main culprit, the brick-and-mortar stores can consider “no questions asked” price matching in their showrooms. Best Buy very boldly implemented a full price matching scheme in order to thwart showrooming in 2013 (Fitzgerald 2013). Although this price matching effort is debatable (McDuling 2014), our research suggests that this is a step in the right direction.

Fifth, our results reveal that consumers’ online search behaviors change when a local showroom is present. The showroom provider should accordingly investigate how such differences occur and develop counter-showrooming strategies in order to convert local visitors to buyers. For example, Target’s “In a Snap” mobile app tracks and analyzes the online searching behavior of customers visiting its local stores (Howland 2014). Some other common methods employed by various local retailers include investing in better mobile sites, providing in-store Wi-Fi, and helping customers shop on their own websites (Somerville 2013). These strategies are in line with our findings that showrooming indeed erodes sales.

Finally, we also performed additional analysis (presented in Appendix D) to investigate the relationship between search and sales (assuming a customer still remains in the shopping process<sup>21</sup>) to understand the underlying conversion process. We find that Amazon buyers who spend more minutes per page spend more dollars at Amazon and this effect is magnified for experience goods. We also find that customers spend more time at the sites with higher priced products and experience goods, which suggests Amazon should provide a higher quality search experience for these types of products. We find that when shopping for experience goods, customers are more likely to come to Amazon directly for shopping and, at the same time, less likely to rely on a search engine to start their online search. In summary, these results suggest that if a potential customer arrives directly to Amazon she is more likely to become a buyer. Therefore, Amazon should follow a more targeted marketing strategy to convert these customers.

## Limitations and Future Work

The scope of our analysis is limited by the depth and breadth of available data. We do not have access to the offline purchase data of either Circuit City or the alternative Best Buy stores. This lack of in-store data limits our ability to disentangle the sales substitution and sales dampening values separately. In addition, our data, which is a representative panel of customers in the United States, may not account for online sales from those users who were not captured in the data prior to Circuit City closure. In spite of these limitations our results regarding online product searches provide clear evidence of the role that showrooms play in the customer purchase journey.

Since the time of our study period (2008 and 2009), online technology has advanced considerably as smart phones and other smart devices proliferate. This has led to other complex, online retailing phenomenon such as webrooming and reverse showrooming where customers examine products online and then purchase them offline (Wolny and Charoensuksai 2016). However, the showrooming metrics we propose in this paper can also help gauge these new phenomena.

This study spawns several other directions for future research. For example, our research shows that improving online product searches will help counter the adverse effects of a showroom closure. Future research can further examine the existing product information revelation mechanisms such as product reviews and recommendation systems from the showroom angle. For example, how do existing information revealing mechanisms fare against customers who require a showroom experience? How would managers change the design of information revealing algorithms when expecting a new inflow of customers due to a neighborhood showroom closure? Also in this study, we have focused on an omnichannel retailer’s offline store closure. An area of future research could study the impact of an offline only retailer’s showroom on various online competitors. All these await exploration in future research.

## Underlying Mechanism: Customer Movements When an Offline Store Closes

In this appendix, we describe and clarify the value that a physical showroom brings to its online and offline competitors. We describe the mechanisms at play when the Circuit City store is open and subsequently closes and its impact on the online and offline players with respec to search and sales. We also highlight the portion of the data that we have and those that we do not have due to proprietary access.

There are fundamentally two types of customers who visit a Circuit City store that we are concerned with in this research:

• Customers who visit a Circuit City store and buy in the store itself, called Offline Buyers

Customers who visit the Circuit City store, learn about the product fit and then purchase the product from an online channel, called Showroomers

We do not consider the Circuit City store visitors who do not purchase either in the Circuit City store or the online channel but instead purchase at a different offline store. We also do not consider online-only buyers who do not visit the offline store in our mechanism since they are no influenced by the offline store.

## Impact on Sales When an Offline Store Closes

## Impact on Search When an Offline Store Closes

When an offline store closes, the buyers and showroomers have similar options for searching for the product information. What we measure is the online search intensity associated with the online sales substitution and sales retention. Both types of online sales can come after a visit to an offline store where the customers get product fit data or directly to the online store in which case we expect them to search more due to lack of product fit information. The buyers who move online (Sales Substitution) could purchase through the online channel after showrooming at a different offline store (\$B1) or without showrooming (\$B2). Similarly, the showroomers who stay online (Sale Retention) could do so with showrooming (\$E1) or without (\$E2).

Since after the offline store closure some customers would shop online without showrooming at another store (B2 and E2), we expect the overall online search (pages and minutes per dollar) to not decrease. If an increase in search exists, after the offline store closing, then it can be attributed to the online customers who ceased to conduct showrooming (B1 and E1). Hence, we can establish the value of an offline store to online competition this way.

Let Y denote the previous online search intensity when the store was open. After the store closure the search intensity is driven by (B1 + E1) who have not had a showroom experience and (B2 + E2) who have gained product knowledge at a showroom.

Hence:

• Search intensity of (B2 + E2) > Search intensity of (B1 + E1)

Now since search intensity of (Y) is similar to the search intensity of (B1 + E1) (both groups are showroomers) we expect the overall search intensity of (B1 + E1 + B2 + E2) to be greater than search intensity of (Y); that is,

• Search intensity of (B1 + E1 + B2 + E2) > Search intensity of (Y)

The presence of a nearby offline store (e.g., Best Buy store) will reduce search costs for customers who want to conduct showrooming before making an online purchase. This means that both B2 and E2 will decrease and concomitantly B1 and E1 will increase. The result would be an overall dampening of online search in the presence of an alternate offline store (e.g., Best Buy store).

## Appendix E

## Effect of a Physical Store Closure on Other Online Retailers

Our main results consider amazon.com and bestbuy.com as the two online competitors to the physical Circuit City store. However, there migh be a concern that the effect of the Circuit City store may also be felt by the other smaller online consumer electronic stores (some of whom may have their own physical stores). In our data, the top five online stores account for almost 50% of the total sales. The rest of the sales are spread across 187 online sellers. In fact, the four other major online sellers are all competitors to amazon.com in the consumer electronics space. ## Coarsened Exact Matching

There might be concerns that the demographics of the customers from the control and treated zip codes may not be the same. This might invalidate our comparison of the outcome variables between the treated and control groups.

To address this concern and match zip codes in the control and treated groups for similar demographics, we chose the average household age, average income and average household size as our covariates for matching. We then employed a coarsened exact matching algorithm (CEM) which belongs to the class of monotonic imbalance bounding (MIB) matching methods.

The CEM algorithm coarsens (or groups) each of the demographic variables and assigns them to a number of bins or strata. We used automatic coarsening bin sizes (quartiles, quintiles, deciles, centiles and percentiles), as implemented in Iacus et al. (2009). Since we are interested in 1:1 matching, all zip codes in each bin that do not have a treated and control zip code pair were dropped.

The algorithm we used is outlined in Berta et al. (2017) and is structured in the following manner:

• Each of the three variables are coarsened into different bins. The algorithm starts with quartiles (4) and subsequently increases the number of bins to centiles (100).

The zip codes are exact matched between the treated and control zip codes within each bin or strata.

Strata that do not have treated or control zip codes are eliminated.

An imbalance measure called L1 is computed which measures the distance between the multivariate histograms of the original and matched sets.

• The bins are adjusted repeatedly until the L1 measure of the matched set is less than or equal to the L1 of the original set.

In our implementation, the algorithm assigned 4 bins each to the average household age and average income and 64 bins to the average household size which brought the final L1 measure to 0 for the matched set.

The implementation of our CEM algorithm yielded 55 matched zip codes each in the treated and control groups that have similar demographics.
