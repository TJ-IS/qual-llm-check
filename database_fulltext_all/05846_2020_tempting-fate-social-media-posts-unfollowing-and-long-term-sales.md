# TEMPTING FATE: SOCIAL MEDIA POSTS, UNFOLLOWING, AND LONG-TERM SALES

Shuting (Ada) Wang

Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York, 55 Lexington Avenue, New York, NY 10010 U.S.A. {shuting.wang@baruch.cuny.edu}

Brad N. Greenwood School of Business, George Mason University, 4400 University Drive, Fairfax, VA 22030 U.S.A. {bgreenwo@gmu.edu}

Paul A. Pavlou C. T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, 4750 Calhoun Road, Houston, TX 77294 U.S.A. {pavlou@bauer.uh.edu}

In this study, we examine how a firm’s decision to post on social media affects the propensity of its followers to (1) purchase its products and (2) unfollow the firm. Using a unique dataset from a large fashion retailer which frequently sends mass non-customized information-only posts on social media (i.e., non-coupons), findings indicate that such posts increase short-term purchases by 6%. However, these posts also increase followers’ propensity to unfollow the firm by 280%, significantly reducing long-term sales. Strikingly, this punitive effect of social media posting appears to manifest under very specific circumstances. Results indicate that these changes in unfollowing and long-term sales are larger if posts are sent in more crowded cities or during commuting hours (suggesting that the stress formed by personal crowding and the follower’s environment may play a key role in followers’ reactions to social media postings). These results underscore the potential downsides of sending blanket, non-customized, messages to followers, notably during times of intense psychological stress, as well as avenues to avoid such pitfalls.

Keywords: Social media, fan pages, social media posting, unfollow, purchases, long-term sales, crowdedness, population density, peak traffic hours

## Introduction

The proliferation of social media, and the low-cost access to consumers it offers, has led to a bevy of firms leveraging such platforms to communicate with consumers. Indeed, recent surveys (eMarketer 2017) estimate that 8 out of 10 small firms, and 99% of large firms, have established social media pages. Different from traditional email message marketing campaigns, which are easily ignored, social media posts allow firms to instantly communicate with their followers (i.e., consumers who follow the firm on social media). Yet, despite the strong desire of firms to leverage social media, concerns about the potential negative effects of overusing social media have surfaced, especially when firms send non-customized social media posts to their followers en masse. This creates a potential risk for firms, as repetitive posting may alienate followers and drive them to “unfollow” the firm (Fournier and Avery 2011; Goldstein et al. 2014; Kaplan and Haenlein 2010).

Why might consumers who select into following firms be driven to unfollow? When consumers follow firms on social media, heterogeneous motivations are at play (Tsai and Men 2013). Some may be interested in receiving coupons or discounts. Others may want to receive firm-specific content. Some might follow because they simply like the firm or want to socialize with other like-minded consumers. Yet, the overwhelming majority of posts are not customized to the individual. Such non-customized posts can create disutility, and even annoyance toward the firm (Bailey and Iqbal 2008; Xia and Sudharshan 2002). Moreover, receiving posts from the firm is not costless, requiring the time and attention of followers, which often means interrupting what they are doing. And when followers repeatedly receive irrelevant social media interruptions, the annoyance accumulates, leading some followers to unfollow the firm. This is concerning as the long-term cost of unfollowing is not fully understood by researchers or firms. It is this gap we seek to address.

Theoretically, the effect of social media posts on long-term purchase behavior, as it relates to unfollowing, is murky at best. The clear benefit of posting is advertising and a subsequent spike in short-term purchasing. Like any other advertisement, a social media post elicits purchases by informing and persuading consumers of the firm’s products (Assmus et al. 1984). Yet, as discussed, the non-customized posts, which comprise the majority of the posts sent on social media, may also yield negative consequences in the form of unfollowing. Unfollowing can have serious implications. Chiefly, the firm loses its ability to use social media to directly communicate with those consumers in the future (Dodds et al. 1991). Unfollowers may even turn to competing firms, thereby affecting the firm’s long-term competitiveness. This leaves the manager with a conundrum. Posting may yield profit in the form of short-term purchasing. Yet, if the firm alienates potential future purchasers by invading their social media pages with unwanted posts and advertisements, long-term sales might be cannibalized. Unlike traditional marketing costs, which can be carefully managed within firms, the cost of unfollowing appears in the long term, and is hard to measure. This creates an incentive for firms to post more often, especially in the case of information-only posts, because they are pure advertising. Unlike coupons or discounts, which have direct costs, information-only posts have near zero marginal costs, making them an attractive marketing tool for firms.<sup>2</sup> And while leveraging information-only posts is popular among firms on social media, an empirical examination of their potential negative effects in the long term is conspicuously absent from empirical research.

In this study, we bring this empirical tension to bear by examining the impact of non-customized information-only posts on (1) consumer unfollowing and (2) long-term sales. In doing so, we ask two questions. First, what is the effect of receiving non-customized information-only posts on a follower’s propensity to unfollow the firm? Second, as the impact of unfollowing extends in the long term (Crosby et al. 1990), what is the effect of non-customized information-only posts on long-term sales? Results indicate, consistent with prior work (Xie and Lee 2015), that social media posts increase sales immediately after posts are made. However, results further suggest that such posts are strongly correlated with unfollowing, and yield a significant decrease in long-term sales.

The negative impact of social media posting in the long term highlights the need for firms to hone their strategies by carefully evaluating when and where to post. Motivated by the notion that environmental factors make people sensitive to interruption (Andrews et al. 2015; Hennessy and Wiesenthal 1997; Zhou et al. 2017), and the fact that consumers are increasingly accessing social media on mobile devices (Oulasvirta et al. 2012), we also explore the moderating role of the environment on the effect of social media posts. Given that mobile users are sensitive to their environment (i.e., location and time of mobile access; Mallat et al. 2009), we posit that crowding is likely to exacerbate unfollowing and prevent purchases (Altman 1975). We thus ask a third question: how does environmental crowdedness moderate the impact of noncustomized information-only posts on (1) unfollowing and (2) long-term sales? Strikingly, results indicate that the punitive effects of posting can be ameliorated by targeting offpeak hours and less densely populated locales.

To examine these questions, we leverage a longitudinal dataset from a large Chinese fashion retailer who regularly posts on WeChat, the leading Chinese mobile social media platform. Since 2014, WeChat has allowed firms to create business accounts and pages on its platform. Firms can post to their individual WeChat pages, and users may follow or unfollow them at will. After deciding to follow a firm, users receive alerts of new posts on the fan pages in the same way as they receive notifications for messages from their friends. Our data capture the retailer’s social media posts during a 5- month period, as well as the hourly purchases and unfollowing of the focal retailer’s roughly 80,000 followers.<sup>3</sup> These data enable us to execute pooled regressions, a regression discontinuity, and a field experiment to identify the effect. Further, these data grant us access to follower information at the hourly level, which allows us to examine if the effect of social media is moderated by environmental factors (viz. environmental crowdedness). Due to the time window for social media posts (5:00 p.m.\~8:00 p.m. on weekdays) by the focal firm, we are able to take advantage of the congestion associated with peak traffic hours (i.e., rush hour), as an exogenous moderator to people’s within-city practical experience of environmental crowdedness.

Results indicate several findings. First, after controlling for post and consumer characteristics, we find that noncustomized information-only posts increase consumers’ propensity to unfollow. Economically, there is a 280% increase in propensity to unfollow on the day of posting. This translates into a 19% increase in the annual attrition of followers. Second, and strikingly, although non-customized social media posts do increase same-day purchases by roughly 6%, this initial windfall is overwhelmed by decreases in cumulative sales in the long term. Practically, results indicate, on the margin, one additional non-customized post decreases cumulative long-term sales by 7%. Third, findings also indicate that these punitive effects are relegated to postings that occur at specific times and locations. In fact, the penalty for posting accrues almost exclusively at times and locations where there is a spike in environmental crowdedness (i.e., in larger cities and during rush hour). In contrast, while the firm still receives a spike in short-term purchases for posting outside these windows, it accrues little to no penalty when posting in smaller cities or outside rush hour.

Our study contributes to extant literature in at least three ways. First, although scholars note the value of social media in enhancing consumer-firm relationships (Hollebeek et al. 2014; Jahn and Kunz 2012), the impact of “firm-generated” social media on users’ decision to terminate the relationship, or the secondary implications thereof, has received scant attention. We extend this work by showing not only the longterm implications of firm-based social media use, but also when those effects may be attenuated or exacerbated. Second, while prior work on the economic value of social media has focused on how social media posts generally increase sales (Aral et al. 2013; Hutter et al. 2013; Rishika et al. 2013), our findings provide cautionary evidence on the potential downsides of over-exploiting non-customized social media advertisements. Notably, we show the negative impact on longterm sales that results from firms poorly managing their posting strategies. Finally, this work contributes by examining the moderating role of the environment (i.e., environmental crowdedness) on the effect of social media, an area that has received limited attention to date from information systems scholars. As people increasingly access social media on mobile devices (Oulasvirta et al. 2012), the environment is likely to shape their reactions and outcomes. While prior work has noted the positive role of environmental crowdedness in consumer reaction to mobile ads (Andrews et al.

2015),<sup>4</sup> we show a negative moderating effect of environmental crowdedness on unfollowing and long-term sales. This stresses that whom to target, and when to post, on social media must be carefully considered by practitioners when firms elect to focus on information-only posting, which they overwhelmingly do.

## Prior Work and Hypotheses Development

## Background

While extant research has worked extensively to quantify the commercial value of social media posts, the lion’s share of work has focused on the effect of “customer-generated” content (e.g., social media posts or blogs by consumers) (Goh et al. 2013; Hutter et al. 2013; Lee et al. 2015; Rishika et al. 2013; Stephen and Galak 2012). As a result, the effect of firm-generated social media has received less attention, with notable exceptions. A summary of this work, both consumergenerated content (CGC) and firm-generated content (FGC), and their effects on consumer behavior is presented in Table 1.

Literature on FGC can be broadly divided into two distinct, but related, streams. In the first, scholars have argued that a firm’s social media posts strongly affect user engagement (Cvijikj and Michahelles 2013; Luarn et al. 2015; Miller and Tucker 2013). To the extent that social media enhance the ability of firms to reach consumers (Kietzmann et al. 2011), it is unsurprising that firms have increasingly invested in their social media presence, and begun to actively engage with consumers through a variety of social media—for example, fan pages (Heller Baird and Parasnis 2011), integrated media content (Goh et al. 2013), and broader social media platforms like Facebook (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010). Hutter et al. (2013), for example, find that FGC can enhance the intensity of consumer engagement, in the form of both brand awareness and word of mouth (WOM). Similarly, Stephen and Galak (2012) observe that FGC can facilitate consumer participation in the form of shares and online WOM. These studies generally suggest that social media posts may enhance the consumer relationship if properly managed, thus benefitting firms in the long term.

The second stream focuses on the advertising value of information-only posts for firms (i.e., posts which do not offer financial rewards in the forms of coupons or discounts), and explores how posts may affect brand awareness and purchase intent (De Vries et al. 2012; Hutter et al. 2013; Lee et al. 2015; Xie and Lee 2015). As discussed, information-only posts differ from coupon or discount-based posts, inasmuch as they operate as pure advertisements. In this stream, researchers find that posts can affect purchases by informing, persuading, and reminding consumers of the firm’s products. As followers see posts in their timelines, they learn about the firm’s products and become better informed (Hutter et al. 2013). Social media posts may also persuade followers to choose a product by (1) providing product attribute information, (2) improving product perceptions, and (3) developing attachment to the firm by encouraging interaction and fostering a sense of community (De Vries et al. 2012; Laroche et al. 2012). Finally, after persuading followers to consider a product, social media posts may encourage purchase by reminding followers of the firm’s products through the display of product images, or encouraging followers to engage on social media (Goh et al. 2013). These perspectives suggest that social media posts may have advertising value and increase purchasing.

Despite the depth of this work, notable gaps persist. First, while social media success stories are abundant, limited work has been devoted to the potential negative implications of social media posts (Duncan and Caywood 1996; Gurãu 2008). This is problematic, because the most commonly used form of posts (viz information-only posts) create incentive problems. Namely, as they do not have notable, or sometimes even observable, costs, there is an incentive for firms to overuse them in order to reach customers. This stands in direct contrast to coupons or discounts, where material costs are directly observable (which may explain why they are rarely used). Second, while scholars have clearly noted that FGC may enhance the consumer relationship (Rishika et al. 2013), there is also evidence that non-customized posting on the part of firms can alienate and annoy customers (Tsimonis and Dimitriadis 2014). This again comes to an incentive problem for managers in determining if and when to post. To fill this gap, we explore the ramification of social media posting in terms of unfollowing and long-term sales. Finally, we explore when and where, in the form of environmental factors, managers should post in order to ameliorate any deleterious effects of unfollowing, thereby allowing firms to leverage social media posts.

## Social Media Posts and Unfollowing

Why might social media posts drive followers to unfollow the firm? Prior literature indicates three potential reasons: interruption (Bailey and Iqbal 2008; Xia and Sudharshan 2002), privacy (Bélanger and Crossler 2011; Dwyer et al. 2007), and violation of content expectation (Lyren 2014).

Literature on interruption has primarily argued that digital media significantly disrupts a consumer’s execution of tasks, which can lead to their irritation and alienation in the long term (Bailey and Iqbal 2008; Xia and Sudharshan 2002). The basic notion is that interruption induces annoyance (Bailey et al. 2001; Kuhmann et al. 1987), thereby leading to alienation. Empirical evidence from digital application design (viz notifications of incoming messages) corroborates this logic, suggesting that while consumers generally enjoy receiving information in real time, they may also find messages deeply intrusive and irritating (Hudson et al. 2002). As a result, interruptions can negatively affect people’s attitudes toward both the information shown, as well as the interrupter (Xia and Sudharshan 2002). In the context of social media, the content and timing of social media posts should be carefully tailored based on followers’ needs. If it is not, the firm risks losing followers by inadvertently cultivating a negative attitude toward its products (Evans 2010). The fact that social media posts by firms are rarely customized according to consumers’ needs or differentiated from other social media messages (e.g., those from a consumer’s friends) only adds to the case for alienation. Thus, irrelevant non-customized social media posts may be difficult to ignore (as can be done with email marketing). Although consumers may follow firms on social media if they are interested in them (Heller Baird and Parasnis 2011), non-customized social media posts are likely to interrupt, annoy, and alienate followers.<sup>5</sup>

Concomitant work in information systems also suggests that intrusion into a consumer’s social media space may be perceived as an invasion of privacy (Dwyer et al. 2007; Gross and Acquisti 2005; Madden 2012). While consumers often share private data (e.g., names, addresses) on social media, they also view social media as a private space (Ellison et al. 2011; Kaplan and Haenlein 2010; Madden 2012). It is generally understood that when consumers decide to follow a firm on social media, some private information may be shared with the firm, as will any interactions on the fan page. Thus, even though the consumer signed up to follow the firm, it is not difficult to see how consumers might become concerned about their privacy. Put another way, even though privacy concerns may not be at the forefront when a consumer makes the decision to follow (Logan 2014), direct posts may serve as a reminder of the fact that firms leverage private data from social media. Such privacy concerns are amplified in the present era, when both firms and platforms have begun to leverage followers’ private information for commercial use. WeChat, for example, sends personalized ads to consumers based on their browsing history on the platform, which has driven many users to quit the platform (Grigg 2018).

Finally, given that users have selected into following the firm, it is worth considering why they would be annoyed or feel interrupted by posts. Theoretically, when users follow, they expect benefits from social media posts. Yet, such benefits will only manifest if firms send the right type of posts to customers such that they can derive utility from following. It is, therefore, easy to see why, when firms send generic blasts to all followers simultaneously, negative utility could accrue (Goldstein et al. 2014), notably if the consumer was expecting to receive discounts or cost reductions in the form of coupons. Thus, violating the expectations of followers might manifest for one of two reasons. On the one hand, lack of customization might make an advertising content irrelevant to the follower. On the other hand, if the follower expected rewards in the form of coupons for following, not receiving such discounts would also create disutility.

To corroborate the plausibility of intrusion, privacy, and violation of expectations as mechanisms influencing the decision to unfollow the firm, we first directly survey brand followers on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk.<sup>6</sup> In doing so, our goal is to determine why followers, who volitionally follow firms on WeChat and Facebook, unfollow the firms. Results (Appendix B, Table B1) show that, as might be expected, annoyance, interruption, privacy, and content expectations are among the primary reasons followers decide to unfollow (even from those firms they elect to follow). A nontrivial number of respondents unfollow because “posts are not useful or interesting” or “I am not interested in this brand anymore.” This underscores the risk of sending non-customized (content or timing) posts to consumers. As suggested by responses, consumers have heterogeneous expectations when following firms: some of the respondents follow firms because of coupons or discounts (which are rarely, if ever, offered), some do so to consume interesting content about the firm, and others due to an emotional attachment to the firm (i.e., a favorable brand attitude). When firms fail to meet expectations, the chances of posts causing irritation and unfollowing is likely to rise (Goldstein et al. 2014).

The above discussion indicates a cost and benefit tradeoff behind users’ reaction to firms’ posting. On the cost side, interruption, privacy, and failed expectation all exist. In the scenario of non-customized and information-only posts, any of these could easily dominate the lack of utility derived from posts without coupons or discounts, thereby leading to unfollowing. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): When a retailer sends non-customized information-only posts on social media fan pages, those posts may be positively associated with unfollowing.

We empirically test this hypothesis in the context of the retail industry where a fashion retailor sends one post per week on social media. We discuss the generalizability of these hypotheses later in the “Discussion” section.

## Social Media Posts and Long-Term Sales

Thus far, our theorizing suggests there may be negative implications of non-customized social media posting (viz unfollowing). Unlike the observable costs of traditional marketing expenses, which can be carefully managed by firms, the impact of unfollowing would likely manifest in the long term, making it significantly harder to measure. Thus, it may be overlooked or ignored when an immediate spike in sales is observed (De Vries et al. 2012; Hutter et al. 2013). We extend thought on this subject by examining the impact of posting on the sales consumers make in the long term. Social media posts often generate benefits in the form of purchasing, as they largely act as advertisements (Barry and Howard 1990; Meyers-Levy and Malaviya 1999). Yet, as suggested by H1, non-customized information-only posts may also induce unfollowing if they irritate or annoy followers.

There are three plausible reasons unfollowing may lead to a long-term negative effect on sales. First, when users unfollow a firm, they no longer have easy access to product information on fan pages, that is, they no longer receive relevant information (posts) that trigger purchases (Dodds et al. 1991). Second, the negative interaction associated with unfollowing may reduce future purchases by decreasing affinity for the firm and pushing consumers to competitors (Poels and Dewitte 2006; Shimp 1981). This is intuitive, as the now alienated customer might avoid the brand in favor of one that has not annoyed them. Finally, as a sign of a degraded relationship, unfollowing may have a lasting effect by continuing to reduce a consumer’s propensity to purchase the firm’s products after she unfollows (Holbrook and O'Shaughnessy 1984), despite the lack of interruption from the firm after unfollowing. If the increase in short-term purchases facilitated by the social media post cannot compensate for the longterm loss in future sales, social media posts may reduce longterm sales.

Further, as the number of posts rises, these negative effects will likely intensify as the effects on interruption, annoyance, and privacy intrusion become more acerbic. Simultaneously, the advertising effect of posts will diminish, especially when consumers have been fully educated about the firm and start to turn a blind eye to the content in the posts (Meyers-Levy and Malaviya 1999). As unfollowing increases, the advertising value of future posts will degrade, because only existing followers receive posts. Thus, future posts will be less widely viewed and less influential among consumers, making the increase in purchases, facilitated by social media posts, smaller and smaller. On the cost side, the negative implications of unfollowing would likely increase when firms excessively post; the reason being that when firms post more, the chances that posts fail to meet the expectations of followers are likely to rise. As a result, excessive posting may be even more interruptive and annoying (Goldstein et al. 2014), thereby causing unfollowing and further losses in future sales. If this is the case, the more firms post, the more their long-term sales will suffer.<sup>7</sup> Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): When a retailer sends non-customized information-only posts on social media fan pages, those posts may be negatively associated with long-term sales, even in the presence of a short-term increase in sales.

## Moderating Role of Environmental Crowdedness

Our theorizing thus far raises the possibility that noncustomized social media posts might degrade long-term sales. What is equally intriguing to consider is circumstances under which these effects might be intensified or ameliorated. In what follows, we consider the moderating role of the environment, which is becoming increasingly important as consumers access social media from their mobile devices, and the effect of tethered access falls away. Intuitively, mobility allows environmental factors to play a role in a way that tethered devices do not (because the customers are immersed in an environment they do not fully control; Altman 1975). It is, therefore, unsurprising that scholars have begun to examine environmental influence on consumer behavior (Altman 1975; Andrews et al. 2015). Furthering the earlier discussion about annoyance, interruption, and privacy intrusion, and owing to the fact that environmental crowdedness affects stress levels and tolerance toward interruption (Hennessy and Wiesenthal 1997; Zhou et al. 2017), we theorize how environmental crowdedness might moderate the proposed relationship between unfollowing and long-term sales.

Ecological considerations of the impact of crowdedness and population on human behavior have captured the attention of psychologists for decades (Evans and Wener 2007; Stokols 1972). Crowdedness refers to a psychological state in which individuals feel discomfort during interactions with a relatively high density of people (Choi et al. 1976). Due to difficulties in assessing psychological state, population density (the ratio of people to a physical space) has been widely used as an indicator of crowdedness (Freedman 1975). Evans and Wener (2007), for example, found that train crowding during rush hours (i.e., passengers per unit of space, spikes personal stress). Rivera-Batiz and Marti (1995) found that school crowdedness (i.e., students per teaching space) affects instruction and learning. Qin (2005) found that people living in more crowded cities (i.e., greater population density) are at a higher risk of suicide. Overall, the work suggests that environmental crowding plays a role in a person’s psychological state and decision making (Andrews et al. 2015; Choi et al. 1976).

Related to our study, crowdedness has also been viewed as a moderator for people’s sensitivity to interruption (May et al. 2005). One common explanation is that increased crowding exposes people to greater levels of physical and social stimulation, as reflected in the congestion or noise in crowded areas (Stokols 1976). A crowded environment may induce anxiety (Sundel and Sundel 2017) and reduce cognitive capability (Maxwell 2006) by creating a sense of imbalance and disequilibrium. This is often because intense and crowded visual and auditory data can overwhelm the brain (Wells et al. 2016). As a result, people living in crowded cities are less patient than their suburban peers (Wetterer 1999), and are forced to cope by allocating less time to each stimulus (Stokols 1976). Further, when crowdedness yields environmental stimulation that stresses people’s mental capacity, they are forced to tighten their tolerance to each stimulation (Bailey and Iqbal 2008), suggesting interruptions from social media may be perceived as more annoying in a crowded environment.

Privacy theories further suggest that crowdedness makes people more sensitive to intrusion (Altman 1975). As spatial density increases in a crowded environment, physical activities are constrained. A lack of physical space disrupts people’s perceptions of control over privacy and may expose them to unfamiliar social contact (Burgoon 1982). To adapt to this loss of control, stricter mental privacy boundaries are enacted to filter out unwanted interactions from physical surroundings (Altman 1975; Brehm 1972). Such barriers are especially strong in the presence of others, notably if they are viewed as unpredictable, malevolent, or generally unknown to the individual (Stokols 1976). Thus, it is easy to see why crowding may cause people to adjust their boundaries, explaining why people in crowded cities are more isolated as compared to their suburban peers (Fischer 1981) and why commuters in crowded trains use mobile devices to avoid unwanted encounters (Andrews et al. 2015).

We investigate the role of environmental crowdedness at the city level, using two measures: population density and peak traffic hours to proxy locations and times of crowdedness (Andrews et al. 2015; Keiji et al. 2015). While population density is typically used as an indicator of crowdedness, people’s perception of crowdedness may also vary by time (i.e., day-of-the-week and time-of-day). During traffic peak hours (i.e., rush hour), people flood into trains and buses, intensifying the density and crowdedness already experienced within crowded cities (Wicklund 1974). Indeed, there is significant empirical evidence that people feel more crowded on public transit and during heavy commuting times (Hirsch et al. 2014; Lundberg 1976). In large cities, most people commute by public transit, resulting in subways and buses being overcrowded. Research has shown that subway passengers have, on average, less than 2 square feet of area to themselves during rush hours (versus 12 square feet during off-peak) (Andrews et al. 2015). Coupled with the noise, traffic jams, and delays, physical crowdedness during peak hours is likely to elevate people’s emotional or psychological discomfort, leading to an intense feeling of crowdedness (Choi et al. 1976; Evans and Wener 2007).

The connection between crowdedness, the volume of noncustomized social media posts, and the reaction of unfollowing is clear. People who are excessively crowded are forced to process excess physical and psychological stimuli. Excessive stimulation lowers people’s tolerance of unsolicited stimulation (viz social media posts), especially when they face tasks that require concentration (Frankenhaeuser 1981). At the same time, crowdedness may affect people’s desire for privacy. In crowded cities, personal space may be invaded, stimulating a sense of uncertainty and lack of control (Chan 1999) and prompting defensive reactions (Walden et al. 1981). The lower tolerance toward annoyance and interruption and the higher sensitivity to privacy intrusion should make non-customized social media posts more annoying and intrusive in more crowded locations (and less invasive and annoying in less crowded locations). Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The unfollowing penalty associated with non-customized information-only social media posting might be stronger in more crowded environments and weaker in less crowded environments.

Following our logic in H3, it is reasonable to conclude that the intensified effects of unfollowing brought on by crowdedness will also intensify the effects of non-customized posts on long-term sales. While the marketing literature suggests that social media posts may have a positive impact on short-term purchases (Assmus et al. 1984), crowdedness will likely turn such posts into pariahs, undermining their sales benefits and altering consumer attitudes (Basheer and Ibrahim 2010). It is easy to see why posts that are perceived as more irritating (i.e., those sent during times of increased crowdedness) might have smaller, or even negative, effects on purchases (Burton and Lichtenstein 1988).

The privacy and interruption literature corroborates this perspective (Madden 2012). When negative attitudes toward interruption are amplified (i.e., in crowded environments), the beneficial impact of social media posts on short-term purchases is likely to be further attenuated, and an unfavorable impression of the posting firm is likely to manifest (Burton and Lichtenstein 1988). If crowdedness moderates unfollowing (H3), it should intensify the long-term sales loss due to unfollowing. As discussed, people will be more likely to unfollow when receiving social media posts in a crowded environment, since such posts are more annoying and intrusive. As the number of unfollowers increases, the firm loses its ability to contact its follower base through social media, further impeding marketing efforts. Worse, when environmental crowdedness elevates negative attitudes toward the posts to an extent that it affects the consumer relationship, the reduction in long-term sales will be exacerbated (Suh and Yi 2006). Each of these explanations suggests crowdedness will attenuate the positive return on purchases from social media posts, suggesting that the negative effects on sales will be smaller in less crowded environments, and larger in more crowded environments. Therefore:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): The long-term sales penalty associated with non-customized information-only social media posting may be stronger in more crowded environments and weaker in less crowded environments.

## Context and Key Variables

## Context and Data

To test our hypotheses, we leverage data from a top Chinese fashion retailer that maintains a fan page and posts once a week on the social networking platform WeChat. This retailer operates roughly 10,000 brick and mortar stores in China across more than 200 cities. WeChat is the leading social media platform in China, with over 846 million active users spending on average 66 minutes a day on the app. Similar to social media platforms like Facebook, WeChat users can connect with friends, send messages, share content, and comment on each other’s posts. In 2014, the platform began to allow commercial firms to create accounts and fan pages. Firms post to build awareness, and consumers can follow fan pages at their discretion. While posts on the fan page are visible to the public, only followers receive notifications about new posts (in the same manner as messages from their friends). Through these notifications, firms can instantly communicate with their followers. By clicking on the notifications or visiting the fan page directly, consumers can view the details of the firm’s posts. Examples of business accounts, fan pages, and notification pages on WeChat are presented in Figure 1.

The fashion retailer represents an ideal context for several reasons. First, the setting of WeChat and the posting behavior of our focal retailer are similar to other retailers. A comparison of page settings across WeChat and Facebook, the two leading social media platforms in the world, suggests settings are largely similar. Both platforms allow users to follow fan pages they are interested in, allow firms to choose their own posting strategy (i.e., content and time), and allow followers to mute or unfollow fan pages at will. To ensure the focal retailer is representative, we compare its behavior to the top-10 fan pages on Facebook, as determined by the number of followers. In doing so, we retrieve post information for the month of August 2018 on the number of followers, average number of likes and shares, and information about existing promotions and coupons. Results are in Table A1 and suggest the focal retailer is posting in a way that is not out of the ordinary compared to other firms on social media. Post frequency is within a standard deviation of the mean, post quality (as determined by the like rate) is comparable (in fact slightly better), and information-only posts dominate the types of social media posts used by firms (underscoring that coupons are almost never used).

Second, when testing the impact of social media posts, it is reasonable to expect the retailer strategically selects the time of posting according to their promotional activities. If this is true, the retailer would be posting in a manner which is endogenous to factors occurring in the competitive environment. However, communication with social media managers of the focal retailer indicates that the schedule of social media posts is managed independently from other marketing endeavors. As a result, the social media team makes a single post each week (see the schedule in Table A2), with almost all posts being sent by a centralized team to all followers. This requires the posting strategy to be independent of local advertising and promotion campaigns, despite each store having its own schedule of promotions, and precludes the retailer from adjusting its schedule to meet idiosyncratic contextual or strategic considerations.<sup>8</sup> And while it could be argued that four posts per month may not meet the threshold required to alienate or annoy followers, this will simply bias our estimates toward zero.

Third, to find the best time to post, social media managers were engaged in an active period of experimentation and were deliberately sending social media posts at different hours during the day (from 5:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. during weekdays, and from 10:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. on weekends).<sup>9</sup> While the social media team did not engage in any formal endeavor to establish a causal link between posts and purchases, it does bolster the case that the social media team was not explicitly reacting to consumer behavior (an assumption we explore in the “Results” section). As one manager noted, “We did not set a treatment or a control group for an A/B test because we did not want to waste any chance to promote the brand to all followers. However, we attempted to explicitly test this effect by randomly selecting an hour in one day (5:00 p.m. \~ 8:00 p.m. on weekdays, and 10:00 a.m. \~ 6:00 p.m. on weekends) to send the posts.”

Fourth, our data cover the entire purchasing history of each consumer. This offers the benefit of observing all purchases over the course of the retailer’s relationship with its followers. We note that we focus on offline purchase data recorded in the retailer’s loyalty program (in which members enjoy a 5% discount). We do so because the retailer does not have its own online store, and online purchases from third-party marketplaces cannot be tracked at the individual level. As 70% of the retailer’s sales were captured by the loyalty program, this distinction is of little relevance (discussed below).

Due to the size of the follower population (more than one million followers in 2016), we collect data on all followers who followed the retailer on WeChat during the first month of the official fan page launch (April 20, 2015—May 20, 2015). These rich data provide us daily information on consumer behavior of 79,364 followers spanning the five subsequent months (April 2015—September 2015) across 89 cities. Furthermore, these data allow us to access to demographic data, viewing behavior, following status, purchase histories, and location data at the individual level.

## Key Variables

## Dependent Variables

There are two dependent variables in this study. The first dependent variable (to test H1 and H3), Unfollow , is a dummyvariable capturing whether individual i unfollowed the retailer on day t. The second dependent variable (to test H2 and H4), CumulativePurchase , captures the cumulative number of purchases individual i makes between the day she follows the retailer and the end of the sample. That is, the accumulation of CumulativePurchase continues even after individual i unfollows. We use CumulativePurchase as an index for long-term sales.

Before discussing our independent variables of note, we make three distinctions. First, given that our primary data source is offline sales, this explicitly raises the question of channel switching. However, managers at the firm note no meaningful shift in the proportion of online to offline sales during the sample period. We discuss this further in the “Results” and “Discussion” sections. Second, although our dependent variables are defined at the day-level for the main estimations, our data allow us to build empirical models at the hourly level as robustness checks (in “Social Media Posts and Unfollowing (H1)” below). Finally, we replicate our analysis with customer lifetime value (CLV) as an alternative measure for long-term sales. As this CLV measure is calculated in the absence of multi-year data, we retain these results in the Appendix A.

## Independent Variables

There are three main independent variables in this study. Our independent variable for the first DV (Unfollow), is Post . It is a dichotomous indicator which captures whether the retailer sent a social media post to individual i on day t. Once consumers unfollow the retailer, they are dropped from the analysis for the testing of H1 and H3 (as is common in survival models).

Our key independent variable for the second dependent variable (CumulativePurchase), is CumulativePost . This measure captures the cumulative number of social media posts the retailer has sent to individual i between the day she follows the retailer and the end of the observation period. That is, the accumulation of CumulativePost continues even after individual i unfollows and the value of CumulativePost is not influenced by whether individual i is following the retailer. Different from the estimation of the model for Unfollow, estimation of the model for CumulativePurchase includes all data after users unfollow the retailer. In doing so, we capture the impact of social media posts on purchases in the entire period. As a robustness check, we replicate our analysis when the value of CumulativePost ceases to cumulate after users unfollow the retailer. Results are consistent.

To capture environmental crowdedness, we leverage two commonly used measures: (1) population density (Andrews et al. 2015; Langer and Saegert 1977; Rappaport 2008), and (2) peak traffic hours (Andrews et al. 2015; Keiji et al. 2015). Population density, is operationalized as population per square kilometer in the city in which consumers live. The source is the National Bureau of Statistics of China. Given that the measure of crowdedness is correlated with other variables that may confound our estimations (e.g., the socioeconomic development in each city), we take three approaches. First, we include location (store) fixed effects, as socioeconomic development should be largely time invariant during a five-month period. Second, we replicate our analysis after controlling for a host of these confounding factors (see Tables 13 and 14). Finally, we leverage a second measure, peak traffic hours, to capture an exogenous change to people’s experience of crowdedness within a city (in “Moderating Role of Environmental Crowdedness”). ## Empirical Analysis

## Social Media Posts and Unfollowing (H1)

When testing H1, our outcome of interest is a consumer’s propensity to unfollow the fan page after a social media post. Following extant literature (Willett and Singer 1993), we use a discrete-time hazed model. As discussed by Willett and Singer (1993), the main benefit of leveraging a discrete time estimator, as opposed to a continuous estimator like the one proposed by Cox (1972), is the ability to include time varying covariates. Intuitively, this also allows us to include fixed effects to partial out unobserved heterogeneity (recall the Cox Proportional Hazard only allows for the inclusion of shared frailty, which is tantamount to a random effect), and get around the identification problems that numerous scholars have observed (Heckman and Singer 1984). In doing so, we build a longitudinal dataset for each user’s daily following decision, and we regress Unfollow on $P o s t _ { i t } . ^ { 1 0 }$

As the dependent variable is binary, the intuitive estimator would be a logit model. However, when having non-repeated events as the dependent variable (Unfollow), the use of logit regression to estimate individual-fixed effect models with discrete-time data will fail when any covariate is a monotonic function of time (Ln(DaysSinceFollow)) (Allison and Christakis 2006). To remedy this issue, we replicate our estimations using a city-fixed-effect logit, a store-fixedeffect<sup>11</sup> logit, an individual-random-effect logit (Table 5), Cox Proportional Hazard (Table A4), and a field experiment (Table 17). We also consider a more flexible approach to account for unobserved individual heterogeneity: a linear probability model (LPM) with individual-fixed effects, as suggested by the literature (Fernandes and Paunov 2012; Jacobson and Von Schedvin 2015). Given the limitations of LPMs<sup>12</sup> we are cautious when interpreting the results of a discrete-time hazard models (Tutz and Schmid 2016). What is critical to note is that results are consistent across all estimations (viz logit, Cox, linear, and A/B test).

We incorporate a robust set of controls. First, we include LagSinceLastPost to control for the number of days since the previous post sent to individual i to account for the frequency of posts (Bailey et al. 2001). Second, we control for UnitPrice , a measure for the average discounts on day t in the store that individual i followed, as prices may correlate with both unfollowing and the timing of the Post. Third, we control for total number of posts (PreviousCumPost ) and purchases (PreviousCumPurchase ) individual i has received or made before day t. Fourth, we also include a logtransformed indicator of the number of days since individual i followed the retailer (Ln(DaysSinceFollow) ). Finally, we include week fixed effects and day-fixed effects (Monday, Tuesday, …, Sunday) to control for time specific shocks experienced by all individuals. Formally:

$$

$$

where H is the vector of individual-fixed effects, R is the vector of time dummies, and å indicates the error term. Robust standard errors are clustered at the individual level.<sup>13</sup>

## Moderating Role of Environmental Crowdedness

To capture the moderating effect of crowdedness (H3 and H4), we leverage two measures: the log of population density, Ln(PopDensity) (Langer and Saegert 1977; Rappaport 2008) and peak traffic hours (Andrews et al. 2015; Keiji et al. 2015). Ideally, the use of peak traffic hours should reduce endogeneity concerns by exploiting exogenous withincity changes. The definition of peak traffic hours comes from the Traffic Report of China.<sup>14</sup> Posts sent on weekdays between 5:00 p.m. and 7:00 p.m.<sup>15</sup> are cast as peak hour posts (PeakHourPost<sub>it</sub>). All others are cast as NonPeakHourPost<sub>it.</sub>. Data on weekends are excluded.

Before proceeding, it is worth noting that peak traffic hours may also influence people’s propensity to view a post (because they are actively commuting). We control for individual viewing of posts using View (when testing H3), and CumulativeView (when testing H4). View is a dummy of whether individual i viewed any social media post by the retailer on day t. CumulativeView is a measure of the total number of views individual i made during our observation period. To further ensure robustness, we examine the interaction between population density and peak traffic hours. As the variance in crowdedness from peak to off-peak hours is greater in more densely populated cities (Gaode 2015), people should be more likely to experience the variation in crowdedness in more crowded cities (Figure 2). Thus, we should see a stronger moderating role of peak traffic hours in the impact of social media posts on unfollowing/long-term sales, in more densely populated cities. It is worth noting that our measure of crowdedness could be correlated with socioeconomic development (such as income, GDP, brand influence, and etc.) in each city. To ensure robustness, we replicate our analysis controlling for those potential confounds (Table 13 and Table 14). Detailed discussion is in the next section.

We next consider using the regression discontinuity model, which offers us a means to identify the effect. Used extensively in economics (Hahn et al. 2001; Imbens and Lemieux 2008), the principle behind an RD is that, in the presence of potential endogeneity, the researcher can exploit an “as good as random” threshold in order to ensure identification (Lee 2008). In our setting, the treatment threshold is the reception of an additional social media post. A consumer was “treated” (i.e., received one more post) if she followed immediately before a social media post and a consumer was “untreated” (i.e., received one less post) if she followed immediately after the post. We execute the RD design with both a 2-hour window and a 24-hour window around each social media post.

In the simple 2-hour RD, we compare the long-term sales of consumers following one hour before the post to those consumers who follow one hour after the post. Plausibly, those consumers who followed immediately before or after a social media post should be roughly the same (an assumption we assess later in this section), and any discontinuous jumps in the proposed long-term sales across the treated and untreated groups can thus be attributed to the discontinuous changes in the consumers’ treatment status. Roughly 1,300 followers followed the retailer within a two-hour time window of a post. In the 24-hour RD, we include everyone who followed in the day of a post.

Consistent with Equation 2, Ln(CumulativePurchase) is the dependent variable. We control for CumulativePost, calculated based on a day not an hour, so all individuals followed on a same day (no matter before or after the posts) will have a same value for it. Controls such as Ln(MonSinceFollow) are also included. The variable FollowAfterPost , therefore, captures whether user i followed the fan page after a post (recall that individuals who followed immediately after a post would not receive the “additional post” treatment). Our expectation is that the effect of FollowAfterPost will be positive and significant, as users who have received fewer posts will be less likely to unfollow the retailer, and more likely to generate sales in the long term. Our estimator is an OLS with store-fixed effects. Formally:

$$
\begin{array}{c} L n (C u m u l a t i v e P u r c h a s e) _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} F o l l o w A f t e r P o s t _ {i} \\ C o n t r o l V a r i a b l e s + S ^ {\prime} \eta_ {0} \end{array}\tag{[3]}
$$

where S is the vector of store dummies. Robust standard errors are clustered at the store level.

Before discussing our results, we assess three key assumptions of our RD. First, the “as good as random” assumption of the assignment of the treatment. That is, individuals do not exert control over their value of the treatment (i.e., the treatment is exogenous to the individual conditional upon controls). While one might argue that the post itself is causing consumers to follow the retailer, evidence from Table 6 (Columns 3 and 4) suggests there is no significant change in the number of new followers in the hours after the post, and certainly none in the one-hour window. Still, assuming the user might decide to follow the retailer after seeing a post shared by friends, it is reasonable to assume she might click on the post to access the retailer’s fan page.<sup>16</sup> Our data show that only 13 (of 1,315) consumers in our RD clicked the fan page before they followed. We remove these observations. Second, the “comparability of agents around the cutoff.” To validate this assumption, we compare the demographics of individuals who followed one hour before/after posts (Table 8). Results suggest the two groups are not significantly different across demographics, such as age, gender, ratio of reporting age, ratio of reporting gender, city population density, and status of following a competing brand, thereby providing support for the assumption. We believe we can credibly claim that the process of new followership is organic, and people did not exert control over their value of the treatment (following before or after the post). Third, “continuity of potential outcomes.” That is, the outcome would have been continuous at the cut-off in the absence of the intervention. To validate this assumption, we examine the long-term sales of consumers who followed in all hours of the posting day (24-hour window). We include data on all consumers who followed on the day of a post and replicate estimation of our Equation 3 after controlling for HourOfFollow , hour in the day consumer i followed the retailer and HourOfFollow Sqrt . Further, we plot the average of the fitted value of LnCumulativePurchase over each hour of the treatment day in Figure 3. As can be seen, there is a sharp discontinuity in LnCumulativePurchase on either side of the cut-off (i.e., post). This supports the assumption that the outcome would have been continuous absent the intervention. Further discussions of the RD assumptions can be found in the “Robustness Checks” section.

Results of the RD are in Table 9 (2-hour) and Table 10 (24- hour), and show a significant and negative relationship between posts and long-term sales. In Table 9, the coefficient of FollowAfterPost suggests a negative effect of social media posts on long-term sales because those consumers who follow the retailer immediately after a post, and therefore do not receive the post, purchase more than those who followed the retailer just before the post. This supports for H2. To ensure robustness, we replicate the analysis with followers who followed the fan page within a four-hour window and capture the effect for one, two, and three hours after the posts. Results are consistent and available upon request. In Table 10, we also see a consistently positive coefficient of FollowAfterPost, even after controlling for the hour of following. This once again provides support for H2.

It is worth noting one concern with these estimations: channel switching (i.e., individuals purchasing products online as opposed to in stores). According to the firm, 92% of sales took place offline during the sample period. Given the 7% decrease of the long-term offline sales, this would require an 80% increase in online sales to counterbalance the observed effect $( 7 \% \times 9 2 \% / 8 \% = 8 0 . 5 \% )$ However, as mentioned, managers observed no material shift in the balance between online and offline sales during the sample. Taken in sum, these results provide strong support for H2.

## Moderating Role of Environment Crowdedness and Unfollowing (H3)

We next explore heterogeneity in the effect to determine under what conditions the punitive effects of posting might be intensified, minimized, or even eliminated. Possessing this knowledge would allow the firm to exploit the financial windfalls in short-term purchasing without suffering the subsequent penalties of unfollowing. To test H3, we examine the moderating role of crowdedness in unfollowing. Similar to Equation 1, the estimation is a discrete-time survival model. Owing to the difficulty of interpreting interaction terms in non-linear models (Hoetker 2007), we estimate the moderating effect of crowdedness using LPM with heteroskedastic-consistent robust standard errors.

Results are in Table 11. In Column 2, we see that Post×Ln(PopDensity) is significant and positive (p < 0.001), which suggests the effect of social media posts on unfollowing is more intense in cities with greater population density, suggesting firms should avoid posting in more crowded cities.<sup>17</sup> In other words, more people unfollow after a non-customized post in a crowded city as compared with a less crowded city. In Column 3, the coefficient of PeakHourPost is positive and greater than the coefficient of NonPeakHourPost,<sup>18</sup>suggesting that unfollowing accelerates when posts are sent during peak traffic hours. Finally, Column 4 indicates that the interaction of Ln(PopDensity) and PeakHourPost is positive and significant (p < 0.001). A comparison of the coefficients across the interactions indicates the effect of peak traffic hours is greater in more crowded cities. All estimates consistently support H3. Intuitively, these results suggest firms should target smaller areas at off-peak hours.

## Moderating Role of Environment Crowdedness and Long-Term Sales (H4)

To examine H4, we replicate Equation 2, as interacted with our measures of crowdedness. Our main estimator is an OLS with store-fixed effects. When estimating the role of crowdedness in the long term, CumulativePost is transformed into two measures: CumulativePeakHourPost , the number of peak-traffic-hour posts sent to individual i, and CumulativeNonPeakHourPost , the number of off-peak-traffic-hour posts. Results are in Table 12. As expected, Column 2 indicates that population density intensifies the negative effect of posts on long-term cumulative sales $\left( \mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ , that is, the penalty to long-term sales is greater in more crowded cities and almost non-existent in smaller cities. Column 3 corroborates this finding, showing a negative and significant (p $< 0 . 0 5 )$ relationship between CumulativePeakHourPost and long-term sales. Strikingly, and consistent with the story that firms should avoid peak hours, we observe no deleterious effect of CumulativeNonPeakHourPost. Once again, this suggests that peak posting exacerbates the negative effect of social media posts and long-term sales, but off-peak posting does not. Column 4 provides additional support for H4, showing a negative and significant interaction between CumulativePeakHourPost and Ln(PopDensity) $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and a stronger interaction than CumulativeNonPeakHourPost and Ln(PopDensity). Thus, the decrease in long-term sales is greater in more crowded cities, especially when the post is sent during peak traffic hours. Finally, we observe no punitive effect of posting in smaller areas off-peak hours. This suggests firms should target smaller cities or post during offpeak hours in order to capture the short-term rents associated with posting, while avoiding the penalties that come with unfollowing.

To ensure robustness we replicate these estimations while controlling for socioeconomic development. Results are in Table 14. The negative and significant CumulativePeakHourPost $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , as well as the significantly negative interaction between CumulativePeakHourPost and Ln(PopDensity) $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ , remain consistent with the estimates without socioeconomic controls, and support H4.

## Robustness Checks

Our findings rely on several identifying assumptions, the violation of which would call into question the robustness of our results. We therefore subject our work to a battery of falsification tests to rule out alternate possibilities. These include (1) an investigation into the effect of social media posts on short-term purchase, (2) a field experiment (A/B test), (3) additional validation of the Regression Discontinuity, (4) an exploration of the role of post characteristics (e.g., post frequency, post quality, post content) and consumer characteristics (e.g., following duration and previous purchase amounts) in the impact of social media posts, (5) a replication of our base analysis of unfollowing using a traditional survival analysis (i.e., a Cox proportional hazard model), (6) an examination of when the decrease in long-term purchase occurs (before or after unfollowing),(7) validation of the 5-month window, (8) replication of our analysis using customer lifetime value, (9) an exploration of the effect of peak traffic hours across cities with different commuting methods, and (10) an examination of whether social media posting is a reaction of the retailer to past sales. A summary and results of these tests are in Table 15. In each of these tests, results consistently indicate that while posting by firms stimulates short-term purchases, there is a penalty to unfollowing and long-term sales when posting in a crowded environment. In the interest of space, we discuss the first three critical checks of our basic claim of identification. A comprehensive discussion of all other robustness checks is available in Appendix A.

## Effect of Social Media Posting on Short-Term Purchases

During the course of our theorizing, one tacit assumption we have made is that social media posts stimulate short-term purchase. Given the state of extant literature, such an assumption is not unreasonable (Hutter et al. 2013; Rishika et al. 2013; Stephen and Galak 2012). However, it is important to validate such an assumption. While we theorize that social media posts will have a negative impact on long-term sales by increasing unfollowing, an alternative explaination in our context is that the observed posts are ineffective as ads and do not significantly influence purchase. Such an effect would undercut the validity and generalizability of our finding. To investigate such an effect, we use a fixed-effect logit model. We then regress $P u r c h a s e _ { i t } ,$ a dummy variable indicating whether individual i made a purchase on day t, on $P o s t _ { i t }$ conditional upon the controls in Equation 1. To ensure the robustness of our analysis, we replicate the same estimation with different definitions of short term (e.g., purchase within one to five days). Results are in Columns 1–5 of Table 15 and indicate a significant increase in short-term purchases after the retailer makes a social media post $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Given the inherent difficulty in calculating marginal effects in logit models, we replicate these estimations using an LPM (Column 6 of Table 16). Results indicate that a single social media post increases the probability of daily purchases by 0.0162% from a baseline of 0.25% (without social media posts), or a 6% increase in the probability of purchase. Intuitively, this corroborates our baseline assumption that social media posts spike short-term sales. Estimations at the hour level show consistent results (Table 6). Detailed discussion can be found in the previous section.

## A Field Experiment (A/B Test)

In the main analysis, we have taken several steps to corroborate the main findings from the paper. To further achieve robust statistical identification, we introduce a field experiment (an A/B test) conducted by the retailer. This experiment was conducted in a single large Chinese city in 2017. To execute the experiment, the retailer randomly separated followers in a focal city into two groups, sent one post to group A (treated group) in one day, and sent no posts to group B (control group) in the same day, thereby allowing them to explicitly capture how different groups reacted to the presence or absence of a post. Results are in Table 17, and remain consistent with our main analysis. As can be seen, the treated group was 17 times more likely to unfollow the retailer and 16 times more likely to make a same day purchase. However, sales over the next two months were 22% lower among the treatment group, suggesting that there is a negative effect in the long term. Once again, these results provide broad corroborative evidence for H1 and H2.

## Further Validation of the Regression Discontinuity Design

Besides the three key assumptions of RD discussed in the “Empirical Analysis” section, we further diagnose the validity of the RD via falsification checks suggested by Imbens and Lemieux (2008). First, tests of the RD should explore both linear and nonlinear relationships between the forcing variable (i.e., following) and the outcome (cumulative purchase). In the 24-hour analysis, we have assumed a nonlinear relationship between the forcing variable and the outcome by including both HourOfFollow and HourOfFollow\_Sqrt. To explore a linear relationship, we replicate this analysis with only HourOfFollow and exclude HourOfFollow\_Sqrt. Results are consistent. This should ensure results are not sensitive to alternative specifications.

Second, we examine the continuity structure of the covariates. In most cases, the reason for a discontinuity is the treatment itself. However, it is possible that there is a discontinuity in the value of covariates (suggesting the co-variates are the driving the observed change rather than the treatment). To safeguard against this, we replicate the analysis using the covariates age, gender, ratio of reporting age, ratio of reporting gender, city population density, and following a competing brand as dependent variables. Results yield no significant relationship between these variables and posts.

Third, we include covariates in the estimations. This should alleviate the concern that the difference in outcomes before and after the cutoff are a result of the heterogeneity across individuals closest to the cut-off. In doing so, we replicate the analysis by including covariates such as age, gender, ratio of reporting age, ratio of reporting gender, city population density, and status of following a competing brand as control variables. Results are consistent.

## Discussion

## Key Findings

In this study, we examined the role of non-customized social media posts, specifically information-only posts with advertising content, in affecting consumers’ decisions to unfollow a firm and purchase in the long-term. Sending social media posts is a popular marketing tool for firms as it can elicit short-term purchases with seemingly little cost. However, findings suggest social media posts can alienate followers, causing them to unfollow, and jeopardizing long-term sales. The cost of unfollowing, by virtue of being difficult to measure, has not been fully examined by scholars; meaning the impact of social media posting on long-term sales has remained unclear. Using a dataset from a large fashion retailer, findings suggest that non-customized informationonly social media posts can negatively impact the firm’s longterm sales, despite the short-term benefits posts may yield. Yet, these penalties can be avoided by timing posts properly (i.e., in less crowded cities and off-peak hours). Results show that the penalty for posting only manifests when firms post in densely populated area, or when they post at times of peak travel stress. These findings stress the need for firms to rethink their social media strategies, and be judicious in their communication patterns.

## Generalizability

Given the idiosyncrasies of the empirical investigation, it is important to ensure the generalizability of our findings. As suggested by the idea of structural causal models (Tafti and Shmueli 2020), we discuss the generalizability from four angles: (1) the WeChat setting, (2) the retailer’s social media posting behavior, (3) the proposed mechanisms of annoyance, privacy, and content expectation across platforms and countries, and (4) environmental factors that may relate to consumer response.

As discussed, a comparison of fan page settings on WeChat and Facebook, the two leading social media platforms in the world, yields little observable differences across the settings of these platforms. Specifically, on both platforms, consumers can follow and unfollow at will, firms can post, and notifications to followers are similar (see the discussion in the “Context and Key Variables” section). It is, therefore, plausible that the observed effects are not limited to the WeChat setting.

It is also worth considering if the retailer’s posting behavior is representative. In doing so, we compare the post frequency, content, and quality of the retailer with those of the top-10 fan pages on Facebook (Table A1 in Appendix A). The retailer sends posts at the frequency of 1 post per week. This post frequency is lower than 8 of the top 10 fan pages on Facebook, indicating that the result is unlikely a function of an extraordinary post frequency of the retailer. A calculation of our economic effects<sup>19</sup> suggests the punitive effects should manifest in settings where retailers send posts more frequently than every 48 days (e.g., all the top 10 fan pages). Regarding post content (viz posts being information-only), our findings speak to much of firm behavior on social media platforms. Results from Facebook indicate the posts made by firms are almost exclusively information-only. Coupons do not appear to be a strategy employed by firms on social media. In terms of post quality, at least in theory, a high-quality post should be one post in which the contents meet customers’ expectation. A post of poor quality should be the opposite. Given that different customers are likely to have different views of the same content based on heterogeneous tastes, the quality of posts would generally be disappointing if not customized (at least to a subset of followers), leading to unfollowing and decrease in long-term sales. From this perspective, it is likely other firms face these same challenges, given that most retailers are sending non-customized posts (i.e., at least a subset of followers will dislike the content).

To ensure that our theoretical mechanisms (interruption, annoyance, and privacy intrusion) generalize outside of WeChat and China, we surveyed American and Chinese users of Facebook and WeChat (discussed in the subsection on “Social Media Posts and Unfollowing). Details are in Appendix B. Results support annoyance, interruption, privacy intrusion, and expectation violation as mechanisms across American and Chinese users of Facebook and WeChat. Given this evidence, we believe the proposed effects are not unique to Chinese consumers.

Finally, we explore environmental factors (e.g., definitions of long-term sales and environmental crowdedness) that may undermine external validity. In the main analysis, we find consistent impact of posts on long-term sales with 2-month, 3- month, …, 5-month cumulative sales. Yet, it is worth exploring the lower bound of the effect. In doing so, we replicate our analysis of long-term sales with one-week, twoweek, and so on, up to an eight-week time window. Results (Table 18) show that, with the exception of the analysis using a one-week window, shorter time horizons (e.g., two and three weeks) show a positive relationship between CumulativePost and cumulative purchase (showcasing the short-term effect demonstrated in the paper). However, in Week 4 and 5 an inversion occurs, with the effect becoming insignificant. The cumulative effect becomes significant and negative in Week 6. This supports our argument that when we consider the cumulative sales in the long term, the positive impact of posts on sales gives way to an overall negative effect. By Week 8 (two months) the coefficient of CumulativePost stabilizes to roughly the level we observe in the main model, suggesting that, in our context, the long-term punitive effects of posts are visible from 6 weeks on, and stable from 2 months on. With regard to population density, results in Table 12 indicate that the negative effect of posting on long-term sales manifests when posts are sent in city with population density greater than 7.38 people per square km (e^(0.0162/0.00825)=e^2) (i.e., most large cities in the United States).

## Contributions and Implications for Practice

Three notable contributions stem from this research. First, although prior literature suggests attempts to contact consumers on social media may create annoyance (Fournier and Avery 2011; Hutter et al. 2013; Rishika et al. 2013), we are able to demonstrate a direct link between posting and unfollowing. This measure of the unanticipated costs of social media posts (viz unfollowing and the subsequent decrease in sales, offers several benefits over self-reported measures of annoyance, interruption, or privacy concerns; Tucker 2014). Unfollowing is immediately visible, providing a means to observe the change associated with the timing of social media posts. Considering the inherent tension between the positive role of social media posts in purchases, and the negative role posts may play in unfollowing (Holbrook and O’Shaughnessy 1984; Taylor et al. 2011), our findings yield important implications for theory and practice. Chiefly, social media posts should not be viewed as a “costless” or “risk-free” way to reach consumers. Firms should consider the costs of unfollowing by strategically posting on social media to maximize benefits while minimizing fallout by carefully timing posts in specific areas.

Second, we extend prior studies on the business value of social media by considering the effect on long-term revenues. Prior work has overwhelmingly focused on short-term purchases (Hutter et al. 2013; Stephen and Galak 2012). Coupled with our ability to effectively observe unfollowing, we are able to take a broader view of the effect of social media posts. Strikingly, evidence does not support a longterm increase in revenues from posting (despite an increase in short-term purchases). Instead, a significantly decrease in long-term sales is observed, notably when firms post in the wrong locations or times (i.e., when users are crowded). This underscores the need for firms to be careful when leveraging social media to connect with followers, and illustrates the challenges of social media use. Further work is needed to probe the boundary conditions of these effects.

Third, this work offers insights on the interaction of customer behavior with social media and the environment, an increasingly prominent phenomenon that has received scant attention from IS researchers. The adoption of mobile devices grants people ubiquitous access (Oulasvirta et al. 2012), allowing environmental factors (e.g., light, noise, crowdedness) to interfere with the user experience. Thus, where and how devices are used is increasingly important for the success of new technologies. Our work contributes to this space by highlighting one factor, that is, environmental crowdedness, and how it may moderate the influence of social media (Aral et al. 2013; Laroche et al. 2013; Miller and Tucker 2013; Rishika et al. 2013). While prior work has begun to examine the impact of environmental factors on consumer reaction to coupons (Andrews et al. 2015), limited work has expanded this corpus of thought to information-only posts. Our work pushes this boundary by raising the fundamental question of when to target consumers, and illustrates how to ameliorate the penalties firms may face if they elect to focus on information-onlyposting, which theyoverwhelmingly do. Although our work focuses on crowdedness, rich opportunities to extend this research exist, for example, weather (Levy and Galili 2008), differences across physical and digital channels (Levin et al. 2003), industries (Ackerberg 2003), or search/ experience goods (Ackerberg 2003; Levin et al. 2003).

This work also has implications for marketing managers who wish to leverage social media for marketing purposes. Firms naturally seek immediate and costless gains, and the lure of short-term purchasing on the day of each social media post may incentivize firms to post to excess. However, we demonstrate that posting on social media is not costless. Specifically, non-customized information-only social media posts may create negative externalities in the form of consumers unfollowing the firm; notably if the firm posts in the wrong location or at the wrong time. To the extent that this unfollowing reduces future sales, the negative implications should be considered when firms design their social media strategies. Such findings suggest that in order to overcome these costs, firms should explore and experiment with the host of options they are imbued with, ranging from: customization, to added benefits (e.g., coupons or discounts), to the thoughtful selection of which subgroup of the followers they are targeting with posts. Given that the cost of posting is higher in big cities (i.e., cities with a higher population density), and during times of commuting stress, we believe it is incumbent upon managers to use this information when targeting their followers. It would be prudent, for example, to post less frequently in large urban areas. And when such posts are made, they should be made at times when followers are not already overwhelmed or overcrowded. By judiciously targeting their customer bases, managers should be able to reap the shortterm benefits of posting without cannibalizing long term sales.

## Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

This paper is subject to certain limitations, which offer fruitful opportunities for future work. First, as the firm uses only third parties for its online channel, where individual-level sales cannot be tracked, we are compelled to leverage offline purchases. This implicitly raises the issue of channel switching. If there is a large migration from offline to online sales, our conclusion that social media posts reduce long-term sales may be biased. However, as discussed, offline sales represent 92% of total sales. Therefore, it would require an 80% increase in online sales in order to counterbalance the 7% decrease in long-term offline sales (7% × 92% / 8% = 80.5%). Although managers at the retailer report that the mix of online and offline sales remained unchanged in the observation period, this is still worth noting. Second, our context is limited to a single product type (luxury shoes). To the degree that the role of social media posts may vary across product categories, future research could investigate whether the negative effect of posts is supported in other contexts. For example, the role of social media posts in sales may vary across products with lower purchase frequency (e.g., cars) and commoditized search products (e.g., books) (Assmus et al. 1984; Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010; Hutter et al. 2013). Third, our study focuses on information-only posts. It is clear that discounts and coupons would change this calculus for both the firm and the follower. The exploration of these dynamics provides a rich opportunity for future work. By exploring both the tangible (i.e., coupon costs in the form of lost revenue) and intangible (i.e., lost sales opportunities as a result of unfollowing) costs scholars can contribute to this emerging stream of literature.

## Concluding Remarks

There is a growing concern that firms are rushing into social media without fully understanding how consumers react to social media posts (Aral et al. 2013; Fournier and Avery 2011; Laroche et al. 2013). This lack of understanding of social media, coupled with limited means by which long-term empirical evidence may be gathered, has stunted research on the long-term implications of social media use. Our analysis of nearly 80,000 followers of a major fashion retailer allows us to explore such issues. Findings indicate that although social media posts have the power to elicit short-term purchases, non-customized information-only social media posting can lead to unfollowing and lower long-term sales. Further, results indicate that these penalties can be ameliorated with proper strategies, that is, sending social media posts to consumers in less crowded cities and during less crowded hours of a day. Integrating these results with the competing benefit from short-term purchases, we conclude that practitioners must better understand the pitfalls of exploiting social media as a marketing tool, and adjust their social media strategies to include environmental factors.

## Controlling for Post and Consumer Characteristics

To ensure that our empirical findings are robust across posts and consumers, we replicate our base analysis accounting for post and consumer characteristics. Our findings in the main analysis remain consistent after controlling for these variables, suggesting that our findings can be applied in more generalizable cases across posts and consumers.

Empirically, we control for social media post characteristics, such as the role of post frequency (i.e., controlling for LagSinceLastPost in Equation 1 and post content (i.e., replicating our analysis when excluding the posts which mentioned existing promotions). To ensure that our findings are not a result of the low quality of posts, we replicate our analysis by controlling for post quality. Based on the “likes” rate (the number of likes of each post/ the number of views of each post), we divide all posts into three groups: the top one-third of posts by like rate (high-quality posts), the middle one-third as mid-quality posts, and the bottom one-third as low-quality posts. Such an approach is intuitive, if users like a post more, it is presumable that the post is of higher quality. Results in Table A3 indicate when the post is moderated by post quality, the impact of posts on unfollowing remains significant and positive (i.e., posts result in unfollowing the retailer). Interestingly, results show that posts with higher liking rates accelerate the amount of unfollowing. While striking at first, this may be a function for the retaile sending group-targeted content, which increases the likes from a specific segment of the retailer’s followers, but is irrelevant to other groups. For example, if the retailer sends a post specifically about women’s shoes, it may draw the attention of and be liked by more female followers, while simultaneously perceived as irrelevant and irritating to male followers. Obviously, while this explanation is speculative, it is commonly observed. What is critical is that the post quality does not appear to change the basic relationship between social media posting and unfol lowing. A replication of the analysis with click rate show consistent result (Column 2 of Table A3). All the results above all help alleviate the concern that the firm may adjust its social media posting behavior according to privacy concerns and attention that it has received in recen years.

We also replicate our analysis controlling for consumer characteristics (e.g., following duration and previous purchase amounts). Results suggest that consumer characteristics do not appear to change the basic relationship between social media posting and unfollowing Interestingly, we find that following duration (i.e., how long one consumer has been following the retailer) significantly decreases the negative impact of social media posting, and the negative impact of posting is stronger for new followers than old followers. One plausible explanation is that a longer following duration indicates loyalty customers, and loyalty customers are less likely to get annoyed by those social media posts

## Cox Proportional Hazard Model

As we use a discrete survival model to test the impact of social media posts on unfollowing in the main analysis, we replicate our estimations with a continuous (Cox) survival model to ensure results are not an artifact of model selection. Since individual-fixed effects are not feasible in such an estimation, we estimate pooled regression, with city fixed effects (i.e., stratification on cities), and with store fixed effects (i.e., stratification on stores), respectively. The variable Unfollow is coded 1 if individual i unfollowed the retailer on day t, 0 otherwise. Followers that do not unfollow are censored on that day, and followers are removed from the sample once they unfollow. Results are in Table A4 and remain consistent; the presence of a Post significantly decreases the following time.

## Purchase Behavior after Consumers Unfollow

In the theoretical discussion, we claim that firms’ posts will reduce future purchase from consumers if they are annoyed and decide to unfollow. To ensure the veracity of this claim we need to explore when the decrease in purchasing happens (as it relates to unfollowing). We thus execute two empirical tests.

First, we separately estimate the effect of social media posts on short-term purchases before and after consumers unfollow the retailer. We do so to ensure that the loss of sales occurs after consumers unfollow, and that the impact on purchases between followers and unfollowers is different. In doing so, we regress short-term purchase on posting for consumers when they are following and when they have already unfollowed the retailer. Results are in Column 7 and 8 of Table 16 and suggest that social media posts significantly influence short-term purchases only before the consumers unfollow the retailer and did not influence purchases after they unfollow. This is critical as consumers do not receive notifications after unfollowing. Thus, there should be no advertising effect on their short-term purchases. Obviously, this model cannot be run on the long-term CumulativePurchase because of how the variable is calculated (i.e., sales over the entire time window).

Second, as seen in Column 1–6 of Table 16, we include the variable AfterUnfollow in the model of short-term purchases. This dichotomous indicator captures whether or not the follower has already unfollowed the retailer at the time when the post is released, and it should capture the difference in purchase behavior for followers and unfollowers directly. As can be seen from the coefficient of AfterUnfollow, there is a negative relationship between unfollowing and short-term purchase, suggesting that the difference between follower purchases and unfollower purchases is nontrivial. This further supports H2 by indicating that unfollowing is negatively associated with future purchase.

## Justification of the Long-Term Window

It is worth acknowledging that sales in the “long-term” is somewhat ambiguous, and classical definitions from economics (i.e., when both factors of production and cost are variable) offer little guidance in our context. As a result, we have been cautious to repeat our analyses using varying windows, viz 2-month, 3-month, 4-month, and 5-month samples, with results remaining consistent under each sample. Yet, it is worth considering whether our results would change if the sample window was extended beyond the 5-month window. To further alleviate this concern, we replicate our base analysis of unfollowing and long-term sales with a 12-month sample. We note that although our contacts at the retailer were not able provide a full year on the entire sample, they were able to provide us a 12-month sample comprised of 30,000 randomly selected followers in the headquarter city. These data capture customer unfollowing and purchase data from January to December 2016. We replicate our base estimations with these data (the crowdedness estimations cannot be estimated due to the nature of the data). Results are in Table A5 and remain consistent with the effects observed in the main estimations. This broadly suggests that the length of time window does not significantly bias the results.

## Customer Lifetime Value as a Measure for Long-Term Sales

To further support the impact of posts on firms’ long-term financial success, we examine the impact on customer lifetime value (CLV) as an alternative measure for long-term sales. In doing so, we replicate our analysis of long-term sales with a simplified version of the CLV mode borrowed from the marketing literature (Andon et al. 2003). The calculation is below:

$$
\mathrm{CLV} = \mathrm{GC} \times \left(r / (1 + d - r)\right)
$$

where GC is yearly gross profit per customer, r is the yearly retention rate, d is the yearly discount rate. As in our data we only have data of 5 months, we mimic yearly data based on the average in the time window of each analysis. Results with this measure as our dependent variable are in Table A6. Results remain consistent with the effects observed in the main estimations.

## Justification of Effects of Peak Traffic Hours

We have used peak traffic hours as a measure of crowdedness, based on both prior literature (Andrews et al. 2015; Keiji et al. 2015) and traffic data across cities (Gaode 2015). To further support the validity of this measure, we explore heterogeneous impacts of PeakHourPost across cities based on alternative commuting methods (i.e., public transportation versus private vehicles).

Theoretically, the moderating role of peak traffic hours should be greater in cities where people use more public transportation than private vehicles (Beirão and Cabral 2007), the reason being that when people commute via public transit they are more likely to physically experience the crowd in limited space. In China, most people commute using public transportation, resulting in subways and other forms of public transportation being especially crowded during peak commuting hours. To the degree that physical proximity is a key catalyst of the stres induced by crowdedness, proximity that is absent when commuting by a private vehicle, it stands to reason that cities more reliant on public transportation would be affected to a greater degree. To examine this moderator, we collect data on the private ownership of automobiles from the Chinese Department of Transportation, the total miles of subway lines in each city from Gaode (the second largest navigation app in China).<sup>20</sup> Because Gaode only publishes data for large cities, this analysis is based on data of the largest 26 cities in China. As city access to commute methods may correlate to local incomes, we also control for the average income for the residents in each city. We then replicate our estimation as moderated by public transportation.

Table A7 indicates that the moderating effect of peak traffic hours on unfollowing varies significantly across cities based on access to public transit. From Table A7, we see the effect of peak traffic hour posts only manifests in cities where people have increased subway access. In contrast, increased ownership of private vehicles exhibits no such effect.<sup>21</sup> This is expected, as consumers are more likely to experience crowdedness during peak-traffic hours when they commute by public transit. These results offer further support for H3 that consumers will unfollow the retailer based on levels of environmental crowdedness when the posts are received.

Table A8 further replicates the estimation of long-term sales using the moderating effect of public transit access. Results indicate that the moderating role of peak traffic hours in long-term sales is also moderated by access to public transportation. More specifically, results indicate that the moderating effect of peak traffic hours on long-term sales intensifies in cities where people use more subway trains, thereby providing additional support for H4.

## Analytical Model of Firm and Follower Behavior

To support our H2, we use an analytical model to illustrate the tradeoff between the benefits and the costs of posing on social media.

We discuss the benefits and the costs of posting starting from a simple case where firms send one and only one post to customers. In order to compare the impact on short-term sales with the impact on future sales, we borrow the idea of present value. Then we induce our findings to a more generalized scenario where firms repeatedly send posts.

To begin with, we hereby define several key variables in the model:

• $\mathrm { F _ { i } }$ as the number of followers right before the post i is sent;

$\mathrm { U _ { i } }$ as the unfollowing rate for post i, which is defined as the number of unfollowers due to post i divided by the number of followers at the beginning of post i ;

• $\mathrm { X } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ as the average impact of post i on the present value of the immediate sales per existing follower;

$\mathrm { Y } _ { \mathrm { i } } { \mathrm { a s } }$ the average impact of post i on the present value of the futures sales per unfollower;

$\Delta \mathrm { B } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ as the present value of the change in immediate sales resulted from the advertisement value of post i (the benefit);

• $\Delta \mathrm { C _ { i } }$ as the present value of the change in future sales resulted from the negative effect of post i (the cost);

$\mathrm { R _ { i } }$ as the net present value of the impact of post i and based on the definitions, $\mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { i } } = \Delta \mathrm { B } _ { \mathrm { i } } - \Delta \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { i } }$

In the case of firms sending one post only, the tradeoff of benefits and costs of the posting is simply the sign of $\mathsf { R } _ { 1 }$ . Based on the definition, the benefit of a single post $( \Delta \mathbf { B } _ { 1 } )$ is a function of the initial number of followers $\left( \mathrm { F } _ { 1 } \right)$ and the average impact on immediate sales $( \mathrm { X } _ { 1 } ) _ { : }$ , while the costs of a single post $( \Delta \mathbf { C } _ { 1 } )$ is a function of the initial number of followers $\left( \mathrm { F } _ { 1 } \right)$ , the unfollowing rate(U ), and the average impact on future sales per unfollower $\left( \mathrm { Y } _ { 1 } \right)$ . Therefore, $\mathrm { R } _ { 1 }$ can be rewritten as

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {1} = \mathrm{F} _ {1} \times \mathrm{X} _ {1} - \mathrm{F} _ {1} \times \mathrm{U} _ {1} \times \mathrm{Y} _ {1}
$$

Divide F1 from both sides, we get

$$
\mathrm{r} _ {1} = \mathrm{R} _ {1} / \mathrm{F} _ {1} = \mathrm{X} _ {1} - \mathrm{U} _ {1} \times \mathrm{Y} _ {1}
$$

where $\mathbf { r } _ { 1 }$ is the average net impact of post i per existing customer.

As discussed in the paper, the value of $\mathrm { \Delta X _ { l } }$ and $\mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ depend on many other factors, such as the content and the quality of the post and the timing of the post. For example, a better designed post might lead to a higher level of $\mathrm { \Delta X _ { l } }$ and lower level of $\mathrm { \Delta U _ { 1 } }$ . The tradeoff of the benefits and costs will depend on these factors.

We then generalize our discussion to a more practical case where firms repeatedly send n posts, with a few assumptions:

• The initial pool of followers will not change, that is, our discussion does not include new followers attracted by other activities done by the firm as this is out of the scope of our study.

• The posts will not attract new followers outside the initial pool of followers (no spillover effect). Empirical support can be found in Table 11.

• Those who unfollow due to the previous posts will not follow again in the foreseeable future, which is supported by the fact that such event seldomly happened in our data.

Based on these assumptions, the number of followers prior to post i ( i \$ 2) is

$$
F _ {i} = F _ {i - 1} \times (1 - U _ {i})
$$

Therefore, the net present value of the impact of all these n posts is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} R _ {i} & = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(r _ {i} \times F _ {i}\right) = r _ {i} \times F _ {1} + r _ {2} \times F _ {1} \times \left(1 - U _ {1}\right) + r _ {3} \times F _ {1} \times \left(1 - U _ {1}\right) \times \left(1 - U _ {2}\right) + \dots + \\ & \quad r _ {n} \times F _ {1} \times \left(1 - U _ {1}\right) \times \left(1 - U _ {2}\right) \times \dots \times \left(1 - U _ {n - 1}\right) \end{array}
$$

Divide F from both sides, we get

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} R _ {i} / F _ {1} = r _ {1} + r _ {2} \times F _ {1} \times (1 - U _ {1}) + r _ {3} \times F _ {1} \times (1 - U _ {1}) \times (1 - U _ {2}) + \dots + r _ {n} F _ {1} \times (1 - U _ {1}) \times (1 - U _ {2}) \times \dots \times (1 - U _ {n - 2})
$$

We then decide the sign of the tradeoff function, $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } R _ { i } / F _ { 1 }$ , based on our theorization. According to our discussion in H2, when firms repeatedly send social media posts, the return on these posts in terms of immediate purchases is generally short-lived and diminishes quickly (Meyers-Levy and Malaviya 1999) while the loss in future sales associated with unfollowing is likely to expand (Goldstein et al. 2014). Tha is,

• $\mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { { i } } }$ is decreasing when n increases;

• $\mathrm { U _ { i } }$ is increasing when n increases; we note that as unfollowing rate cannot be greater than 1 by definition, 1-U will always be positive

These will lead to two interesting findings:

(1) $\mathbf { A s } \mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is decreasing when n increases, r may eventually become negative when n is big enough. For example, r can be negative when the impact of posts on immediate sales resulted from the advertisement value of post i (the benefit) disappears after the first few contacts bu the loss in future sales due to the post i still exists.

(2) The curve of the tradeoff function, $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } R _ { i } / F _ { 1 }$ , could have two scenarios as Figure C1:

• Scenario 1: $\mathbf { r } _ { 1 } \leq 0$ , the net impact per existing customer is not positive since the first post. Given that r is decreasing when n increases, the value of $\mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { { i } } }$ and $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } R _ { i }$ would always be negative, suggesting that firms’ posting on social media hurt their long-term sales.

• Scenario 2: $\mathbf { r } _ { 1 } > 0 _ { : }$ , the net impact per existing customer is positive of the first post. Given that r is decreasing when n increases and r could eventually become negative when n is big enough, the value of $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } R _ { i }$ would have an inverted u-shaped relation to n, suggesting that when firms’ number of posting on social media passes the turning point (where the marginal sales increase of one post cannot cover the marginal sales loss), the net impact of posting on sales would decrease and eventually become negative. As discussed earlier, the value of the turning point would depend on many factors such as the content and the quality of the posts and the timing of the posts. Specifically, better designed posts might lead to a higher value of the turning point.

The above discussion suggests that theoretically there should exist a turning point and the net impact would eventually become negative when n continues to increase. Then the empirical question is, whether we can observe the turning point in the real business context and how long it will take for the net impact to become negative.

Interestingly, in our empirical study, we find that the posts sent by our focal retailer, which does not post in a way out of (if not better than) the ordinary on social networks (e.g., posting at a relatively lower frequency and designing post comparable to the top-10 most popular fan pages on Facebook) has already results in a negative net impact in a 5-month period. This result suggests that firms’ posting on social media may hurt their long-term sales.

Based on the above discussion of the two scenarios $( { \bf r } _ { 1 } \le 0$ and $\mathbf { r } _ { 1 } > 0 )$ , we can say that in general the current practice of firms’ posting on social media will hurt firms by reducing their long-term sales.
