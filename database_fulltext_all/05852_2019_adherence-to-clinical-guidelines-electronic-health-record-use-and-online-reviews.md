# Adherence to Clinical Guidelines, Electronic Health Record Use, and Online Reviews

DANISH H. SAIFEE, INDRANIL R. BARDHAN, ATANU LAHIRI, AND ZHIQIANG (ERIC) ZHENG

ABSTRACT: To increase transparency of healthcare quality, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) initiated the Physician Quality Reporting System (PQRS). However, the impact of PQRS on physicians is unclear, particularly as related to their online reputation. Is there an association between a physician’s online reputation and her adherence to clinical guidelines stipulated in the PQRS? Is online reputation associated with use of electronic health records (EHR)? To investigate these questions, we combine data on online physician reviews with the PQRS data on clinical guideline adherence and EHR use. Unlike prior research, which primarily uses clinical outcomes as proxies for care quality, our study uses adherence to clinical guidelines, a process measure that reflects physician conformance with evidence-based clinical practices. In addition, we focus on EHR use at the physician level, in contrast to the usual approach of examining it at the aggregate institutional level. Consistent with the economic theory of credence goods, we observe no significant relationship between physicians’ adherence to clinical guidelines and their online reviews. Although there is some evidence of association between EHR use and their overall rating, similar relationships are not consistently observed for individual dimensional ratings. Overall, the online reputation of a physician exhibits minimal association with her actual clinical activities and is mostly driven by latent topics in the textual reviews — implying that the ability of online reviews to inform prospective patients of care quality might be quite limited. Therefore, patients should be cautious when using online physician reviews, and policymakers should increase the accessibility of PQRS and other similar data to help patients make informed physician choices.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: clinical guidelines, physician quality reporting, online physician reviews, electronic health record, credence goods, online eWoM.

## Introduction

There is no clear consensus among researchers on the efficacy of online reviews in the context of healthcare. On one hand, Gray et al. [18] showed that there is no significant association between physician website ratings and clinical outcomes such as blood pressure or low-density lipoprotein controlled. This lack of association is also echoed in the work by Saifee et al. [36], which argues that physician reviews do not necessarily reflect the actual clinical outcomes for chronic conditions. On the other hand, Lu and Rui [28] examined the validity of online star ratings of cardiac surgeons in the context of coronary artery bypass graft surgeries, and found that patients operated by cardiac surgeons with a lower rating do exhibit a higher likelihood of mortality, vis-à-vis those with a higher rating or no ratings. Likewise, Bardach et al. [1] documented a positive association between patient ratings on Yelp and clinical outcomes such as the readmission and mortality rate.

Notwithstanding questions over their reliability, online reviews of physicians continue to rise in popularity among healthcare consumers. According to a recent study [20], approximately 60 percent of patients now consider online reviews to be an important factor in their selection of physicians. Moreover, in a recent survey [4], 28 percent strongly agreed that a positive online review of a physician would cause them to seek care from that physician, while another 27 percent indicated that a negative review would cause them to avoid her. Interestingly, many physicians have also begun to monitor these online reviews closely, looking for ways to boost their ratings on review sites, such as Yelp, Vitals, and RateMDs. There have even been instances in which physicians have filed defamation lawsuits over negative patient reviews [36].

In the view of this backdrop, we take a closer look at online reviews, and scrutinize once again what they actually capture. Do they faithfully reflect the quality of the care delivery process? This is an important question, because prior studies on physician reviews mostly focused on the association between reviews received by a physician and clinical outcomes (e.g., mortality rate, readmission rate) experienced by her patients [28, 36]. Although the ultimate objective is to improve clinical outcomes, using outcome-based variables as proxies for quality is fraught with obvious risks. For example, the outcome is often influenced by extraneous factors such as a patient’s financial condition, prior medical history, social support, and idiosyncratic characteristics that are unique to each patient. All these factors significantly complicate the task of identification in an empirical setting. Therefore, our effort to examine care quality from the process side is not only novel, but also somewhat advantageous from an empirical perspective.

To answer our broader research question, we investigate the following: (1) Do physicians who adhere more closely to evidence-based clinical guidelines stipulated by the Physician Quality Reporting System (PQRS) receive better online reviews? (2) Do physicians who demonstrate use of certified EHRs as required by the EHR incentive program receive better online reviews? (3) Does use of a certified EHR by a physician moderate the relationship between her adherence to clinical guidelines and online reviews? and (4) Finally, if the aforementioned clinical activities and practices of a physician do not explain her online reputation, what might possibly explain it?

The first question is about the relationship between the quality of care delivery and online reputation, while the second question is about the association between use of information technologies (IT) and online reputation. The third question is to allow for the possibility that guideline adherence and technology use could act as substitutes for, or complements of, each other, suppressing or amplifying the effect of the other. These questions are not only important from a practical perspective, but also from a theoretical one. Prior research on patient-perceived dimensions of quality in healthcare has identified infrastructure, process of clinical care, personnel quality, and safety indicators as key determinants of patients’ overall experience [12]. Infrastructure includes facilities, equipment, as well as IT, which can contribute to a patient’s well-being. Clearly, EHR falls under the purview of IT infrastructure. Process of clinical care, another patient-perceived dimension, refers to the experience of the patient with clinical processes, whereas safety indicators refer to his perception with respect to precautionary measures taken. Since the PQRS offers an extensive set of guidelines, covering issues such as preventive screening, care plan, pain assessment, follow-up plan, and documentation of high-risk medication, adherence to the PQRS guidelines ought to be tied to both the process of clinical care and safety indicators. Naturally, adherence merits consideration as a potential determinant of patient satisfaction. Finally, personnel quality refers to the skill and experience of the staff; although our research questions do not consider this aspect explicitly, we do control for physician experience in all our models.

With regard to our last research question, it has been argued that healthcare is a credence good [15]. If so, the quality of care delivery could be difficult for patients to assess, leading to physicians receiving inconsistent, and perhaps misleadingly favorable reviews on their performance regardless of the actual quality of care. However, the same might not be the case with more tangible elements of the service, for example, wait times, scheduling issues, attentiveness of the staff, and other similar hedonic aspects [16]. Thus, reviews that overemphasize these hedonic aspects might not be as favorable in general, and physicians who mostly get reviewed on these topics might not fare as well compared to their peers. The purpose of the last research question is to examine to what extent this is actually happening in our context.

We answer our research questions empirically, using a combination of unique datasets. To measure online reputation, we collected textual reviews as well as various numeric/star ratings from a large online patient portal. Online reputation, our main dependent variable, was then measured as either sentiments expressed in the textual reviews or numeric (star) ratings. For clinical guideline adherence and EHR use, we leveraged data from the PQRS.

Our empirical analysis leads to several important findings. First, there is no evidence of a direct relationship between physicians’ adherence to clinical guidelines and their online reviews. Second, although there is some evidence of an association between EHR use and the overall star rating, we found little evidence of similar relationships with individual dimensions of service. Further, there is no evidence of a moderating effect of physician participation in the EHR incentive program on the relationship between guideline adherence and online reviews. The conspicuous lack of a connection between adherence to clinical guidelines and online reviews is consistent with the credence nature of healthcare services. This aspect of healthcare services can make it hard for patients, who may lack deep and broad knowledge about care provided by their physicians, to effectively evaluate the quality of care delivery, thereby making online reviews less useful from the viewpoint of a prospective patient. Interestingly, we observed that topics latent in the textual reviews have a significant relationship with the review valence. Specifically, reviews centered on hedonic and peripheral attributes of the service tend to be more negative, when compared to those focused on physician performance or surgical aspects.

## Physician Quality Reporting System (PQRS)

The PQRS was initiated by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) to streamline numerous quality-reporting and measurement programs at hospitals and group practices across the country. The program collects data on various dimensions of care delivery, including prevention, care coordination, and resource utilization. It uses financial incentives to encourage eligible professionals (physicians) to report data on the quality of healthcare delivery. When the PQRS was originally implemented in 2006, it financially rewarded providers who volunteered to disclose quality information. In 2015, however, it moved from a voluntary program to a mandatory one, imposing penalties on providers for failing to participate. Starting in 2017, the PQRS is no longer a stand-alone program, and has become an integral part of the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS). Nevertheless, the penalty remains in force — a failure to satisfactorily report data on healthcare quality — can lead to negative payment adjustments. For physicians who participate, the CMS collects information on their adherence to clinical guidelines (or “performance rate” as it is commonly called) as relevant to their specialty and the type of patient population served.

Broadly, the PQRS program offers providers an opportunity to assess and compare their performance relative to their peers’ [23]. However, there is very limited literature on these aspects of the PQRS. It is also unclear whether physicians see enough merit in this program. For instance, a survey of physicians by Federman and Keyhani [13] found that a majority of participating physicians are skeptical about the impact of PQRS on the quality of care provided by them to their patients. In other words, the value of PQRS to physicians is still very much an open question, notwithstanding the faith shown by the CMS in the merits of the program. We are among the first to address this issue by empirically examining whether adherence to clinical guidelines stipulated by the PQRS eventually translates into better patient perception and consequently better online reputation for physicians. Furthermore, since prior research on the relationship between care quality and online patient perception often uses outcome-oriented proxies for care quality (e.g., mortality rate or readmission rate), our choice of a process-based proxy (guideline adherence) fills a conspicuous gap in the literature on online physician reviews.

On a related note, to improve the quality, safety, and efficiency of patient care, the CMS has also been providing financial incentives to physicians to adopt and demonstrate meaningful use of certified EHR technology. In fact, there has been a significant push towards rapid adoption and use of EHRs since the enactment of the HITECH Act in 2009. That push has already begun to pay dividends. As of 2016, approximately 50 percent of all office-based physicians in our dataset participated in the PQRS EHR incentive program, a major improvement since 2009. To participate in this program, an eligible professional (physician) has to use certified EHR technology<sup>1</sup>; they are required to submit the clinical quality measures using a direct EHR product or EHR data submission vendor certified in the EHR technology.

Adoption and usage of certified EHRs by physicians has the potential to improve the workflows of their daily work and their interactions with patients. This, in turn, can impact their relationship with patients and thereby affect their reputation. Thus, in light of the increasing role of EHR systems in physicians’ daily routines, taking into account both the direct and moderating roles of EHR technology has become quite important. Intriguingly, there is little prior research on the benefits of EHR use at the physician level. Most of the prior studies focus on clinics and hospitals, possibly because it is difficult to obtain data on EHR use at the individual physician level. Fortunately, however, the PQRS program has improved access to such data, making this study possible. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to empirically address all three issues at the physician-level — adherence to clinical guidelines, EHR use, and online reputation — in a single, unified research framework.

## Related Literature

Our study is positioned at the intersection of: (1) the broader research on consumer reviews; (2) the (more closely-related) stream of online physician reviews; (3) the literature on adoption and use of health IT, particularly EHRs; and, finally, (4) the economics literature on credence goods.

## Consumer Reviews

Over the past two decades, the first stream — the literature on consumer reviews — has grown considerably. There are two main branches of this literature. The first branch examines supply or production of consumer reviews and addresses a wide variety of questions, including word-of-mouth and social influence [2, 11, 34], evolution of ratings [17, 31, 33], reporting biases [15, 25], and review manipulation [32]. Researchers have also examined whether the product type itself has a bearing on the incentive to write reviews [10]. The main focus of this branch is essentially the antecedents of consumer reviews.

The demand or consumption side has also received considerable attention. It has been found that not only potential consumers read and rely on such reviews, but the product ratings in many cases are also commensurate with the actual sales [5, 7, 19]. Chevalier and Mayzlin [6] also made an interesting observation that consumers actually read the review text, rather than relying solely on the star ratings in the reviews. Another important finding is that reviewer characteristics matter in shaping an online community’s judgment of products and reviews [14]. There is also significant literature on the economic relationship between product differentiation and the role of online reviews [8, 26]. Interestingly, Luo and Zhang [30] showed that reviews can have even bigger implications, as buzz generated by them can impact the market value of a firm.

Researchers working on the consumption side have also focused on the issue of perceived and actual usefulness of online reviews [28, 36]. In their study of Amazon’s online reviews, Mudambi and Schuff [35] demonstrated that product type — whether the product is a search good or an experience one — has a bearing on the relationship between review characteristics and perceived helpfulness. A similar observation has also been made, with respect to the role of the product type, by Huang et al. [21]. Both studies caution that review content, as well as how that content is interpreted, depends critically on the product type. In light of these findings, the helpfulness of online physician reviews becomes even more questionable; many healthcare services are best described as credence and, if prior research is any indication, their credence nature itself can have serious implications.

## Online Physician Reviews

Closely related to this study are research studies that examine the association between objective measures of care quality and online reviews [1, 18]. As mentioned in the introduction, there is no consensus among researchers in this stream about the usefulness of online reviews. To elaborate, Lu and Rui [28, p. 2560] argued that, for certain healthcare services such as cardiac surgeries, care quality may be observable: “family members can observe at least whether their beloved survived after the surgery in hospital or not,” and “they can infer how well the surgery was performed and how good the surgeon is, although their influences may not be completely accurate.” They also demonstrated that reviews are somewhat helpful to patients in sorting out physicians—surgeons with negative reviews tend to deliver worse clinical outcomes compared to their peers with positive or no ratings. Notwithstanding this finding, whether online reviews are useful remains an open question. This is because many healthcare services, such as chronic disease care, are fundamentally different from surgeries; physician performance is far less observable in their cases [36].

There is another point of contention in prior research, which is the choice of the outcome variable. For example, some studies use mortality rate as the outcome, but such a choice is meaningless in other contexts where the patient has small probability of dying immediately, such as in the case of chronic diseases. In those contexts, other outcomes, such as patient readmission rates, may be more applicable. Clearly, no variable fits all, and the findings depend critically on what outcome variable is chosen. In fact, this is another advantage of focusing on the process side — such as guideline adherence — instead of outcome variables, particularly when evaluating a diverse set of physicians.

Nevertheless, a lack of consensus thus far indicates that more systematic studies are warranted. We address this need from a new perspective, focusing on the care delivery process as opposed to relying on outcomes that often are hard to measure precisely and objectively, in order to reveal whether the clinical activities of a physician are indeed associated with her online reputation. In more general terms, we contribute to the literature on the supply side of reviews, by examining whether reviews faithfully capture what physicians really do, both in terms of guideline adherence and IT use.

## Use of EHR Systems by Physicians

Even though many studies have been conducted over the past two decades to examine various aspects of adoption and use of health IT systems at the hospital level, there is still a lack of clear understanding on their benefits at the physician level. Broadly, EHRs not only help physicians but also assist staff members in building a stronger professional relationship with patients. For instance, a review paper [22] that surveyed prior studies on the use of EHRs by physicians in an outpatient setting found evidence of a positive or neutral relationship between EHR usage and patient satisfaction in six of the seven articles, and a negative relationship in one study. In our research, we study whether there is any relationship between participation in the EHR incentive program by physicians and their online reviews. We thus extend the existing literature on EHR use by physicians and their relationship with patient feedback to an online review setting, a setting that is continually becoming more relevant to the healthcare sector.

## Credence Goods

The term credence was originally suggested by Darby and Karni [9] to characterize goods and services whose quality information is never understood by consumers. Since healthcare involves a very complex combination of multiple clinical steps—including, but not limited to, accurate and timely diagnosis, educating the patient about relevant medical conditions, carrying out the required medical procedures, following up with patients, and developing a comprehensive care plan-significant credence qualities assessment of the quality of care is often quite challenging from a patient’s perspective.

More recently, it has been suggested that consumer reviews and word-of-mouth could play a significant role in the context of professional services that possess credence qualities [15]. Whether online ratings actually contain information on credence qualities is, however, an open question. Our work complements existing studies in this domain [18, 28] by investigating whether online reviews are associated with physicians’ adherence to clinical guidelines. We also shed light on whether use of certified EHRs by physicians improves their patients experience.

## Research Questions

Figure 1 depicts our research framework, which comprises four research questions. The first question, RQ1, is to understand whether online reviews faithfully reflect the quality of the care delivery process, as measured by physician adherence to clinical guidelines stipulated in the PQRS program. It is, in fact, reasonable to believe that a higher level of adherence by a physician would have a favorable influence on online reviews received by her. This is because the PQRS guidelines cover items such as care plan, preventative screening (tobacco/alcohol use, body mass index check, cancer screening, etc.), appropriate vaccinations (e.g., pneumonia prevention for older adults), pain assessment and follow-up, documentation of current medications in the medical record, documentation of high-risk medications, and so on. These items have the potential to improve a patient’s experience with respect to the process of care, as well as his perception with respect to the precautionary measures taken; both of these improvements are critical to achieving a higher level of patient satisfaction [12]. At the same time, however, because of the credence nature of healthcare, it may be quite difficult for many patients to clearly assess and evaluate the activities of their physicians objectively with respect to any metric, let alone adherence to clinical guidelines. To resolve this quandary empirically, we ask:

Research Question 1: Do physicians who adhere more closely to clinical guidelines receive better online reviews?

The second question, RQ2, aims to examine whether online reviews reflect some of the benefits we expect from participation in the EHR incentive program. From a theoretical angle, EHR systems are an integral part of the overall infrastructure, which is a critical determinant of patient satisfaction in a healthcare setting [12]. From a practical perspective, a certified EHR helps physicians in many ways. For example, it can provide in-depth information about the medical history of patients under treatment. When a patient goes back for a routine check-up, it can provide the physician and her staff ready access to relevant medical history about that patient with regard to treatments, symptoms, medications, tests, hospitalizations, and even her family medical history. This, in turn, can save substantial time for clinicians, who may otherwise have to acquire the same information through retrospective conversations with the patient or by reviewing previous documentation [24]. Overall, by reducing redundant conversations and paperwork, an EHR system can streamline, and thereby improve, interactions between clinicians and their patients. On the flip side though, there is a concern that EHR use may actually increase a physician’s workload. For example, she may need a substantial amount of extra time to complete documentation required by the system, which can adversely impact the clinical time she can spend with her patients and may even strain her relationship with them. Again, we seek to resolve this issue empirically:

## Research Question 2: Do physicians who participate in the PQRS EHR incentive program receive better online reviews?

Use of certified EHR systems can not only help physicians follow clinical guidelines more closely, but can also make the care delivery process more transparent. For example, these systems can remind a physician and her staff to follow up with patients regularly, provide reminders about recommended tests, and help clinicians adhere to care protocols and monitor treatment. At the same time, they can also make details of the care plan, as well as information on various follow-ups and overall progress, readily available to the patients as well as the PQRS. One would expect this to have an impact on patient satisfaction since “the outcome of the physician-patient encounter depends on communication” [12, p. 565]. Since participation in the EHR incentive program by physicians can positively influence the relationship between adherence to clinical guidelines and their online reviews, we ask the following:

## Research Question 3: Does the use of EHR systems by a physician positively moderate the relationship between her adherence to clinical guidelines and patient-generated online reviews?

Although our primary focus is on the three questions described above, we also explore the role of hedonic attributes. This is because healthcare is considered to be a credence good, and it is quite likely that clinical practices might not be reflected in the online reviews of physicians. However, a patient’s satisfaction (or a lack thereof) with respect to certain hedonic attributes might still be able to explain the observed variation in review valence across physicians. Put another way, if reviews that primarily discuss clinical aspects of care are uninformative and overly positive across physicians (regardless of their actual performance) and reviews that emphasize non-clinical aspects of the service are more informative and critical, a physician’s online reputation could be negatively correlated with the share of the latter type of reviews (as a percentage of the total number of reviews received). To investigate whether this is indeed the case, we employ additional explanatory variables constructed from the textual content of the reviews and ask the following:

Research Question 4: Do the underlying themes of textual reviews explain the variation in the online review sentiment scores and ratings across physicians?

## Data and Variables

We obtained research data from three sources: (1) Vitals.com, a public physician review platform; (2) Individual Eligible Provider Public Reporting and Physician Compare National Downloadable File (2015); and (3) Provider Utilization and Payment Data (Physician and Other Supplier Public Use File). The first dataset provides data on online reviews of physicians, including textual comments and star ratings posted by patients. It contains physicians from the Dallas-Fort Worth (DFW) area, who had at least one review prior to 2016 and at least one later. We used this dataset to construct our dependent variables. A key aspect of our analysis is that we integrate this dataset with the other two obtained from the CMS. After matching physicians by their national provider identifiers across the CMS datasets and then by their first and last names in the review dataset, we ended up with approximately 1,100 physicians.

The DFW metropolitan area in North Texas, which we focus on in this study, is fairly representative of the entire U.S. The median age in DFW is 34.8 years (vs. 37.9 for the United States), the median household income is \$63,812 (vs. \$57,617 for the United States), the poverty rate is 12.7 percent (vs. 14 percent for the United States), and the median property value is \$189,100 (vs. \$205,000 for the United States).<sup>2</sup> Regarding racial diversity, 75.7 percent (vs. 78.9 percent for the United States.) are Caucasian or Hispanic, and 15.2 percent (vs. 12.3 percent for the United States) are African. Focusing on a single geographic region allows us to reduce the potential impact of disparities across regions. In particular, rural markets can be quite different from urban centers, as patients in rural areas may have fewer healthcare providers to evaluate and are, therefore, less likely to need the help of online reviews to select physicians.

The second dataset was obtained from the CMS. Both of our key explanatory variables, EHR\_Use and ClinicalAdherenceRate, were constructed from this dataset. The third dataset was created by the CMS to share information on services and procedures provided to Medicare beneficiaries by various physicians and other healthcare professionals. It contains information on utilization, payment (allowed amount and Medicare payment), and submitted charges, organized by National Provider Identifier (NPI), Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) code, and the location of service. These details are garnered from the CMS administrative claims data for Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in the program. This dataset also includes provider demographics (name, credentials, gender, complete address, and entity type) from the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System (NPPES).

## Dependent Variables

The main dependent variables are SentimentScore and OverallRating. These were constructed from the reviews, where a patient assigns a star rating — on a 5-point scale — to a physician, in addition to writing a textual review. Figure 2 shows a snapshot of a typical review.

![](/api/attachments/V8NKPU8S/fulltext/images/e3cf2bfc4165e8d456982904d96cb0c201bf6253c54d4b645babe78f9b283591.jpg)  
Figure 2. A Sample Review

As observed from this figure, the patient not only assigns an overall star rating but can also rate several dimensions of service. The overall rating appears in the top part of the figure, while the rest appears below the textual review. Our variables, including SentimentScore and OverallRating, were obtained by averaging the corresponding individual review variables across all reviews received over the period January 2016 to October 2018. For a physician, OverallRating is thus the average of all overall star ratings received by her during the aforementioned three-year window. Similarly, SentimentScore is the average of the sentiment scores of all her textual reviews received over the same period.

The sentiment score of a review is essentially a measure of how positive or negative the review text is in terms of sentiments expressed in it. Here is a brief explanation of how we calculated the sentiment score for a given textual review. Loosely speaking, a textual comment can be thought of as a collection of positive and negative words. It can be made of just one or two words (e.g., “amazing doc”) or can be quite detailed as shown in Figure 2. Nevertheless, to correctly identify the sentiment expressed in a word, we must consider words in the vicinity of the sentiment word (that is, N-Gram) [27]. For example, the word “good” is a positive-sentiment word, but when used in “not good,” it becomes a negative-sentiment word. To account for the effect of words in the vicinity, we used the Sentiment function in a new sentiment-mining package in R called SentimentR,<sup>4</sup> which incorporates latest advancements in sentiment analysis. This sentiment-mining tool considers various types of valence shifters, such as negators, amplifiers, and de-amplifiers. By default, it treats five words before and two words after each polarized word as possible valence shifters.

To examine our research questions in further detail, we leveraged the star ratings available for the seven different dimensions displayed in Figure 2. For each physician, we averaged these ratings received during the three-year time window. These seven dependent variables are: DiagnosisRating (accurate diagnosis by physician), FollowupRating (appropriate/timely follow-ups by physician), SpendsTimeRating (time spent by the physician with a patient), BedsideMannersRating (bedside manners of the physician), StaffCourtesyRating (friendliness of the staff), PromptnessRating (staff promptness), and AppointmentEaseRating (ease of scheduling appointments for a patient).

## Key Explanatory Variables

These were constructed from the PQRS data for the year 2015. EHR\_Use takes a value of 1 for physicians who successfully participated in the PQRS EHR Incentive Program, and 0 for the rest. ClinicalAdherenceRate for a physician is the average of her performance scores (out of 100) across guideline measures stipulated by the PQRS. These performance scores are based on the information reported by the physician to the PQRS. Here is an account of how we used these rates. Suppose that an internist (physician) reported on the following three clinical measures stipulated by PQRS: (1) documentation of current medications in the medical record, (2) preventive care and screening for unhealthy alcohol use, and (3) use of high-risk medications in the elderly.

The first two measures in this example are non-inverse measures, implying that a higher score indicates a higher adherence. However, the third one is an inverse measure, for which a lower score implies a higher adherence. Now, suppose that the internist achieved scores of 70 and 85 on the first two measures out of a maximum of 100, and 20 in the third one. To calculate her adherence, we had to first invert 20. We did so by subtracting 20 from 100, and obtained 80. The physician’s adherence rate was then calculated as the average of the three scores as (70 + 85 + 80)/3 = 78.33.

## Control Variables

We employed several clinical controls to capture the clinical characteristics of a physician. These variables are necessary to control for physician attributes including heterogeneity across patient populations treated by the physicians. For each physician, ClinicalAdherenceMeasureCount is the number of clinical guideline measures that the physician reported to the CMS. NumPatientsClinicAdherenceMeasure is the number of patients who are eligible to be a part of one of the guideline adherence measures reported by her. GraduationYear indicates the year of her graduation from medical school. GenderFemale equals 0 if the physician is male, and 1 otherwise. NumHCPCS is the total number of unique HCPCS (Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System) codes. NumServices denotes the number of services provided by her. NumMedicareBeneficiaries is the number of Medicare beneficiaries receiving her services. BeneficiaryAvgAge is the average age of the beneficiaries, as determined at the end of the calendar year or at the time of death. BeneficiaryAvgRiskScore is the average hierarchical condition category (HCC) risk score of the beneficiaries. NumberofRatings represents the number of overall ratings received by each physician during our study period. Summary statistics for all these variables are provided in Table 1.

As evident from Table 1, the star ratings received by physicians tend to lean towards the higher end. Controls<sub>i</sub> represents the clinical controls for each physician, and $\varepsilon _ { i }$ is the idiosyncratic error.

## Review Topics

We now address our last research question, RQ4. From the analysis thus far, it is apparent that clinical variables such as adherence or EHR use provide minimal insights into what drives online reputation of physicians. The question then is: what actually drives it? To answer this question, we delved into the content of textual reviews in order to understand the themes underlying them.

The content in each textual review can be summarized by one or multiple latent topics/themes, which can be identified using a topic modeling analysis such as the latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) [3, 37]. We identified four latent topics as shown in Table 4.<sup>6</sup> We selected four topics because it is the minimum number that provides a clear categorization of the clinical experiences reported in the textual reviews. In Table 4, fifteen terms most closely associated — in a probabilistic sense — with each topic are also listed. For example, a textual review with stems (words) such as “offic” (office, offices, etc.), “wait” (wait, waiting, waits), or “appoint” (appointment, appointments, etc.) tell us that the underlying topic is primarily the physician’s staff, whereas one with “doctor,” “care,” “best,” “person,” or “physician” is predominantly about the physician.

Based on these topics, we constructed additional control variables as follows. For a given physician, TopicSurgery represents the proportion of her reviews for which the most prominent latent theme is the surgical/procedural competence. TopicStaff is the proportion of reviews where the most dominant theme is promptness of the staff in the physician’s office (wait times, appointment-scheduling experiences, etc.). TopicPhysician is the proportion of reviews where the most prominent theme is the physician herself (which includes time spent by her, as well as the treatment offered and her listening skills). The baseline variable against which these three variables are interpreted is TopicOverallCare, which represents the proportion where the most prominent underlying theme is the overall care provided by her and her staff.

Why are we interested in these topics, and why do we want to include them in our regression specifications? The reason is apparent from Figure 3, which depicts the distribution of positive and negative sentiments across the aforementioned topic categories. To further clarify, we categorized a textual review as positive if its sentiment score is greater than or equal to 0, and negative otherwise. The key takeaway from Figure 3 is that the reviews concerning the topics surgery and physician tend to be far more positive compared to the rest. In contrast, a majority of textual reviews concerning the physician’s office staff happens to be negative. This shows that the review topics are potentially important to explaining the variation in our dependent variables. Hence, we added the topic variables as controls and re-ran our 2SLS estimations; since the four topic variables add up to one, we needed only three of them.

In sum, Tables 5 and 6 further reinforce our point about the credence nature of healthcare services. Apparently, the quality of the care delivery process is not being accurately captured by online reviews: what is captured instead are patients’ perceptions about the hedonic aspects of the service. The more they write about the staff, the more negative their feedback tends to be. ## Robustness Checks

We now present a series of robustness checks. For each check, we provide the motivation as well as a brief discussion of our findings.

## Fixed Effects

To account for unobserved, time-invariant heterogeneities across physicians as well as time fixed effects, we use a two-way fixed effects estimation model. Note that physician fixed effects also account for unobserved hospital-level time-invariant factors. To set up our panel, we collected an additional year of the PQRS data. As a result, there are two years in our panel, 2015 and 2016. For each period, the dependent variables were constructed based on approximately the next two years of review data, to keep the panel balanced. More specifically, for 2015, the dependent variables were constructed based on reviews received from January 2016 to October 2017. For 2016, they were constructed based on reviews received from January 2017 to October 2018. There are a total of 178 physicians in the final balanced panel, since not every physician had relevant data for both years. The results from this panel estimation are shown in Table 7.

As evident from Table 7, these results are very similar to what we obtained from 2SLS. Aside from a positive association between EHR\_Use and OverallRating, there is no evidence of a significant relationship between a physician’s activities and her online reviews. The relationship between a physician’s clinical adherence and her online reputation continues to be insignificant. Finally, similar to the previous section, the review topics continue to play a dominant role.

## Review Manipulation

Review manipulation is often a major concern in studies of online reviews, which can potentially confound the analysis. Online physician reviews are no exception. It can be argued that lower quality physicians (for example, those who do not adhere to the PQRS guidelines) may be more likely to manipulate online reviews (by fabricating positive reviews to inflate online ratings). To investigate whether review manipulation is indeed an issue, we employed a subsample analysis based on additional data collected from Yelp. To clarify our motivation, Yelp uses a proprietary algorithm to identify potentially manipulated reviews. Reviews found to be potentially manipulated are labeled either “not recommended” or “removed” [29]. On the flip side, reviews that are deemed authentic (not manipulated) are termed “recommended.” We used the first and last names of the physicians to match Yelp data with data collected from Vitals.com. There were 310 matches between the two datasets. Of these 310 physicians, 240 did not have “not recommended” or “removed” reviews. The reviews for these 240 physicians can thus be considered free of manipulation.

Of the 240 physicians mentioned above, 119 had no missing values for any of our explanatory variables and the two key dependent variables. Accordingly, we ran a subsample analysis based on these 119 physicians, once again using the 2SLS estimation with topic controls. The results from this new analysis are reported in Table 8. As evident from this table, our findings with respect to the lack of explanatory power of the main clinical variables — clinical guideline adherence and participation in the EHR incentive program — are quite robust and not influenced by review manipulation. There is still no evidence to suggest that a higher level of adherence or participation in the EHR incentive program by a physician can translate into higher review scores.

## Review Sparsity

Review sparsity is another issue that we need to reckon with. Some physicians received very few reviews, and their online ratings may not be reliable as a result. To ensure that such sparsity is not biasing our findings, we ran another subsample analysis. This analysis excludes physicians who received fewer than five overall ratings. The results of the subsample analyses presented in Table 9 indicate that our findings are qualitatively robust. There is still no evidence that a greater level of adherence or EHR use is associated with a better online reputation. Furthermore, the topic variables have very similar coefficients, reinforcing our earlier findings.

## Discussion

In this work, we explored the antecedents of online physician reviews with three focal questions of interest. First, we examined whether reviews truly capture important facets of care delivery. We did so by examining the association between online reviews and adherence to clinical guidelines stipulated by the PQRS. Second, we studied the relationship between physician participation in the EHR incentive program and online reviews, including possible moderating effects on the relationship between adherence and online reviews. Finally, we investigated whether review scores highlight information other than care quality, specifically hedonic attributes such as appointment-scheduling ease, promptness, and courteousness of the physician and her staff. Our unified research framework makes a unique contribution to the current literature on health IT systems, physician performance, healthcare quality, and patient perception in online social media.

One of our key findings is that there is no clear relationship between a physician’s adherence to the PQRS guidelines and online reviews received by her. Likewise, there is no consistent relationship between a physician’s participation in the EHR incentive program and her online reviews. Although there is some evidence of association between EHR\_Use and OverallRating, the relationships with individual dimensional ratings and sentiment scores are predominantly insignificant. Finally, there is no evidence of a moderating effect of EHR use on the relationship between guideline adherence and online physician reviews. These findings echo the credence nature of healthcare services—it can indeed be quite difficult for an average healthcare consumer to objectively evaluate the quality of various clinical processes and services. Viewed another way, both stricter adherence to clinical guidelines as well as participation in the EHR incentive program are important contributors to the quality of care delivered by physicians, but it seems unlikely that patients are able to assess the value of the actions of physicians with respect to guideline adherence or EHR use, let alone evaluate the benefits accrued therefrom. This finding is in stark contrast to the general belief — prevalent in the context of services and products other than healthcare, such as books, movies, or hotels — that online reviews do contain meaningful signals of product/service quality. Apparently, healthcare is different, and so is perhaps the usefulness of online reviews in the context of healthcare.

When we introduced topics embedded in textual reviews as explanatory variables, however, we found that review topics do explain a sizeable portion of the variance in review scores. Specifically, reviews that emphasize the physician’s staff tend to be more negative compared to the ones concerning the physician herself as well as those concerning the surgical/procedural aspects. Thus, a physician whose textual reviews are relatively more focused on the hedonic aspects of the service — the performance of the staff with respect to scheduling, wait times, or friendliness — tends to receive a lower overall rating. This lesson is salient. While patients may be unable to accurately evaluate a physician’s clinical competence or the quality of care delivered, they are still able to use the online forum to voice their dissatisfaction with respect to various hedonic attributes. As a result, reviews emphasizing such attributes can easily end up becoming the overriding factor, separating one physician from another with respect to online reputation.

Nevertheless, given the mixed results in prior research and contextual differences, we cannot claim our findings to be the ultimate verdict. Additionally, there are other websites (such as RateMDs.com or HealthGrades.com) that collect and display very similar reviews, with some visible discrepancies between them. For example, promptness on one website could be referred to as punctuality on another. Furthermore, some rating dimensions are available only on one website but not on the others. Since the information that is collected and displayed has an effect on the efficacy of online physician reviews, the sensitiveness of our results with respect to the information collected and processed cannot be ignored. Put another way, validation studies using data sourced from websites other than the one used here, or on geographic regions outside of North Texas, would be of considerable interest.

## Conclusion

A phenomenon intimately related to rapid adoption of social media is the rising popularity of online platforms which host detailed reviews of physicians available to healthcare consumers. However, what these platforms actually offer to consumers has remained a mystery, with conflicting conclusions drawn by prior researchers. To address this gap, we bring in a fresh perspective — that of process-based metrics, such as adherence to evidence-based guidelines — to a growing research stream that has previously relied on outcome-based measures as proxies for care quality [28, 36]. Our finding that review scores are not associated with the processbased variables lends support to the claim in prior research that healthcare exhibits credence characteristics [15], which can make it hard for patients to assess the actual quality of care. Furthermore, our finding with respect to reviews being overly reflective of the hedonic side of the service corroborates findings from surveybased research [16].

The key implication of our findings is that online reviews of physicians are not necessarily reliable or informative of care quality. So, we must not overly rely on them to ensure a smooth transition to patient-centered care and efficient healthcare marketplaces. Rather, policymakers must be proactive in sharing any information on provider quality that they possess with the general public. In fact, the PQRS data is already public. However, it seems that patients are either not aware of it or they do not find it easily accessible and understandable. Perhaps, policymakers can also make better data visualization tools available, since raw data might not appeal to consumers who are used to intuitive star ratings. If our research is any indication, additional care quality data, along with proper visualization tools, have the potential to supplement what healthcare consumers can glean from existing online reviews. On one hand, online reviews can educate consumers about the hedonic aspects of the service, and on the other, objective measurements of care quality can shed light on the utilitarian side. Bridging the information gap between providers and patients would, in fact, be critical to ensuring healthcare transparency as well as preventing patients from choosing (or avoiding) physicians based on incomplete information. Doing so might also bring comfort to physicians who seem to have grown wary over the proliferation of online review sites.

Opportunities to extend this line of research abound. For example, even though participation in the PQRS program is now mandatory and carries a penalty for nonparticipation, a significant number of providers still do not participate in the PQRS program. It would be useful to account for possible self-selection bias that may result from such a behavior on the part of physicians. Likewise, as more recent years of data become available, it will be possible to expand the panel model as well as the scope of our study to include causal inferences with respect to the pathways and relationships of interest.
