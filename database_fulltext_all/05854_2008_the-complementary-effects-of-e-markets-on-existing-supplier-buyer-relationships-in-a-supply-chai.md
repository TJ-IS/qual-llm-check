# The Complementary Effects of E‑Markets on Existing Supplier–Buyer Relationships in a Supply Chain

Mu Xia and Nan Xia

Mu Xia is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems in the College of Business at the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Texas at Austin. He is interested in e-business standards, online communities, and the formation and evolution of electronic markets. His papers have been published in journals such as the Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, and Electronic Markets.

Nan Xia is a Visiting Assistant Professor at the Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee. She received her Ph.D. from the Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California. Her research interests are business-tobusiness e-commerce, the integration of e-business in the supply chain, and product variety management. Her work has been published in Transportation Research, Part B: Methodological and accepted for publication in Marketing Science.

Abstract: E-markets have been established in many industries as a sourcing option for buyers. The existing literature focuses on the substitutional effect of e-markets on the traditional supply chain, yet in many situations, e-markets are used by buyers as a benchmarking tool in negotiations with traditional suppliers. This paper examines the role of e-markets in price negotiations and relationship-specific investments. We find that e-markets can be an effective tool to stimulate the traditional supplier’s relationship-specific investments, lower the procurement prices, and improve the buyer’s profitability and the supply-chain efficiency. Therefore, e-markets can complement rather than substitute for the traditional relationship-based supply chain. When there is quality uncertainty in the e-market offering, two effects of quality uncertainty on e-market adoption are identified. Better quality on average will increase e-market adoption, but surprisingly, increasing quality dispersion of e-markets will also help. Therefore, e-markets should strive to enlist suppliers with better quality products, but do not need to worry too much about the quality dispersion. Having better price transparency will also help in attracting more business from buyers.

Key words an d phrases: electronic market, negotiation, outside options, supply chain.

The late 1990s an d early 2000s have seen the rise and fall of many e-marketplaces. In the late 1990s, e-markets were established in many industries. Despite being touted for their ability to benefit firms through dynamic pricing as well as lower transaction costs, many e-markets failed dramatically, never reaching their expected potential [5, p. 97]. The lack of activities was often blamed for e-markets’ drastic failure. Many suppliers, especially those with long-term relationships with buyers, were reluctant to participate in e-markets out of the fear that their products would be commoditized and they would be forced to compete on price. On the buyer’s side, e-markets introduce new suppliers, which for buyers created a greater risk of acquiring defective or inferior goods [3]. Moreover, the decision of buying into a business-to-business (B2B) e-market is often made by people in charge of procurement, who may feel their own role of negotiating deals with and managing relationships with suppliers being threatened by the e-market [9].

Despite the decline, hundreds of e-marketplaces still survived [20]. For many buyers, e-markets remain as a sourcing option or a benchmarking tool [9]. For example, Dow Chemical participates in Omnexus, a plastics industry e-marketplace, and Elemica, a B2B exchange for chemical transactions to source their inputs, while running its own private extranet with long-term suppliers [10]. IBM and Hewlett-Packard are reported to use prices from spot markets such as Converge, a global exchange for electronic components, as a benchmarking tool during their negotiation with dynamic random access memory (DRAM) suppliers [14]. Our own interview of executives of a major commodity e-market also confirms that buyers indeed often use the market as a benchmark to negotiate with their existing partners. In this case, buyers can get the best of both worlds—the stability of a long-term relationship as well as the price advantage from the e-market.

Yet the e-market’s role as a complement to buyers’ existing relationships has not been studied in-depth. The existing studies on e-markets in the information systems and electronic commerce literature mainly have dealt with new issues on e-markets, such as network structures [26], offering selective subsidy to suppliers [23], the adoption of open platform procurement systems versus proprietary ones [17], information privacy [33], the application of options and futures [29], and the sustainability and growth prospect of e-markets [12]. Among the articles, a notable common theme is the impact of network effects on both sides of the market. However, only a few articles examine e-market’s role when it coexists with existing relationships. Lee and Whang [21], motivated by Hewlett-Packard’s exchange as a secondary market for resellers to trade their products online, study the dynamics between a manufacturer and its many resellers under the existence of such a secondary market. Kleindorfer and Wu [18] investigate the integration of spot markets and contract relationships in capital-intensive industries. However, the question that many companies have with the existence of e-markets, “How does it change my relationship with existing suppliers/ buyers?” remains unanswered.

In this paper, we depart from the traditional view of the “either-or” choice between online and offline sourcing, or the view of the two as substitutes, and concentrate on explaining the complementary effects e-markets have on an existing offline buyer–supplier relationship. Specifically, we aim to study the dynamics between a supplier and a manufacturer in a supply-chain context when the manufacturer can use e-markets as an outside option to negotiate with the existing supplier (which is also called “long-term supplier” in the paper). Examples of such e-markets include Converge and Alibaba, a global B2B e-market where participating suppliers list their negotiable prices on the Web site.

We model a noncooperative game of interaction between a supplier and a buyer (manufacturer) where the buyer has an e-market as a sourcing option and sells to the consumer market. The long-term supplier can make a relationship-specific investment to reduce the production cost. Compared to the market equilibrium without the e-market, we find that the e-market option for the buyer can stimulate the traditional supplier’s investment, reduce the procurement prices, and increase the sales volume, which improves the buyer’s profitability and even supply-chain efficiency. We further analyze the market equilibrium when there is quality uncertainty in the e-market offering. Two effects of quality uncertainty on e-market adoption are identified. For e-market providers, our results suggest that improving on the overall e-market product quality would facilitate the adoption of e-markets. Interestingly, we find that increasing the quality dispersion may also help.

Placed in a supply-chain context with an end-consumer market, our research also complements the understanding of the relationship between supply-chain management and e-markets. The two seem to have opposite focus in the traditional view: supplychain management emphasizes coordination, relationship, and long-term strategic cooperation between vertical partners in the supply chain, whereas e-markets encourage horizontal competition and support transaction-based cooperation. Our result demonstrates that the e-market option may help improve supply-chain coordination, showing the complementary role of e-markets in supply-chain management.

## Literature on Electronic Markets

Electron ic mark etplaces have received ex ten sive atten tion from researchers recently (e.g., [1, 6, 11, 19, 24, 32]). However, most of the literature deals with e-markets as a separate way to buy or sell without considering the traditional, non-e-market trading relationships. Therefore, they do not explain if and how e-markets can sustain in the existence of the “old way” of trading relationship–based contracts. When an e-market exists for the buyer who is in a one-to-one relationship with a long-term supplier, how does it change the negotiation result between the two partners? Will it lead to better supply-chain coordination? How will the classic underinvestment result [15, 28]) change? These are important questions that need to be answered for e-markets to assume any role in e-business.

Related to the questions above is the research on the integration of spot markets and long-term contracts. Wu et al. [30] study contracts between a single supplier and one or multiple buyers in a capital-intensive industry where capacity is costly and has to be reserved well in advance of production. Subsequent articles [18, 29] have a similar setup. Lee and Whang’s [21] model studies the two-period trading between the manufacturer and the resellers. Their research is motivated by an electronic market in the hi-tech industry that featured short product life cycle and high demand uncertainty. The resellers order from the manufacturer in the first period and then trade inventories among themselves in a secondary market. They observe two effects in the secondary market, a quantity effect on the sales of the manufacturer that is indeterminate, and an allocation effect that always improves supply-chain performance. Moreover, the resellers are always better off given the secondary market.

In a more recent study, Tunca and Zenios [27] examine the competition between the e-market procurement auctions and long-term relational contracts. The manufacturers use a reverse auction for the procurement of low-quality parts and a relational contract for procuring high-quality parts. They identify conditions under which the two procurement channels coexist or one dominates the other. They find that high-quality premium will discourage the existence of the e-market.

Grey et al. [14] survey the e-market literature to answer the question “How can e-markets add value to supply chains in which long-term buyer–supplier relationships are important?” Their extensive survey identifies three benefits of e-markets—better resource allocation, better information aggregation and dissemination, and better risk management. Better resource allocation refers to better matching of supply and demand. While theoretically elegant, the market approach is shadowed by long-term supplier–buyer relationship in the real world, thanks to fragmentation, imperfect competition, externalities, or transaction costs in many industries. Therefore, the authors point out, e-markets can serve a complementary role in industries—the majority of transactions are still conducted between suppliers and buyers in the traditional way while the e-market serves as a channel for suppliers to offload excess inventory and for buyers to address periodic shortages. They also suggest that e-market prices can serve as a benchmark for contract price negotiations as well as for the supplier to evaluate investments in production capacity. Yet, as a survey paper, it does not offer any analytical results.

In this paper, along the same dimension, we examine the role of e-markets in price negotiations and relationship-specific investments. Recognizing the importance of the long-term supplier–buyer relationship, we argue that e-markets can be used not only as a channel to address supply or demand fluctuations but also an option for the buyer to utilize in negotiating with the supplier, therefore changing the supplier’s relationship-specific investments beforehand.

A buyer’s effort in acquiring pricing information in the e-market depends on the characteristics of the e-market. Because suppliers are likely to avoid participating in e-markets with high price transparency [2, 33], successful e-markets often restrict price transparency to attract and retain suppliers [25]. It is also reported that considerable price dispersion has been observed in B2B e-markets [13]. Therefore, buyers’ cost of searching for the desired product is nontrivial even in an e-market.

Our work differs from Wu’s stream of work with his coauthors in the role of e-markets [18, 29, 30]. In their work, contracts between the supplier and the buyer are negotiated in advance while e-markets or spot markets serve as a backup when demand is realized. In our paper, we do not consider the capacity constraint. Instead, the supplier can choose to make relationship-specific investments to lower the cost of production. Furthermore, the e-market is only used by the buyer as an outside option in negotiation with the supplier, which implies that the buyer still prefers to buy from the supplier if the supplier can match the e-market price. Our work is also different from Tunca and Zenios [27] in that we study the complementary role of the e-market to the relational contract when the buyer uses the e-market as an outside option to press the long-term supplier in the negotiation.

In our paper, the buyer has an outside option, the e-market, and uses it to bargain with the supplier noncooperatively. The paper focuses on the complementary impact of e-markets on the traditional supplier–buyer relationship. We choose to use a noncooperative bargaining model because it serves our purpose in two aspects: compared to take-it-or-leave-it contracts, it better reflects the real negotiation setting where both parties have some bargaining power; compared to the cooperative bargaining model (Nash bargaining model), it can better accommodate outside options, which is supported by experimental results reported by Binmore et al. [4].

## Buyer–Supplier Bargaining with E-Markets as an Outside Option for the Buyer

Con sider a two-firm supply chain with a supplier and a buyer (manufacturer). With an existing relationship, the buyer negotiates with the supplier on the transfer price contract and makes purchases.<sup>1</sup> The buyer sells products to the consumer market where he or she faces a downward-sloping demand. Assume the inverse demand function is $r = a - b Q .$ for $0 \leq r \leq a ,$ , where r is the retail price and Q is the demand.<sup>2</sup>

Besides the long-term supplier, there is an e-marketplace where the buyer can search to find the price quotes from participating suppliers. We use the term “search cost” to encompass costs in finding supplier information, verifying supplier information, posting request for quotes (RFQs) and qualifying suppliers, and so forth. The buyer can then use the price quote as a benchmarking tool in the bargaining with the longterm supplier. The buyer first invests in searching to find a good price in the e-market.<sup>3</sup> The relationship between the buyer’s investment in searching and the e-market price the supplier could get is not fully studied in the literature. To efficiently describe the relationship without too much distraction from the purpose of our paper, we make the following assumptions: the e-market price the buyer could get is $t = t _ { 0 } - x _ { b } ,$ , where $t _ { 0 }$ is the e-market price if no searching is done, and $x _ { b }$ is the amount of price reduction achieved by his or her investment on searching. The cost of investment is given by $I _ { b } = \mathsf { \mathbf { \alpha } } _ { b } x _ { b } ^ { \mathrm { ~ 2 ~ } }$ , where $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { \mathbf { \mathit { X } } } } _ { b } > 0$ is a scaling factor and is a function of the e-market. According to Soh et al. [25], successful e-markets often feature low price transparency so as to attract suppliers’ participation. Moreover, this function models the decreasing returns to the investment on searching: getting a better price becomes increasingly difficult as the buyer searches more.<sup>4</sup> From now on, we use the term “search cost” to represent the buyer’s investment in searching.

After the e-market price is acquired, the buyer brings it to the supplier as the outside option in the bargaining process. If the supplier decides to participate, he or she can first make some relationship-specific investment to lower the unit production cost, as a way to improve the margin and the profit. That is, the cost reductions are only effective for the transactions with this buyer, such as through improving the interorganizational information system that links with the buyer. Assume the unit production cost is $c = c _ { 0 } - x _ { s } .$ , where $c _ { 0 }$ is the original cost and $x _ { s }$ is unit cost reduction achieved by the supplier’s investment. Similar to the buyer’s search, the cost of investment is given by $I _ { \mathrm { { s } } } = \alpha _ { \mathrm { { s } } } x _ { \mathrm { { s } } } ^ { 2 } .$ where $\alpha _ { _ s } > 0$ is a scaling factor. Similar assumptions have been made in the literature to model the relationship between unit production cost and research and development (R&D) expenditures [16].

After the cost-reduction investment is made, the bargaining process begins. The buyer uses the e-market price as an outside option to negotiate with the supplier over the transfer price $p .$ The bargaining process is described in detail below.

Generally speaking, the buyer values the long-term relationship with the supplier, so that if the supplier offers the buyer the same utility as does the e-market, he or she would choose the long-term supplier. The reason could be the large switching cost or the uncertainty about the e-market suppliers. The buyer usually has less information about the e-market suppliers, and therefore is less assured of the product quality they provide compared to the existing supplier. For example, IBM and HP use prices from spot markets as a benchmarking tool during their negotiation with DRAM suppliers [14]. Even without an e-market, companies may use new potential suppliers as outside options to pressure incumbent suppliers for better prices, as Toyota did with its traditional suppliers, known as keiretsu: “the drive is on to replace expensive materials, benchmark Toyota’s auto parts against Chinese-level pricing, and squeeze its Japanese suppliers further by relying more on non-keiretsu parts makers” [7].

Finally, the buyer sells the products to the consumer market, choosing the retail price r and ordering the corresponding quantity Q from the supplier who fulfills the order.

When no e-markets exist, the solution of maximizing the joint profits of the supplier and the buyer is provided by the following proposition.

## Equilibrium Investments and Contract Without E-Markets

The analysis of this model aims to provide a benchmark to measure the impact of the e-market on the supplier–buyer relationships.

If there is no e-market for the buyer, the sequence of events is the following:

•	 At Stage 1, the supplier determines his or her investment $I _ { \phantom { } _ { s } }$ to reduce the production cost.

•	 At Stage 2, the buyer and the supplier bargain over the transfer price $p$ according to the alternating-offers procedure.<sup>5</sup>

•	 At Stage 3, the buyer orders quantity Q from the supplier and sells to the consumer market at retail price r.

We assume they only negotiate on price $p .$ . After the price is set, the retailer can order any quantity $Q .$ . Even though they do not negotiate on quantity, the negotiation result p takes Q into account implicitly.

Lemma 1: If the supplier and the buyer’s relative bargaining power are $\boldsymbol { \eta } _ { \mathnormal { s } }$ and $\boldsymbol { \eta } _ { b } ,$ respectively, then the resulting transfer price p in the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) of the bargaining is

$$
p = \frac {\eta_ {s} a + (\eta_ {s} + 2 \eta_ {b}) c}{2 (\eta_ {s} + \eta_ {b})}.
$$

With symmetric bargaining power $\boldsymbol { \eta } _ { \mathnormal { s } } = \boldsymbol { \eta } _ { b } \left( = 1 / 2 \right)$ , we have

$$
p = \frac {a + 3 c}{4}.\tag{2}
$$

From now on, we use the version of symmetric bargaining power (symmetric friction) to focus on the effect of the e-market option on the equilibrium outcomes. The results can be easily extended to asymmetric bargaining power.

At Stage 1, taking into account possible reactions at the subsequent stages, the supplier determines the optimal investment $I _ { \phantom { } _ { s } }$ (or equivalently, the unit cost reduction x ) to maximize his or her profit.

Proposition 2 (Decentralized Solution Without E-Markets): When there is no e-market, the equilibrium investment, transfer price, retail price, and selling quantities are

$$
\begin{array}{c} I _ {s} ^ {d} = \frac {9 \alpha_ {s} (a - c _ {0}) ^ {2}}{(3 2 b \alpha_ {s} - 3) ^ {2}}, x _ {s} ^ {d} = \frac {3 (a - c _ {0})}{3 2 b \alpha_ {s} - 3}, p ^ {d} = \frac {8 b \alpha_ {s} (a + 3 c _ {0}) - 3 a}{3 2 b \alpha_ {s} - 3}, \\ r ^ {d} = \frac {4 b \alpha_ {s} (5 a + 3 c _ {0}) - 3 a}{3 2 b \alpha_ {s} - 3}, Q ^ {d} = \frac {1 2 \alpha_ {s} (a - c _ {0})}{3 2 b \alpha_ {s} - 3}, \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where the superscript d denotes the decentralized solution.

Compared to the first-best solution, the supplier underinvests in cost reductions, which results in higher unit production costs, higher retail prices, and less channel profits.

Proposition 2 states that the supplier underinvests in the decentralized setting, which is because he or she can only internalize half of the benefit of the investment. Although the buyer would like larger investment by the supplier, he or she cannot give the supplier any additional incentive for investment. As a consequence, the unit production cost is higher, and the buyer charges a higher retail price and sells fewer quantities than in the first-best solution.

## Equilibrium Investments and Contract with E-Markets as an Outside Option

With the existence of an e-market, the buyer invests in searching, which is presented by $I _ { b } ,$ and uses the e-market price t as an outside option in negotiating with the supplier over the transfer price $p .$

The bargaining process is described below. At time 0, the buyer makes an offer of $p .$ The supplier either accepts the offer or makes a counteroffer at time $\Delta > 0$ . In response, the buyer has three choices: (1) accept, (2) reject and make a counteroffer $\Delta$ time units later, and (3) reject and opt out, in which case negotiations terminate in disagreement. This process of making offers and counteroffers continues until a player accepts an offer or opts out.<sup>6</sup>

The payoffs are as follows. If they reach an agreement at time tD on $p \in [ c , a ] \triangleq X .$ player $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s } \ ( i = s , b )$ payoff is $U _ { i } ( p ) \mathrm { e x p } ( - d _ { i } \tau \Delta )$ , where $U _ { i } ( p )$ is player i’s payoff if they reach an agreement at time $^ { 0 , }$ and $d _ { i }$ is player i’s friction or discount rate. If they do not reach an agreement because the buyer opts out at time tD, then the buyer obtains a payoff of $\mathfrak { w } _ { b } \mathfrak { e x p } ( - d _ { b } \mathfrak {tau } \Delta )$ , where ${ \mathfrak { O } } _ { b }$ is the buyer’s outside option, which is the utility the buyer obtains if he or she purchases from the e-market. Finally, if the players perpetually disagree (i.e., each player always rejects any offer made to him or her, and never opts out), then each player’s payoff is zero. Again, we focus on the symmetric friction $d _ { s } = d _ { b }$ and the small time interval $\Delta  0$ with the reason stated earlier.

As shown later, in the SPE of the bargaining, the buyer either accepts the supplier’s offer or opts out to the e-market immediately after the bargaining begins.

Theorem 1 states that when the pressure ratio is sufficiently low, the e-market does not have any effect on the existing supplier–buyer relationship—the buyer does not invest in searching while the supplier would participate and invest in cost reductions, and the final transfer price is less than the e-market price. In the region where the pressure ratio is moderate, the buyer invests in searching, the supplier invests in cost reductions, and the outside option of the e-market price is binding. When the pressure ratio is sufficiently high, the buyer invests in searching, but matching the e-market price is so costly for the supplier that he or she would rather quit, so the buyer exercises the e-market option. Indeed, only recently when the other suppliers could provide parts in a sufficiently low price did Toyota start to use them as outside options to bargain with its existing suppliers [7]. When the initial price from the outside suppliers become low enough, Toyota engages in identifying suppliers and uses their price as a benchmark for bargaining with existing suppliers. Toyota, however, does not replace the traditional suppliers with the new ones—they merely complement the relationship.

Figure 2 illustrates Theorem 1. When the original e-market price $t _ { 0 }$ is high enough or the initial cost $c _ { _ { 0 } }$ is low enough (Region I), the buyer procures from the long-term supplier without investment in searching, as it is unrealistic to find a low e-market price. In contrast, when $t _ { 0 }$ is low or $c _ { 0 }$ is high (Region III), the buyer exercises the e-market option, as it is unprofitable for the supplier to match the e-market price. In Region II, where $t _ { 0 }$ and $c _ { 0 }$ are moderate, the buyer procures from the reliable long-term supplier, but he or she searches in the e-market and the e-market price is effective in the bargaining process.

The observation that the buyer searches in the e-markets but finally procures from the long-term supplier has an interesting implication: if more and more buyers use the e-markets as outside options, we may expect to see an increase in e-market price quotations without a significant increase in the transaction volume through e-markets. This may apply to the situation where the e-market is not mature and has not established the reputation so that the buyers would rather buy from the long-term suppliers and use the e-markets purely as a bargaining tool. The following corollary describes how the equilibrium investments change with market characteristics and investment cost parameters.

Corollary 1: (a) As the search cost parameter $\alpha _ { \boldsymbol { b } }$ or the original e-market price $t _ { o }$ increases, both buyer’s and supplier’s investments decrease, and the e-market op tion is less likely to be effective. As the search parameter $\alpha _ { \boldsymbol { b } }$ or the original e-market price $t _ { 0 }$ increases, the buyer invests less in searching the e-market. With less pressure to match the e-market price, the supplier invests less in cost reductions too. On the other hand, as the cost reduction parameter $\mathbf { \alpha } \propto _ { _ s }$ increases, the supplier invests less, but the buyer is more willing to search to press the supplier: because if the buyer does not, he or she will end up with a higher trading price in the bargaining.

Corollary 1 also shows how the likelihood of each outcome in Theorem 1 changes with the parameters. ## The Effect of E-Markets on Supplier–Buyer Relationships

We now compare the equilibrium outcomes with and without the e-market options to show the effect of e-markets on supplier–buyer relationships. The comparison provides insights on the complementary role of e-markets in the existing supply chain. Specifically, we examine how the e-market option affects the procurement contract with the long-term supplier, the supplier’s investment in cost reductions, and the supply-chain performance by contrasting all relevant statistics from the two cases.

Proposition 3 (E-Market Effect on Investment and Payoffs): ${ \mathit { I f } } \ t _ { _ { O } }$ is sufficiently low or $c _ { o }$ is sufficiently high (when $( a - t _ { o } ) / ( a - c _ { o } ) > K _ { _ 3 } )$ , then under the e-market competition, the supplier’s investment in cost reduction is greater,<sup>7</sup> the total selling quantity is larger, the buyer and the consumers are better off, and the supplier is worse off than the benchmark no e-market case. Otherwise, when $( a - t _ { o } ) / ( a - c _ { o } ) < K _ { 3 }$ , the outside option is not binding, and the supplier’s payoff does not change.

When $t _ { \phantom { } _ { 0 } }$ is high or $c _ { _ { 0 } }$ is low, the e-market option does not affect the equilibrium. When $t _ { \phantom { } _ { 0 } }$ is sufficiently low or $c _ { _ { 0 } }$ is sufficiently high (i.e., the pressure ratio is high), using the e-market as an outside option stimulates the supplier’s investment (as long as he or she participates), lowers the trading price, and improves the buyer’s profitability whether he or she procures from the long-term supplier or the e-market. The long-term supplier is always worse off under the competition from the e-market, although he or she invests more in cost reductions to reduce the profit loss. The consumer surplus is higher due to the lower retail price and larger quantity sold. Consumers always benefit from the introduction of e-market competition.

Now we analyze the effect of the e-market on the supply-chain profit, which is the sum of the buyer and the existing supplier’s profit. The effects of the e-market option on supply-chain performance are two-sided: on one hand, the supplier’s underinvestment is moderated and the transfer price is lower, which reduces the double marginalization and improves supply-chain efficiency (if the supplier participates); on the other hand, there is the additional search cost to make the e-market option effective. If the benefits of reduced double marginalization outbid the search cost, the supply-chain profit increases. Otherwise it decreases. The exact conditions for which the channel profit increases or decreases are not in a clean form. To provide more insights, we first examine the extreme case where $\mathbf { \alpha } _ { s } \to \infty$ and $\alpha _ { \boldsymbol { b } }  \infty$ . The condition $\alpha _ { _ s } $ ∞ applies to the case when the supplier does not have the ability or opportunity to invest in cost reductions because of, say, the budget or time constraint. The condition $\alpha _ { _ b } $ ∞ refers to the perfectly competitive e-market where the e-market price is exogenously determined. We find that the supply-chain profit always increases as long as g, the e-market pressure ratio, is large enough. Then we extend the result to the case with more general $\mathbf { \alpha } \propto _ { _ s }$ and $\alpha _ { b } .$ The following proposition characterizes the conditions under which supply-chain profit increases under the e-market option.

Proposition 4a states that when the e-market is perfectly competitive and the supplier does not have the option to invest, the supply-chain profit increases as long as the pressure ratio is large enough so that the outside option is binding $( \gamma > 3 / 4 )$ . As the supplier does not have the option to invest, the effect of the e-market as the outside option is acted upon the transfer price—the e-market option gives the buyer a leverage for a better price, which indirectly reduces the double-marginalization effect and thus improves the supply-chain efficiency. Because there is no search cost, the channel profit will always increase if the e-market outside option is binding.

In the more general case, Proposition 4b states that when the cost-reduction investment is costly, the e-market option helps to stimulate the supplier’s investment and improve supply-chain profit if the pressure from the e-market is high enough. Therefore, the benefits from improved supply-chain efficiency outbid the buyer’s search cost, resulting in a better supply-chain performance.

## The Impact of Quality Uncertainty on E-Market Adoption

In the models above, we assume that e-market suppliers provide goods of the same quality as the long-term supplier does and it is known to the buyer. In reality, the buyer may not know the quality of the e-market offerings. In this section, we extend our model to the setting where there is quality uncertainty of the products provided by the e-market suppliers. The quality uncertainty is in a general sense, which may encompass uncertainty in not only the quality of the product but also trust, delivery, and payment terms that can be difficult to assess with new suppliers.

Assume the product quality provided by the long-term supplier is $s _ { 0 } ,$ which is a constant. The quality of goods provided by the e-market supplier is $s = s _ { 0 } - \varepsilon$ , where e is a random variable with mean m and variance $\sigma ^ { 2 }$ which are public information.<sup>8</sup>

The buyer will not know the exact e-market quality if he or she procures from the long-term supplier. However, if the buyer decides to procure from the e-market supplier, the quality uncertainty is realized after he or she commits to the purchase and before he or she sells to the consumer market. Therefore, the buyer can adjust the retail price according to the quality realization to maximize his or her profit. When the buyer sells the products to the end market, customers can detect the quality, either through consumer review or word of mouth. Therefore, the inverse demand function is assumed to be $r = a - b Q + s$

## The Effect of E-Market Quality Uncertainty on E-Market Adoption

There are two parts to the effect of quality uncertainty—the variability of the quality and the expected value of the quality. The intuition was that if the buyer is uncertain about e-market quality, he or she is more likely to buy from the long-term supplier, and the e-market is less likely to be adopted. However, our result indicates that as the variability of e-market quality increases, the buyer is more likely to adopt the e-market. More intuitive of the results is the effect of the expected value of e-market quality. As it decreases, the buyer is less likely to adopt the e-market.

Accordingly, we define the two conflicting forces at play: the “quality asymmetry effect,” brought by $\sigma ^ { 2 } ;$ , and the “information asymmetry effect,” brought by m.

The quality asymmetry effect refers to the effect of the variance of quality. As the consumers are willing to pay more for goods of better quality, the buyer can charge a price premium and sell at a larger quantity if the realized e-market quality is high. Therefore, the multiplicative effect leads to profit gains that more than offset the profit loss when the e-market quality is low. Consequently, the variance of quality increases the buyer’s expected utility if he or she procures from the e-market, and consequently decreases the bargaining price, forcing the supplier to invest more. As a result, the buyer’s profit increases and he or she is more willing to search or adopt the e-market. The quality asymmetry effect increases with the variance $\boldsymbol { \sigma } ^ { 2 } . { } ^ { 9 }$

The information asymmetry effect, on the other hand, refers to the effect of asymmetric information in the e-market. Because it is difficult for the buyer to assess the e-market product qualities beforehand, and the contracts with e-market suppliers are usually short-term, the e-market suppliers have an incentive to provide lower quality to save the production cost. Therefore, we would expect $\mu > 0 .$ Compared to the results in the third section where $\mu = 0$ , the lower expected e-market quality reduces the buyer’s expected utility if purchasing from e-markets, and therefore increases the bargaining price and relieves the pressure for the supplier’s investment. As a consequence, the buyer’s profit decreases and he or she is less likely to search or adopt the e-market. The higher the m, the larger the information asymmetry effect.

The final outcome is the combined effect and depends on the relative magnitude of the two forces. If the information asymmetry effect dominates the quality asymmetry effect, the buyer is less likely to adopt the e-market; otherwise, the e-market is more likely to be adopted.

Our results also have implications for e-market providers. When the original e-market price is low but not low enough (the middle case of Theorem 2), the buyer gets a price quote from the e-market, but ends up purchasing from the long-term supplier. In other words, the e-market is used purely as a benchmarking tool in bargaining. How could the e-market providers possibly react to this non-revenue-generating involvement? Our analysis points out two approaches for helping the e-market providers. First, increasing the average product quality from e-market suppliers could make the buyer more likely to adopt e-markets. Thus, it is important for the e-market to establish a quality image and a good reputation so that the e-market suppliers are more respected for their often lower price. However, for small e-market providers who may not be able to improve on the average quality of their offerings, our result suggests that increasing the quality dispersion may help, and may even make the buyer more likely to adopt the e-market if he or she can adjust the price according to the realized quality.

## Discussion and Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we study the effect of the e-market on the long-term supplier–buyer relationship when it is used by the buyer as an outside option in the negotiation process. We place it in a supply-chain context where the supplier can make relationship-specific investment to reduce the production cost. Comparisons between the market equilibrium with and without the e-market option are drawn. We find that as the pressure ratio, a measure of the severity of the e-market threat for the supplier, increases, the buyer’s procurement channel shifts from the traditional supplier to the e-market. When the pressure ratio of the e-market is moderate, it stimulates the long-term supplier to increase his or her relationship-specific investment. Our analysis shows that the presence of e-market options always increases consumer welfare, and it may even increase the supply-chain profit if the pressure ratio is high.

We further analyze the effects of e-market quality uncertainty on e-market adoption, which are two-sided. On one hand, the information asymmetry gives the e-market suppliers incentive to provide goods of lower quality, which eventually hurts the buyer’s profit and e-market adoption; on the other hand, the buyer may benefit from the variability of the e-market quality if he or she can adjust the consumer price accordingly. From the perspective of e-market suppliers, improving on the quality and reputation of the e-market would facilitate its adoption. From the buyer’s point of view, the e-market quality uncertainty can be utilized if he or she could implement pricing in response to the realization of quality or the demand.

Our results carry rich managerial implications for players in industries with the presence of the e-market. For the buyer, using e-market as the outside option can help him or her achieve better profitability, but if the buyer wants to keep the longterm supplier for the reliability, he or she should use an e-market price that is not aggressively low. For the traditional supplier, his or her profit suffers with the threat of e-market competition, because of the lower transfer price or even loss of the procurement contract. The underlying reason is that the supplier is passive and can only work with the buyer without any outside option of his or her own.<sup>10</sup> Our results imply that the supplier will also have an incentive to participate in the e-market, maybe a different one, to gain some bargaining power in the negotiation, even if the supplier does not actually have any transactions. This explains why more and more suppliers are participating in e-markets in addition to selling additional capacity. In turn, the growing e-market suppliers will attract more buyers to the e-market, which creates a positive feedback. Hence, we expect to see more participation in e-markets if more buyers use the e-market option in the bargaining.

The message of our results for the e-market providers is that the large volume of price quotations may not imply a large volume of transactions if more buyers use the e-market as outside options. This may apply to the situation where the e-market is not mature and has not established the reputation so that the buyers would rather buy from the reliable long-term suppliers and use the e-markets purely as a bargaining tool. Our results suggest that the e-market providers can boost the e-market sales/ adoption by improving on the quality and reputation of the e-market products. Indeed, such practice is common for many e-market providers. For example, Converge has developed quality control processes to ensure that the product sold through the exchange market meets the quality standards [8]. At the same time, to generate more business, e-markets should provide high price transparency, because from Corollary 1, we know that higher price transparency will lead to a lower search cost (a ) for the buyer, and the e-market option is more likely to be effective.

One unique feature of our model is that we assume the system is “open,” in the sense that the long-term supplier and the e-market suppliers are different, and can sell to the buyer only through the designated channel. By contrast, existing models on the coexistence of contract-based relationships and e-markets (e.g., [27]) study a “closed” system, where all players can participate in both channels. Our model provides an alternative observation of the industry, where firms in the e-market, if not trading surplus products, are often lesser known, smaller-sized firms that hope to reach potential buyers—whom they otherwise would have no way of reaching—through the e-market.<sup>11</sup> By contrast, the existing supplier, often bigger and more established than its e-market counterparts, does not want to join the e-market for fear of competing only on price. While buyers prefer to do business with the existing supplier for quality assurance, they still use the e-market as a benchmark for bargaining. We think this reflects the diversity of suppliers in many industries.

Although the model presented is a one-shot game, it also applies to the longterm contracts, where the existing supplier is willing to make relationship-specific investments. We also studied the multiperiod game where the long-term supplier’s relationship-specific investment is cumulative. The result still holds that the e-market option stimulates traditional supplier’s investment. In addition, we find that compared to the static game, the long-term supplier will invest more and his or her chance of winning the contract over the e-market suppliers is larger in the dynamic game. (We omitted the analysis due to the length constraint.) The results provide insights that complement our findings in the static game.

Some technical assumptions are made to facilitate the analysis. First, we do not explicitly consider the switching cost. However, we do implicitly consider the effect of switching cost when we assume that the buyer prefers the long-term supplier over the e-market if he or she could reach the same profitability using the two options. With the switching cost, the e-market will be less likely to be searched or adopted. As the switching cost becomes sufficiently large, the e-market will have no impact on the traditional supply chain at all. Moreover, we model the cost of investment in the e-market option as a quadratic function for sake of analytical tractability. The same qualitative results are expected to hold under more general convex functions, but the analysis would be cumbersome and is left as a topic for future research.

Another important assumption we make for the sake of analytical tractability is that we assume the supplier’s investment is fully relationship specific. This is the main reason why in the result, the supplier can never be better off. In reality, any investment within a firm should be expected to benefit more than just one relationship. If we relax the constraint and allow the supplier’s other business to benefit from the investment—that is, we consider the “spillover” effect of the investment—the supplier may be better off. Therefore, the supplier will have more incentive to cooperate with the buyer’s participation in e-markets. For example, it is reported that Delphi, as a supplier of Toyota, sends its technicians to Toyota to collaborate on the blueprints for new parts, and invites Toyota to offer suggestions for improving productivity. With only less than 10 percent of its Asian revenue from Toyota, any quality and productivity gain will benefit Delphi in more ways than just improving the making of the parts for Toyota [7].

Future research may also focus on empirically testing the propositions derived from our theoretical model. Specifically, studies of those companies that use the e-market as a bargaining tool may help explain whether the impact of e-market on information technology investment (relationship-specific investment), procurement price, and each party’s economic performances predicted in this paper holds in real-world environments.
