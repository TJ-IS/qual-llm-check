# Contextual influences on technology use mediation: a comparative analysis of electronic medical record systems

Elizabeth Davidson<sup>1</sup> and Mike Chiasson<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Developing, implementing and assimilating information technologies into organizational practices pose significant technical and social challenges. In this paper, we explore technology use mediation (TUM) processes in two cases of electronic medical record system use in two healthcare organizations. In our analysis, we examine contextual influences on mediation and consider TUM processes and outcomes in projects utilizing modern systems development methods. We found that crucial TUM actions occurred during systems development phases as well as during system use, that mediation was vitally important with these specialized IT artefacts, and that system configuration required changes to software infrastructure and code. Organizational size influenced the availability and the effectiveness of mediation resources; with adequate resources committed to the task, the institutional environment presented substantial, but not insurmountable, challenges to technology use mediation. We consider implications for practice and suggest future research directions.

## Introduction and motivation

Developing, implementing, and assimilating information technologies into organizational practices pose significant technical and social challenges. An information technology’s implications for day-to-day practice may not be apparent during planning stages, when attention is focused on overall business goals and implementation strategy (Davidson, 2002). The period after adoption and initial implementation of software becomes critical to adjust social practices and technical features to assimilate a system into practice (Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). Orlikowski et al. (1995) refer to this process as metastructuring of technology in use, and illustrate a form of organizationally sanctioned metastructuring, technology use mediation (TUM).

In this paper, we use TUM as a conceptual lens, to examine how software technologies and social practices are mutually shaped during systems development activities such as commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) customization and configuration, component-based development (CBD) prototyping, and implementation. We consider how TUM actions may occur during information system (IS) development activities as well as during use in these settings, and whether TUM processes are evident in a broader range of technical and organizational settings than earlier studies suggest. In doing so, we highlight contextual influences on the process and outcomes of TUM.

In our analysis of two organizations implementing electronic medical record systems (EMRS), we found mediation actions were evident in systems development activities, as the software technology was established, and in one case, reinforcement, adjustment, and episodic change actions were also evident following implementation. Successful mediation was more viable in the larger organization (a hospital) due to a substantial and sustained commitment of organizational resources. In the smaller organization (a clinic), mediation depended on external mediator resources and was less effective. Even though the IT artefacts (EMRS) being developed and implemented were specialized, mediation was essential to contextualize the IT artefact. We also note that the institutional context (i.e., healthcare) and existing legacy technologies heightened the need for mediation, while making the process more difficult.

In the next section, we review the literature on TUM and highlight areas in which TUM concepts might be extended to a wider range of technical and social context. We describe the comparative case methodology we used to analyze TUM in theoretically interesting and distinct settings. We review key findings from each case, including TUM phases, mediators, contextual influences, and outcomes. In our discussion, we consider how context influenced the trajectory of TUM in these cases and the implications of our study for future research and for practice.

## TUM and contextual influences

Orlikowski et al. (1995) defined TUM as the ‘deliberate, ongoing, and organizationally-sanctioned intervention within the context of use that helps to adapt new communication technology to that context, modifies the context as appropriate to accommodate use of the technology, and facilitates the ongoing effectiveness of that technology over time’ (p. 424). Individual-level structuring occurs when users appropriate a technology in day-to-day practice, sometimes using the technology consistently with existing institutional practices and embedded assumptions about use, and sometimes reshaping practice to the technology’s capabilities (Orlikowski, 1992; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). However, TUM differs from individual-level structuring in that a subset of organizational members – often other users – act as mediators as they modify the technology and the institutional environment through their explicit (and sanctioned) actions, and thus influence the technical and institutional context in which individual-level structuring occurs. TUM also differs from implementation support activities such as training or job redesign, to include ongoing and episodic adjustments to the technology and to institutional properties of the organization. Thus, TUM can be considered a form of metastructuring of technology in use (Orlikowski et al., 1995, p. 425).

In their study of the adoption and assimilation of a communications technology (newsgroups), Orlikowski et al. (1995) identified four types of metastructuring processes. During establishment, mediators set up the technology and propose how it will be used (p. 438). Reinforcement entails assistance and encouragement to users and maintaining the technology’s reliable performance (p. 440). Adjustment actions include refining rules and procedures around systems use and adjusting the technology as needed (p. 429). Finally, episodic changes are significant reassessments and restructuring of the technology and related practices, which may be triggered by external events such as the release of a new version of the software (p. 440).

Orlikowski et al. (1995) developed the TUM framework to explain how users contextualize general purpose computer-mediated communication technologies (CMCs) and how their actions can result in institutional changes in practices. Research on mediation processes has focused on similar general purpose information technologies, including intranets (Scheepers, 2003), electronic messaging systems (King & Xia, 1997), and groupware technologies (Orlikowski, 1996; Orlikowski & Hofman, 1997; Bansler & Havn, 2003), typically in large, for-profit organizations. The applicability of TUM concepts in understanding a wider range of social and technical settings has not yet been explored. Doing so may extend our understanding of TUM processes and possible outcomes.

## TUM with specialized IT artefacts

Orlikowski et al. (1995) posited that TUM may be less important with specialized information technologies, because they require less contextualizing than general purpose technologies like CMCs (p. 442). However, all technologies have a degree of interpretive flexibility (Bijker, 1995), and informational technologies are particularly adaptable (Orlikowski, 1992, 1996). We posit that TUM does occur with specialized technologies and that TUM actions may be as influential during systems development and implementation as they are during use.

With specialized software technologies, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) packages (or in our cases electronic medical record systems), establishing the physical system involves extensive configuration, module selection, and possibly customization; TUM thus begins with decisions about which software features to implement. Moreover, specialized software packages present significant knowledge barriers associated with configuring the system and assimilating new work processes (Robey et al., 2002). Contextualizing these types of systems may involve identifying and reconciling (mis)- matches between the package and organizational practice, rather than envisioning totally new uses. Determining how organizational practices should change and how the software system should be activated or configured are, we posit, contextualizing actions. Organization members who participate in these development and implementation activities act as technology use mediators, as they specify the initial configuration of practice and software prior to actual use. Although contextualizing begins during systems development (with establishment), tacit assumptions may not surface until users are actively engaged with prototypes or pilots (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Davidson, 2002). Reinforcement and adjustment actions may be needed to overcome unexpected problems.

## TUM with malleable technologies

Although CMCs have a high degree of interpretive flexibility in use, their underlying code is generally not malleable; a CMC’s technical features are contextualized by switching pre-determined features on or off. Similarly, COTS software packages and component-based development methods offer the opportunity to ‘tweak’ a software technology without touching underlying code. With these methods, systems development challenges have shifted from software design and coding to package selection, configuration, and integration with legacy systems (Boehm & Abts, 1999). With component-based development methods, applications are assembled and integrated from software ‘building blocks’ (i.e. components), available in-house or from commercial vendors (Robey et al., 2001); technical challenges involve determining how software works and how to integrate software, rather than how to build software to meet specific requirements.

Although such approaches to systems development initially raised expectations about the prospects for fast, less expensive, and relatively trouble-free ‘turnkey’ software implementation (Bianchi et al., 2003, Iribarne et al., 2004), these expectations are often not realized in practice (Leung & Leung, 2002). With COTS or CBD, the technical system (software) presents the organization with an array of features, functions, capabilities, and constraints. COTS packages rich in features and functions may overwhelm the organization’s ability to adopt all of its capabilities (Brooks, 1995). Uncertainty about the reliability and performance of components is an issue with CBD approaches (Robey et al., 2001). Instead of simple ‘plug-and-play’ implementation, organizations can be faced with substantial changes to procedures, practices, and work roles in order to accommodate the social design assumptions embedded in software, as well as the need to customize, configure, or otherwise modify the software system (to the degree possible) to fit with legacy systems and practices. The latter may require modifying underlying code or building system interfaces to allow the technology to operate. In these situations, TUM might extend beyond superficial contextualization to include substantial modification to the software system code.

## TUM in institutionalized industrie

TUM does focus analytic attention to institutional change within an organization through IT use, but TUM studies, typically conducted at firms in a subset of forprofit industries, have not considered how highly institutionalized practices may affect the process and outcome of TUM. All organizations operate in a technical and institutional environment that constrains action to some degree (Scott, 1987), but organizations in highly institutionalized industries face particularly high barriers to change (Scott et al., 2000). For example, regulatory agencies require organizations to conform to particular practices; professional groups exert normative pressures on organizations to adopt and maintain sanctioned ways of operating. In such settings, technology use mediators could face significant challenges to IT-related changes in practice. In this study, we examine two healthcare organizations. Healthcare, a highly institutionalized industry, is a relatively unexplored economic sector in IS research, one which may challenge taken-for-granted assumptions about organizational information systems (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004).

## TUM in small organizations

Orlikowski et al. (1995) suggest that mediators’ closeness to the context of use, their familiarity with users’ technical abilities and their legitimacy among users may be key to their success. We agree these characteristics are desirable but note that such resources may not always be available in an organization. With large ERP installations, for example, external consultants typically play a major role in helping the organization overcome learning barriers (Robey et al., 2002). Whether external sources for TUM, such as consultants, experts within a community of practice, or vendors, can provide viable alternatives to inhouse mediation resources is an interesting question. This question is particularly relevant for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) (see a definition of SMEs at http://europa.eu.int/ISPO/ecommerce/sme/definition.html.). SMEs tend to be dependent on consultants as sources of IT expertise and, generally, IT resources and capabilities are in short supply (Hussin et al., 2002). TUM studies have focused on information system phenomena in large organizations where resources for technology use mediation (in-house staff or funding for consultants) are more likely to be available. Whether SMEs possess sufficient organizational resources to support effective TUM processes is an unexplored question.

## Research design and methods

We have argued that TUM concepts are applicable to a broad range of social and technical settings and that social and technical context may influence the trajectory and outcomes of TUM processes. To investigate contextual influences on TUM, we draw on two organizational case studies that differ in theoretically interesting ways from typical settings of TUM research (see Table 1).

Both case studies involved an electronic medical record system utilized in healthcare settings. An EMRS involves electronic creation, storage, and access to healthcare information at the patient level. The functionality of an EMRS may span clinical order entry, test results reporting, and clinical guidelines practices, depending on what functions are implemented in a given healthcare organization. These systems provided an opportunity to examine TUM processes associated with specialized IS. Each case presents a different configuration of functional structure (specialization) and code malleability. Healthcare is a highly institutionalized organizational field: healthcare work practices reflect not only organizational histories but also industry ‘best practices,’ the professional training, duties, and responsibilities of clinical professions, and regulatory oversight and controls. IS research has focused on large organizations, giving relatively little attention to small and medium-sized organizations (Ballantine & Levy, 1998). The two organizations examined here differ in organizational size (medium vs micro-sized) and resources.

The first research site is a medium-sized, acute care hospital located in the western United States (thereafter referred to as ‘the hospital’). The first author conducted the field study approximately 2 years after the system’s initial implementation. Data were collected primarily through interviews with 31 clinical personnel (physicians, nurses, lab technicians, pharmacists, quality managers) and IS managers; the research site also provided documentation about system planning, system use, and analysis of clinical outcomes. Interviews were recorded and later transcribed. Data from the case study have been analyzed to examine changes in organizational structure (Davidson & Chismar, 1998), communication patterns (Davidson, 2000) and linkages between healthcare strategy and IT planning and implementation (Davidson & Chismar, 1999). For this paper, data were re-analyzed to focus on technology mediation actions and processes.

The second research site is a small, outpatient, cardiology clinic located in western Canada. The second author conducted an action research (AR) project with clinic and university personnel over a 4-year period. The action researcher created a diary from interviews, meeting minutes, field notes, design and programming notes, and other documentation, and saved successive versions of the EMRS prototype for comparisons and analysis of system change. These data were cataloged as a historical record of the action research project and evolving EMRS prototype. Data were analyzed to examine AR processes and outcomes (Chiasson & Dexter, 2001). For this paper, data were re-analyzed to focus on technology mediation actions and processes.

To examine TUM actions and processes in these cases, each author produced a detailed case narrative, highlighting evidence of the four TUM stages identified by Orlikowski et al. (1995), the actions and activities of technology mediators, and contextual influences on the trajectory and outcomes of TUM activities. Together, we critiqued, elaborated, and assessed data interpretations in each case, to more accurately interpret data using TUM concepts as an analytic lens. In this process, we utilized qualitative data presentation and analysis techniques (Miles & Huberman, 1994) and comparative case methods (Yin, 1994). Appendix A presents the results of one such analytic exercise.

## Findings and analysis

In the next sections, we describe the research sites of these two cases and present key findings about TUM processes, actions, and actors in each site.

## Case 1: clinical order entry in an acute care hospital

The research site is a private, non-profit urban acute care institution with 500 beds and 3200 employees, located in the western U.S. The hospital’s project to select and implement a clinical order entry and EMRS took 3 years and cost over \$18 million; the system was initially brought on-line at the end of 1995. At the time of the field study, the system had been operational for 2 years and was handling 40,000 clinical orders per week. The hospital has had success in improving patient care and reducing costs, evidenced in a variety of self-assessments, external audits, and the receipt of a prestigious industry quality achievement award. These successes resulted in large part from an organizational environment conducive to TUM, a process institutionalized as part of the hospital’s disease management and quality improvement programs.

TUM stages Each of the four stages of TUM was apparent in the case, beginning with establishment actions through which IS professionals and clinical personnel customized and configured the software package. Although the hospital purchased a software package from one of the leading vendors in the EMR system market, extensive technical customization and adjustments were necessary to tailor the user interface (a series of character-based, menu-driven screens) for each clinical department’s practices. Over 20,000 individual screens were developed during system implementation and the first years of use. Each set of interface screens, termed a ‘pathway,’ instantiated a care treatment protocol (called a clinical ‘pathway’), such as sets of orders for laboratory tests, medications, and so on.

Physicians, nurses, pharmacists, laboratory technicians, and quality assurance staff assigned to the project acted as mediators during this stage, as they developed departmental order sets and specified adjustments to the system interface. Their actions served to contextualize the application package to the hospital’s existing practices and routines. A major thrust of the project was to influence physician’s decision-making at the time clinical orders are issued, in ways that would reduce costs and improve patient care. In addition to encoding clinical practices into the departmental order sets, informational reminders such as the availability of lower-cost generic drugs or warnings of inappropriate uses of certain antibiotics were encoded into the interface. When presented with such information at the point of creating a clinical order, physicians tended to make different choices. Moreover, the convenience of selecting predefined departmental order sets encouraged physicians to accept the organizationally sanctioned clinical pathway, increasing their compliance to standard practices.

In the months after initial implementation and training, the hospital continued to commit substantial mediation resources to reinforce use of the EMRS. Nurses who had participated in the implementation project continued to assist other nurses and physicians to adjust their use of the system. For example, they encouraged and assisted physicians to create personal orders sets (POS) in the system from their preferred and commonly used sets of orders. Nurses’ actions sometimes reinforced existing practice as well. In the initial flurry of training, nurses offered a lot of one-on-one training and support to physicians to encourage them to enter their own orders directly into the electronic system. However, nurses came to accommodate the preferences of individual physicians who resisted doing direct order entry themselves. An average rate of 60% direct entry by physicians has persisted for several years. Similarly, system features that slowed the order entry process for physicians, such as checking for drug interactions, were disabled in the EMRS. Generally, when adjustments were made to the system, physicians’ preferences and convenience were weighted most heavily, reinforcing institutionalized clinical hierarchies and professional practices.

Ongoing adjustments to the EMRS arose in large part from systems integration issues. The hospital was unable to finance replacement of all of its clinical systems at once, and the pharmacy and laboratory systems were considered state-of-the-art. This implementation decision required the IS department to build data exchange functions and develop a ‘datagate’ (middleware) to exchange information between the admitting, pharmacy, laboratory, and billing system and the EMRS. The intricacies of passing clinical orders between systems caused numerous communication and procedural difficulties. For example, nurses could no longer simply consolidate duplicate paper orders for lab tests, and neither nurses nor laboratory technicians were willing to countermand physicians’ electronic orders. To reduce duplicate orders (without inconveniencing physicians), the technical system was adjusted to hold orders in the ‘datagate’ for several hours to allow automatic scanning for duplication. A group of clinicians, assigned permanently to the IS department, acted as mediators to resolve these unanticipated procedural glitches through technical and procedural changes.

Other adjustments to practice include decentralizing some care management decision to nurses on patient floors, with centralized utilization managers monitoring status via the EMRS. Similarly, respiratory therapists were authorized to customize physicians’ general orders for therapy, again with physicians’ ability to monitor decisions through the system. The clinical group in the IS department acted as technology mediators, to vet proposed changes put forward by special interest groups of clinicians and enact approved changes into the EMRS interface.

The hospital’s initial use of the EMRS to automate order management activities has expanded through episodic changes to the system infrastructure. After the initial period of establishment, reinforcement, and adjustment, hospital managers undertook a project to create a data repository of clinical data, drawn from the EMRS and other systems. This repository has become an important source of information for quality improvement teams, utilization review personnel, and benchmarking studies of physicians’ cost efficiency. This episodic change to the EMRS spurred further TUM activity. Notably, crossfunctional disease management taskforces of physicians, nurses, and other clinicians used the data warehouse to develop clinical pathways for treatment of high incident/ high-cost diseases. Clinical pathways were translated into system order pathways and coded into the EMRS interface. These activities built on and extended earlier mediation actions.

TUM resources During the establishment of the system, 10 physicians worked (and were paid) for 10 h of work per week; nurses, pharmacists, and other clinicians were temporarily reassigned to the project during implementation and installation/training phases. These groups worked to define departmental order sets and work processes related to the system. After system establishment and initial reinforcement phases, these groups transformed into permanent organizational resources to resolve procedural issues (adjustments) and to carry out episodic changes. The Medical Informatics Sub-Committee (MIS), a group of senior clinical administrators, primarily clinical department heads and nursing directors, met periodically to evaluate recommended enhancements to the system and to set priorities for the IS group. Special Interest Groups (SIGs) for pharmacy and nursing continued to act as problem-solving groups, to work on procedural problems and to evaluate suggested improvements to the system. The group of EMRS analysts, composed of two nurses, a laboratory technician, and a pharmacist, resided permanently in the IS department to propose or evaluate system-related procedural improvements and code changes to the system interface. This group took on boundary spanning roles between the IS department and clinical areas by working closely with the SIGs, MIS Committee, and disease management task forces. As fully trained and clinically licensed clinicians, these individuals understood the language and context of use, and they had legitimacy and influence among other clinicians.

Contextual influences on TUM As a medium-to-large sized organization, the hospital had sufficient organizational resources to support TUM activities on an ongoing basis. The hospital chose to interface the EMRS with legacy systems in other clinical departments. This created a complex, tightly integrated technical infrastructure that contributed to procedural problems. Responding to infrastructure problems required TUM interventions to adjust the technology or the work practices (or both). Mediators faced significant professional barriers to changing clinical practices, particularly physicians’ prerogatives related to system use, but institutionalizing TUM resources with disease management programs facilitated ongoing changes in clinical protocols.

TUM outcomes The TUM actions described above enabled the hospital to do more than automate clinical order entry practices; they helped the hospital to integrate EMR technology into day-to-day clinical practices as well as into ongoing quality improvement programs. With its network of committees working on changing practices and contextualizing the EMRS, the hospital achieved a number of clinical care improvement and cost containment goals, such as increased compliance to standard protocols, reduced inappropriate antibiotic use, faster response to clinical orders, and reduced costs. Radical changes in institutional structures for clinical practice did not occur (nor were they intended). Nonetheless, the actions of clinicians who participated in TUM activities heightened centralization of clinical decision-making and influenced individual physicians’ actions, while using the EMR system.

## Case 2: EMR prototyping in an outpatient clinic

Our second case involved an action research project to develop an EMRS for use in a small outpatient cardiology clinic. The clinic provides healthcare through traditional physician/patient visits and the prescription of diet, exercise, and medications. At the time of the study, the clinic employed six physicians, two dietitians, and a clinic administrator. The clinic was also involved in atherosclerosis research.

The initial goal for the AR project was to develop an EMRS (‘SoftHeart’) to address three areas in order of importance: (1) clinical (patient care), (2) research, and (3) administration. Upon completion, SoftHeart included various used and unused EMR modules: lipids data, lab results, medical history, physical examination data, medications taken and prescribed, diet and exercise, and quality of life information. Administrative information included patient demographics, referring physician information, and a clinic scheduler. A risk calculator analyzed and presented data on the risk of heart disease to promote patient/physician communication and action planning. SoftHeart supported 10–15 personnel: physicians, dietitians, exercise physiologists, and clinical researchers.

TUM stages The clinic did not purchase a packaged EMRS. Instead, the SoftHeart team decided to use rapid prototyping methods, drawing generic components from a PC-based, database package (FoxPro) to construct an EMRS. They expected they would be able to build on an existing administrative system, utilizing patient data contained in another stand-alone system and adding functionality to build the EMR features cited above. During an extended and contentious establishment phase, desired system features were identified and agreed to, and the generic database was configured and contextualized to reflect data field storage, user interfaces, and data processing requirements. Establishment entailed significant social and technical challenges that extended throughout the 4 years of the AR project.

Since the generic database package (FoxPro) could be configured and functionality developed to address a wide variety of clinical, research, and administrative practices, deciding which practices to include and prioritizing the design and introduction of system features had to be negotiated among diverse clinic stakeholders. Herein lay a major source of social problems. Although there was high-level agreement at the beginning of the AR project about general priorities for the EMRS, major stakeholders expressed a wide range of expectations and preferences for the system, particularly as the generic IT artefact took shape through subsequent prototypes and the malleability of the system became evident.

The first conflict arose between the AR team, which hoped to introduce IT into the exam room as an integral part of patient/physician interactions, and the funding agency that provided grant resources for the project. These agencies doubted the ability of an EMR system to change patient behavior. Some also suggested that computerized systems would destroy rapport between the provider and the patient. To accommodate these concerns, the team gave up the idea of having display screens in exam rooms, and instead focused on new reports for the paper-based patient file; reports were to be generated from the data in the existing clinic administration system. Second, the clinic operated in a decentralized manner, with each of six physicians essentially running their own shop, but drawing upon the common patient files to do their work. This made it difficult for the AR developer to reach agreement with clinical staff on system features. Despite repeated attempts to initiate discussions with clinic physicians, nurses and administrative staff to determine feasibility of system plans, his access to clinicians was limited, particularly when the clinic changed its physical location and organizational affiliation to a new hospital. Once they were finally settled into the new hospital, the AR developer essentially moved into the clinic. There, he attempted to accommodate the expectations of diverse internal stakeholders and to assess the fit between proposed system changes and cognitive and behavioral routines evident in practice.

The AR developer made a major presentation of SoftHeart to the clinical staff about 2 years after the project’s initiation, to introduce clinicians to the system’s features, to get their feedback on how to further elaborate the system, and to alert the administrative staff of possible changes to practices and procedures that use of the new system would entail. The physicians and research staff was generally excited by the presentation, but the social and technical possibilities made evident by the prototype brought forth a barrage of conflicting suggestions for the system. Each group within the clinic could now envision SoftHeart providing something for its specific needs. Physicians focused on the potential of the database to store new data, improve information access, and improve information presentation for clinical and research purposes. However, they also expressed concern that current information practices would be disturbed by SoftHeart. Dietitians believed the system would provide computer resources in the consultation room for quickly calculating patient dietary intake, and printing tailored letters for patients. The administrative coordinator saw the project as an opportunity to produce a windowsbased version of the administrative system she had created and now controlled.

With no clear direction for the system’s further development and without a single managing authority within the clinic, and with continuing reliance on an outsider (the AR developer) to mediate between the technology being designed and clinical practices, and with little budget, the developer had difficulty prioritizing and managing conflicting requirements. In an attempt to alleviate some of these issues, he decided to focus on using the existing administrative system as the data source and technical foundation for SoftHeart. The developer wanted to tap into the administrative system’s underlying data without touching the interface, because the clinic coordinator wanted to continue using the easyto-use interface built for this system. The AR developer later learned that the administrative system lacked technical flexibility: it was built as a single-user, standalone system and locked the database records in a way that prohibited any other program from using the data at the same time.

Given this technical limitation, the AR developer realized that he would need to rebuild all of the administrative functions, as well as clinical and research practices, into SoftHeart, and in doing so, could replace the existing system. Towards this end, the AR developer created a series of prototypes and reviewed them with the clinic coordinator, who rejected each one, citing differences between SoftHeart and the existing system. After many months, the conflict between the AR developer and the coordinator was brought before the clinic director, the other physicians, and research staff members. Realizing that the SoftHeart prototype could replace the administrative system, they dismissed the coordinator and threw their support behind SoftHeart.

Despite this apparently decisive action, the AR project remained in limbo for a number of months, until the research group associated with the clinic took ownership of SoftHeart. This group of three to four clinical researchers had 3 years previously been involved in a dispute with the clinic’s administrator over the development of a new EMR/administrative system. Having lost this battle, their vision had turned towards the development of an integrated wide-area network (LipidLink) for the clinic’s and its partners’ collection of data for lipid research. The merger between SoftHeart and LipidLink appeared to be ideal. The LipidLink project team eventually obtained funding from a pharmaceutical company for computers, networking equipment, and an IT support staff member. After 4 years of involvement, the AR developer withdrew from the project, as funds became available to hire permanent IT staff. The SoftHeart prototype was turned over to the LipidLink team, which finalized and implemented the system with the functionality cited above.

As a result of the extended and contentious negotiations over the social practices and technical features to be built into SoftHeart, TUM activities did not progress past the establishment phase to actual clinical use, until the end of the AR project. Instead, TUM activities cycled through periods of technical adjustments and episodic changes to the emerging system prototype, outside the context of use in day-to-day practice. Adjustments occurred periodically, as over 10 functioning prototypes were constructed, presented and evaluated by clinic personnel. Three episodic change phases occurred as the result of major project reorientations, for example, early in the project due to funder’s concerns, mid-way through the project, and with the merger with LipidLink. Because the system was not used in day-to-day practice during the AR project, reinforcement of planned changes to practice through EMR system use did not occur. The system was later successfully implemented and has been used for several years; changes in administrative, clinical and research practices may have resulted from use, but these occurred outside the scope of the AR project reported in this paper.

TUM resources The clinic lacked sufficient clinical resources and technical know-how to conduct the SoftHeart project. Instead, a group of university IS researchers, who were interested in healthcare, information systems, medicine, and health economics, and one of the clinic’s physicians was the primary driver behind the project, and the major resource for technology development and proposed changes to clinical practices.

The lead action researcher, at the time a Ph.D. student with an interest in healthcare and a background in IT, took on the role of system developer. By specifying and negotiating Softheart’s features and functions, he also assumed the role of technology use mediator during the establishment phase. His outsider status both facilitated and constrained the setting up of physical parameters and clinical features in the EMRS. First, the developer’s experience with database and software – technical skills not available within the clinic – allowed him to develop SoftHeart and to work with the clinic’s existing administrative system. However, the developer was inexperienced with clinic settings, which required him to observe patient–physician interaction and learn the heart disease literature in order to develop an IS intervention. As an outsider, the developer had limited access to the clinic staff at the outset, hampering the articulation of cognitive and behavioral routines that could be supported by the IT. He also lacked the authority of position or clinical training to promote changes in clinical practice he envisioned. He was, however, a free resource to the clinic. Given the limited staff and their responsibilities for delivering clinical care, it is doubtful that SoftHeart could have been built without his participation.

Contextual influences on TUM Developing a comprehensive, specialized IT artefact (an EMRS) was the goal of the project, but choosing how to use IT to support these practices was an ambiguous, equivocal task. Although the clinic is a small organization, it represented a complex array of clinical practices and competing stakeholder goals for an EMRS. Each physician had his/her own preferences for how to conduct clinical practice. Clinical practices developed through the physicians’ (and other personnel’s) formal training and experiences. There were no ‘handbooks’ or few written procedures covering the variety of clinical procedures that were potentially influenced by the EMRS. Using a general database package (FoxPro) for component-based prototyping increased the equivocal nature of the TUM process as the AR team attempted to establish a functioning prototype. The clinic was also severely hampered by lack of organizational resources to devote to TUM activities. Reliance on an external mediator, while it made the project possible, also made it harder to resolve establishment issues and move forward into use. Subsequent phases of TUM (reinforcement, adjustment, episodic change) would similarly have been hampered, if internal TUM resources had not emerged (the clinic researchers).

TUM outcomes The outcomes of TUM actions were mixed in this project. The action research team’s attempts to develop a software package that would influence clinical practice towards use of IT in patient/ clinician interactions did not materialize because of funder objections, the lack of active involvement of clinical staff in technology design activities, and the takeover of the project by clinic researchers, who redirected design and use of the technology towards their own interests (obtaining and sharing research data). In its final implementation at the clinic, SoftHeart was primarily focused on research and administration tasks, reinforcing institutional practices with the EMRS rather than establishing new IT-related practices. Users were using the software to examine patient data after visits, for administrative purposes, and for querying the data for clinical research. SoftHeart had fully replaced the clinic’s standalone administrative system, but it had not replaced the manual files used during the patient consultation. Instead, it provided new reports for clinicians and patients to examine during clinic visits.

## Discussion

We have argued that TUM occurs in a wider range of social and technical settings than is evident in the existing body of TUM research, and that TUM processes and outcomes are influenced by contextual factors such as the nature of the IT artifact (general purpose versus specialized), malleability of the software, institutional influences from the organizational environment and organization size. To investigate these issues, we examined TUM actions and activities in two settings that varied from each other and from typical TUM studies. We found evidence of TUM processes in each case, but mediation actions and outcomes differed. Examining contextual influences in these cases helps us understand socio-technical boundaries of this theoretic framework.

Although Orlikowski et al. (1995) posited that specialized technologies might require less contextualization, we found that, on the contrary, TUM was an essential process in the contextualization and appropriation of a specialized IT artefact, that is, an EMRS. The hospital began with a highly specialized IT artefact – a COTS EMRS package – in which expected uses, clinical functionality, and technical infrastructure were articulated and encoded. Nonetheless, clinical staff members performed extensive, detailed, and organizationally sanctioned mediation activities to contextualize the software package and adjust clinical practices to accommodate the system. Moreover, the specialized nature of the IT artefact, rather than reducing mediation, increased operational fragility and the need for reinforcement and adjustments to maintain the operational fidelity of the EMR system.

The clinic’s experiences also highlight the importance of TUM with specialized IT artefacts, although the trajectory of TUM phases was markedly different. The SoftHeart system ended up as a specialized IT artifact, but it began with the action research team’s broad intentions to design a socio-technical system using a generic database package. The uses for this technology in clinical practice were neither self-evident nor simple. The clinic is a small, homogeneous organization compared to the hospital, but physicians’ practices were varied and unique. Moreover, stakeholder groups had different priorities for how to apply IT capabilities in their work. Softheart emerged through negotiations over a series of prototypes, each presenting an array of technological possibilities and social practices. This finding is consistent with Bansler & Havn’s (2003) finding that TUM involves extensive sensemaking. The AR developer’s actions and interactions with clinic personnel during the prolonged and contentious establishment phase were sensemaking actions, as interpretations of SoftHeart emerged through these negotiations.

TUM has generally been associated with technical adjustments to an IT artefact, such as turning features on or off or installing new versions of a software package, but not changes to underlying software code. Our case analyses suggest that TUM adjustments may necessitate a broader range of changes to a software technology’s structure, at least with specialized IT artifacts such as EMRS. At the hospital, contextualization and adjustments to the technical system ranged from nurses and doctors creating POS at a computer on a hospital ward, to clinically trained system support staff coding departmental order sets as system interfaces, to IT staff turning features on or off in system code or encoding changes to the legacy system interfaces. At the clinic, SoftHeart’s code was highly malleable. The developer produced multiple prototypes, as he worked around the constraints of a legacy system and attempted to reconcile three competing institutional orders (administration, clinical, and research), before the technical system was ‘fixed’ and a system installed for operational use.

We examined TUM processes in two healthcare organizations. These organizations operated in a highly institutionalized industry, in which organizational practices reflected not only local structures and institutions but also the influences of professional groups and regulatory agencies. One might argue that in such settings, practices are so highly constrained that TUM is not feasible; that is, only technical, not social, adjustments are practical. The clinic’s experiences developing SoftHeart support this conjecture. Although the project began with the hope of enhancing physician/patient interactions in the exam room with IT use, negotiating such changes proved to be too difficult, first with project funders, and later with the clinicians themselves. Soft-Heart was instead designed and configured to produce paper reports, consistent with the clinic’s established practice, and to focus on research and administrative tasks, more than clinical practices. Similarly at the hospital, physicians retained considerable autonomy in clinical decision-making and in EMRS use. Nonetheless, subtle institutional changes in clinical practice did occur at the hospital through extensive TUM.

An underlying assumption about TUM is that organizational resources are used for contextualizing software, adjusting practice, reinforcing system uses, and so on. Mediation tasks can conceivably be carried out by inhouse staff or external personnel such as consultants or action researchers, assuming the organization has sufficient financial resources, although Orlikowski et al.’s (1995) study suggested that knowledgeable users, who have in-depth knowledge of work practices and organizational legitimacy, are the most effective mediators. Experiences of Case 1 support this proposition. The hospital had a sizeable clinical staff, and physicians, nurses and others could be drawn out of day-to-day care delivery activities to act as mediators (at some cost to the hospital). As knowledgeable insiders, they had detailed understanding of current practices and legitimacy. The clinic in Case 2, on the other hand, was a micro-sized organization. Its experiences developing SoftHeart highlights problems that may arise if there are insufficient organizational resources to support TUM processes. The clinic had no in-house staff who could feasibily take on mediation roles. The clinic’s solution was to allow the AR developer (using external funding) to take on TUM responsibilities. However, using an external mediator contributed to many of the issues discussed earlier, and the system remained in the establishment phase until inhouse staff became actively involved in the project.

## Future research and implications for practice

Although practitioners acknowledge the importance of dealing with organizational and social issues in systems development, successfully dealing with these issues remains a significant challenge (Doherty & King, 2001). We posited in this paper that the theoretic framework of TUM, although it was developed and has been applied primarily to general purpose CMCs, provides a useful analytic lens to examine the mutual shaping of social and technical possibilities using modern systems development approaches, and that TUM processes occur in a variety of contexts and with a range of IT artefacts. However, ours is a preliminary study of contextual influences on technology use. These two cases provided useful empirical data to examine mediation in these setting, but findings cannot be generalized to other settings without empirical testing (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Further research will be needed to extend our understanding of the range of IT artefacts and social contexts in which mediation occurs, and to further investigate contextual influences on TUM process and outcomes.

Our analysis suggests that implementing specialized software technologies entails TUM processes and that TUM actions begin before the system goes into operational use and continues during system use. Extending the TUM framework in these ways reduces earlier distinctions between ‘general purpose’ and ‘specialized’ IT artefacts and suggests that a continuum of mediation processes may exist that spans IT artefact types. The TUM framework then provides a vocabulary and analytic focus for investigating the interplay of social and technical influences in modern systems development approaches such as COTS and CBD. For example, compared with traditional approaches, technology users play a larger role in selection and configuration activities in CBD (Robey et al., 2001). TUM gives us a framework to identify when and how various organizational actors participate in CBD activities and to assess the implications of their actions on technology acceptance and organizational change. With specialized COTS projects, which present knowledge barriers for configuring and assimilating social and technical features (Robey et al., 2002), TUM highlights possible issues with the effectiveness of external mediators, such as industry consultants who often play a large part in such efforts within and across organizations.

Little attention has been given to IT/IS phenomena in SMEs, and even less to micro-sized organizations such as private practices of physicians, lawyers, and other professionals. Whether IS theories developed in large organizations apply to SMEs or to micro-sized organizations is an empirical question, requiring theory testing and possibly, theory elaboration or refinement, for these contexts (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). This is particularly relevant to TUM concepts, which have been applied in larger organizational settings. Because small organizations often lack IT resources and an IT knowledge-base, adequate and appropriate TUM may be difficult. Whether TUM resources develop within communities of practice or among boundary-spanning actors such as consultants and software vendors is an unexplored question. If they do, can such TUM resources be effectively mobilized to support mediation processes within SME and micro-sized organizations? Understanding the facilitating or subsidizing TUM processes among SMEs and micro-sized organizations, particularly when IT use is mandated through professional or governmental regulation, has important implications for policy.

The need for and scarcity of resources to engage in TUM is particularly worrisome in small organizational contexts such as private physician practices. Small clinics and private physician practices are increasingly being pressured to adopt EMRS. For example, in the United States President Bush has proposed a plan for full electronic medical record use within a decade. Small practices will likely face configuration and practice assimilation knowledge barriers, similar to those identified by Robey et al. (2002) for ERP systems. Organizational learning and adjustments to practice needed to fully integrate an EMRS into healthcare practices are substantial barriers to adoption (Bodenheimer & Grumbach, 2003). Some physicians will achieve baseline use of EMR technology without significant mediation, but reaching high levels of use across the population of physicians in small group practices (estimated at over 60% in the U.S.) may not occur without significant mediation at the individual practice level. To overcome knowledge barriers and to configure EMR packages in ways that facilitate good healthcare practices, support within the physician’s practice or from reliable third parties is essential for small physician practices (Lippman, 2000; Holbrook et al., 2001).

However, the availability and affordability of TUM resources to support EMRS use in small practices will be scarce. The cost of diverting employees (in this case, physicians or nurses) to TUM tasks is steep. Physicians in small practices already are hesitant to adopt EMRS due to expected productivity losses (ACR, 2003). Software and hardware purchase costs can be financed, but staff costs for TUM are harder to estimate or to anticipate. The need for ongoing mediation may be mistakenly considered as a one-time implementation task (e.g., training, system setup) or ongoing technical support (e.g., backup, upgrades) expenses. If so, the full range of opportunities to apply IT to improve healthcare outcomes may be missed. Facilitating the development and deployment of TUM resources to physician practices, across communities of practice such as professional associations or from government healthcare agencies, may be essential to achieve the substantial financial and healthcare outcomes anticipated from investments in EMRS.

Our papers examined EMRS in two national settings – the United States and Canada. Our cases did not provide evidence of national differences. However, it is reasonable to posit that regulatory and funding differences at the national level might influence the feasibility and effectiveness of TUM in industries such as healthcare. Since TUM activities require financial resources, differences in national funding approaches might facilitate or constrain TUM within organizations. Comparative cases in different national settings will highlight issues and opportunities.
