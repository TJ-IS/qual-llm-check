# Reconceptualizing rhetorical practices in organizations: The impact of social media on internal communications

Jimmy Huang <sup>a,</sup>\*, Joa˜o Baptista <sup>a</sup>, Robert D. Galliers <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

<sup>b</sup> Bentley University, 175 Forest Street, Waltham, MA 02452, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 23 May 2012 Received in revised form 10 September 2012 Accepted 4 November 2012 Available online 11 November 2012

Keywords: Rhetorical practices Intra-organizational communication Social media Interpretive Multiple case study research

## A B S T R A C T

While intranets have become a central information hub for employees in different parts of an organization, they have also played a key role as a rhetorical tool for senior managers. With the advent of social media, this is increasingly so. How such technologies as these are incorporated into organizations’ ‘rhetorical practices’ is an important, yet under-researched topic. To explore this research agenda, we examine the effects of social media on established and emerging flows of rhetorical practices in organizations, focusing in particular on the expanding, and in some cases switching, roles played by senior management and employees. We conceptualize organizational rhetorical practices as the combination of strategic intent, message and media, and discuss the interplay between rhetors and their audience. Adopting an interpretive, multiple case study approach, we study the use of social media in three multi-national organizations in the telecommunications industry. Our findings reveal that social media enable and facilitate the shaping of organizational rhetorical practices by (i) adding multivocality; (ii) increasing reach and richness in communication, and (iii) enabling simultaneous consumption and co-production of rhetorical content.

## 1. Introduction

Recent technological advances in intranet systems have shifted organizational communication from conventional channels, such as printed media, face-to-face interactions and email to such company wide web-based platforms as these [7,38]. This shift has recently been accelerated by the advent of social media which are defined as ‘‘a group of Internet-based applications that build on the ideological and technological foundations of Web 2.0, and that allow the creation and exchange of User Generated Content’’ [33,61]. While many organizations may only partially and selectively incorporate certain aspects of the array of applications and features available, the growing incorporation of social media in organizations has led to a further significant reshaping of intraorganizational communication [21,25,45]. While the intranet still plays a major role as a centrally managed repository of ‘official’ information, various forms of social media, such as blogging and wikis, have been introduced to allow employees to engage in conversations across functions, regions and hierarchical levels [39]. Two issues that emerge from this new trend are clear. First, when incorporating social media into an organization’s communication, some adjustment in its usage is often required to fulfill the organization’s communication needs and to comply with its governance principles [6]. Such adjustments can potentially provide a rich setting to extend our understanding of how social media are used in different contexts. Second, social media stimulate engagement, participation and exchange of information [33,53] in a manner that can challenge established central control of internal communication channels [14], thereby providing another fertile research opportunity. Balmer [3] describes such shifts as these by contrasting univocality with multivocality [4,64]. Balmer [3] refers to univocality as the control of what is often a single source of voice that is legitimized by an organization to present the organization and avoid ambiguity in the communicated messages and their intended meanings. Multivocality suggests the fostering of a communication culture within which alternative and multiple views can be freely voiced and contested. Even though embracing multivocality through social media is not the only way to enhance the strategic values of internal communication, multivocality, facilitated by social media, is believed to be more beneficial than univocality in stimulating employees engagement and facilitating cross-functional innovation by providing a means by which different ideas, viewpoints and concerns are freely expressed, effectively exchanged, consulted and consolidated [3,64]. One of the most important questions for senior management raised by Balmer [3] is whether they should retain control by pursuing univocality or loosen their grip on discourse by embracing social media and multivocality.

Cheney et al. [11] highlight the importance of research in this general topic area and argue that the tension between univocality and multivocality has had an impact on various aspects of organizational communication. They see this impact as being particularly apparent in the rhetorical practices in organizations. To establish the conceptual grounding for our research, we incorporate two of the prior accounts [11,48] and define rhetorical practices as ‘‘embodied, materially mediated arrays of human activity’’ [48, p. 2] of using persuasive language for the purposes of negotiating, obtaining and sustaining support and consensus [11]. Rhetorical practices represent a key aspect of management and leadership in any organization [11]. Notwithstanding the growing importance of rhetorical practices and associated communication technologies – in this case social media – for organizations, Huang and Galliers [27] suggest that our understanding of this phenomenon is still lacking and call for more research in this arena. The rationale underpinning the examination of organizational rhetorical practices and social media in conjunction is twofold. First, the potential of social media to revolutionize the very basis of communication has rapidly become a reality [22,33,39]. Without taking into account the influence of social media, we run the risk of under-theorizing their influence on organizational rhetorical practices. Second, while the need for adjustment when adopting social media in organizational contexts has been recognized [6], how such adjustments may facilitate rhetorical practices, and how rhetorical practices may influence the use made of social media, are yet to be explored. The current research is therefore an attempt to improve our understanding of the influence of social media on rhetorical practices within organizational contexts.

The structure of the paper is as follows. We first conceptualize rhetorical practices in the context of organizational communication. With this as background, we review the use of social media in corporate intranets. Having explained the methods underpinning our research, we provide empirical details of the three cases depicting the dynamic interplay amongst the various conceptual components of rhetorical practices in the organizations studied. We then compare and contrast our findings with the current literature to establish our theoretical contribution – in particular a reconceptualization of existing models of rhetoric with a view to incorporating the effects of social media on internal communications in organizations. We conclude by identifying theoretical and practical implications of our research.

## 2. Conceptual foundations

Rhetoric represents ‘‘the humanistic tradition for the study of persuasion’’ [11, p. 79]. While rhetoric is common to all types of communication, in an organizational context, rhetoric is used with specific intent, often to negotiate, generate and reinforce consensus in situations of uncertainty and emerging possibilities [23,50,51,56]. Organizational rhetorical practices are not merely the mechanism that helps to establish and maintain consensus in times of uncertainty and ambiguity [12,50,62], they also symbolize the ‘‘logics of human relations’’ [11], serving a constitutive function in shaping institutional change [19,56]. With these underlying theoretical concepts in mind, it should be noted that, while rhetoric and rhetoric practices are essentially a distinctive form of communication, not all communications are deployed for rhetorical purposes [12]. This distinction serves as a crucial conceptual boundary in the way in which we frame our research and establish our theoretical foundation.

For rhetorical practices to be persuasive, several distinctive yet interrelated components are required: (i) the rhetor; (ii) the rhetor’s strategic intent; (iii) the message; (iv) the media deployed, and (v) the intended audiences [11,12,15,23,31,50,59]. Fig. 1 groups three of these elements: (i) strategic intent, (ii) message and (iii) media, to represent the key conceptual components of ‘‘rhetorical resource’’ [31]. Rhetorical resource ‘‘serves the interests and actions of multiple constituents within ambiguous contexts’ [31, p. 240]. This is because, once rhetorics are displayed within the public domain (e.g., through meetings, newsletters, strategy plan and/or intranet), they become available for all organizational members to draw on with a view to making sense of ambiguity and to achieving consensus. While strategic intent reflects why the rhetor initiates the rhetoric, the message represents what the rhetor wants to communicate. The media is an indication of how organizational rhetorical practices are carried out. We use the term ‘media’ in an encompassing sense to include all types of communication channels because multiple types of media are often available and are used by actors, in combination, for effective communication [33].

In order to convince their audiences, rhetors develop messages to convey intent, which is often strategic in nature. The type of message will depend on the intended purpose [11], as illustrated by a study by Vaara and Tienari [59], where the CEO’s rhetorical intent was to overcome resistance during the restructuring of his organization by emphasizing that redundancies were for the greater and long-term well-being of the company. As suggested by Hirsch [26] and Alvesson and Ka¨rreman [1], even though content and strategic intent matter greatly, the selection and deployment of media to promote the rhetoric can significantly impact the outcome. The result is reflected in audiences’ cognitive and/or behavioral changes, or in many cases, their resistance to change [19]. It should be noted, however, that not all organizational rhetorical practices come equipped with an adequate feedback mechanism [10]. The dotted line in Fig. 1 is included to indicate the different possible ways for rhetors to receive feedback–feedback that can be made available via certain media to certain audiences. Feedback is represented using a dotted line because it is not always available in the context of organizational rhetorical practices. Drawing on the existing literature, we further conceptualize these elements of organizational rhetorical practices below.

A rhetor is an institutionally authorized speaker who represents the voice of an organization or part thereof [12,15]. In understanding rhetoric in organizations, while it is important to recognize who the rhetors are, it is also necessary to take into account who and what they represent. According to Crable [12, p. 119], ‘‘organizational affiliation is not merely an attribute or characteristic of rhetors; it is the sine qua non of rhetor identification.’’ Typically, the number of rhetors is kept limited to avoid disrupting an organization’s univocality [11]; in particular, when dealing with external audiences, such as in branding [24] and crisis management [2]. Organizations limit the number of rhetors to enable senior management to retain control of communication and avoid alternative voices challenging and undermining authority [10,51]. However, by limiting the number of voices, organizations hinder the exchange of different perspectives [50] and wider representation of diverse interests, especially in the context of innovation and change [23,41,50] and empowerment [14].

As Balmer [3] and Cheney et al. [11] argue, the tension between univocality and multivocality can be a trade-off for many organizations. This is because the two represent an ‘either-or’ decision to which management has to commit. Maintaining both is traditionally viewed as not being viable because, once organizations allow multiple voices to be heard, they immediately lose control over the dominant discourse within the organization. However, ultimately, the flow and variety of communication is influenced by the institutional norms and culture of the organization in which they are embedded [5,31,62]. While this point is raised by Balmer [3] and Cheney et al. [11], they do not account for the effects resulting from the alternative approaches which management might consider in managing the trade-off. For instance, can organizations simultaneously benefit from univocality and multivocality? If so, what are the factors and mechanisms which allow organizations to do so? Our study addresses both of these questions.

A message with strategic intent refers to a rhetor’s representation of a rhetorical situation by which (s)he aims to persuade an audience. According to Crable [12, p. 120], ‘‘rhetors who actually represent purely their own views may be extinct, if they ever existed. Instead, rhetors speak for, or represent, certain—sometimes multiple, overlapping, or complementary—‘organized interests’.’’ One of the key functions of the message is to achieve consensus by providing the intended audience with a ‘‘transcendental big picture’’ or a ‘‘simplified and understandable official view’’ to guide the way by which they can make sense of circumstances and take action as a result [50]. Hence, what underpins the message construction process is not just the strategic intent of the rhetor and the selection of suitable media, but also the cultural assumptions and institutionalized norms on which he/she draws. As noted by Cheney et al. [11], rhetoric ‘‘reproduces and reinforces the cultural assumption on which it is based’’.

Sillince [50] suggests that different messages, and in many cases their effective blending and ordering, are required to leverage different strategic intents. Cheney et al. [11] categorize strategic intent in four areas: (i) responding to the contingencies/ uncertainties that the organization is encountering; (ii) anticipating future situations and emphasizing the need to address them (e.g., [59]); (iii) changing or modifying the audiences’ ‘framing’ to make sense of what is going on (e.g., [32]), and (iv) shaping and sustaining an organization’s identity (e.g., [52]). Due to the intertwining of the message and the strategic intent embedded in it, each cannot be examined in isolation.

Media is the vehicle by which a message is delivered to certain audiences, and through which feedback may be provided back to the rhetor. Different media have the potential to be more or less useful in particular rhetorical situations [11]. The selection of suitable media depends on the content of the message and the rhetor’s strategic intent, as well as on the relationship and extent to which there is shared understanding between rhetor and intended audience [59]. Specifically, the rhetor needs to take into account the media’s ability to reach target audiences, and the degree of richness that the media can provide. According to Evans and Wurster [20], reach refers to the number of potential audiences to be targeted at any one point in time, while the level of richness is viewed as three interrelated elements, namely: (i) the ability to accommodate the bandwidth of the message; (ii) the ability to customize the message, and (iii) the level of interactivity that can be mobilized during the communication. They propose that the relationship between reach and richness is a trade-off that the rhetor has to consider. In other words, when the message is developed for broader reach, its richness may be compromised, and vice versa.

Even though conventional channels, such as via paper, email, and face-to-face communication, remain important for communicating in organizations, intranets have gradually been adding to such channels as these. Intranets are often used for strategic purposes – to reinforce key corporate values; policies; strategy and culture, for example – through text; sound; images and video, as well as through page layouts; designs; branding, and navigations [7]. Intranets also enable wider reach across organizations and facilitate knowledge sharing and the building of communities of practice (e.g., [8,54,60]). They can in some cases, however, be developed with insufficient capacity to permit customization and facilitate interactivity. They can also lead to unintended consequences [46], such as the creation of ‘‘electronic fences’’ – barriers to communication in other words [42]. Organizations have recently started to incorporate social media as additional services on their intranets, capitalizing on the participative and interactive nature of these new tools [9,34]. One of the opportunities sought by organizations is to use social media to increase reach, without compromising on richness, as had been the case with traditional media.

Audiences and their feedback is one of the key challenges in managing internal communication in organizations because of the complex, inconsistent and potentially conflicting nature of feedback. As noted by Crable [12, p. 118], ‘‘just as rhetors’ realities are inseparable from their organizational affiliation, the same may be said of their audiences: Any ‘one’ audience member may have several or many organizational identifications which, while sometimes overlapping or conflicting in interests, all help define that individual’’. Organizational rhetorical practices may therefore have contradictory effects, such as in fostering behavioral and cognitive change [51], while at the same time encouraging resistance to change and dissent [19,56].

The role of feedback depends on the media used to carry the rhetorical message. For instance, such media as corporate reports and mission statements do not have an intrinsic feedback mechanism. Indeed, it is often the case that organizations do not provide formalized feedback mechanisms for their audiences. Cheney and McMillan [10, pp. 98–99] comment on the legitimacy of informal responses by suggesting that ‘‘the rhetorical predicament of organizational members who rely largely on informal messages to respond to the formal pronouncements of a hierarchical organization: because their efforts are not authorized by the organization, the members lack organizational legitimacy in their attempts to persuade.’’

Feedback is valuable for rhetors because they can sense how effective their rhetoric has been, but at the same time it is difficult to manage and can at times generate unintended consequences and potentially undermine the strategic intent of the message. Ultimately it can destabilize institutionalized beliefs by allowing alternative voices to compete for domination with the official view [31,56]. Hence, the selection and design of the feedback mechanism represents two choices commonly available to a rhetor [57]. The first choice is to create the rhetoric for the audiences’ passive consumption. To do so, it is crucial for the rhetor to insert control hoping that the audiences will understand and interpret the rhetoric as intended. The second choice is a more participative approach than the first one by engaging audiences in real debate and the co-construction of the rhetoric. Often, the choice made by the rhetor also reflects an organization’s rhetorical culture [6] and its institutionalized norms [5], which are there to be used as references but more importantly to be contested [31].

While prior studies review the effects of social media in organizations, there is as yet little understanding concerning its effects on internal communication, specifically on its effects on the ability of senior executives to persuade and manage staff. We contend that existing conceptualizations of rhetorical practices in organizations do not fully capture the dynamic role that social media can play in organizational communication, nor – more specifically – the role of feedback in this context. Summarizing, the literature suggests that organizations face three key trade-offs when deciding how to enact rhetorical practices by using different and often mixed channels; they need to consider a choice between (i) ‘univocality’ and ‘multivocality’; (ii) ‘reach’ and ‘richness’, and (iii) controlled ‘consumption’ and more participative modes of rhetoric ‘development’. However, some literature also suggests that the use of social media offers the possibility to break with these trade-offs, allowing for greater reach and richness, while being both a passive consumer and a contributor. While we are interested in exploring how the incorporation of social media in an organization’s intranet influences different components of rhetorical practices, individually and collectively, we also seek to learn how social media can potentially alter the decisions made about the trade-offs identified above. With the overarching aim of exploring ‘how the incorporation of social media in an organization’s intranet influences the different components of organizational rhetorical practices’ in mind, the following section depicts our methodological design and considerations to unravel this question.

## 3. Research method

This particular study is part of an on-going research initiative aimed at examining the development, usage and governance of intranets in large multi-national organizations in high velocity industries [17,18], such as financial services and telecommunications [5–7]. It focuses on three organizations in the telecommunications industry, and explores the impacts of social media on the rhetorical practices in these organizations.

## 3.1. Research design

We adopted an interpretive, multiple case study approach because of its strengths in exploring research phenomena that are relational in their construction and subjective in their sensemaking [61]. Our aim was to enrich the existing theoretical landscape of organizational rhetorical practices by incorporating the effects of social media into our inquiry. With this objective in mind, the set of conceptual components, critically reviewed and synthesized in Fig. 1, served as the ‘‘sensitizing device’’ [35] to frame our research, guide our data collection, and facilitate its analysis and interpretation [44].

The three organizations were selected given their extensive use of digital media as a platform for internal communication and collaboration. Although all three organizations used social media to some extent, there were distinctive features regarding their use in each organization. While one case organization allowed open participation through blogs, wikis and other social media tools, the other two organizations restricted publishing rights and maintained central control. Such variations not only permit us to challenge the taken-for-granted assumptions about how social media might be used in an organizational context, but also enable a comparison of different approaches in the adoption and incorporation of social media, and of the impacts arising from its use on each organization’s rhetorical practices.

## 3.2. Data collection and analysis

Data collection was divided into two distinctive yet connected phases. The first consisted of exploring the functionalities, general usage and governance of the intranet in the three organizations. The initial emphasis was placed on understanding the structure, rationale and control that each organization used to manage its intranet environment, and aimed at establishing an overall understanding of the context of each case organization with a specific emphasis on its intranet usage. As a result of the data collected from the initial phase, we were able to narrow down our foci onto: (i) organizational rhetorical practices, instead of general usage of intranet, and (ii) the impacts of the social media being used. These newly revised foci were helpful to assist us in framing our research in a more focused manner. Specifically, the diverse landscape of organizational rhetorical practices characterized by each case forced us to identify common conceptual elements across all cases, so that meaningful comparison amongst them could be generated. For instance, different roles enacted by organizational members engaged in the rhetorical practices, intents behind their engagements and approaches that they deployed to actualize their intents were identified as some of these key conceptual elements. These conceptual elements formed the basis for us to examine and make sense of the impacts of social media that we have already observed. Also, these conceptual elements were useful pointers in informing us as to what further empirical evidence would be required in our later phase of data collection. The iterative process, in particular between our revised foci, data collection and emerging findings [16,44], also enabled us to refine our conceptual framework.

Based on the refined conceptual framework, we examined, in the second phase, the flows and landscape of organizational rhetorical practices within each case organization. Specifically, we investigated the ways in which the use of social media were organized, encouraged – and in one case, implicitly discouraged – governed and embedded into the day-to-day functioning of the organizations, thereby shaping and reshaping the social reality of rhetorical practices. To do so, we emphasized the articulation of individuals’ insights and experiences toward the use of social media, their direct and indirect involvements in organizational rhetorical practices and the changes arising from their social media usage. The focus of the second phase was to collect sufficient empirical evidence to depict the emerging conceptual framework and use this framework to interpret, in a highly iterative manner, the data we had collected [44]. During the second phase, we conducted a total of 30 semi-structured interviews with different stakeholders in the three organizations, including a sample of employees who were users of the intranet and social media tools, as well as managers. Appendix 1 provides a list of the roles and positions of the people interviewed in each organization.

Each interview normally lasted between 1 and 2 h, and consisted of two parts. Initially, discussion was fairly open, and dealt with the evolution of, and services available for, internal communication. This was followed by a second, more structured part, which focused on key themes related to organizational rhetorical practices, as conceptualized in Fig. 1. While the first part of the interview was geared toward capturing insights related to intranet usage and the trajectory of change, the second aimed to encourage interviewees to reflect on their personal experiences and observations about the impacts of social media on organizational communication and organizational rhetorical practices. Screenshots, such as webpages and blog posts from each organization’s intranet, were collected. For each organization, an average of approximately 100 screenshots was collected. Moreover, documentation, including governance and strategy documents and steering group meeting minutes, were reviewed and incorporated into the analysis. These disparate sources of data helped us to form a picture of the rhetorical practices in each organization. We paid particular attention to any changes in control following the introduction of social media. Research notes were also taken, incorporating on-site observation and informal discussions. A summary of each day’s fieldwork was produced for the purpose of documenting the research effort and generating additional insights from each site visit.

Given the relational nature of the phenomena under study, we continuously iterated between the data we collected, the emerging findings and relevant literature [16,43]. The conceptual components outlined in Fig. 1 were used to analyze the data with the help of analytical techniques such as visual mapping [40] and narrative analysis [28]. Recall that our aim was not to interpret and decode particular discourses, but to attempt to bring meaning to the processes and contexts that enabled more, or less, effective rhetorical practices in each of the organizations. We examined each case individually, and then conducted a cross-case analysis by using Fig. 1 as the conceptual guideline. We placed emphasis on exploring how each organization addresses the three trade-offs: the tension between ‘univocality’ and ‘multivocality’; the trade-off between ‘reach’ and ‘richness’, and the choice between controlled ‘consumption’ and engaged ‘production’ of rhetorical resources. By synthesizing the way in which these trade-offs are approached, we were able to identify and conceptualize the impacts of social media on organizational rhetorical practices.

## 4. Case findings

Using the conceptual framework as the ‘‘sensitizing device’’ [35] to interpret our data, the emphasis of this section is threefold. First, we elaborate how social media has been adopted and incorporated into each case organization’s intranet, as well as how it is used. Second, we identify and depict empirical insights related to each of the six components of Fig. 1, namely the rhetor; strategic intent; media; message; audience, and feedback. Third, we outline the influence of social media on the changing flows and dynamics of organizational rhetorical practices within each case. Each case is discussed in turn.

## 4.1. Case 1 – company DX

DX is a leading global telecommunications service company with a presence in 50 countries and employing over 245,000 employees worldwide. Since early 2010, the main strategic aim of the DX Group has been to consolidate its independent business units to achieve a more synergistic operation. The emphasis has been on integrating its fixed network (i.e., its traditional land line business) with its more recently acquired mobile networks, with a view to providing more integrated and innovative services and products. As part of this restructuring, a new brand for the group has been created and actively promoted.

In this context, DX’s intranet is perceived as the most important medium for internal communication, not just because of its wide reach globally, but also because of its capacity to broadcast a unified voice to communicate and reinforce corporate values and strategy. As expressed by one of the interviewees in the CEO office, who is responsible for managing internal communication (AV)<sup>1</sup>:

‘‘The intranet is the most important information channel for employees. There are around 145 k employees in Germany and 130 k of these have regular access to the intranet. 80–90 k people access the intranet on a daily basis.’’

One of the key features of DX’s intranet is called ‘StoryBox’, which provides information related to corporate strategy, branding and values. DX’s CEO has been one of the main contributors to the contents to ‘StoryBox’. One of the regional publishers responsible for one of the main sites and managing a team of 20 editors (YD) said:

‘‘StoryBox is an area where you can get the main information on presentations, guiding principles, etc. When there’s a new strategy, [name of CEO] talks about this in his video. This is very important – sometimes 70–80 k watch it. His video is every four weeks or ten times a year.’

The strategic intent of the intranet, as a way to promote corporate identity and enhance senior management rhetoric reach, is illustrated by one of the interviewees in Marketing responsible for corporate branding (CK):

‘‘There is a strategic project to bring branding and communications in-line across sites. There are some departments that take a bit longer to adapt to the new branding. We want a new tool that would enable us to make the switch more quickly.’

However, it is not just the message that needs to be constructed in a manner consistent with the strategic aims of the organization, the medium used to communicate the message is also designed and deployed for the intended purpose. The range of different subsites on the intranet – historically developed by different departments – is now seen as an issue that can potentially affect synergy in communication and in reinforcing DX’s corporate brand and values. Consolidating these different intranets is perceived to be critical in achieving a more unified view of the organization by the employees. As suggested by one of the communication managers (CH) interviewed:

‘‘The brand is consistent but we have different entities that you click to see different layouts, navigation and colours. We’re going through a big restructure to be more ‘One company’. At the moment we have different intranets but what we want is one intranet.’’

One of the main social media features on DX’s intranet is blogging. For example, employees can engage with the CEO via his blog and by entering or voting on questions that are answered in his regular video broadcast. However, a specialist team curates these interactions and only a small number of leaders, apart from the CEO and CFO, use the intranet to blog. Overall, the intranet, even with social media incorporated, does not reveal significant interactivity between executives (rhetors) and their audiences. In general, there is a perception that employees are not yet very comfortable with ‘‘putting their heads above the parapet’’ (according to SG – a content maintainer in HR). What is also evident, however, is the growing demand for interactivity by some. This was stressed by one of the interviewees in corporate communications (GF):

‘‘We have a community of young people who are making wider use of interactive tools to support communication between senior leaders and employees, but we’re still in the beginning stages of this. We tackle this through our print magazine where we choose topics that are relevant to employees. Most of the stuff on the intranet is one-way communication and includes interviews, coverage of events, etc. However, [name of CEO]’s blog is changing this and more people are using these services. Other senior executives, with the exception of the head of finance who’s active on the intranet, are not so visible on the intranet.’’

The lack of a formalized and established feedback mechanism in DX’s intranet reveals some choices made by the management team in terms of organizational rhetorical practices: it suggests a preference for keeping rhetoric under control by the central team, for instance. Despite the existence of social media tools, feedback is not enabled or used because the culture and norms neither encourage nor support such behavior. One of the users expressed this well:

‘‘Our intranet isn’t used for collaboration or dialogue so there’s little reflection. We have the ‘Direct to [name of CEO]’, but it doesn’t support dialogues. I think that if we have information on the intranet then we should be able to talk about it, and at the moment we can’t do this. I think we are on the right track, but we’ve only moved a few centimetres since we started.’

To summarize, it is clear that organizational rhetorical practices within DX have been dominated by a very small number of rhetors, primarily the CEO and CFO. Their strategic intent – an element of their rhetoric – was to create and promote brand consistency across different parts of DX. In other words, the rhetoric of ‘One Company’ was there for the passive consumption of attentive organizational members. The tools could technically support more active engagement but senior management had preferred not to seek active engagement with employees and the collective production of sense-making in regard to this initiative (i.e., univocality was preferred to multivocality). The selection and use of media reflected the fact that its social, media-enriched intranet was the channel by which DX could gain widest reach. By contrast, the interactive and participative features of social media were muted to ensure the preferred univocality. There is thus a degree of similarity between what has been highlighted in Fig. 1 and the landscape and dynamics of organizational rhetorical practices in DX. Rather than interpreting the similarity as the lack of impact of social media on organizational rhetorical practices, our account of DX indicates that it is the manner in which the tradeoffs are approached that can significantly tailor (in this case, inhibit) the potential of social media. Again, the approach taken by DX in managing the trade-offs is a reflection of the organizational culture and institutional norms that guide the social construction of its rhetorical practices.

## 4.2. Case 2 – company NX

NX is one of the largest manufacturers of mobile devices, with over 130,000 employees spread over 120 countries. Two of NX’s recent strategic foci are to sustain its product leadership with disruptive technology and to renew its structure, with a strong emphasis on speed, results and accountability. As its CEO stated, ‘‘NX is at a critical juncture, where significant change is necessary and inevitable in our journey forward’’. Building on the corporate mission of connecting people, NX’s organizational rhetorical practices, and its associated use of its intranet, is illustrative of this ethos. With an extensive coverage of subjects and functionalities offered, the intranet is designed with users in mind, rather than merely for top management to broadcast their views. As a Senior Communications Manager (ET) put it, the NX intranet ‘‘is the primary communication method, if we don’t consider email and phone. Phone, SMS and emails are probably the main ones, but for projects and general information, the intranet is the main method.’’

One of the distinctive features of NX’s intranet is the number of rhetors it affords. Many senior managers have adopted this platform, in particular through the use of blogs, to broadcast their messages to a wide audience. The role of rhetor is not just played by senior managers, however. There are a growing number of domain experts, project managers and community leaders who have been using blogs and wikis to position themselves as an active voice in the organization, across regions and functions. As stated by one of the users (a team leader), we have ‘‘a very active blogosphere . . . some teams are spread all over the world and use wikis to work collaboratively.’’

By examining screenshots (for anonymity they are omitted from this paper), it became evident that some of the senior managers have used their blogs to outline the rationale behind some of their decisions (for instance, to enter a new market in South America or the selection of a new venturing partner). Some of these blogs have received extensive comment from employees, ranging from the positive to the extremely negative. Some request clarification regarding, or even questioning, NX’s strategy moving forward. What is insightful is the amount of effort that the various bloggers spend in responding to comments and concerns. For example, a recent on-going discussion on the intranet has focused on organizational restructuring, with messages from senior management, project leaders and employees, all of whom are interested in voicing their concerns and suggestions. As one of the interviewees responsible for change management (MK) noted:

‘‘We have recently announced that we are totally restructuring the organization in January and we have set up a change blog. It’s very active with lots of people commenting on change.’

Employee comments have been used by senior management to refine their message thanks to continuous monitoring and their attempts to make sense of views expressed in the blogs and the user comments on the project website, thereby demonstrating their commitment to open communication and their appreciation of the feedback received. Thus, feedback was actively encouraged by senior managers, who made it ‘‘OK for employees to say what they think’’ (GJ), and who proactively sought comment and responded to messages: ‘‘managers respond back to comments. If questions are raised, they’re added to Q&A and managers reply back to people.’’

Creating and maintaining norms that promoted engagement seems to have played a significant role in encouraging employees to establish two-way communication in this organization. Interactivity is enabled by a sense of openness of communication that is unconstrained by hierarchy, as illustrated by the following quotes from two stakeholders responsible for managing the intranet platform (LA and IS respectively):

‘‘The intranet now supports two-way communication better than before because some leaders are now blogging and anyone can comment. If the CEO says something that people don’t like, people will make comments.’’

‘‘Another aspect of interactivity is the discussion forum. Users can post anonymously. For example, a colleague moving from Finland to the UK could ask about average salary in the UK. We have an etiquette document but I’m not sure where it is.’

Encouraging open and free participation with little or no intervention has become an important governance principle; one that has been gradually established and accepted by organizational members, many of whom are actively engaged in rhetorical practices. As one of the corporate communication team members (CV) commented:

‘‘In the last eighteen months, the intranet has become much more interactive. All news stories can be commented upon and rated. There’s no censoring at all. People are very happy to comment and have very candid comments, which is very good.’

Despite the high level of interactivity and openness observed in NX, many believe that they are still on a learning curve in nurturing organizational rhetorical practices that can more fully respond to the true essence of the company ethos of ‘connecting people’.

Following the top-down approach, there is a growing awareness of, and demands for, wider intranet use, as well as on-going development of an organizational culture that is even more conducive to horizontal and bottom-up communication. As stated by the Head of Collaborative and Virtual Workspace (PM):

‘‘There’s lots of good push information: the company-to-employee channel. We’re trying to push an employee-to-employee and an employee-to-company channel.’

Reflecting on the above, what seems to be of note about the NX case is that it has revealed certain pattern and dynamics of organizational rhetorical practices that have departed from those outlined in Fig. 1. Specifically, we see a highly vibrant landscape where social media has been incorporated extensively into NX’s organizational communication, and embedded in its day-to-day functioning. It is evident that the rhetors are not simply its top management. Instead, domain experts, project managers and community leaders are all encouraged to utilize the intranet by using the participative features of social media as a means of engaging and communicating. Despite the large number of rhetors, the messages that have been contested remained relatively focused, in particular concentrating on the reorganization. What seems to be distinctive in this case is not just the organization’s culture and institutional norms that encourage multivocality, but also its ability to manage the process of moving from univocality to multivocality. Furthermore, the richness of communication does not appear to have been traded-off in relation to the extent of reach afforded by the media being used. As a result, the rhetoric of ‘reorganization’ is not simply a matter for managers to broadcast in the hope of achieving buy-in. Rather, and because of the feedback enabled by social media, the engagement and contribution of organizational members into the rhetoric of reorganization is a reality.

## 4.3. Case 3 – company BX

BX is a large organization providing voice, data and networking services primarily in the UK, but also servicing customers across 170 countries. With close to 100,000 employees, its main strategic foci include improving customer service delivery; continued efforts to reduce costs, and investing for the future. By this BX means prioritizing long-term investments that emphasize sustainability, rather than short-term returns. Thus, and as noted by the CEO, one key strategic priority is that of ‘‘being a responsible and sustainable business leader’’.

The intranet plays a major role in company strategy as a way to save costs and drive organizational change, and is classified as the primary medium for communication. One of the key roles is to communicate corporate values such as ‘being straightforward’, in the words of the Intranet Manager. He stresses the importance of this corporate value to the development of the intranet by revealing that it has driven the development of the intranet in terms of navigation and content; it is, for example, featured in training manuals for all publishers. The following (MM) captures this view:

‘‘We see evidence of the corporate values in people’s actions; not just their words. For instance, people using the intranet are increasingly following BX’s value around being straightforward. We’ve cut out the long- winded, waffle-on articles. We’ve refined and reduced information down to key points. Blogging is quite illustrative as well. We don’t do any moderation. It’s a risky strategy, but people are professional and straightforward in putting their opinions forward.’

However, the most striking feature of BX’s intranet is how it is truly integrated and embedded into the organization’s day-to-day functioning. Many users have perceived BX’s intranet as more than just a channel for searching and exchanging information. Rather, it plays a much larger role for many employees. As one of the users put it:

‘‘I would say the intranet is THE primary information channel based on other people confirming that that’s the case in addition to my own experience as well. It’s alive and evolving. It’s the life-blood of the company.’’

Compared to the other two intranets studied, BX’s intranet is more service-oriented. While corporate news is visible, it takes up far less space. Instead, more space is dedicated to ‘Blog Central’, where User-Generated Content is featured. This suggests that the platform is more open – not exclusive to senior management – indeed, it gives privileged voice to employees. This support by senior management is illustrated by their active engagement in exchanges with employees through discussion forums, blog comments and regular on-line chats – a practice led by example by the CEO. BX, like NX, presents itself as illustrative of an organization where moving from univocality to multivocality has not negatively affected senior management’s rhetorics. Rather, data from employee surveys supplied by BX suggest that this move has made employees feel closer to senior management and more willing to trust them. One of the stakeholders (SH) responsible for a popular site on the intranet commented:

‘‘We have a fairly popular blogging platform. Anyone can share opinions. We have BX Views, which invites hot topics from employees on BX Today. Anyone can write in comments. Depending on the discussion, anyone, up to and including the CEO level, can get involved in providing views or add to the debate.’’

BX’s intranet has the widest reach of the three organizations in terms of penetration in the population of employees and level of embeddedness in organizational processes. It is also the richest in terms of the variety of ways in which social media can be used – a point made by one of the members (CM) of the intranet crossfunctional management team:

‘‘There are Q&As to put questions to executives online. There are things like blogs that senior people use for such purposes. There are also podcasts of people making presentations, etc.’’

The high level of engagement in using social media is also shown by one of the stakeholders interviewed, who referred to the fact that senior leaders actively participate in discussions online. He (RC) added:

‘‘Your Views is an online forum. It features a new topic every week related to BX Strategy, product or policy. A specific manager sponsors it. Sometimes this manager comes back and challenges or provides answers.’’

On-line participation and feedback has been a key element in the senior management communication strategy. They are acutely aware of the value of monitoring discussion and using feedback as part of their organizational rhetorical practices. This engagement is increasing as more senior managers spend increasing amounts of time blogging and commenting on posts. One of the senior leaders (PK) told us that:

‘‘Many executives have blogs within their Lines of Business sites. Following in [senior leader name]’s footsteps, executives schedule periodic on-line chats with employees . . . executives weigh in on on-line debates, blog posts and comments.’’

BX’s executives, starting with its CEO, have been actively seeking constructive dialog and debate via Q&A sites and on-line chats. However, it is not just senior managers who are changing the landscape of blogging, the change is more widely spread in the organization. ## 5. Discussion

We now review the data presented in the previous section using the concepts and theoretical background developed in Section 2. Our aim is to explore the role of social media in changing rhetorical practices in organizations. We have structuredthe discussion intotwo main parts. The first discusses changing flows of rhetorical practices by outlining differences observed in the three cases and by drawing conclusions in terms of the role of social media in establishing new ways of communicating and performing rhetorical roles in the organizations. The second discusses the effects and consequences of greater feedback on rhetorical practices in organizations.

## 5.1. Social media in new organizational rhetorical practices

Analysis across the three cases reveals different rhetorical modes of engagement with social media. Using the conceptual components presented in Fig. 1 as a basis for interpretation and comparison across the cases, we show that the changes brought about through the incorporation of social media are greater than simply adding an additional channel for an organization’s rhetorical practices. More fundamental changes are at work.

Adopting the conceptual basis developed in Section 2, we capture the rhetorical flows found in each of the three organizations in the three sub-figures of Fig. 2. Fig. 2.1 represents DX; Fig. 2.2 represents NX, and Fig. 2.3 represents BX. Fig. 2 as a whole displays the landscape and flows of rhetorical practices across the three organizations. We use a triangle to represent each organization’s hierarchical structure. We use a block arrow to symbolize the relationship between the rhetor and audiences, and a dashed arrow to represent feedback. The number, size and strength of these arrows indicate the dynamics of rhetoric practices in each case.

As can be seen from Fig. 2, flows of organizational rhetorical practices are least changed in DX. Indeed, when comparing Fig. 2.1 with Fig. 1, the organizational rhetorical practices of DX match the flows commonly portrayed by prior studies. While DX has adopted social media, it is still keeping central control of what is published on these channels. While there are some limited opportunities for employees to publish on these or any other digital channels, only a few can act as rhetors for specific strategic intents. The main purpose of social media in DX is to increase the reach of its messages, and not to increase richness, engagement or multivocality.

In the case of NX, as represented in Fig. 2.2, there are broader uses of social media. For example, NX uses blogs to facilitate open discussion of the plans for restructuring the organization – a highly sensitive issue. The discourse is not dominated by senior management, however; indeed, any employee can be a rhetor. We note that senior managers have adapted their communication practices to incorporate feedback and on-going engagement with employees. This has also enabled them to react more quickly to emergent issues by scanning comments and blog posts on a regular basis. This level of engagement and use of feedback is not seen in the DX case. Moreover, the co-existence of different rhetorical practices, and use of different social media (e.g., wikis and online forums), for different organizational tasks (e.g., managing geographically dispersed projects and running communities of practice), suggests the possibility for organizational rhetorical practices to take place horizontally, alongside the more typical topdown practices of DX.

The BX case, as illustrated in Fig. 2.3, goes even further. Here, we have a landscape of rhetorical practices in which rhetors from different organizational levels are encouraged and supported to cultivate vibrant exchanges. BX demonstrates the most open use of social media tools: it is not just the technology that affords the diversity of its organizational rhetorical practices, however. This diversity and fluidity is largely related to its organizational culture and the way by which its rhetorical practices have been governed.

Even though bottom-up organizational rhetorical practices are still incipient, a multi-directional rhetorical flow is emerging – something that is not, as yet at least, evident in the other two cases.

Notwithstanding the differences between the three cases, some commonalities of organizational rhetorical practices are evident. Fig. 3 provides a revised version of the model of rhetorical practices in organizations developed in Section 2, based on the findings from our three case studies.

Our findings show that the use of social media in organizations has the potential to increase the number of rhetors and feedback mechanisms. As a result, more voices and messages can be generated and there is no longer a clear distinction between rhetor and audience: indeed, any organizational actor has access to the same set of rhetorical resources and has the ability to act as a rhetor. We depict this change by adding bi-directional arrows to the two entities in our revised model. We also reflect the point that the distinction between rhetor and audience is no longer clear by including another rhetor to illustrate that any individual is now both a receiver and producer of organizational rhetoric. In addition to the traditional source of institutional power [56], new rhetors can gain support, credibility and legitimacy through their expertise and their ability to engage in, and interact with, various audiences (i.e., other rhetors). The power to set the dominant themes in organizations may be, thereby, no longer the privilege of senior executives alone [55]. Being active and engaging in discourses, as in BX, seems to be more effective in establishing reputation and convincing others of strategic intent.

Feedback also takes a much more predominant role and becomes part of the rhetorical resources available to rhetors in our revised model – much more so than that suggested in the literature we reviewed. Monitoring and managing feedback in a context of multivocality is one of the key factors in making rhetorical practices more or less successful in organizations where social media has been implemented and used with open communication in mind. Rhetors are aware of the effect of feedback and will incorporate this in their strategies and tools for managing rhetoric in their organization. For this reason, we added feedback in the model as one of the rhetorical resources available to rhetors. As noted, when social media are incorporated into a company’s intranet and used to increase participation, the level of feedback fosters transparency, interactivity and efficiency [45].

One of the key findings from the cross-case analysis that reinforces findings from previous studies is the importance of organizational context in shaping intranet and social media use [5]. Social media enables multi-directional rhetorical practices but the extent of participative behavior by employees is determined by the dominant norms and culture of the organization. Confidence and trust is a prerequisite, as in any virtual context – not just trust in the technology but in the intentions of management as well [29,37,48]. Individuals will tend to take on the role of a rhetor when there is a supportive organizational context in which active participation and free exchange of viewpoints is valued and encouraged [23,50].

## 5.2. Effects on internal communication

We have attempted to shed light on the effects of social media on the rhetorical practices of employees and senior managers in organizations. Our findings suggest that social media can gradually eliminate trade-offs inherent in traditional models of rhetorical practices in organizations that are based on central control (as depicted in Fig. 1). The use of social media allows for: (i) multiple voices as well as targeted communication (uni- and multivocality), as well as (ii) simultaneous wider reach and richness, and (iii) combined consumption and production of rhetorical content. This represents a major shift from existing models of rhetorical practices in organizations, as discussed further below.

The case of DX is where the effects of social media are less visible since univocality is still the main priority. Increasing reach is important for senior management but not at the expense of keeping central control of organizational rhetoric. Thus, not all employees could be rhetors, and the level of interactivity remains limited. DX’s organizational rhetorical practices are more concerned with controlling consumption, rather than engaging with audiences in the co-creation of strategy. In the case of NX, we saw the co-existence of univocality with multivocality. While the former reflects NX’s corporate agenda for driving reorganization, the latter is nurtured to reinforce this agenda. While the extent of reach was similar to that found in the DX case, NX displays a greater degree of richness in its social exchanges, in particular its emphasis on interactivity between the top-down and horizontal forms of organizational rhetorical practices.

Although NX’s rhetorical practices are more concerned with consumption, the organization is expanding the level of engagement between employees and senior management. This is, however, less for the purpose of co-creation and more as a means of achieving buy-in. The only organization showing significant success in overcoming the limitations associated with the three trade-offs described previously is BX. The BX case demonstrates that organizations can indeed move toward full exploitation of the properties of social media without compromising on any of the trade-offs. BX’s cultivation of a healthy dual relationship between univocality and multivocality as part of its organizational culture – by encouraging more rhetors to participate in organizational rhetoric – is illustrative of this point. This is somewhat akin to the notion of ‘prosumption’ [57], where production and consumption occur and prosper simultaneously.

However, responding to the three trade-offs has facilitated the change in landscape in BX’s organizational rhetorical practices. The two main changes in BX’s organizational rhetorical practices can be explained by using the notion of ‘fluidity’ [49], and ‘contextual ambidexterity’ [22]. As suggested by Schreyo¨gg and Sydow [49], the notion of fluidity characterizes one of the most distinctive qualities in an organization’s ability to facilitate continuous adjustment in its ways of organizing, with a view to effectively cope with increasing internal complexity and environmental turbulence. Drawing on their work, we apply the notion of fluidity to refer to an organization’s ability to regulate and accommodate the adjustments in, and changing flows of, rhetorical practices. Based on the three cases presented herein, it is clear that BX has demonstrated a much more pronounced ability to permit adjustments and changing flows in its organizational rhetorical practices than is the case with DX and NX. In other words, the BX case shows a higher level of fluidity than do the other two. The notion of contextual ambidexterity refers to an organization’s ability to concurrently address demands that have previously been viewed as trade-offs [22]. As argued by Gibson and Birkinshaw [22], such a unique ability can derive from an organization’s context–typically ‘supportive’, and promoting the values of ‘trust’, ‘stretch’ and ‘discipline’. These contextual characteristics and the ambidexterity facilitated by them echo what we have observed in the case of BX. This is specifically so in the way by which social media are used, and how its usage nurtures its organizational rhetorical practices. By comparison, such supporting contextual characteristics are less visible in the case of either DX or NX.

The level of fluidity is an important parameter when considering rhetorical strategies that enable rhetors to deal with uncertainties, ambiguities andcontingencies [11,13]. This is particularly important for organizations that operate in highly dynamic environments [17,18]. This is the case because, as noted by Schreyo¨gg and Sydow [49, p. 1252], ‘‘organizational capabilities themselves have to become fluid to enable organizations to continuously create new (combinations of) resources’’. However, when fluidity in organizational rhetorical practices is under-developed, it can potentially lead to a ‘‘lock-in’’ effect, which narrows the scope of alternative views and thus limits action [50]. This status quo changes when the tools (social media) and culture (norms and expectations) work together to encourage new behavior. What is apparent, particularly in the case of NX and BX, is that the right environment is important to create and sustain fluidity in organizational rhetorical practices by nurturing and developing an open and engaging environment in which organizational members, regardless of role, have confidence to participate.

As noted by Cheney et al. [11], a rhetorical context creates its own dynamics and turbulence. Based on the three trade-offs identified from our synthesis of previous studies, it is clear that the causes of such turbulence are indeed complex, contradictory and multi-faceted. While organizations might decide to concentrate on just one such trade-off – as in the case of DX – others exhibit greater ambidexterity, committing themselves to addressing such trade-offs simultaneously – as in the case of NX and BX. As argued by Tushman and O’Riley [58] and Gibson and Birkinshaw [22], the ability to be and remain ambidextrous is strategically valuable. Furthermore, ambidexterity in rhetorical practices can enable organizations to benefit from greater fluidity by nurturing feedback in communication, Our study thus contributes a vision of how organizations might benefit from social media by changing their rhetorical practices and becoming more ambidextrous, as in the BX case. Fluidity requires an appropriate culture and governance that, once legitimized, enables ambidextrous behavior in the rhetorical practices of organizations.

## 6. Conclusion and implications

This study contributes to the IS literature a conceptual foundation to explain the potential impact of social media in the rhetorical practices of organizations. It builds on the intellectual tradition of rhetoric in organizations, and analyzes in detail the adjusted use of social media in changing the flows and dynamics of organizational rhetorical practices. By investigating the three sets of trade-offs inherent in organizational rhetorical practices, we depict how, through addressing these trade-offs, each of the cases manifests its own approach in shaping its distinctive meanings, usage and landscape with respect to social media, and its associated organizational rhetorical practices. The collective construction of social media facilitates and is enabled by the organizational rhetorical practices that it affords. By highlighting how the mutual influences between rhetorical practice and social media unfold, we have presented an account of organizational rhetorical practices that are dynamic, interactive and relational. This conceptualization is further expanded by relating our findings to concepts of ‘fluidity’ and ‘ambidexterity’.

Dynamic and relational, organizational rhetorical practices offer discursive insights and emphasizes linguistic considerations not available through variance theories [23]. The revised conception presented in Fig. 3 draws analytical attention to the dynamics and possible shifts of organizational rhetorical practices, and suggests a theoretical perspective on the plural roles (i.e., both rhetor and audience) that a social actor can potentially play through the use of social media. In this regard, further inquiry into organizational rhetorical practices can enhance our understanding of power distribution and redistribution in digital communication [7]. The revised framework helps explain why actors can gain and sustain their persuasive power through capitalizing, simultaneously, on the reach and richness of social media. Furthermore, the framework suggests that embracing the interactive and participative features of social media can increase the opportunity for different actors to contribute to, and draw on, available rhetorical resources [30,63]. Additional studies will be needed to identify triggers and circumstances that contribute to the growing – or diminishing – dominance of those rhetorical resources, to assess still further the implications for fluidity and ambidexterity in organizational rhetorical practices.

The methodological approach used here focused on unpacking the influence of social media on the changing flows of organizational rhetorical practices. To do so, we reviewed and synthesized prior studies that have explored social media and organizational rhetorical practices to create a theoretical framework that we then operationalized to analyze the three cases. It became evident during the analysis that the framework derived from the literature was unable sufficiently to capture the interactive and dynamic nature of organizational rhetorical practices in all three cases. A reconceptualization was therefore required, and undertaken, based on the empirical evidence presented. We trust that this reconceptualization will prove to be valuable in future research with a view to assessing how different organizational rhetorical practices emerge and are used by different stakeholders before, during, and after IT implementations.

In addition to these theoretical contributions, the conclusions drawn provide insight into the practices of rhetoric in organizations. While intranets and social media are increasingly becoming the central information hub for employees in different parts of an organization, they have also played a key role as a rhetorical tool for senior managers. How organizations actually manage the incorporation of such technologies as these in their rhetorical practices is important. This is particularly so as organizational players learn more about communication flows and the cocreation of strategic initiatives. The three sets of trade-offs, and the elaboration of how they have been addressed by each of the case organizations considered in this study, provide a starting point for identifying key challenges that organizations may face, and the decisions that they may need to consider, when adopting new media in their communication environment. Two of the three cases documented herein demonstrate the point that organizations do not have to be constrained by these trade-offs. Rather, with the nurturing of an open rhetorical culture, and institutional norms that embrace the emancipating characteristics of social media, organizations can actually by-pass these trade-offs – and, more importantly, benefit from them.
