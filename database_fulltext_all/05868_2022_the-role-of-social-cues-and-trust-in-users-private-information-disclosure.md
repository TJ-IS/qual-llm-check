# THE ROLE OF SOCIAL CUES AND TRUST IN USERS’ PRIVATE INFORMATION DISCLOSURE<sup>1</sup>

Lior Zalmanson Coller School of Management, Tel Aviv University, Ramat Tel Aviv, ISRAEL {zalmanso@tau.ac.il}

Gal Oestreicher-Singer Coller School of Management, Tel Aviv University, Ramat Tel Aviv, ISRAEL {galos@tau.ac.il}

Yael Ecker Department of Social and Legal Psychology, Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz, Mainz, GERMANY {yaeecker@uni-mainz.de}

Across different domains, websites are incorporating social media features, rendering themselves interactive and community-oriented. This study suggests that these “friendly” websites may indirectly encourage users to disclose private information. To investigate this possibility, we carried out online experiments utilizing a YouTube-like video-browsing platform. This platform provides a realistic and controlled environment in which to study users’ behaviors and perceptions during their first encounter with a website. We show that the presence of social cues on a website (e.g., an environment in which users “like” or rate website content) indirectly affects users’ likelihood of disclosing private information to that website (such as full name, address, and birthdate) by enhancing users’ “social perceptions” of the website (i.e., their perceptions that the website is a place where they can socialize with others). We further show that the presence of social cues is more likely to enhance users’ social perceptions when users are primed to perceive the website as trustworthy, as opposed to untrustworthy (through the presentation of trust cues such as data protection disclaimers). Moreover, we rule out users’ privacy concerns as an alternative mechanism influencing the relationship between social cues and information disclosure. We ground our observations in goal systems and trust theories. Our insights may be beneficial both for managers and for policy makers who seek to safeguard users’ privacy.

Keywords: Privacy, information disclosure, trust, social cues, social features

## Introduction

Social interactions are increasingly becoming integral to the internet usage experience, with more and more online domains—even those that are not inherently socially oriented— adopting social features. Websites that once offered only basic informational or monetary transactions now give users opportunities to “like” or rate content and to express their views, as well as to share their favorite content, read other people’s views, and form social relationships with other users (see, Stephen & Toubia, 2010). Indeed, users accessing the internet for almost any purpose have come to expect some form of social exchange or connection to other users in a similar situation to theirs (Dewan & Ramaprasad, 2014).

It is not difficult to understand why people welcome the opportunity to feel a sense of community instead of a solitary experience of browsing and searching for information.

Human beings are, by nature, highly social creatures; they grow and thrive through social interaction (Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Reis et al., 2000). And websites, too, benefit when they provide their users with opportunities to fulfill their social needs: Social features encourage users to produce content (e.g., through contributions to on-site conversations) or to organize existing content in a manner that enhances website usage for themselves and for others and strengthens the website community. Further, compared with passive consumers, users who engage in social participatory actions show greater loyalty and commitment to the website, in addition to higher willingness to pay for the site’s services (Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Burtch et al., 2015; Dewan & Ramaprasad, 2014; Oestreicher-Singer & Zalmanson, 2013).

However, the growing popularity of social features on online platforms may trigger additional, unexpected usage behaviors that warrant consideration. In particular, this research explores the idea that the same need that drives people to seek out social interactions on a website and to contribute to it may also cause people to disclose personal information to the website—even if the user has no prior familiarity with the website.

Indeed, research shows that the extent to which users, especially adolescents, voluntarily disclose information online is continually on the rise (Pew Research Center, 2014; Shin & Kang, 2015). Such behavior could be a cause for concern in cases in which users do not explicitly consent to disclosing information, or even realize they are doing so, and thus have limited understanding of the repercussions of their actions. One obvious repercussion is the risk of data breaching, an overwhelmingly common phenomenon (Baker et al., 2011). Yet even in cases in which breaches do not occur, the consensus is that the accumulation of personal data in the hands of private companies is a growing source of concern, owing to risks of exploitation and other concerns for users’ personal safety.

Surprisingly, the rise in information disclosure is happening in parallel to an increase in users’ privacy concerns (Angst & Agarwal, 2009; Pew Research Center, 2014). In other words, users are reportedly worried about the security of their personal data, but they still choose to reveal such data online on many occasions. This discrepancy, which has raised interest in the IS field, is referred to as the privacy paradox (Acquisti et al., 2015; Adjerid et al., 2018; Pavlou, 2011). One explanation provided for this paradox draws from the idea that, when deciding whether to reveal personal details, users are often in a state of uncertainty and feel that they possess insufficient information to make the decision (Acquisti et al., 2015). Such uncertainty (in addition to the risk that any personal information might be misused) is amplified when the user is not very familiar with the website being used. Accordingly, a user deciding whether to reveal information to such a website might compensate for uncertainty by searching for environmental cues indicating whether information disclosure would be desirable (Acquisti et al., 2015; John et al., 2011). If the website offers such cues, intentionally or unintentionally, users may be encouraged to reveal personal information that might jeopardize them in the future. We suggest that social features provided on an unfamiliar website can constitute cues that guide the data disclosure decision. Specifically, we suggest that such features trigger users’ perceptions that the website is a social environment, that is, a place for making social connections. We then address the question: Does users’ exposure to an onsite social environment induce their private information disclosure to the website upon their first encounter?

A key premise of our research is that when users perceive a website as a place for making social connections—owing to the presence of social features—they become more likely to behave as they would in a real-life social situation. As we will elaborate in what follows, disclosure of private information is integral to behavior in new social situations in which people are trying to make a connection. Accordingly, if the website asks the user for personal information, exposure to social features (i.e., cues indicating the presence of a social environment) may have a carryover effect of encouraging the user to reveal that information. We note that we are not focused on the act of revealing information as part of the interaction with social features (for example, within a review or a comment). Rather, we focus on users’ likelihood of responding to a website’s direct requests for personal information (including identifying information, such as name or birthdate). Moreover, we propose that these effects are triggered by the existence of social features, regardless of whether the user actually uses them.

Further, we expect trust to play a moderating role in this context. Specifically, as elaborated in the next section, we suggest that the extent to which an individual perceives a website as a place for making social connections—and, consequently, becomes motivated to disclose personal information—is likely to depend on the extent to which he or she perceives the website as generally trustworthy (as opposed to untrustworthy).

We tested these ideas in a website called VideoBook, a YouTube-like experimental platform specially designed for studying user behavior. VideoBook provides the opportunity to study users’ behavior and perceptions in a realistic environment and under lab conditions. In using a designated research platform, we were able to construct an encounter with a new website and to avoid the potentially confounding influence of prior knowledge that users might possess regarding the privacy protection standards of existing commercial websites.

In the experiment, we manipulated the social features of the video-sharing platform, meaning that some users were exposed to an onsite social environment that included features such as a “like” button, a comment field, and others, whereas other users were not. We also exposed users to different cues designed to elicit the perception that the website is either trustworthy or untrustworthy. After users browsed the platform for some time, we measured the degree to which they were willing to disclose personal information (such as full name, address, and birth date) as well as the extent to which users perceived the website as trustworthy. We show that the disclosure of private information can be explained by the user’s perception, elicited by exposure to the social environment, that the website is a place where he or she can socialize. For convenience, in what follows we refer to this perception as a user’s “social perception(s)” with regard to the website. Notably, we show that the association between exposure to a social environment, social perceptions, and consequent information disclosure is not conditional on users’ active engagement with the website’s social features; it is sufficient for these features simply to be present on the website. We further confirm our expectation that the association between social cues on a website and users’ social perceptions is moderated by the presentation of trust cues. Moreover, we rule out the user’s general level of privacy concerns as an alternative mechanism for the relationship between social features and information disclosure.

## Theoretical Development

## Social Goals and Personal Disclosure

While online social interaction is a relatively recent phenomenon, decades of behavioral research have explored the underlying needs and motivations of people in social interactions. Research in this vein builds on the premise that people have a basic need to belong, which leads to a general motivation “to form and maintain interpersonal bonds” (Baumeister & Leary, 1995, p. 497).

The need to belong causes people to formulate social goals, such as “establishing a friendship” or “being popular.” To achieve these goals, individuals engage in behavioral schemes that encourage social bonding, and personal disclosure and emotional openness are cornerstones of these behavioral schemes (Erikson, 1963). In other words, without a certain level of openness and disclosure, the experience of intimacy and closeness that fuels human connection is impossible.

Consequently, individuals commonly disclose personal information about themselves to others when pursuing social goals such as popularity and acceptance. For instance, research shows that the need for popularity predicts disclosure of feelings on social networks (Utz et al., 2012).

Notably, research from the domain of goal systems theory suggests that an individual might engage in this form of goaloriented information disclosure even when this behavior cannot be expected to lead to the formation of a social bond. According to this theory, humans have a mental network that connects goals and means (Shah et al., 2002). When a certain means is used regularly to pursue a goal, a hierarchical mental connection between the two is formed. Consequently, when the mental representation of a certain goal is activated—for example, when an opportunity for achieving the goal arises— it causes an automatic activation of that goal’s means.

Consider, for instance, an individual, John, who believes that, in order to achieve the goal of finding a good job, it is necessary to dress well (i.e., dressing well is a means of achieving that goal). Thus, John’s mental representation of the goal “finding a good job” is connected to the mental representation of its means, “dressing well.” Goal systems theory posits that, when John’s goal of finding his next job is activated (e.g., when he plans to meet a prospective employer), the means of dressing well will also be mentally activated (i.e., John will feel motivated to dress well). Importantly, the mental activation of the goal’s means can have behavioral consequences, that is, actual goal-pursuit behavior (Bargh & Gollwitzer, 1994); in our example, this means that John will put on his best-looking suit. A recent study provides evidence for the latter idea with regard to the activation of social goals: Ozimek and Förster (2017) manipulated the activation of a specific social goal—the goal to make competitive social comparisons—and found that participants in whom that goal was activated subsequently showed an increase in Facebook consumption (whereas participants in whom the goal was not activated did not show a comparable increase). If we assume that individuals use Facebook as a means of carrying out competitive social comparisons (i.e., Facebook usage is a means in the service of the goal), then the researchers’ findings suggest that activation of that competitive social goal activated the corresponding means—Facebook consumption— resulting in an increase in Facebook usage.

Because the activation of means through goals is automatic, it is possible that the activation of a goal might activate a means that is unsuitable for the pursuit of that goal under the circumstances at hand (Bargh et al., 2008). For example, many individuals perceive competitive behavior as a means of achieving goals that relate to success. Thus, people in whom such goals are activated may engage in competitive behavior—even in situations in which competitive behavior is inappropriate, and cooperation is called for (Bargh et al., 2001). Returning to John’s story, one might assume that John perceives “dressing well” as a means of pursuing the goal of “finding a good job” because dressing well can make a good impression on a prospective employer. Goal systems theory proposes that if John’s goal of finding a good job is activated when no employer is physically present—for example, if John is participating in a telephone interview in which no one will see him—he may nonetheless find himself wearing his best attire.

Herein, we suggest that a similar logic might apply in the context of information disclosure online. Specifically, activating the goal to form interpersonal connections might automatically activate self-disclosure behavior as its means, given that such behavior is an established means of drawing in potential connections—and such activation might occur even if the individual is aware that he or she is not likely to make a new, bona fide interpersonal connection by disclosing information, as in the case of a website asking users for their personal data. In the following subsection, we discuss how the presence of social cues on a website might elicit a user’s perception that the website is an environment where he or she can make social connections. And once the user’s social perceptions of the website are activated, these perceptions will trigger the user’s social goals, which will in turn lead to information disclosure.

## The Influence of Social Cues on Personal Disclosure Online

The salience of a person’s social goals is influenced by characteristics of his or her current situation (Fitzsimons & Bargh 2003; Omarzu, 2000). In particular, exposure to different types of “social cues” can activate people’s social goals (Morrison et al., 2007). According to the discussion above, such goal activation subsequently activates the means that serve these goals, resulting in behavioral consequences. Indeed, one study showed that participants who were exposed to word primes such as “support” and “friendly”—cues that presumably activated the social goal of cooperative behavior—behaved in a more cooperative manner in a resource-dilemma task compared with participants who were not exposed to such primes (Bargh et al., 2001).

Online disclosure might be especially sensitive to social cues because many online domains can be defined as “mixed environments”—involving both transactional and social elements (Heyman & Ariely, 2004). Because people apply different rules to functional transactions than they do to social transactions, they might behave differently on a website when induced to perceive it as social as opposed to purely utilitarian. Specifically, they might engage in cooperative behaviors, such as self-disclosure and emotional openness, without expecting functional gains.

Another factor that might heighten individuals’ sensitivity to social cues when making decisions regarding disclosure of personal information online is the fact that such decisions are usually made under conditions of uncertainty (Acquisti & Grossklags, 2012; Acquisti et al., 2015; Adjerid et al., 2018). Users typically lack knowledge about the information that companies have about them, the manner in which that information is used, and the possible consequences of that use (Park, 2013). Such uncertainty encourages people to make heuristic decisions, that is, to use mental shortcuts that do not require complete information (Evans, 2008, Adjerid et al., 2018). Although heuristics are efficient and helpful in uncertain situations, decision-makers who rely on them may become susceptible to irrelevant external influences. Indeed, research shows that users are often influenced by seemingly irrelevant situational cues when making decisions about their personal information (Acquisti et al., 2015; John et al., 2011). Researchers have suggested that this observation can explain the privacy paradox, discussed above (Adjerid et al., 2018; Norberg et al., 2007)—the discrepancy between users’ privacy intentions and disclosure behavior. The current paper builds on this idea by proposing that online self-disclosure can be automatically influenced by social perception, eliciting behavior that deviates from rational decision-making.

## The Role of Trust in the Relationship Between Social Cues, Social Perceptions, and Information Disclosure

Trust is formally defined as the belief that someone or something will deliver an expected value (Pavlou, 2003). In relations involving uncertainty, interdependence, and fear of opportunism, trust is a crucial factor that can enable individuals to perceive the other side in a positive manner, despite such concerns (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). In particular, it is widely established in psychological literature that trustworthiness plays a major role in interpretations of others social behavior and in the subsequent social decision-making that is determined by such interpretations (Cosmides & Toobey, 2000; Winston et al., 2002). Both behavioraleconomic and neurological approaches show that actual social behaviors, particularly in relation to cooperation, are largely determined by the favorable social interpretations that results from high trust (Adolphs &Tusche, 2017; Declerck et al., 2013). For instance, Wout and Sanfey (2008) found that a rapid implicit assessment of facial trustworthiness determined participants’ subjective impressions of a partner’s social intentions and affected their decisions on whether to cooperate with that partner. This literature suggests that, in our context, the role of trust in the relation between exposure to social features and subsequent social (cooperative) behavior is in shaping the social perceptions elicited by those social features. Once social perceptions have been formed—with high trust—cooperative behavior is more likely.

These ideas align with the broader intuition that social cues are unlikely to always elicit social perceptions (i.e., perceptions that a viable opportunity to socialize has arisen). For instance, although a smile or eye contact can constitute social cues in personal communication, they are unlikely to increase our social perceptions of a person we think is false and intends to take advantage of us. In other words, a person’s interpretation of the meaning of social cues to which he is exposed strongly depends on the extent to which he trusts the entity (person, site, etc.) that generated those cues.

Our theory regarding the role of trust in the relationship between exposure to social cues and consequent social perceptions is further supported by findings from the field of relationship marketing, which focuses on interactivity between consumers and firms. This literature shows that trust is a required antecedent for the formation of a long-lasting relationship between the “trustee” and the “trustor” (Mayer & Gavin, 1995; Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Accordingly, when the trustee puts forward social cues, the trustor’s likelihood of interpreting these cues as reliable—that is, concluding that the trustee is offering an opportunity to establish a true connection—is likely to depend on the extent to which the trustor perceives the trustee as trustworthy (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Moreover, given that Mayer and Gavin’s integrative model defines trust as the willingness of the trustor to be vulnerable, this process may culminate in heightened willingness of the trustor to “let his guard down” and be exposed (Morgan & Hunt, 1994).

Summing up the above, we suggest that a website user who perceives his environment as trustworthy (as opposed to untrustworthy) is more likely, when presented with social cues, to perceive the website as a place where he can establish social connections, and thus to have his social goals activated. This activation, in turn, is expected to lead to information disclosure behavior as the means for attaining social connections. Conversely, a person who has low trust in her environment is less likely to have her social goals activated by the presence of social cues.

## Research Hypotheses

Our discussion above converges to the idea that users exposure to social cues on a website can increase their tendency for information disclosure. At the same time, this effect is not a direct one. That is, it is not enough for social cues to be present to affect a user’s behavior. Rather, as detailed in the previous section, the social cues must first elicit the user’s social perceptions of the website. Once the user perceives the website as a place where she can establish social connections, her goal to form such connections becomes activated, leading to information revelation. Thus, the effect of social cues on information revelation is mediated by social perceptions.

Formally, we hypothesize:

H1. Exposure to social cues on a website indirectly (positively) affects users’ level of personal information disclosure through its effect on users’ social perceptions of the website.

Further, as elaborated in the previous subsection, we propose that the extent to which a user perceives a website as a place where she can make social connections is likely to be affected by her level of trust in that website.

Thus, we hypothesize:

H2. The mediated effect of social cues on information disclosure is moderated by trust. Specifically, when users perceive the website as more (vs. less) trustworthy, their exposure to social cues is more likely to enhance their social perceptions of the website.

Figure 1 portrays the proposed model. We note that the capacity to test this hypothesis is limited by the possibility that trust perceptions are influenced by the presence of social cues—that is, the very presence of social cues on a website might strengthen (or weaken) users’ trust in that website. We suggest that, given the ubiquity of social features on contemporary websites, users are unlikely to perceive such features as a distinctive characteristic that signals a website’s trustworthiness. Nevertheless, this concern is addressed and tested as part of our empirical analysis, as elaborated in the following sections.

## Experimental Design and Measurements

## Experimental Context: The VideoBook Website

Our goal is to test how social and trust cues interact to affect users’ social perceptions of a website and their consequent information disclosure behavior. To accomplish this, it is not sufficient to analyze data collected from active real-world websites, because such sites are characterized by established social features with which users have past familiarity. For this reason, we chose to empirically examine our hypotheses in a controlled experimental setting: a YouTube-like video site named VideoBook, described in past work by Zalmanson and Oestreicher-Singer (2015). The website provides users with sessions in which they can browse various videos, which are displayed on a built-in video player (see Figure 2).

![](/api/attachments/QHHH9E94/fulltext/images/d53edc2e6a708e07dc497845a8e57ac1140b58e0b7ec9fd0b47d162a603bf407.jpg)

Videos in VideoBook were taken from the Vimeo.com website. Vimeo.com is one of the largest video content websites in the world and specializes in artistic, high-quality videos. By using Vimeo.com as a source (as opposed to YouTube. com, for example), we avoided the risk of encountering an unplanned and uncontrolled distraction or interruption in the form of an online ad by a third-party website. In order to make sure that the quality of users’ experience would not be influenced by the specific videos they chose to watch, the videos to which users were exposed were limited to highresolution nature videos. Specifically, we used a random sample of videos in the category of “nature” on Vimeo with durations of 120–150 seconds, ensuring that the content would generally be of high quality (for a description of a similar procedure, see Zalmanson & Oestreicher-Singer, 2015). We argue that, compared to music videos or narrativeled video clips, the nature genre is probably less strongly associated with cultural differences and diverse personal tastes. Thus, our selection of videos enabled us to reduce variance resulting from users’ personal video preferences.

In this experiment, we tested our hypotheses by manipulating (1) VideoBook users’ exposure to social cues on the website—such that some used a version of VideoBook in which they were able to rate, like, and comment on the videos, as well as to see others’ comments (see Figure 2), whereas others used a version of VideoBook in which these social aspects were not present (see Figure 3); and (2) the extent to which users were likely to perceive VideoBook as trustworthy, by exposing some users to a trust-inducing message and others to a distrust-inducing message. We subsequently measured users’ tendency for information disclosure, their social perceptions of VideoBook (i.e., the extent to which they perceived VideoBook as a website where they can make social connections), and their privacy perceptions (in addition to other information).

## Participants and Design

We recruited 412 participants (57.8% women; ${ \cal M } _ { a g e } \ = \ $ \~34.47)<sup>2</sup>) through the Prolific website, a crowdsourcing platform initiated by researchers at Cambridge and Oxford, UK.<sup>3</sup> Prolific is considered to be an equal alternative to the more popular Amazon Mechanical Turk, with the advantage being that participants on Prolific are more naive in terms of basic experimental tasks and more diverse in their geography (Peer et al., 2017). All participants were registered as U.S. residents. Participants completed the studies over the Internet and were paid 1.3 GBP for their participation. We randomly assigned participants into four conditions in a 2 (social cues: present vs. absent) × 2 ((manipulated) trust: high vs. low) between-subjects design.

## Procedure

All participants (in all scenarios) first viewed a screen presenting the following introduction to VideoBook: “You are about to participate in a platform for sharing creative video work. This video-sharing platform is called ‘VideoBook’.”

Next, participants were exposed to the trust manipulation. Our trust manipulations were selected on the basis of a pretest in which we identified prompts that were effective in establishing or impairing users’ trust. The full details of the pre-test can be found in Appendix A.

Specifically, participants in the high-trust condition saw the following message, which was designed to elicit trust (Figure 4): “We care about your privacy! We have been using advanced data protection technology to keep our users’ data private, in accordance with the EU’s most recent general data protection regulations (GDPR). Our site has been confirmed by our security team as highly secured.”

Participants in the low-trust condition saw the following message, designed to elicit distrust: “Warning! Our first priority is to provide our users a safe environment to share their content. However, various problems have occurred recently. If you encounter any problems or have concerns on this issue, please contact us.” The message was displayed both in English and in Russian (Figure 5).<sup>4</sup> Moreover, while browsing the video website, users in the low-trust condition encountered eight incidents of website glitches (Figure 6). Glitches are generally defined in the Oxford dictionary as “a sudden, usually temporary malfunction or irregularity of equipment.” Our reasoning behind the incorporation of glitches is based on past research in IS showing that similar flaws can lower trust in a website (Everard & Galletta, 2006; Everard & McCoy, 2010). The glitches on the website occurred momentarily (one second each time) and only scrambled the titles of the videos. They did not interfere with users’ ability to continue viewing the video or to engage with social features. The first glitch occurred after 60 seconds, and subsequent glitches occurred at 40-second intervals. These glitches enabled us to demonstrate a consistently unsafe website that may elicit low trust.

After being exposed to the trust/distrust-inducing message, participants proceeded to a video-watching session. The duration of this session was 6 minutes, during which each participant could interact with the videos on the website (Figures 2 and 3). The participant was able to switch at any time to a new video by choosing between four other video options that appeared to the right of the main video, or by pressing a “pick random video” button that appeared under the main video. Participants were also able to pause the video using a button located under the video.

In addition, during the video-watching session, participants in the social-cues-present condition observed the following social features (Figure 2): a current average star rating (randomly assigned a number on the scale between three and five), a like/dislike button, the number of users watching the same video (randomly assigned between 12 and 75), and a comment field, where they could read comments left by others (randomly chosen from the original video; the comments were limited to four at most in order to avoid overcrowding the layout of VideoBook).<sup>5</sup> Moreover, after the user had browsed the site for 20 seconds, a popup appeared with a suggestion: “Your vote matters! We just wanted to let you know that the videos you ‘like’ will be nominated for the ‘Community Favorites Award’!” Our use of this popup was modeled after research equating social cues with communal language and framing users’ social participation as helping the community (Nechushtai & Zalmanson, 2018). It is important to note that this pop-up did not require the users to “like” the content or replaced the “like” button. It was simply used to direct users’ attention to the communal importance of the “like” feature. The message only included an “OK” button, which closes the pop up. Participants in the social-cues-present condition could engage with the website’s features as follows: rating the video on a scale of one to five stars, marking the video as liked or disliked by clicking on the like/dislike button, and adding a new comment in a free-text box.

## Measurements

All the measures below are based on questions posed to participants in a popup survey window (Figure 7). The survey was made to look as if presented by VideoBook, so the participants would answer as if they were responding to the website itself, rather than to researchers conducting a study. The survey included the title “Help us improve” alongside VideoBook’s logo. Moreover, it was embedded in the VideoBook website itself rather than in a separate window or website, to simulate a website’s request for additional information.

Trust Manipulation Check. To measure participants’ perceptions of trust towards VideoBook, we used three items based on Gefen’s (2002) work on trust measurement in online contexts. Our three items focused on VideoBook’s general trustworthiness, integrity, and benevolence on a scale of 1–7 (1 = not at all; 7 = very much). The items are described in full in Appendix B. We calculated each participant’s trust score as the average of his or her responses to these three items (Cronbach’s á = 0.895).

Social Cues Manipulation Check. To verify that participants in the social-cues-present condition were aware of the social features present on VideoBook and perceived them as features characterizing a social network, we included the following item, which participants rated on a scale of 1–7 (1 = not at all; 7 = very much): “VideoBook is a social network.”

Willingness to Disclose Personal Information. To measure participants’ willingness to disclose personal information, VideoBook’s popup survey (described above) presented a list of seven personal questions (Figure 7), preceded by the following instructions: “Help us improve: We would like to know a few personal details about you to improve our analysis.” As noted above, the popup was integral to the website and prominently featured VideoBook’s logo, to clarify that the questions were being issued by the website itself. To avoid the possibility that participants would feel compelled to answer in order to receive payment, the popup provided the following clarification: “Some of the questions below are voluntary. You can decline to answer by leaving the text box empty and checking the “I’d rather not say” option. Declining to answer these questions will NOT affect your payment.” Participants were then asked to state their age, gender, main occupation, city of residence, zip code, birth date, and full name. The first two questions, age and gender, were mandatory; the remaining five were voluntary. For the latter questions, we recorded only whether a response was given, and not the content of the response.<sup>6</sup> Our measure of personal disclosure for each participant was the number of answers he or she provided for the last three questions (zip code, birth date, and full name). The choice of this measure was based on Sweeney’s (2000) findings that these three items of information can give away the complete identity of a person in the United States.

Perceptions of VideoBook as a Website that Facilitates Social Connections with Others (“Social Perceptions”). We measured participants’ social perceptions by adapting relevant measures from Gefen and Straub’s (2003) scale of social presence, which focuses on users’ sense of human contact, and from Preece’s (2001) scale of sociability, which focuses on the ability to socialize with other users. Participants rated their agreement with each of 7 items by rating a 7-point scale (the items are described in full in Appendix B). We calculated each participant’s social perceptions score as the average of his or her responses to these items (Cronbach’s á = 0.933).

General Attitude Toward VideoBook. To measure participants’ general attitudes toward VideoBook, we asked them to respond to the following questions by rating a 7-point scale: “How enjoyable was your experience?” (1 = not enjoyable at all; 7 = very enjoyable); “How likely are you to recommend VideoBook to your friends?” (1 = not likely at all; 7 = very likely); “What is your general impression of VideoBook?” (1 = very low; 7 = very high). We calculated each participant’s general attitude score as the average of his or responses to the three items (Cronbach’s á = 0.921).

General Privacy Concerns. To measure participants’ general privacy concerns (that is, their general, non-website-specific, beliefs about privacy on the internet), we adapted Malhotra et al.’s (2004) IUIPC (Internet Users’ Information Privacy Concerns) questionnaire into 10 questions about awareness of privacy practices, collection of data online, unauthorized secondary use, improper access, and so on. Participants rated their agreement with each item by rating a 7-point scale. We calculated each participant’s privacy concerns score as the average of his or her responses to these 10 items (Cronbach’s á = 0.854). This measurement later serves to rule out alternative mechanisms.

Attention Check. To verify that participants were indeed paying attention to the content of the survey items, we included two attention checks throughout the experiment. In one of the checks the participants responded to the following item: “Please mark the answer ‘3’ here.” We also implemented an instructional manipulation check (IMC) to further increase the statistical power of the experiment. IMC has previously been used as a strict measure of participant attentiveness and as an attention filter (Kittur et al., 2008; Oppenheimer et al., 2009). In our version of Oppenheimer et al., the IMC question was “How likely are you to share your opinions on a new website?” and the options ranged from unlikely to very likely. In a longer block of text that appeared before this question, participants were told that the aim of the following question was to test their attention, and that they would indicate having read this text by skipping the question. Thus, if a participant answered the question, he or she was disqualified as “not paying close attention.” In our empirical estimation we verified the results by running the analysis twice. This attention test was found to be challenging, and only 60% of participants passed it (i.e., skipped the question). We have therefore decided to include in our main results those who passed the lenient test, even if they failed the strict IMC test. However, we provide an additional analysis that includes only those users who passed both the strict IMC and the more lenient attention check in our robustness tests.

Additional Measures. Participants reported their online savviness (“How many hours per day do you spend online?” (responses ranged from 1 hour to 12 hours)) and their video savviness (“How many hours per day do you spend watching online videos?” (1 hour–12 hours)). Then, participants reported whether they had experienced technical problems, whether they had any comments about the study, and whether they had participated in a similar study in the past.

## Results

## Data

To enhance the validity of our results, we took strict measures to ensure the quality of our participants’ input. Thus, of the 412 participants recruited for this study, we excluded users for the following reasons (some participants were excluded for multiple reasons): users who were idle for more than twothirds of their time on the website and were suspected to be “away from keyboard”; participants who watched two segments or fewer of the 2-minute videos or more than 14 segments of 2-minute videos over the duration of the 6-minute session, as this behavior hinted that users were not focused on content consumption; participants who indicated that they had participated in a similar study in the past. Ultimately, 384 participants entered the analysis (58.1% women; $M _ { a g e } =$ \~34.1).

## Manipulation and Independence Checks: Trust and Social Cues

A t-test on perceptions of trust found a significant effect of trust condition $( t ( 1 , 3 8 2 ) = - 4 . 9 8 5 , p < . 0 1 )$ , such that participants in the high-trust condition indeed expressed greater trust in VideoBook (M = 5.09, SD = 1.18) compared with participants in the low-trust condition (M = 4.44, SD = 1.39).

For our social cues manipulation check, a t-test found that participants in the social-cues-present condition were significantly more likely than participants in the social-cues-absent condition to perceive VideoBook as a social network (4.52 vs. 3.37, respectively, on the Likert scale; t(1, 382) = -7.081, p < .001).

Finally, we tested whether trust perceptions were indeed independent of the presentation of social cues, as assumed in the framing in our hypotheses. That is, we checked whether the presence of social cues made users perceive the website as more or less trustworthy. We used a t-test to compare trust perceptions between users in the social-cues-present and the social-cues-absent conditions. We did not observe a significant difference between those who were exposed to social cues $( M { = } 4 . 8 7 , S D { = } 1 . 2 6 )$ and those who were not (M = 4.70, $S D = 1 . 4 0 ; t ( 1 , 3 8 2 ) = - 1 . 3 1 5 , p = . 1 8 9 )$

## Hypothesis Testing: Moderated Mediation

To test our hypotheses, we needed to use a unified model that would allow us to test the association between the presence of social cues and information disclosure as an effect mediated by social perceptions, and to test the moderating role of trust in the social cues–social perceptions association (see Figure 1). Thus, for our analyses, we used the PROCESS macro for mediation moderation analysis (Hayes, 2012; Model 7, Figure 8) We entered social cues and trust as exogenous variables, social perceptions as a mediator, and information disclosure as the dependent variable. Confidence intervals of the indirect effects were established using bootstrapping.

We begin with H1, which addresses the indirect path of the effect of social cues condition on information disclosure, as mediated by users’ level of social perceptions of the website. Indeed, we found an indirect effect of the social cues condition on information disclosure via social perceptions (b = 0.13, 95% CI [0.029, 0.26]), supporting H1.

We note that the path model revealed no significant direct effect of the social cues condition on information disclosure (b = -0.2, p = .08). This finding supports our conjecture that, in order to enhance information disclosure, social cues must elicit users’ social perceptions of the website (which may trigger their social goals); these perceptions, in turn, lead to information disclosure.

Next, we turn to H2. We observed a significant interaction effect of trust condition and social cues condition on social perceptions (b = 0.68, 95% CI [0.16, 1.20], se = 0.27, p = .01). In other words, for participants who were exposed to the high-trust cues, there was a stronger association between the exposure to social cues and participants’ social perceptions of the website. Thus, H2 is supported as well.

Overall, the indirect effect of social cues on information disclosure was larger among participants in the high-trust condition (estimate = .26, 95% CI [0.15, 0.39]) than among participants in the low-trust condition (estimate = .13, 95% CI [0.05, 0.23]). The full results of the model can be found in Appendix C.

## Alternative Explanations and Robustness Checks

We carried out several additional analyses to rule out possible alternative explanations and to more precisely characterize the nature of the phenomena observed. First, we compared the social-cues conditions and the trust conditions in terms of participants’ individual characteristics (i.e., age, gender, online/video savviness—the only personal information we were authorized to collect; see footnote 5) to ensure that bias in these characteristics did not drive the results. Second, we examined whether the exposure to social cues or trust cues might have led to differences in terms of users’ enjoyment and general attitudes toward the website. Third, we reran our main analysis taking into consideration only users who passed the strict attention check. Fourth, we tested whether social cues may have affected users’ general privacy concerns, which might serve as a possible alternative explanation. Fifth, we tested participants’ social activity on the website to examine whether, as predicted, exposure to social features drives the effects observed or whether the effects are a product of actual engagement with those features. Last, we examined whether our mechanism requires the presence of other users social activity.

## Testing Random Assignment: Individual Characteristics

Table 1 presents participants’ individual characteristics by experimental condition. Though the distribution of participants across conditions was random, there might nevertheless have been differences between experimental conditions that biased our results. To refute this possibility, we conducted 2 (social cues: present vs. absent) × 2 (trust: high vs. low) ANOVAs on age, online savviness, and video savviness. (Given that participants indicated their age range rather than their exact age, we estimated each participant’s exact age as the middle point of his or her age range.) There were no significant differences in participants’ age, online or video savviness between experimental conditions (ps > 0.5). Finally, a logistic regression analysis found no significant differences in the ratios of men and women between experimental conditions. We can therefore conclude that the results we observed are not driven by demographic differences between the groups.

## The Effects of Social Cues and Trust Cues on General Attitude

Another potential explanation for the influence of social cues on user perceptions and behavior is that the presence of social cues or trust cues on a website might change or even improve the website experience in general. Such an effect might have caused the websites in the different conditions to differ in their inherent quality to their users.

Social Cues: We used a t-test to compare between participants in the social-cues-present and social-cues-absent conditions in terms of their general attitudes toward VideoBook (measured as their ratings of their general impression, interest level, etc.; see “Measurements” subsection above). We found no difference between the two conditions (all items p > .29).

Trust Cues: We used a t-test to compare between participants in the low-trust and high-trust conditions. In this case we observed that participants in the high-trust condition perceived the website significantly more favorably (M = 4.5) than did participants in the low-trust condition $( M = 3 . 9 , p < . 0 1 )$ This result is not entirely surprising, given that the specific low-trust cues we used were likely to have elicited a suboptimal user experience. In light of this finding, we reran our full model, controlling for general attitude (i.e., with general attitude as a covariate).

We found that while general attitude did have a direct effect on information disclosure $( b = 0 . 0 9 , p < . 0 5 )$ , our hypotheses were still supported. Namely, we observed an indirect effect of exposure to social cues on information disclosure via social perceptions (b = 0.05, 95% CI [0.002, 0.13]), supporting H1. We also observed a significant interaction effect of trust condition and social cues condition on social perceptions (b = 0.45, 95% CI [0.04, 0.85], se = 0.20, p = .03), such that the indirect effect of social cues on information disclosure was larger among participants in the high-trust condition (estimate = .14, 95% CI [0.02, 0.26]) than among participants in the low-trust condition (estimate = .09, 95% CI [0.01, 0.17]), supporting H2. The full model results can be found in Appendix D.

## Strict Attention Checks

As noted in the “Measurements” section of the paper, we ran two attention checks: a lenient check (in which participants were asked to “Please mark the answer ‘3’ here”), and a strict IMC (described in detail in the “Measurements” section). Our main results were based on a sample that included all the participants who passed the lenient attention check, regardless of their success in the strict attention check. To increase the robustness of our results, we reran the main analysis with only those participants who passed the strict attention check. In this case, the sample comprised only 233 participants (57.9% women; average age \~35.4), substantially fewer than in our main analysis. We note, however, that similar (and even lower) success rates on IMCs have been documented in previous experiments involving populations recruited from online subject pools (such as Prolific) (Hauser & Schwartz, 2015).

We reran our moderated mediation model and were able to replicate each one of our results. Specifically, we found an indirect effect of exposure to social cues on information disclosure via social perceptions $( b = 0 . 1 3 , 9 5 \% \mathrm { C I } [ 0 . 0 2 , 0 . 2 9 ] )$ supporting H1; we found no significant direct effect of exposure to social cues on information disclosure $( b = - 0 . 0 6 , p$ = .688); We found a significant interaction effect of trust condition and social cues condition on social perceptions (b = 0.80, 95% CI [0.11, 1.48], se = 0.34, p = .02), supporting H2.

## Testing the Relationship Between Social Cues and General Privacy Concerns

In general, it seems relatively unlikely that users’ general privacy concerns can explain the relationship between exposure to privacy cues and consequent information disclosure behavior. First, we would not necessarily expect a single encounter with a website to influence users’ overall beliefs regarding privacy online. Moreover, even if such an influence did take place, users’ information disclosure behavior might not necessarily align with their privacy concerns—in accordance with the privacy paradox, discussed in the introduction (Adjerid et al., 2018; Norberg et al., 2007; Smith et al. 2011). Yet, it is nevertheless possible that these assumptions do not hold in our context. For example, it might be the case that when a user is exposed to a vibrant and positive social online environment, the “friendliness” and social encouragement she experiences might carry over to a more carefree attitude when the user is faced with a survey on general privacy preferences. In this case, privacy concerns might serve as an alternative mechanism mediating the relationship between exposure to social cues and information disclosure behavior.

To investigate this possibility, we first compared the two social cues conditions in terms of participants’ privacy concerns. We observed no differences between the two conditions. The average privacy concerns score for users in the social-cues-present condition was 5.93, compared with 5.95 for users in the social-cues-absent condition $( p = . 8 6 5 )$ .

Next, we reran our path model, now adding privacy concerns as a possible mediator alongside social perceptions. Again, we used the PROCESS macro for moderated mediation analysis (Hayes, 2012; Model 7; Figure 9). We entered social cues and trust as exogenous variables, social perceptions as our first mediator, privacy concerns as a second mediator, and information disclosure as the dependent variable. Confidence intervals of the indirect effects were established using bootstrapping.

Analyzing the new moderated mediation model, we did not find a significant interaction effect of trust and social cues on privacy concerns $( b = - . 2 2 , p = . 1 7 )$ . Moreover, we did not observe a mediation effect via privacy concerns, for either high or low levels of trust (high-trust condition: estimate = .04, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.12]; low-trust condition: estimate = -.03, 95% CI [-0.11, 0.04]). Last, we note that adding privacy concerns as a mediator did not impact the significance of the social perceptions path. Thus, privacy concerns do not play a significant mediating role in the relation between exposure to social cues and information disclosure. Full results of the model can be found in Appendix C.

## Testing the Effect of Participants’ Usage of Social Features

In the social-cues-present condition, participants were able to rate, like, and comment freely on the videos, and some indeed used these features: On average, participants in this condition rated videos 1.12 times during the video session, liked/ disliked videos 1.14 times, and commented on the videos 0.39 times. To rule out the possibility that the observed associations were driven by participants’ “activities” on the website, that is, their usage of the social features, and not simply by their exposure to the website’s social environment, we took the overall number of user activities in the website as a covariate in our regressions. When looking at participants who were exposed to the social features (and thus could choose to perform social activities), while controlling for their social perceptions, privacy concerns, and trust condition, we found that the overall number of activities that users carried out using the website’s social features did not affect their information disclosure $( b = 0 . 0 1 4 , p = . 5 4 )$ . Moreover, the effect of the overall number of user activities on social perceptions (controlling for privacy concerns and the trust condition) was not significant $( b = 0 . 0 4 , p = . 1 0 )$ . Taking these results together, we can conclude that the effects are not driven by the usage of social features but rather by the exposure to social cues.

## Presence of Social Features Versus Presence of Social Activity

Our social cues consisted of actual features such as a like button, a rating field, and a comment field, as well as information indicating other users’ social behavior on the VideoBook website. This information included the number of people “currently viewing,” the number of past likes and rates, as well as actual comments. An important question, therefore, is whether the effects we observed were attributable to the mere availability of the social features, or whether they were also dependent on participants’ capacity to observe that other users had engaged with those features. To address this question, we ran an experiment (n = 376, 56% women, $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } =$ 34.6), where all participants underwent a procedure identical to that described above, with one difference: the social-cuespresent group was changed so that the social features presented to participants were “empty” of any indication of past users’ activity (no prior likes, rates, or comments) (referred to, for convenience, as the social-cues-empty condition). For example, the “comment” section did not include any comments. It was made to look as if the participant was the first user to encounter the website.

We reran our analyses, using the same PROCESS macro for moderated mediation analysis (Hayes, 2012; Model 7, Figure 8). We again entered social cues and trust as exogenous variables, social perceptions as a mediator, and information disclosure as the dependent variable.

We found an indirect effect of exposure to social cues on information disclosure via social perceptions only in the hightrust condition (estimate = .15, 95% CI [0.06, 0.26]) but not in the low-trust condition (estimate = .08, 95% CI [-.001, 0.17]). Specifically, exposure to empty social cues (compared to no social cues) increased social perceptions only in the high-trust condition. This finding may suggest that when users are exposed to social features that do not indicate prior users’ activity, their social perceptions are enhanced only when they perceive the website as highly trustworthy. Once trust is sufficiently high, social cues increase information disclosure via increased social perceptions (a pattern similar to that observed in our main experiment). We note, however, that the interaction effect of trust and social cues in the empty case was not significant (p = .18), limiting the capacity to draw strong conclusions about the role of trust in enhancing social perceptions among users who are exposed to empty social cues.

## Discussion and Conclusions

Our analyses show that, when a user is browsing a website for the first time, the presence of an active social environment on that site indirectly affects the user’s likelihood to disclose personal information to the website. In particular, the exposure to these social cues interacts with the user’s perceptions of the site’s trustworthiness to affect her social perceptions of the website—that is, her perceptions of the site as an environment where she can make social connections. These social perceptions, in turn, affect the user’s likelihood of disclosing personal information.

Specifically, our analyses showed that social features that demonstrate an active social environment can indeed cause users to perceive the website as an environment where it is possible to make social connections, and this perception carries over to enhance information disclosure (H1). This finding supports our theory, grounded in the domain of goal setting, that the presence of an active social environment can activate individuals’ social goals (through enhancement of social perceptions), leading individuals to disclose information, as they might in an actual social situation. Our results also point to a moderating role of trust in the relationship between the exposure to social features and users’ social perceptions of the website. In particular, the indirect path to information disclosure was stronger among users who were induced to perceive the website as trustworthy (as opposed to untrustworthy) (H2).

We examined possible alternative explanations for our findings. Specifically, we ruled out the possibility that our results stemmed from biases associated with participants individual characteristics. We also showed that our results were robust to differences in users’ general attitudes towards the website.

Moreover, we ruled out general privacy concerns as an alternative mechanism. These results are in line with prior observations that users’ information disclosure behavior is not necessarily aligned with their stated privacy concerns (the privacy paradox) (Adjerid et al., 2018; Norberg et al., 2007; Smith et al., 2011; ).

We further examined participants’ actual use of the website’s social features, and found that the indirect influence of social cues on disclosure occurred even when participants did not actively use the rating, liking, and commenting features available to them.

Finally, we observed that indirect effects of exposure to social features occurred even among users who were not shown evidence of other users’ engagement with those features (e.g., their likes and past comments). In cases in which the social cues were empty of other users’ activity, information disclosure through social perceptions was enhanced only among users who were induced to perceive the website as trustworthy. In contrast, when social cues revealed prior activity, social perceptions were enhanced in users from both trust conditions—though they were enhanced less among users in the low-trust condition. Put together, it appears that in the case of empty social cues, a higher level of trust may be required in order for those cues to elicit users’ social perceptions and, consequently, to increase information disclosure.

Our findings carry several theoretical implications and contri butions. First, though information privacy research has explored the discrepancies between information disclosure and privacy concerns (Aqcuisti & Gross, 2006), and the connection between trust and information disclosure (Smith et al., 2011), to our knowledge, our work is the first to explore how information disclosure is influenced by the presence of online social features. We find that there is no direct link between exposure to social features and the act of subsequent information disclosure. However, users’ likelihood of disclosing personal information depends on the extent to which they perceive the website as a place where they can make social connections—and these social perceptions, in turn, depend on both exposure to social cues and users’ level of trust toward the website. Accordingly, our work provides an important link between the domains of information privacy and online social networks.

Our work also expands the emerging stream of research on the mechanisms underlying individuals’ behavior on social networks (e.g., Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Dewan & Ramaprasad, 2012; Zalmanson & Oestreicher-Singer 2015). Specifically, our hypotheses regarding the relationships between exposure to social cues, users’ social perceptions, and their consequent information disclosure were grounded in goal systems theory (Shah et al., 2002). We theorized that, in a trustworthy environment, exposure to social cues triggers individuals’ perceptions that they can make social connections on the website— and in doing so, activates their social goals. This goal activation, in turn, activates the propensity to disclose information, given that such disclosure is commonly perceived as a means of creating social connections—and despite the fact that, in the current scenario, disclosure is unlikely to achieve that goal.

We further contribute to research on the development of trust in websites. We showed that users’ sense of trust was not affected by the presence of social cues on the website. In other words, sites that incorporate social features are not inherently considered by users to be more or less trustworthy. Rather, trust interacts with the presence of social cues to affect users’ perceptions and behavior. Specifically, trust levels may influence users’ sensitivity to social cues, as reflected in their consequent perceptions that the website is a place where they can make social connections. These perceptions, in turn, heighten users’ likelihood of disclosing personal information to the website. Notably, our robustness analysis revealed that when social cues were devoid of previous users activity, the indirect relationship between the exposure to social cues and information disclosure (via social perceptions) was significant only among users exposed to high trust signals. These findings suggest that such empty social cues, with no hint of actual social activity, may not be perceived as “social” enough when users’ trust is low.

Finally, our findings contribute to research on the privacy paradox (Acquisti et al., 2015; Pavlou, 2011), in showing that exposure to social cues did not affect general concerns about privacy, and that privacy concerns did not mediate the relationship between social cues and information disclosure. One explanation that has been proposed for the privacy paradox is that an individual’s privacy-related behavior in specific situations should not, in fact, be considered a reflection of his or her broad attitudes regarding privacy; that is, “one might care deeply about privacy in general but, depending on the costs and benefits prevailing in a specific situation, seek or not seek privacy protection” (Acquisti et al., 2015, p. 510). Our findings support this idea, suggesting that information disclosure decisions can be influenced, whereas general privacy concerns are much more stable and are not easily manipulated.

Our findings also carry several managerial and policy implications. First, our results show that users’ tendency to disclose information to a new website depends on their social perceptions of the website. Specifically, users share their personal details with a website when they feel it is an environment that allows them to socialize and make connections. Exposure to social features can, but does not necessarily, elicit such social perceptions. The likelihood that social features on a website will enhance a particular user’s social perceptions increases if the user perceives the website as trustworthy. This finding suggests that a website that wishes to encourage information disclosure can do so both by highlighting its communal nature and by giving users reasons to trust the website (e.g., clear disclosure of the site’s purpose and security measures). At the same time, from the perspective of policy makers and the general public, it may be somewhat disconcerting that a set of cues has the potential to influence information disclosure behavior among users who have no prior familiarity with a website and have been browsing it for only a few minutes.

Our findings are particularly important in light of the high prevalence of social features (and thus social cues) online, which are likely to continue to grow in popularity. Indeed, given the benefits that social features provide in terms of user engagement, retention, and conversion, we do not believe that the implementation of such features should or in fact can be limited. Yet, our work points to several ways in which websites might implement social features in a manner that encourages users to act responsibly with regard to information disclosure. For example, our findings emphasize the role of trust cues in the relationship between users’ exposure to social cues and their likelihood of disclosing information. These findings suggest that it might be worthwhile for policy makers to devote attention to ensuring that websites cannot introduce false or unreliable trust cues. One possible direction might be the implementation of third-party trust cues that are difficult to forge—such as encryption-enabled security certificates that are granted by an external entity. Yet, such a policy only works if users are literate in issues of information security and are familiar with what might constitute a reliable versus dishonest trust cue. Indeed, many major technology companies seek to increase users’ literacy in these matters. For example, Microsoft, Apple, and Google, which developed the world’s most popular browsers, have inserted friendly warnings and popups that help users understand when a website they have entered might be “risky” to their personal and their computers’ security. It might be beneficial for browsers to present such alerts for websites that present dishonest trust cues—for example, a website that declares that it is safe and trustworthy but does not actually comply with a privacy policy or use encrypted data delivery. This way, users who are exposed to social cues can be more cautious and refrain from information disclosure that might jeopardize their privacy and safety.

Our work is not without limitations. The randomized experimental settings and the manner in which they were designed enabled us to control for different effects, as well as to test for causation. However, one might claim that the laboratory setting is also a limitation of the research: For example, participants might not have answered questionnaires honestly, or their behavior might have been motivated by a desire to please the experimenters (such that they disclosed more information than they would have in a more natural setting). Regarding concerns that participants might not have been honest, our objective was not to evaluate users’ stated trust perceptions or privacy concerns as such; rather, we sought to identify differences between the experimental groups. Given that users were assigned randomly to these groups, we have no reason to believe that one group was inherently less honest than another, such that our comparison of the two is expected to be valid. A similar argument holds with regard to the concern that participants may have revealed information out of a desire to please the experimenters. However, it is important to restate that we did take precautions to assure the quality of participation. Users were made aware that the request to enter their personal information was entirely optional, so they would not feel required to write something just to please. Moreover, the entire survey was presented as part of the VideoBook website, rather than as a separate questionnaire initiated by the experimenters, to resemble a request for information common in such websites.

Other limitations include the fact that our findings may be specific to certain types of social cues and trust manipulations or to environments resembling VideoBook (a YouTube-like website) and might not be relevant to other types of cues and websites. Furthermore, our research focused on a first encounter with an unfamiliar website. It is possible that, because of this unfamiliarity, users tried to rationalize the website’s need for identifiable information. For example, users in the social-cues-present condition might have inferred that, like other social networks, VideoBook needed personal information to verify users’ identity. This possibility seems unlikely, given that users engaged with the website anonymously and were not asked to create personal profiles (and were nevertheless able to use the website’s social features); moreover, the reasoning that the website provided for the information request—“to improve our analysis”—did not relate to social networking in any obvious way. Nevertheless, further research would benefit from testing websites that users are more familiar with, as well as websites with a structure that more closely resembles a classic social networking service.

## Trust Prompts Pre-test

In order to identify two introduction messages that would elicit participants’ trust (or distrust) towards VideoBook, we carried out the following pre-test.

Participants. We recruited 240 participants (70% women; $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { a g c } } = 3 6 . 5 )$ through the Prolific website. All were fluent English speakers. Participants completed the study over the Internet, and were paid 0.3 GBP for their participation. We randomly assigned participants into six conditions, where they asked to rate one of six messages.

Procedure. All participants first viewed a screen presenting the following introduction: “In this study, you will read an introduction page of a new website. After reading the message, you will be asked to share your opinion about it.” Next, participants were exposed to one introduction message (e.g., “We keep doing code cleanup, bug fixes and general optimizations. However, lately some users have complained about various errors during browsing our site. If you encounter any problems or have concerns on this issue, please contact us.”). Fou messages were tested as possible candidates to elicit distrust, and two messages were tested as possible candidates to elicit trust. Participants then proceeded to a short survey

Measurements. We measured participants’ attitudes toward the alleged website and its introduction message by asking the following questions, which they rated on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all; 7 = an extreme degree): “Please indicate the degree of SEVERITY of the problem implied by the message”; “Please indicate the degree of TRUST you feel about this website, after reading this message”; and “How comforted do you feel by this message?” Next, we asked them to rate on a scale of 1–7 (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree) their agreement with each of the following statements: “I think this is a trustworthy website,” “I think this is a secure website,” “This message made me feel safe,” “This message made me feel that my personal information will be secured.” Finally, we asked them to respond to the following questions: “If you encountered this message while browsing a new website, would you proceed and enter the website anyway?” (1 = definitely not; 5 = definitely yes), “How realistic would you rate the content of the message?” (1 = definitely fake; 5 = definitely real), and “In your opinion, what is the purpose of this message?” (open ended).
