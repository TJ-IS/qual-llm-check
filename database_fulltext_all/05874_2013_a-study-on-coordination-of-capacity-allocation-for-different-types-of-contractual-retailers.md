# A study on coordination of capacity allocation for different types of contractual retailers

Yeu-Shiang Huang ⁎, Jun-Ming Chen, Zu-Liang Lin

Department of Industrial and Information Management, National Cheng Kung University, Taiwan, ROC

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 15 October 2010 Received in revised form 2 June 2012 Accepted 23 September 2012 Available online 29 September 2012

Keywords: Supply chain management Capacity allocation Non-cooperative coordination

## a b s t r a c t

Because the demand is uncertain and it is impossible to expand capacity immediately, suppliers in supply chains are faced with insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity. As a result, they are unable to satisfy fully the demand from many retailers. Sometimes the use of additional shifts or outsourcing may resolve the problem, but many times, suppliers have to seek ways to appropriately allocate their limited capacity to the retailers. A noncooperative coordinative mechanism is often required which has to be acceptable and bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for all members of the supply chain. This study considers a supply chain with a single supplier and multiple retailers in which the retailers are categorized into two types: ordinary and privileged, according to the steadiness of their partnership with the supplier. The primary concern is to coordinate the capacity allocation within the supply chain with the uses of quantity discount and strategic price-quantity auction with consideration of maintaining fairness and a long-term steady partnership. Our analytical results show that both parties bene<sup>fi</sup>t from these coordination mechanisms since the supplier's pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases because the retailers are incentivized to place orders with higher quantities, lowering the cost of idle capacity and increasing revenues. In the meantime, retailers are able to trim down their ordering costs and fairly compete for limited capacity, which reduces the transaction risks and shortage costs for uncertain demands, and thus increases their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 1. Introduction

Effective supply chain management can enhance the performance of the entire supply chain and provide more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for its members. However, companies nowadays face a globally competitive market in which product life cycles shrink, product variability boosts, and post-sale customer services are desperately desired, so the cost and complexity of supply chain management dramatically increase. In particular, in the presence of con<sup>fl</sup>icting objectives among members of a supply chain, the performance of the entire supply chain would also be seriously harmed. Therefore, in considering the complexity of supply chain, it is of special importance to have an appropriate coordination mechanism to ensure members of a supply chain can maintain long-term partnership and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Coordination in supply chains can effectively reduce risk and uncertainty, usually achieved by upholding a steady supply for each retailer. A vast amount of literature has discussed different methods of pro<sup>fi</sup>t sharing with non-cooperative coordination via quantity, lead time, an quality discount policies [2,3,9,16,18,20,21,24,26,31]. In addition, research on auction mechanism for coordination is also widely studied in the literature [10,14,15,22,27–29,32,34]. Other coordination methods can also be found in the literature, such as market-base coordination [17] and dual sourcing coordination in the presence of insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity [30]. In summary, coordination can be successful with appropriate motivators, e.g. fair pro<sup>fi</sup>t sharing programs, by using effective information to satisfy all members of a supply chain. Therefore, in this study, a noncooperative coordinative mechanism is considered to deal with the capacity allocation problem for a supply chain with a single supplier and multiple retailers.

It is common that short supply due to insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity may occur in supply chains, since the extension of capacity is often expensive and time consuming for companies. In such a case, it is critical to determine an appropriate way to allocate the insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity to multiple retailers. Research on capacity allocation has been studied in straightforward ways in dealing with the problem of allocating insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity [4–6,11,19,30]. The typical use of uniform allocation is unfair for retailers with larger or small demands, since the amount of short supply is born equally by all the retailers. Another popular approach uses order quantities as the criteria for allocating the insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity which gives larger capacities to retailers with greater demands. It is possible that some retailers may exaggerate their order quantities to gain more capacity which thus aggravates the bullwhip effect [23].Therefore, with a more comprehensive perspective, this study contributes to the literature with a non-cooperative quantity discount and strategic price-quantity auction coordination mechanism in which the capacity allocation problem under conditions of insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient and excess capacities can be fairly dealt with for both ordinary and privileged retailers. As a result, the retailers' shortage costs can be reduced and both parties of the supply chain can bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

However, non-cooperative coordination may have to be facilitated by contractual agreements to provide members with guidelines for enhancing pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for themselves as well as the entire supply chain. Contractual agreements are usually contingent and customized according to real needs in practice. Some contracts focus on the order quantity that both parties can agree upon: for example, [1,8] considered a minimum quantity commitment contract and a total order quantity commitment contract, respectively. Some contracts focus on the order delivery schedule for the consideration of just-in-time production: for example, [12,25] deliberated a just-in-time contract and a <sup>fi</sup>xed delivery contract, respectively. Other types of contracts were also proposed with different concerns: [13] proposed a forecast-commitment contract, [7] proposed a revenue-sharing contract, [35] considered a two-stage assembly system contract, and [33] proposed an option-based coordination contract. All the proposed existing contracts may have their suitable implementing conditions with corresponding types of supply chain. Therefore, In this study, two types of retailers are considered, such as ordinary and privileged retailers, according to the steadiness of their partnership with the supplier, and the contractual agreements with respect to each retailer would be facilitated to fairly allocate the excess or insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity, and a coordination mechanism is proposed with the considerations of quantity discount and strategic price-quantity auction.

Based on the aforementioned discussion, a capacity allocation noncooperative coordination mechanism is proposed for supply chains with a single supplier and multiple retailers with which a long-time steady partnership can be maintained among the members and the profit of the entire supply chain can be enhanced. This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 states the research problem of coordination in supply chain management; Section 3 depicts the development of the proposed approach; Section 4 demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed approach by performing analytical investigation, with sensitivity analyses also carried out to probe into the important factors which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the results; and <sup>fi</sup>nally, Section 5 draws the concluding remarks.

## 2. Capacity allocation in supply chains

Suppose that, in a two-echelon supply chain with one supplier and N retailers, the capacity allocation problem is essential for the members, and each individual retailer i faces a different demand $d _ { i }$ with probability density function (pdf) f (.) and cumulative density function (cdf) $F _ { i } ( . ) .$ . The N retailers are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two segments: ordinary and privileged retailers, based on the business relationships and the degrees of information sharing with the supplier. Privileged retailers have had long-term steady partnership and built mutual trusts with the supplier, while ordinary retailers are new or short-term business partners to the supplier. Contractual agreements should be made between the supplier and the retailers to set up criteria for proper capacity allocation, and which have to be based on the considerations of the supplier's production cost $C _ { P } ,$ <sup>fi</sup>xed cost $C _ { F } ,$ and cost of idle capacity $C _ { I } ,$ as well as the retailers' purchasing costs, inventory handling costs, and shortage costs. In addition, both market status and partnership conditions should also be taken in to account in establishing the contractual agreements. Suppose that upon negotiating with the retailers, a lower limit of order quantity l for each retailer i (ordinary or privileged) is settled by mutual agreement with which if retailer i orders $Q _ { i } \geq l _ { i } ,$ , the purchasing cost for unit capacity is $P _ { u } ,$ but if an ordinary retailer i orders $Q _ { i } < l _ { i } ,$ some penalty costs have to be born and the purchasing cost for unit capacity becomes $P _ { P } ,$ where $P _ { P } > P _ { u } .$ . Note that the privileged retailers will always order at least l since it is a customary practice of the long-term steady partnership. According to the contractual agreements, in case of insuf<sup>fi</sup>- cient capacity, the supplier would <sup>fi</sup>rst allocate only the lower limit quantity l to the ordinary retailers if they order more than $l _ { i } ,$ and in addition, since a good relationship exists between the privileged retailers and the supplier, the supplier must ful<sup>fi</sup>ll each privileged retailer's order no matter how great the order size is. Table 1 summarizes the contractual agreements.

With regard to a lack of coordination in the supply chain, both parties will focus merely on their own interests and aim to maximize their own pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. In cases of insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity, in attempting to ingratiate the ordinary retailers with more demands, the supplier will tend to allocate enough capacity to the ordinary retailers with bigger orders <sup>fi</sup>rst to avoid losing such favorable retailers when the market demands become less. Note that the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts gained by such an allocation policy would be greater than by a proportionally rationing one, since more administration cost can be saved due to the less number of orders. In contrast, in cases of excess capacity, in which no quantity discount for coordination exists to attract the retailers to order more, the supplier would have idle capacity left after ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the orders of both ordinary and privileged retailers, which results in large opportunity loss. Both cases would not be able to maintain long-term cooperative partnerships for the supply chain.

However, in order to effectively use the available capacity and trim down the loss from idle capacity, the supplier would often offer various quantity discount programs to coordinate with the retailers for selling more products. On the other hand, when the overall demand exceeds the supplier's capacity, upon satisfying the base demands of the retailers according to the contracts, based on the perception of fairness and openness, a price-quantity auction would be undertaken to let retailers compete for the insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity among the ordinary retailers. Therefore, the proposed coordination mechanism is formulated by the uses of both quantity discount and price-quantity auction for the cases of excess and insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacities.

In cases of excess capacity, for every one more product which retailer i orders will have a discount rate α, resulting in a new lower price for the extra units $( 1 - \alpha ) P _ { u }$ . On the other hand, in case of insuf-<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity, retailer i who is privileged will obtain capacity which can ful<sup>fi</sup>ll his order quantity $Q _ { i } ,$ , but retailer i who is ordinary will have to bid $P _ { i } ^ { ( j ) }$ per unit for $B _ { i } ^ { ( j ) }$ amount of capacity in the jth round of auction (will be discussed later).

The assumptions made in this study are stated as follows:

(1) Since the focal interest of the study is the capacity allocation problem between the supplier and the retailers, it is assumed that there is no competition and conspiracy consideration in the supply market.

(2) With regard to the case with coordination, the supplier will produce after coordination is accomplished, and the amount provided matches the order quantities exactly. Therefore, inventory holding cost is not considered for the supplier.

(3) The supplier only provides a single product to the retailers. The demands of the retailers will not be very different, i.e., there is no dominant retailer in the supply chain, and each individual retailer will not place an order which exceeds the capacity of the supplier.

(4) The supplier's capacity, in any event, is able to satisfy all the privileged retailers' order quantities plus the ordinary retailers contractual lower limit order quantities.

## 3. Coordination with quantity discount and price-quantity auction

It is pragmatic that coordination occurs only if the supplier and the retailers are both under the condition of gaining satisfactory bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the coordination. The pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for the members of the supply chain before and after coordination are analyzed, and the improvement that justi<sup>fi</sup>es proper coordination is capable of impelling the supply chain to enhance its operation performance is designated.

## 3.1. Profits without coordination

In the case that no coordination occurs between the supplier and the retailers, both parties behave according to the contractual agreements and will concentrate on their own business conditions and make relevant decisions to optimize their own pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. When the total order quantity of all the retailers Q is less than the supplier's maximal capacity $K ,$ every retailer's demand can be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, and the surplus capacity is idle without any further promotion action. In contrast, when Q is greater than K, the supplier has to allocate $Q _ { i }$ to retailer i if she is a privileged retailer and $l _ { i }$ to retailer i if she is an ordinary retailer as stipulated in the contracts. Upon accomplishing the contractual requirements, if there is leftover capacity, the supplier will allocate the leftover capacity to the ordinary retailers in turn according to their original order quantity, i.e., Q . The ordinary retailer with the greatest order quantity will have the privilege to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll her order <sup>fi</sup>rst, but the amount she obtains will depend on the amount of the leftover capacity. If, after satisfying such a retailer, there is still capacity left, the remaining leftover capacity will be allocated to the ordinary retailer with the second greatest order quantity, and so on until the leftover capacity has run out.

## 3.2. Profits with coordination

In the case that the supplier and the retailers are willing to coordinate, both parties behave according to the contractual agreements and will negotiate the product price and order quantity to enhance their own pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, respectively. When the total order quantity of all the retailers Q is less than the supplier's maximal capacity K, in addition to satisfying every retailer's demand, the supplier will offer a quantity discount promotion program to attract retailers to purchase more in order to deplete the surplus capacity. In contrast, when Q is greater than K, the supplier has to allocate Q to retailer i if she is a privileged retailer and l to retailer i if she is an ordinary retailer as stipulated in the contracts. Upon accomplishing the contractual requirements, if there is still capacity left, an auction mechanism will be undertaken by the supplier to provide a fair environment for the ordinary retailers to compete for the leftover capacity.

## 3.2.1. Case of excess capacity

## 5. Conclusion

It is pragmatic for members of traditional supply chains to concern their own goods without seeking possible methods of coordination to enhance the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the entire supply chain. In the case of insuf<sup>fi</sup>- cient capacity, the supplier usually would not consider the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of every individual retailer which may result in a condition in which only a very small portion or even no capacity is allocated to some retailers. As such the retailers would face a serious marketing problem because of high shortage costs. On the other hand, in the case of excess capacity, the supplier usually keeps idling the unused capacity or adopts nonsystematic promotion programs which may either lose opportunity gains or waste valuable resources. In this study, the supplier and each individual retailer can settle a contractual agreement in which a lower limit of order quantity is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed to establish a pro<sup>fi</sup>t foundation for both sides. This mutual agreement is also able to reduce the retailer's risk of obtaining no capacity at all and the supplier's risk of having too much idle capacity.

In addition, the use of quantity discount coordination by the supplier is able to attract the retailers to order more quantity which turns the supplier's idle capacity into the retailers' product sales. The win-win situation in which both parties bene<sup>fi</sup>t is thus achieved, especially when the optimal discount rate can be determined by the supplier. Different ways of allocating insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity have been proposed in the literature which often assumes that either original order quantity or equalization distribution can be a criterion to allocate insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity to retailers. However, both criteria are perceived as unfair and can give rise to the possibility that retailers would exaggerate their order quantity in order to have more allocating capacity, thus resulting in the bullwhip effects. To prevent this, in the case of insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity, after allocating the proper quantities to the retailers according to the contractual agreements, the remaining capacity is competed among the ordinary retailer by means of a price-quantity auction which is able to raise the supplier's pro<sup>fi</sup>t and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the risks for the retailers with higher shortage costs. Accordingly, the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of the entire supply chain can be improved and the fairness of the allocation is perceived by every retailer which ensures a long-term steady partnership.

In reality, supply can sometimes be uncertain. The same retailer has to decide the order quantity with consideration of the supplier capacity. In this study, the optimal initial order quantity is <sup>fi</sup>rst determined by each retailer regardless of the cases of excess and insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity, and then, by comparing the available capacity and the total order quantity, the corresponding coordination mechanism is adopted by the supplier when either one case is realized. Further study may deal with the more realistic situation by assuming that the supplier capacity can be modeled by a certain probability distribution and appropriate allocation mechanisms are announced in advance, and the retailers can thus make their optimal ordering decisions regarding both the supply uncertainty conditions and the allocation rules to maximize their expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Further study may also be extended to investigate the situation of multiple suppliers. In addition, multiple products or even seasonal products may be considered to probe into the problem with more realistic perspectives. An interesting direction of further study would deal with a more realistic situation in which different discount rates based on quantity may be offered to the retailers in the case of excess capacity. In such a situation, the coordination process would be very complicated. The uses of stochastic product deteriorating rates would also be interesting in studying the coordination process, although the analysis is expected to be more dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult. A good direction to further extend the proposed coordination mechanism would be to account for the effects of cheating on demand. A model with the use of game theory might be suitable for this analysis.
