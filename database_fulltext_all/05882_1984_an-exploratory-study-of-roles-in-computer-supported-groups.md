## Abstract

The emerging technology of group support systems has the potential to enhance the effectiveness of team work in organizations. One critical factor that has received little attention in technology-supported environments is that of the roles participants fill in meetings. This paper develops a theoretical model of roles in computer-supported meetings and examines the impact of a group support system on roles. An exploratory field study of 10 work teams was conducted to investigate the perceptions of participants about their own roles and the roles that the group support system technology might fill. The study found a gap between the role expectations of meeting initiators and meeting participants, as well as between participants' role expectations and actual roles filled. The group support system technology was perceived to fill an unexpectedly large variety of roles. The study also showed that the group support system assumed some of the roles that participants expected to fill, resulting in fewer roles filled by participants.

Keywords: Group support systems, electronic meeting systems, roles, group interaction process

## Introduction

Few people would dispute that the behavior of small groups is complex and difficult to predict. The characteristics of individual members, how individuals align themselves within a group structure, the characteristics of the task, and the nature of the group environment all affect group processes and outcomes (McGrath, 1984; McGrath and Altman, 1966). But also important are how individuals interact with and perceive one another. One aspect of mutual perception that has a significant impact on both processes and outcomes is that of the roles people fill and see others as filling (Bormann, 1975; Hackman, 1990). A role consists of a dynamic set of both expected and enacted behaviors (Bormann, 1975; McGrath, 1984). For example, group members might fill the roles of gatekeeper, proceduralist, or jokester. Role behavior is both an important theoretical variable and a critical issue in practice (Nutt, 1976). The practical matter of having critical roles filled in meetings has direct implications for improving task performance and satisfaction.

Although roles have been studied in traditional groups, little is known about roles in technology-supported environments. The growing interest in and use of group support systems (GSS) and the general lack of understanding of their impacts (Dennis and Gallupe, 1993) mandate an exploration of roles in these new environments. For example, does a GSS environment affect the fit between group members' role expectations and actual roles filled? Will group members be able to fill a greater diversity of roles in a GSS environment? What roles must be filled for a group to be satisfied with its outcomes and process? Will some roles be taken over by the system itself, thus reducing role demands on human members? These are examples of the questions and issues that motivated this study.

The study has both theoretical and applied objectives. From a theoretical perspective, our goal is to study: (1) the roles that group members are expected to fill and expect themselves to fill in a GSS environment; (2) roles that members feel they actually do fill as they interact with each other and with the GSS technology; (3) the nature of differences between expected and actual roles; and (4) roles that the GSS is perceived to fill. On a purely practical basis, we want to find out what roles need to be filled in a group support system environment and why, so that people who meet in such an environment are able to do productive work from the very beginning and can continue to do productive work as the group matures.

## Background on Group Support Systems

We define the term group support system (GSS) to mean an integrated combination of specialized hardware, software, and procedures to support group activity. Although GSSs may be used in distributed environments, the focus of this study is on synchronous, face-to-face meetings. A typical GSS of this type includes a networked personal computer (or, at a minimum, a numeric keypad) for each group member; software that collects, manipulates, and aggregates individual input; and a large-screen display. In face-to-face meetings, participants can speak verbally as well as electronically via the GSS. The GSS provides the capability for anonymous, simultaneous input, all of which typically is recorded by the system. Immediate feedback is provided through such tools as electronic voting, rating, or multiattribute utility analysis. Structured agendas are also a common element of the electronic process.

Among the first of these types of systems were the University of Arizona's GroupSystems (Dennis, et al., 1988), the University of Minnesota's Software-Aided Meeting Management (Dickson, et al., 1992) and Xerox PARC's Colab (Stefik, et al., 1987). Early research with these environments focused on comparisons of traditional (notechnology) versus GSS-supported groups.

These studies typically examined differences in group outcomes, e.g., decision quality, decision speed, and member satisfaction. Studies that focus on the interaction process are still relatively rare. Meta-analyses of the experimental work have found that GSS groups had greater task orientation, higher decision quality, more alternatives generated, lower consensus, longer decision time, more democratic participation, and in some cases decreased satisfaction than non-GSS groups (Benbasat and Lim, 1993; McLeod, 1992).

Little, if any, work has been done on role behavior in a GSS environment. Prior frameworks for GSS research (e.g., DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987; Gray and GDSS Subcommittee, 1990; Pinsonneault and Kraemer, 1989) do not directly specify roles as a separate input variable, and roles are rarely mentioned as important mediating variables for other effects. For example, Valacich, et al. (1992) present propositions for the effects of anonymity on group process in a GSS environment, but role effects are not discussed, even though we might expect that anonymity would reduce role clarity and strength.

Filling roles could be one criterion for selecting group members, and some studies of member selection have been done. Aiken, et al. (1990) developed a prototype expert system that supports GSS session facilitators during presession planning. Their “Group Selection Module” helps the facilitator or meeting coordinator determine appropriate participants for the meeting. However, their focus is on matching organizational function with meeting objectives rather than on process-oriented roles. Dennis, et al.'s (1990) review of 17 case studies of GSS groups observed that in all but two of the groups, the decision to use the GSS changed the size and composition of the group. Group leaders increased the size and diversity of the group to get more opinions. In spite of these few studies, however, the issue of who is attending a group session has been largely ignored.

The role of the GSS technology itself has been rarely explored in prior work. Smithin and Eden (1986) point out that it is important to construct an appropriate role for the machine. They note that computers still encourage extreme reactions in people, perhaps because we as humans find it difficult to place a computer system in an appropriate role in our social world. This confusion is bound to be exacerbated in a GSS environment, where group members are interacting with one another and with the system. The system is a machine, yet also a partner in the interaction process. How, then, can the system's role be construed? DeSanctis (1989) provides examples of the assumptions that researchers have made about the role of the technology, e.g., as a communication channel, computation function, advisor to the group, recorder or storehouse of information, arbitrator, or imposer of group structure. DeSanctis argues that the roles played by the technology will be limited or partially determined by the goal of the information exchange, e.g., efficiency, coordination, or production.

Poole, et al. (1991) studied four quality assurance teams within a large service organization with respect to how the teams used a GSS during ongoing quality meetings. Groups appeared to perceive the role of the GSS as primarily an organizer, but also as a secretary, a problem solver, and—in one group—a scapegoat. The authors conclude that the general interpretation that groups give to the meaning of the GSS affects how they use it in their work. Their study is strong evidence of the importance of understanding participants' views of the role that a GSS plays in a group.

## Theory and Propositions

## Roles in traditional groups

The history of role theory dates to the 1930s, when sociologists and anthropologists studied roles as a key to explaining the origins of social behavior (Linton, 1936; Mead, 1934). Since then, role theory has emerged as a recognized discipline (Biddle and Thomas, 1966), and roles have been studied at many levels of analysis, including societal, organizational, and small group. Our interest here is with roles in small groups, as embedded in an organizational context.

Building on the sociological roots of role theory, Graen (1976) developed a role systems model in which role behavior is the result of organizational demands, social demands, and personal demands. Katz and Kahn (1978) applied the same ideas to their organizational role theory, which emphasizes organizational factors, interpersonal factors, and attributes of the person. These general concepts also apply to the small group context, where roles are embedded in the group and larger organizational contexts. For purposes of this study and consistent with small group research (Bormann, 1975; McGrath, 1984), we define a role as a dynamic set of recurring behaviors, both expected and enacted, within a particular group context.

The resources that individuals bring to groups based on their roles—as well as other skills and behaviors—have been recognized as important variables that relate to group performance and member satisfaction (Hackman, 1990; McGrath and Altman, 1966; Steiner, 1972; Stumpf, et al., 1979). In a study of meeting effectiveness, Di Salvo, et al. (1989) found that failure to consider the elements of a good group was the top-ranked premeeting problem. Steiner (1972) has argued that “. . . group productivity depends upon the appropriateness of the role system that is imposed upon the members of the group, and upon the adequacy with which members perform their assigned roles” (p. 63).

Few studies of traditional groups have taken a holistic perspective on roles; most researchers have limited their view of roles to specific aspects relevant to a particular study. Role-related variables that have been studied include: task versus socio-emotional orientation; leader versus member communication; position in a communication network; position in or nature of interaction pattern; and formal position within the organization (Hare, 1976; McGrath, 1984; Shaw, 1981). The findings relevant to this study can be summarized briefly. Most groups identify two kinds of role specialists: the idea person who is task-oriented, and the best-liked person who focuses on socio-emotional problems of the group. Role conflict can occur if one person is forced to fill both roles.

Most studies of roles in small groups have been from a descriptive perspective. Benne and Sheats (1948) observed interacting groups and developed a typology that is still cited today. Their typology classifies group member roles into three categories: (1) group task roles, (2) socio-emotional group-building and maintenance roles, and (3) individual roles. The classification was based upon extensive observation of interacting groups and is consistent with a central theme of group research that recognizes the importance of both task-oriented and socio-emotional behaviors (Gustafson and Harrell, 1970). Bormann's (1975) long-term study of groups identifies generic activities (both task and group-building) that group members perform, including:

\- Initiate action

\- Do routine chores

\- Be funny and release tension

• Test soundness of information

\- Divide up group work and assign jobs

\- Gather information

\- Follow orders and do job assigned

\- Give suggestions as to possible ways to do job

\- Draw inferences

\- Evaluate inferences and plans of action

Based on these observed activities, Bormann classifies group roles as: clarifier, initiator, information giver, tension releaser, and information tester.

Studies of interaction patterns also have been a rich source of descriptive information on role behaviors in groups. Interaction studies typically record verbal statements of group members and code them into predetermined categories. The categories used in these studies generally reinforce the broad role categories described above. For example, Olson, et al. (forthcoming) used an interaction coding technique that tracked different aspects of problem-solving activity. The results showed that participation rates varied by organizational role and expertise and depended on who else was in the room and the topic being discussed.

The literature on roles in traditional groups can be summarized as follows. A body of literature exists on specific issues related to roles such as role overload. It is also clear that task-related and socio-emotional, or group building, roles are two important elements of effective groups. The remaining work has consisted of descriptive studies that reveal important roles that must be covered in traditional group interaction. Yet none of this work has been investigated or replicated in a GSS environment.

## Roles in GSS environments

Using a particular GSS could influence who should be a member of a group. As Ellis, et al.

(1988) note: “Groupware may not only alter the way people work together, but it may also change who can work together” (p. 33). A GSS environment allows for greater variation in group members because larger groups can be accommodated (Grohowski, et al., 1990). Examining roles provides an excellent vehicle for studying this aspect of GSS impacts. This study is the first to examine roles in a GSS environment.

Figure 1 shows the theoretical model that is the foundation for this study. Consistent with the McGrath (1984) framework that has been adopted by many GSS researchers, we use an input-process-output framework for identifying the key variables in the model. Input variables are the task, group members, and the technological environment. The interaction process consists of characteristics of interaction and intermediate role outcomes. The specific characteristics of the interaction process result from the context provided by the technological environment. Outcome variables include traditional task outcomes such as effectiveness and efficiency, and group-related outcomes such as satisfaction and cohesiveness.

A brief review of each major component of the model serves to define these concepts in more detail. The first component—the task performed by the group—has been studied for many years, but no comprehensive theory of tasks has emerged. Hackman (1969) summarizes the major views of group tasks as belonging to one of four categories: (1) task qua task, in which a task is defined by its objective properties such as its complexity (Perrow, 1967; Wood, 1986); (2) task as behavior requirements, in which a task consists of the behaviors that group members must undertake to achieve some criterion of success given a particular stimulus situation (McGrath, 1984); (3) task as behavior description, in which the task is described by what subjects actually do in a given situation; and (4) task as ability requirements, in which the focus is on the pattern of personal abilities or characteristics that are required for successful task completion. Consistent with Hackman (1969) and incorporating ideas from Wood (1986), we define a group task as the behavior requirements needed to accomplish stated goals, via an explicit process, using given information. In our study, the tasks can be generally described as tasks that require judgmental/decision-making processes.

The second major component of the model is group members. Group membership includes both characteristics of individual members and how members structure themselves within a group (McGrath, 1984). Shaw (1981) reviews the substantial literature on group membership and the effects of such variables as age, gender, physical characteristics, intelligence, personality, group size, cohesiveness, compatibility, heterogeneity versus homogeneity, status, roles, and norms. Our specific interest in this study is in role expectations of group members.

The third major component is the technological environment, defined as the communication and task performance systems made available to the group (McGrath and Hollingshead, 1994). The most common typologies to describe the technological environment are: (1) support for communication versus support for process (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987; Pinsonneault and Kraemer, 1989); and (2) configuration of communication over time and space, i.e., same/different time and same/different place (Johansen, 1988). Within a same-time/same-place environment—such as was used in this study—the technology can be defined in terms of the commonly available characteristics of an anonymous communication channel, simultaneous input, recording of all keyed input, immediate feedback, and a structured agenda (Gray and Subcommittee, 1990).

The heart of the model is in the interaction process, which is divided into two distinct pieces: (1) characteristics of interaction, and (2) intermediate role outcomes. The characteristics of interaction are partially defined by the communication channel (Daft, et al., 1987; Rice and Associates, 1984), i.e., the technological environment. An anonymous communication channel and simultaneous input are associated with idea stimulation and reduced production blocking (Gallupe, et al., 1991; 1992; Valacich, et al.,

1992). An anonymous communication channel also could promote wide-ranging behavior since during anonymous interaction, participants could assume a role that might contradict the last role they took, e.g., they can play devil's advocate to their own previous comments (Kozar and Zigurs, 1992). Simultaneous input also allows for simultaneous conversations. The recording of all input and immediate feedback provide a continuing commentary on the interaction. Immediate feedback should also result in idea stimulation (Watson and Bostrom, 1991). A structured agenda results in a structured process during interaction. The characteristics of interaction result in intermediate outcomes related to roles, namely, participants fill actual roles and the GSS technology assumes roles.

The last component of the model consists of outcomes—both task outcomes and group outcomes. This distinction has been a common theme in the small group literature (Bormann, 1975). A task outcome is a group product that results from performing the original objectives of the task (Davis, 1969). Quality of the task outcome can be measured in reference to those original objectives, e.g., the correctness of a decision, the efficiency of the process, the creativity of a set of ideas, or the degree of consensus in the group. A group outcome is a group product that is related to the continued functioning of the members as a group. Examples of group products include satisfaction with process, satisfaction with task outcomes, perceived conflict, cohesiveness, motivation, morale, and trust (McGrath, 1984; Shaw, 1981).

The overall model is consistent with what one would predict using an expectancy theory framework (Vroom, 1964). Expectancy theory argues that individuals act in a given way because they expect their behavior to result in a particular outcome—an outcome that is attractive to them. In other words, individuals are motivated to engage in behaviors to the extent that they expect those behaviors to result in positive outcomes for them (Miller and Grush, 1988). For expectancy theory to work, individuals need to understand and believe in the links between effort-performance and performance-reward. Applied to roles, expectancy theory would predict that a greater awareness of the relationship between role behaviors and desirable task and group outcomes would encourage desirable role behavior.

Since there is so little prior work related to roles in GSS environments, this study is by necessity exploratory. Two sets of propositions can be formulated.

## 1. Influence of GSS environment on role perceptions of initiators and participants

Role expectations of group members (both initiators and participants) are expected to be based primarily on their experience with traditional group environments. Because the characteristics of GSS-supported interaction include wide-ranging behavior and may result in some assumption of roles by technology, we expect that:

1a. Initiators will not be able to specify the roles they expect participants to fill in meetings.

1b. Initiators and participants will not agree on the assignment of roles prior to the GSS-supported meeting.

1c. Participants will not be able to predict accurately the roles that they themselves will fill in a GSS-supported meeting.

One characteristic of interaction in a GSS-supported environment is wide-ranging behavior, made possible through the anonymous communication channel and simultaneous input. The GSS makes it possible for participants to speak openly for themselves and for others. All input is recorded, and feedback on input is provided immediately, thus participants do not have to remain in one consistent role throughout the meeting. They can fill a role and move on to another. Therefore:

## 1d. Participants in a GSS-supported meeting will fill a wider variety of roles than they expected to fill.

Several characteristics of interaction—as set up by the characteristics of the technological environment—contribute to role assumption by the technology. Continuing commentary supports moderator and recorder roles through the record of all keyed interactions. The feedback aspect of continuing commentary could represent roles such as motivator, information giver, information seeker, explainer, and idea generator. A structured process is consistent with the roles of proceduralist, gatekeeper, and information seeker. Therefore:

## 2. Prescribed role balance and perceived effectiveness

Prior literature from traditional groups shows that certain types of roles need to be filled for members to be satisfied with group processes and outcomes. Bormann's (1975) studies prescribed that approximately two-thirds of role behavior should be task-oriented and one-third group-building oriented. Exclusion of group-building roles is perceived as detrimental to meeting outcomes. We have the same expectations for GSS meetings, thus:

2a. Participants will perceive meeting outcomes to be effective in meetings where the prescribed balance of task and group-building roles exists.

2b. Participants will perceive group processes to be effective in meetings where the prescribed balance of task and group-building roles exists.

## Method and Measurement

This research was conducted as a field study. Quantitative and qualitative analyses were used to examine the variables of interest. Given the limitations of field research, we were constrained from collecting certain kinds of data. Transcription and identification problems ruled out using audiotaping, and intrusiveness ruled out videotaping. Data were collected by questionnaires, and only GSS-supported groups were studied.

## Data Analysis and Results

## Influence of GSS environment on role perceptions

The first set of propositions addressed the influence of the GSS environment on the role perceptions of initiators and participants.

1a. Initiators will not be able to specify the roles they expect participants to fill in meetings.

The results from the presession questionnaires filled out by meeting initiators showed that initiators had no difficulty identifying the constituency and expertise of participants they were inviting to the meeting. Out of 100 participants, all but one had an identified constituency, and all but four had an identified expertise or other reason for being included. Initiators had more difficulty with role expectations, however. In six out of 10 groups, there was at least one person who was not expected to fill any role; across all 100 participants, 13 percent were not expected by initiators to fill any role.

It is revealing to examine which roles initiators did not expect to be filled. In eight out of 10 groups, there was at least one role from the 12 role categories that initiators did not expect anyone to fill. Table 6 summarizes across all groups the number of times each type of role was left unfilled by the initiator. The sum of these unfilled roles across the groups represents 16 percent (19/120) of the total of possible roles identified.

The results from the post-session questionnaire that asked initiators to rate the relative importance of constituencies, expertise, and appropriate roles confirm their focus on the former rather than the latter. The mean ratings of the eight initiators who responded to this question were: importance of constituencies—4.50 (std. dev. = 0.53); importance of expertise—3.69 (std. dev. = 1.28); and importance of roles—2.88 (std. dev. = 0.83).

## 1e. The technology will assume some of the roles that participants expected to fill in a GSS-supported meeting.

Table 10 shows the number of times by group that each role was mentioned as being filled by the TeamFocus technology. Note that across all groups, every role was mentioned at least once. The top three roles that participants thought the technology had filled were: Recorder, Proceduralist, and Gatekeeper. Task roles for TeamFocus were mentioned a total of 160 times (68 percent of the total), versus group-building roles being mentioned 77 times (32 percent of the total).

We also examined the cases in which the Team-Focus technology assumed a role that a participant had expected to fill. There were 50 cases in which the following three conditions were met: (1) before the meeting, the participant expected to fill a specific role (either occasionally or frequently); (2) after the meeting, the participant did not feel he or she had actually filled that role; and (3) the participant stated that the same role was filled by the TeamFocus technology. Not surprisingly, the greatest number of cases where this happened were with the Recorder, Proceduralist, and Gatekeeper roles. Table 11 shows the number of cases where the TeamFocus technology assumed a role from a person who expected to fill it, either partially or fully. $^{3}$ It should be noted that these 50 cases were spread across 28 participants, i.e., there were many instances where a participant felt that TeamFocus had assumed more than one role from him or her. Specifically, 15 people felt TeamFocus had assumed one of their roles, eight people felt the system had assumed two of their roles, one person felt the system had assumed three roles, and four people felt the system had assumed four roles.

These data provide clear support for proposition 1e by showing that the TeamFocus technology actually did assume roles that participants expected to fill. The next question is whether TeamFocus' assumption of a role from a participant affected that person's perceptions of process or outcome effectiveness. We divided the participants into two categories: those for whom TeamFocus had taken over a role (N = 28), and those for whom this was not the case (N = 72). A t-test showed no significant difference between these two groups, either in terms of perceived effectiveness of meeting outcomes (t = -1.02, p = .31) or process (t = -1.08, p = 0.28).

Prescribed role balance and perceived effectiveness

The second set of propositions addressed the relationship between role perceptions and participant satisfaction with meeting outcomes and processes.

2a. Participants will perceive meeting outcomes to be effective in meetings where the prescribed balance of task and group-building roles exists.

2b. Participants will perceive group process to be effective in meetings where the prescribed balance of task and group-building roles exists.

The single, most consistent prescription on roles is that both task and group-building roles are required for meeting effectiveness and participant satisfaction. As was shown in Table 4, the role classification used in this research was divided into task and group-building categories, based on Benne and Sheats' (1948) subdivision and incorporating later classification schemes.

Table 12 shows the average perceived effectiveness of meeting outcomes (perceived quality) and processes (group behavior assessment) by group, ranked from most effective to least effective in terms of outcomes.4 The ranking is slightly different for perceptions of group process, but the top five and bottom five groups are the same. A natural breakpoint occurs between the top five and bottom five groups on both measures, although it should be noted that ratings for most groups were high. A two-tailed t-test of the differences between the means in the top five and bottom five groups indicated a significant difference for both the outcome measure $(t = 5.84, p < .001)$ and the process measure $(t = 9.47, p < .001)$ . The table also shows the percentage of roles that were task related.

The top five groups in Table 12 had the highest perceived effectiveness with both outcomes and process. But the relationship to role prescriptions is less clear. Bormann's (1975) prescription that no more than two-thirds of a group's role behavior be task-oriented was true for all but one of these 10 groups. However, the one group that exceeded Bormann's prescription (with 83 percent of roles being task-related) was in the top five groups, indicating that this group valued the unusually high task orientation. There was no significant correlation between perceived effectiveness of outcome and percent of task roles (r = -.11, p = .77), or between perceived effectiveness of process and percent of task roles (r = .07, p = .88). Propositions 2a and 2b are not supported, insofar as prescriptions about task and group-building roles are concerned.

## Discussion and Interpretation

## Role expectations and perceptions

The data reflect considerable mismatch between the role expectations of initiators and participants. In only four of the 12 role types was there at least a 50 percent match between an initiator's and participant's expectations about the role to be filled by a participant. At a minimum, this inconsistency will affect what an initiator is trying to accomplish in a meeting, since the role resources that the initiator expects to be at a meeting may not be there. This inconsistency might explain partially why meetings fail to "behave" and evolve the way they were planned. Clearly, there is a greater need for initiators and participants to understand one another's expectations about the role that each person should be filling in a meeting. Part of this problem might be solved as initiators and participants acquire more experience with GSS environments.

## Impacts of group support system on roles

Perhaps the most unexpected finding from the data related to GSS impacts on roles was the great variety of roles that people perceived the TFC to fill. The implication is that participants have a rich view of the technology's role in a meeting. Particularly interesting was the extent to which participants felt the TCF filled group-building roles. Table 10 showed that the group-building roles of Gatekeeper and Motivator were among the top five roles most often mentioned as being filled by the TeamFocus technology. This finding may come as a surprise to some GSS designers and researchers who have lamented the intense task focus of GSS technology and tried to suggest ways in which the socio-emotional climate of the group might be supported (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). It would appear that participants view this technology as already providing considerable group-building support for group process.

It is interesting to speculate on the implications of a software system—rather than a human participant—filling group-building roles. The groups in this study seemed comfortable with that idea, since their perceptions of meeting effectiveness were high. This finding is encouraging for designers who have been suggesting a greater role for GSS technology in meeting process via the incorporation of knowledge-based systems that can act as expert process advisors to the group (Ellis, et al., 1988).

Another unexpected finding had to do with the role of Recorder. It came as no surprise that the Recorder was the most often mentioned role for the TFC technology, with 55 percent of participants stating that the TFC filled a Recorder role. But why was that figure not 100 percent? The software captured every comment typed by participants during the meeting, and a printed record of all the comments was provided. The Recorder role seems so obvious that one might question why only slightly more than half the participants saw the technology that way. Since none of the verbal interaction was captured, participants may have viewed the technology as only a partial recorder. They may also have viewed the system as recording only raw input and not interpretation.

The second role for which more responses might have been expected is the Proceduralist. Thirty-eight percent of the participants felt that the TFC filled a proceduralist role. The provision of a structured procedure is at the heart of most GSSs, and one would expect all group members to view the system this way. Again, we spectulate, but if participants are reluctant to view the technology as filling a specific group membership role, then it may be that the facilitator is seen as the proceduralist rather than the technology. With a different type of GSS—one that does not use a facilitator but is user-driven—we might see more emphasis on the proceduralist role of the technology.

Sixteen percent of the participants viewed the GSS as an Information/Opinion Giver. In this case, the system is acting as a clearinghouse of information during the meeting. But it is surprising that 17 percent of participants saw the technology as an Information/Opinion Seeker. For those people, the system goes beyond a simple channel through which ideas are transmitted to a prompter for ideas and participation.

We expected a paradigm shift in classical role theory when applied to GSS-supproted meetings. In a traditional meeting, people speak for themselves, maintaining consistency in the role they present and enact with respect to each other. In GSS-supported meetings, everyone can speak openly through the anonymous communication channel, both for themselves and for others. The separation in a GSS-supported meeting of physical self from one's ideas and thoughts should have created a flexibility and enlargement in role repertoire. Yet, Table 9 showed that the average number of roles people actually filled, compared to what they expected to fill, decreased rather than increased. Participants expected to fill approximately three to four roles each, but actually filled only two to three roles each. From our model, we argued that immediate feedback and recording of all input would result in wide-ranging behavior, which in turn would result in increased role diversity. Further exploration is required through studying control groups, examining content of participants' comments, and varying the specific characteristics of the technology.

## Process and outcome perceptions

Participants' perceptions of the effectiveness of meeting outcomes and processes were generally quite favorable. Effectiveness of meeting outcomes was rated as significantly higher than meeting process. But the relationship of these perceptions to prescribed roles was less clear. Benne and Sheats (1948) state that the combination and balance of a group's task role requirements are related to the group's stage of progress with respect to the task, while group-building role requirements are a function of the group's level of maturity or stage of development as a group. The cross-sectional data collected here are probably insufficient for unraveling the complex relationship between roles and perceptions of effectiveness. Other factors impinge on this relationship as well, e.g., attitudes toward the group support system and task-related norms within the organization's culture. Clearly, the data from this study just scratch the surface of this issue.

## Summary and Conclusions

The objective of this research was to investigate participant roles in GSS-supported meetings, the impact that the GSS would have on roles, and the roles that the GSS itself would fill. The findings provide support for several aspects of the theoretical model. The technology was perceived by participants as assuming roles, some of which were roles they themselves expected to fill. However, the wide-ranging behavior that was expected to result in a greater variety of roles filled by participants did not occur. It may be that behavior ranges widely within a role rather than across roles. For instance, an idea-generator may be encouraged by the recorded input and immediate feedback to generate more ideas rather than to go on and fill other roles.

Several recommendations can be offered for meeting initiators, participants, and facilitators who use a GSS environment. Given the small amount of attention paid to group process by meeting initiators, it may be helpful to include some reminders about the importance of process during meeting planning. Initiators need to be aware of the importance of group process and not be afraid to focus energy on group-building activities and roles. Initiators also need a greater awareness of roles in general and specifically the roles that group participants can fill. Facilitators can play an important part in improving initiator awareness of roles during the premeeting planning sessions. TeamFocus managers might provide a simple template for meeting planners and initiators to serve as a checklist to assure that both task and group-building roles are considered during member selection.

As for participants, it seems clear that group members could benefit from a better-developed concept of roles that should be filled in meetings, and specifically, the roles that they will fill. Participants should ask themselves what they can contribute from a role perspective. They should also be conscious of the possibilities created by a GSS environment, namely that they have an opportunity for both greater role focus and greater role diversity. Focus is possible through allowing the technology to assume roles that humans find less desirable. Diversity is possible when one begins to understand fully the implications of GSS design. The anonymous communication channel allows participants to assume any role they wish—even that of devil's advocate to their own previously stated positions.

For everyone involved in the TFC environment, the results point out the importance of what might be called “role training.” GSS facilitators provide training on how to use the technology, but participants also need training on the role that the tool plays in the group. For example, one group in this study consisted of participants who were highly skilled technically. They tended to view the TFC from a technological perspective and evaluate it as a computer system, rather than from a group process perspective. Coincidentally, that group’s satisfaction with the process was among the lowest of the 10 groups in the study. Another example is that of the Information/Opinion Seeker. It seems desirable to have participants view the system as filling this role, since the Information/Opinion Seeker encourages people to participate and solicits involvement. Perhaps the greatest contribution of the study is in this area—in showing the complex view that participants can have of technology. This area deserves further study for achieving both optimal GSS design and GSS use.

## Limitations of the study

The major strength of this research—that it is a field study—is also its greatest limitation. Early in the project, the sponsor and the researchers agreed on four rules: (1) keep it simple, (2) keep it short, (3) keep it meaningful, and (4) keep it scientifically sound. Decisions about the number of teams studied, which teams would participate, and how data would be collected were the result of a concerted effort to compromise neither the integrity of the research nor the needs of Team-Focus Center users. For example, the five-minute limit on the time for filling out each separate questionnaire was an attempt to balance collecting needed data versus disrupting the natural process of the meeting. The TFC manager asked groups to participate and attempted to mix internal IBM groups with external customers. The researchers suggested a script for the TFC manager to use in describing the study but had no direct control over its use.

The number of groups in this study is small by comparison to numbers typically used in laboratory experiments. The problem of small numbers is a well-understood limitation of field studies and is offset by the richness of the experience. The data collected in the study were perceptual, limited to the carefully bounded set of instruments, and without benefit of control groups. Doing this type of study with organizational groups is a challenge in any setting, much less in a special environment away from the normal workplace where groups have come to get a job done and not be distracted. Observation was not possible because it risked interfering with the success of the session. The lack of control groups was a limitation necessitated by the exploratory nature of the study. But, despite these limitations, moving into a field setting represents a strength of this study.

Another limitation is inherent in the finding that a gap existed between both initiators' and participants' perceptions of role expectations and actual roles filled. We do not know whether this gap is because of a general lack of “role consciousness” and role understanding regardless of the environment in which the meeting takes place. This limitation reinforces the importance of studying control groups and suggests some interesting manipulations with respect to role training.

Finally, it should be noted that this study struggles with the same difficulty that all other studies of group behavior do, namely the inherent differences between groups. For instance, group 10 had both the lowest perceived effectiveness and lowest satisfaction with process of any of the groups. This comes as no surprise, however, when we note that their task was to develop a vision/mission statement—a task that causes conflict and difficulty in many groups. In spite of these limitations, however, this study is the first to address group member roles in a GSS environment, and the data provide insights previously unavailable.

## Directions for future research

Ample opportunity for future work exists, some of which stems from the limitations of this study. Observation of meeting behavior would provide a comparison of actual role behavior to participant perceptions. Content analysis of the group's interaction could provide more objective evidence of actual roles filled and whether or not wide-ranging behavior occurred. Studying one or a few groups over time would enhance knowledge of how roles change as group development occurs and as tasks change. Role training could be incorporated as a preliminary to the meeting and the results compared to the "baseline" data provided here. A longitudinal study could also provide a better view of how the relative proportion of task versus group-building roles changes as the group develops. Longitudinal studies would help fill the gap in our knowledge of technology-supported groups that have both a history as group and that become familiar with the technology as they work with it over time.

A series of controlled studies that explore key components of the conceptual model can serve to unravel the complex relationships between the characteristics of group interaction and role outcomes. Since little is known about role expectations and perceptions in traditional environments, the logical next step is to collect data from control groups. With a more established basis for comparison, we can suggest further studies that link this work to existing role constructs. For instance, role overload in traditional environments is thought to lead to dissatisfaction with group process. Role overload might be diminished in a GSS-supported meeting in cases where the technology has assumed some role needs. Conversely, role overload might be increased where imbalance in role representation exists; in this case, a group member might feel tremendous pressure to fill multiple roles due to simultaneous conversations.

We may also want to expand our definition of what constitutes the “technological environment” in which groups interact. Some TeamFocus centers use music and lighting to stimulate creativity during electronic brainstorming. These “technologies” are playing the role of idea generators. These are opportunities for broadening our idea of how roles are filled in group interaction.

Some of the findings from this study and the directions for future research are relevant to traditional environments as well as GSS-supported meetings. But a group support system provides a unique kind of environment with great potential for maximizing the effectiveness of human roles. This study has shown how a GSS interacts with and supplements human roles. Further work can examine the intricacies of role behavior in a GSS environment with an eye to helping group members get the most out of an appropriate combination of human and machine resources.

## About the Authors

Ilze Zigurs is an assistant professor in the School of Business and Administration at the University of Colorado, Boulder. Dr. Zigurs holds a Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Minnesota. Her primary research interests are in the use and impact of computer and communications technology for the support of collaborative activity.

Kenneth A. Kozar is a member of the information systems faculty at the University of Colorado in Boulder. He has been on the faculties of several universities and has considerable experience as an MIS practitioner. He and various co-authors were winners in the SIM International Paper Award Competition in 1985 and 1989. He is an associate editor of the MIS Quarterly and on the Editorial Board of IS/Analyzer.
