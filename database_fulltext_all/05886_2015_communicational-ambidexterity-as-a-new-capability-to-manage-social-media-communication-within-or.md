## a b s t r a c t

Central to our inquiry is how organizations manage and accommodate conflicting demands in managing internal communication when they adopt social media that allows for more open and distributed communication. Drawing on ambidexterity theory we investigate and analyze the tensions and capabilities necessary to effectively manage two distinct types of internal communication (1) organizationally produced content and (2) usergenerated content. We propose and unpack the concept of communicational ambidexterity to theorize the capability that enables organizations to accommodate and efficiently manage these two potentially conflicting modes of communication within organizations. - 2015 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## Introduction

Rapid diffusion of social media is (re)shaping the landscape of communication in contemporary society, with deep impact in markets and the functioning of businesses (Boudreau and Lakhani, 2013; Hanna et al., 2011; Pitt and Berthon, 2010) While prior studies have demonstrated various benefits, such as fostering user engagement, participation, knowledge reuse and collective innovation (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2010; Majchrzak et al., 2013; Skågeby, 2010), understanding social media’s impacts and potential implications of their adoption for organizations remains a fertile research ground for IS researchers (Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak, 2010).

In particular, the growing research on social media has highlighted two key areas that are still relatively under-theorized. Firstly, although more attention is being paid to the potential negative and unanticipated consequences of social media (e.g. Champoux et al., 2012; Hildebrand et al., 2013) and its role in social change (Mohajerani et al., 2015), we still often see social media as a technological panacea for addressing communication and social interaction problems (Cook. 2008: Goh et al 2013; Granados and Gupta, 2013). Conceptualizing the dyadic nature and potential of social media in unleashing both positive and negative effects is still under-explored (Baptista et al. 2010). Secondly, studies examining social media-related phenomena primarily take a view from the community or society levels (e.g. Miller and Tucker, 2013; Oh et al., 2013) considering for example how organizations interact with customers (Aral et al., 2013) and other external stakeholders (e.g. Fournier and Avery, 2011; Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson, 2013). However, the effects of social media within an organization, when adopted to support and replace other internal forms of communication and interaction, is a growing and important area of research (Leonardi et al., 2013; Leonardi, 2014; Treem and Leonardi, 2012; Vaast and Kaganer, 2013), but still relatively under-theorized (Majchrzak et al., 2013).

To enrich our understanding and address both of these issues, we conduct an exploratory, in-depth, longitudinal case study at a multi-national company, which we call Tudor Rose Telecommunications (TRT). We study TRT as the company was gradually introducing social media alongside a move to increase mobile work practices. Social media is significant in this context because it allows users to stay connected and ‘‘be present’’ even when physically dispersed (McGriff, 2012; Subramaniam et al., 2013) ultimately contributing to a greater sense of belonging among mobile workers (Haslam et al., 2003). However, social media also has the potential to produce conflicting views and opinions that might over-shadow the message the organization wants to convey to all its employees. This raises an interesting theoretical question – how does an organization accommodate conflicting demands in a more open communication environment, preserving a sense of coherency in internal communication while allowing different (and potentially divergent) voices to be heard? In order to address this research question, we consider the role that social media plays in increasing the visibility of conflicting perspectives and we consider what capabilities and mechanisms surrounding social media can help to address the tension of allowing many voices while still retaining a coherent organizational message.

Our findings demonstrate that social media can be effectively used in a way that reconciles these conflicting demands in internal communication. In conceptualizing our findings, we draw on the theory of ambidexterity (Cao et al., 2009; Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004) to show how a distinctive capability, which we call ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’, was developed in the studied case organization. Our contributions are threefold. In addition to enriching our understanding of how social media has the potential to surface conflicting viewpoints while at the same time retain coherency and integrity in internal organizational communication, we extend the existing intellectual landscape by theorizing a new type of ambidexterity and its enabling mechanisms. In doing this, we make our third contribution, which is to conceptualize communicational ambidexterity as a new organizational capability that naturally develops as organizations learn to accommodate social media as a new way of communicating, sharing and participating in organizational conversations.

The paper is structured as follows. We first introduce the current debates and perspectives that are fundamental in investigating and theorizing communicational ambidexterity. Second, we outline our methodological considerations and research processes that depict the rationale behind our research design and data analysis processes. Third, to illustrate our case findings, we present a narrative, which outlines the modes of communication encountered by TRT and its journey in addressing them. Fourth, in the Analysis and Discussion section, we identify the key characteristics of communicational ambidexterity and outline how various enabling mechanisms function to support this capability. We conclude by identifying the theoretical implications of our findings and areas where future research efforts would be useful.

## Current debates and perspectives

Central to the theoretical contribution of this paper is our proposed concept of ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’. We define communicational ambidexterity as the capability to simultaneously address different and often conflicting communication needs that exist in an organization's internal communication. by achieving complementarity between different communication modes. This concept is developed based on a research process where we iterated between reflecting on relevant literature and our data analysis. Here we refer to communication modes not as distinct communication channels (as is sometimes used in the literature). but rather as distinct communication environments, being either “univocal" or “multivocal’’ (Balmer, 2001; Huang et al., 2013). Conventionally, the term univocal refers to having one single and unambiguous meaning, while the term multivocal represents the opposite, where meaning is manifold and ambiguous. We have extended the conventional use of univocal and multivocal to include the organizational context where communication takes place, while also capturing the new dynamics emergent from the use of social media in this context. Specifically, we use these two terms to capture the differences between (1) institutional, formal, centralized and mostly top-down mode of communication which we characterize as univocal, with (2) multivocality which we use to refer to a more user-centric, distributed informal and inherently participative mode of communication (Huang et al., 2013)

In this section, we outline the key theoretical building blocks that we draw on, namely: conflicting communication modes; the capability of ambidexterity; and its enabling mechanisms.

## Conflicting communication modes

To unpack the above research question, it is essential to understand the tensions associated with communication content from potentially conflicting communication modes. This is inevitable when an organization introduces participative media to support a more open internal communication environment. The use of social media to support internal communication widens participation (Denyer et al., 2011) and interactive dialogue (Miles and Mangold, 2014), which then increases the multivocality of the internal communication environment. However, a more multivocal communication environment also means that sources and content are more diverse, So instead of being controlled and formally published by the organization as expected in a univocal environment, multivocality supports and encourages user-generated content. Despite the potential gains of a multivocal environment in development of social identity (Haslam et al., 2003) and encouraging participation and diversity (McGriff, 2012) there are real challenges in managing this mode of communication concurrently with univocality. This is because, while univocality is based on promoting a single organizational voice, multivocality allows for different voices that might therefore clash with the prevailing voice from central teams. Cooren et al. (2011, p. 1149) highlight the important role of communication as part of the fabric that ‘‘constitutes organizing and organization’’. In environments where multivocal modes of communication are encouraged, communication is open and distributed and characterized by the growth of user-generated content (UGC) (Goh et al., 2013). This contrasts with the univocal mode based on content generated centrally by small teams under the guidance of senior management, which we describe here as organization published content (OPC). In theory, two of the effects of introducing social media within organizations are that it supports both greater reach in disseminating OPC (Huang et al., 2013), and it eases the creation and sharing of UGC (Goh et al., 2013; Razmerita et al., 2014). Therefore, it seems reasonable to expect that the introduction of social media for internal communication should simultaneously benefit the co-existence and co-development of OPC and UGC.

However, developing OPC and UGC concurrently can be challenging for two distinct yet interrelated reasons. First, OPC and UGC require different organizational support to function and flourish. Second, given its institutional and constitutive significance, OPC is often protected as a crucial managerial resource and source of power (Taylor, 2011). Therefore, introducing a multivocal mode of communication to enable UGC will upset established patterns of communication and power relationships previously reinforced by a more centralized control system (Duane and Finnegan, 2003). What particularly interests us is whether multivocality and UGC can exist alongside univocality and OPC, with multivocality potentially complementing univocality in internal communication, despite the above mentioned tensions between these two modes of communication. In other words, is it possible for an organization to become ambidextrous in its internal communication by addressing the tensions associated with being simultaneously multivocal and univocal? And how can an organization develop complementarity between these two modes of communication? To progress our inquiry, we reflect and draw on the theory of ambidexterity. An integrative framework of the ambidexterity literature is developed and presented as Fig. 1 to synthesize two aspects, including the capability of ambidexterity and its enabling mechanisms.

## Part I of conceptual basis: the capability of ambidexterity

Central to addressing conflicting managerial demands is the requirement for simultaneously developing solutions for problems that are often contradictory in their existence and functioning (Andriopoulos and Lewis, 2009; Tushman and O’Reilly, 1996). For instance, Adler et al. (1999) show that it can be highly problematic for organizations to be efficient and at the same time remain flexible. This is because these two capabilities – efficiency and flexibility – function in rather distinctive manners and are each supported by specific organizational settings. Thus, these two capabilities often represent trade-offs that organizations need to manage. Research shows that firms that are able to simultaneously achieve efficiency and flexibility can achieve exceptional organizational performance, and are described as ‘‘being ambidextrous’’ (Duncan, 1976: Tushman and O'Reilly. 1996)

Research on ambidexterity is found across a number of different areas. For example, Bartlett and Ghoshal (1988) illustrate how ambidextrous organizations are able to concurrently ensure global integration and local responsiveness. In conceptualizing organizational learning, March (1991) outlined the paradoxical relationship between knowledge exploration and knowledge exploitation and the need to ensure an organization’s overall variability in learning in order to maintain competitiveness. Later accounts (e.g. Andriopoulos and Lewis, 2009; Cao et al., 2009; He and Wong, 2004) have focused on how exploring new business opportunities can work side-by-side with the exploitation of existing niches. In IS research, ambidexterity has been applied to a variety of different contexts, including deploying conflicting methodologies in systems development (Tiwana, 2010; Vinekar et al., 2006), balancing strategic choices between customized and packaged software products (Napier et al., 2011) and concurrently fostering sustainability with profitability in a telecommunication company (Du et al., 2013). Despite these variations in the focus of ambidexterity research, many have adopted Gibson and Birkinshaw’s (2004) distinction between structural and contextual mechanisms as the conceptual basis for explaining this phenomenon, which is also the basis for this study on internal communication, as we discuss next.

## Part II of conceptual basis: enabling mechanisms of ambidexterity

Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) propose two mechanisms of ambidexterity to synthesize a diverse range of accounts previously published, in particular those based on the intellectual traditions of Duncan (1976) and March (1991). First, in terms of structural mechanisms, the idea of dual structure, originating from Duncan (1976), suggests that ambidexterity is fostered by creating distinct organizational functions or units, each with the ability to address different managerial demands (Tushman and O’Reilly, 1996). For instance, while R&D is structured to tolerate the uncertainty commonly encountered in innovation, manufacturing units are often organized to maximize efficiency by minimizing coordination costs and reducing ambiguity (Adler et al., 1999; Grant, 1996). However, despite the usefulness of structural mechanisms, Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) suggest that these alone are not sufficient and propose that contextual mechanisms are also needed to support ‘‘the behavioral capacity to simultaneously demonstrate alignment and adaptability across an entire business unit’’ (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004; p. 209). Gibson and Birkinshaw indicate that alignment refers to the level of coherence between an organization’s strategic goals and the activities that are performed to actualize these goals. Adaptability on the other hand is an organization’s collective ability to effectively adjust, mobilize and reconfigure internal resources, activities and processes to address the changing demands from its external environment (Ghoshal and Bartlett, 1994). While alignment is facilitated by mechanisms, such as collective trust and organizational support, characterized as ‘‘social context’’, adaptability is enabled by an organization’s ability to introduce and exercise ‘‘discipline’’ of its workforce and to ‘‘stretch’’ them to unleash the workforce’s potential to meet exceptional demands, which is then related to ‘‘performance management’’.

Later accounts, such as that developed by Jansen et al. (2009), reinforce Gibson and Birkinshaw's (2004) view that dual structures alone are far from being sufficient for achieving ambidexterity. This is because dual structures do not automatically address the need to ‘‘mobilize, coordinate, and integrate dispersed contradictory efforts, and to allocate, reallocate, combine, and recombine resources and assets across dispersed exploratory and exploitative units’’ (Jansen et al., 2009, p. 806). Thus, it is clear that structural mechanisms need to be reinforced with contextual, human-centric mechanisms, such as individual abilities and roles, and leadership (e.g. Raisch and Birkinshaw, 2008). For instance, Smith and Tushman (2005) and Kang and Snell (2009) both highlight the importance of individuals’ cognitive capacities in acquiring, processing and making sense of paradoxical situations and of organizational settings, which facilitate or prohibit the development of such capacities. Similar findings are reported by Beckman (2006) who outlines the importance of diversity in a top management team’s experience.

Fig. 1 captures the above discussion by synthesizing key conceptual elements of ambidexterity. Essentially, this figure consists of three components. The first component is the enabling mechanisms, which could be structural, such as partitioning and switching (Adler et al., 1999), or contextual, such as stretch, discipline, trust and support (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004), or a combination of both (Jansen et al., 2009; Tiwana, 2010). The second component refers to dichotomies of distinctive activities of ambidexterity, for example knowledge exploration and exploitation (March, 1991), efficiency and flexibility (Adler et al., 1999) adaptability and alignment (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004), and in our case, univocality and multivocality It is vital to note that the two activities exist inherently in tension, due to differences in their orientations and functioning. The third component represents the distinctive organizational performance that is the outcome of ambidexterity.

## Theorizing ambidexterity in organizational communication

The theory of ambidexterity has not been applied previously to conceptualize the dynamics of organizational communication, in particular when social media is a main feature raising a potential tension between univocality and multivocality. Nevertheless, previous insights can help us theorize communicational ambidexterity. For instance, previous research (e.g., Jansen et al., 2009; Tiwana, 2010 and Napier et al., 2011) has suggested that the theorization of ambidexterity should take into account more than one set of enabling mechanisms in developing ambidexterity as an organizational capability. To achieve this, and given that our study captures the use of social media in simultaneously promoting multivocality and univocality, we aim to identify current research on social media use within organizations in order to add the dynamics of social media to the model of ambidexterity presented in Fig. 1 above. In particular, we identify three distinctive yet interrelated points that we believe are useful for our theorization of this new type of ambidexterity.

The first area focuses on the managerial benefits afforded by social media. In addition to enhancing collaboration, social interaction and participation (Denyer et al., 2011; Haefliger et al., 2011; Wu, 2013), social media broadly contributes to the structuring and sharing of dispersed organizational knowledge (e.g. Majchrzak et al., 2013; Yuan et al., 2013). Razmerita et al. (2014) suggests that organizations rely on different social media tools to support these new practices and behaviors hence it is appropriate to consider the totality of the digital environment rather than each tool in isolation. Our concern was therefore to cover how an organization's communication platform as a whole, including traditional and more modern social media, can contribute to the development of communicational ambidexterity as a new organizational capability

The second area emphasizes managerial issues and challenges arising from the use of social media, such as potential for breaches of intellectual property (Väyrynen et al., 2013), and concerns with ensuring security and privacy (Andriole, 2010) as well as fostering employee engagement (Agerdal-Hjermind, 2014). We note that while there is a dearth of research that focuses on the negative impacts of social media, there are a few more recent papers that have pointed out that social media can be a double-edge sword (Hildebrand et al., 2013). For instance, Miles and Mangold (2014) indicate that social media

However, the specifics of these mechanisms in simultaneously supporting univocality and multivocality needs further examination and this is the focus of our case analysis.

Summing up, while the current literature suggests tensions between OPC (as a univocal mode of communication) and UGC (as a multivocal mode of communication) we suggest that it may be possible for organizations to develop these two modes of communication simultaneously over time. Responding to calls for more research on communication as the constitutive process of organizing (Cooren et al., 2011; Spee and Jarzabkowski, 2011; Taylor, 2011), our research aims to empirically explore this suggestion. The aim of our research is therefore to consider how organizations are able to encourage simultaneously univocal (OPC) and multivocal (UGC) modes of communication when they adopt social media to support internal communication.

## Methods

This study is part of an on-going research project aimed at examining the development, usage and governance of social media for internal communication in large multi-national organizations operating in high velocity industries, such as professional services, finance and telecommunications (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000). The research design involves an in-depth, interpretive and longitudinal case study approach (Walsham, 1995). The strengths of this approach in exploring research phenomena that are highly situated and context dependent are well developed (Pan and Tan, 2011). To achieve our objective of documenting and theorizing communicational ambidexterity we adopt the perspectives and concepts outlined in the previous section as ‘‘sensitizing devices’’ to guide data collection and analysis and eventual theorization of this phenomena (Klein and Myers, 1999).

The selection of the case organization was determined by the requirement to select an organization that makes extensive use of social media as a communication platform to support internal communication. For this reason we chose an organization that was an early adopter of web technology to support large-scale mobile work: we name it TRT. More specifically, TRT was moving to grow its mobile workforce, in particular its engineers, and was facing a managerial challenge: how to ‘‘allow for effective mobile working (i.e., virtual) and at the same time help employees feel a sense of belonging to the firm?’’ This question surfaces not just as an issue of how to establish new mobile work practices, but also how to create a communication environment to facilitate mobile working which at the same time allows these employees to feel a sense of belonging to the organization (despite the reduction in face-to-face communication with other employees and supervisors). The practical problem faced by TRT was thus directly related to our theoretical question. This gave us the opportunity to study a topic of research that was both relevant for the company and theoretically insightful for us. In fact, establishing the practical significance of the study was an ongoing important driving force for the research, which allowed us to stay engaged with the company for a long period of time and also extract deeper insights from our interactions with the organization.

## Data collection

Data collection at TRT started in 2006, and our research effort to continuously document the case organization’s journey in using social media ended in 2011. The data collection has been carried out to fulfil several distinctive yet interrelated purposes. First, we collected data that allowed us to sketch the case organization’s overall communication landscape. This included the functionality, general usage and governance of social media within the organization. Second, we collected data that characterize the promoting and opposing forces in shaping the dynamics of internal communication. Third, we collected reflexive insights that represented individuals’ experiences. Here, we did not limit data collection to just the actual experiences of using social media, but also considered the experiences of various organizational members whose roles are related to the management of social media and internal communication.

A number of different data collection methods were used, including interviews, documentation, informal dialogue and onsite observation, for triangulation and ensuring the reliability of findings (Klein and Myers, 1999; Pan and Tan, 2011). In total, 65 semi-structured interviews have been conducted since March 2006 as part of an ongoing working relationship with an industry group called the Digital Workplace Group (DWG). DWG benchmarked TRT’s intranet annually over a number of years and we followed this process while collecting our own notes in the field. Each year between 2006 and 2011 we conducted 10–12 interviews, following in part a core group of stakeholders (all the core team involved in managing social

## Data analysis

Given the exploratory nature of the research, we have incorporated the technique of continuous iteration between the relevant theoretical perspectives from the literature, collected data and emerging findings (Eisenhardt, 1989; Orlikowski, 1993). Conceptual elements reviewed in the previous section, in particular those presented in Fig. 1, played the role of a ‘‘rake’’, a metaphor originally used by Carney (1972). According to Carney this metaphor is useful because, ‘‘a new rake is not needed for each new job of raking’’ (ibid., p. 6), so that there is no need to constantly reinvent the wheel, given that suitable conceptual tools are already available to be utilized and built on. Moreover, ‘‘the rake does not unconsciously select only those objects which are of interest to us, or which suit our views’’ (Carney, 1972, p. 6–7). By utilizing some of these existing concepts systematically, validity and reliability can be achieved by paying similar amounts of attention to all collected data.

Our data analysis approach contained four interconnected steps, including ‘‘summarizing’’, ‘‘clustering’’, ‘‘displaying’’ and ‘‘comparing’’ the data (Easterby-Smith et al., 1991). Key points embedded in each interview transcript, document and field note were summarized by two of the authors. These steps were carried out during January and March of 2013. By applying the conceptual building blocks shown in Fig. 1, we were able to cluster the data collected from each source into different themes, including enabling mechanisms, activities that existed in tension and behavioral outcomes. We then cross-checked the findings from the two authors to identify and address different views and to ensure the quality and reliability of coding (Pan and Tan, 2011). With these preliminary findings, we then displayed them to articulate their inter-relationships in order to construct a process view of our researched phenomena. For instance, how did one mechanism relate to another, and how did one particular mechanism help address experienced tensions and influence the outcomes? The technique of comparing was carried out to evaluate and contrast the emerging findings with the existing literature in general, and Fig. 1 more specifi cally. We searched specifically for empirical insights that could not be fully explained by the current literature and Fig. 1 in order to identify and ground our theoretical development. For instance, the distinction between structural and contextual mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 1, was useful in terms of analytical separation, but fell short in its ability to capture the dynamic inter-relationships among different mechanisms. To address this shortfall, we revisited our emerging findings to refine our core theoretical offering, in this case the concept of communicational ambidexterity, including its enabling mechanisms, capabilities and behavioral outcomes.

## Case findings

To set the context of our case findings, we chronologically present key events and communication challenges (see Fig. 2). We use a ‘‘rope’’ as a metaphor to symbolize how impacts created by events and challenges are continuous and inter-related. We start our narrative with TRT’s ‘‘Mobile Working Initiative’’ which we trace back to 2005 in TRT’s history.

## Becoming a mobile working organization

In 2005 TRT started an initiative to enable mobile work for most of its workforce so they could work remotely, either from home or from regional local offices. This initiative focused initially on 30,000 field engineers and was aimed partially at saving expensive costs in real estate in London and enhancing employee productivity, while at the same time improving customers’ experience. For instance, before mobile working was introduced field engineers would go to their offices to download their daily work schedules, and then travel to clients’ premises. This strategic initiative was based on the deployment of a new digital communication platform (i.e., intranet) in order to allow for all the areas of the organization to stay connected despite the lack of shared physical office space

One of the implications of this approach was the need to concurrently cater for different communication needs at global (corporate) and local (lines of business) levels. When recalling the situation back in 2006 one interviewee explained: ‘‘there was no formal framework for balancing global/local content, and ensure access to regional and local organizational sites and news feeds.’’ One problem was that local information was located a ‘‘few clicks away’’ from TRT’s homepage, so too many steps were needed to find local information – so there was ‘‘a lot of operational information that people were drowning in’’. TRT also needed to keep staff up to date on what was happening across the organization and increase engagement and a ‘‘sense of belonging’’ as face-to-face contact reduced with the move toward a more mobile working model. ## Organizational response to the growing use of UGC

Over the duration of our involvement with TRT, the intranet became increasingly important as a communication tool within the organization. Several interviewees stated that it was ‘‘the life-blood of the company’’ which is ‘‘alive and evolving’’. TRT’s intranet had gradually become the primary channel of internal communication and the one with the greatest impact and reach across the organization. A significant milestone in TRT history was reached in 2009 (see Fig. 2) when all the field engineers were provided with intranet access and this officially became the platform used to support the work of nearly 90,000 employees across 61 countries.

The growing importance of TRT’s intranet as a central platform for work was accompanied by the growth of social media tools available on the intranet. This initial reluctance to engage with social media by some middle managers did not, however, reflect the attitudes of the top management team. In particular the CEO, was very quick to adopt social media. As shown in various internal reports, the CEO saw great potential in using social media to enhance communication among the growing mobile workforce. This drive from the top of the organization was accompanied by business initiatives to adopt social media within local teams. Senior leaders did not impose cross-organizational systems but instead created a light usability and navigation framework. This allowed local teams to operate independently and adjust the platform to their specific communication needs. After about 4 years, in particular after the implementation of the cultural change initiative (see below), engagement with and among employees through social media became the norm and most senior management including the CEO were active in online forums and Q&As. Leadership setting the example played an important role to breakdown the reluctance among the middle managers to use social media as the main communication channel within TRT. Secondly, the focus on using social media as a strategic driver for the business caused tension between a central message that the organizational leaders wanted to communicate and competing demands from employees’ interests and needs at the local level. Although they were interested in hearing from their leaders, they were also interested in sharing and communicating horizontally across the organization. Indeed, growth in adoption originated more from UGC services that allowed employees to communicate and share rather than from adding feedback features to established OPC services, as detailed in Table 1. The following quote by the Chief Editor of TRT’s intranet shows this growth in the use of UGC services:

‘‘We are seeing changes on Blog Central. Today, we have 450 blogs. 350 are active; 100 have limited activity. We have realized that blogging has moved well beyond enthusiasts and technologists. It is now used by ‘normal’ people. People want blogs for a wider range of reasons.’’

However the tension mentioned previously between using social media as a strategic tool and local demands is also visi ble in the following quotation:

‘‘For social media there is not a common mindset. There are several collections of mindsets. In TRT there are enough early adopters to get momentum. Now we are at the ‘here come the normal people’ stage, people doing everyday jobs that have a need to communicate and find that social media is the most efficient way to do that in terms of benefit against effort.’

Our findings show that despite this tension, the growing use of social media and the expansion of UGC was not at the expense of centrally controlled and sanctioned OPC communication. Rather, our analysis demonstrates that UGC and OPC services were managed differently (see Table 1 for different governance mechanisms). For instance, services, such as TRT Today, TRT A-Z and TRT Directory strongly supported OPC, while services such as Blog Central and un-moderated discussion forums supported UGC. The growth of UGC as a much quicker and easier way to publish news and information attracted interest from site owners who felt that they could overcome the more robust and strict management of OPC by using UGC tools and services. The management team was concerned that this would compromise trust in the information provided to users, as it would be sanctioning information without appropriate oversight.

Another concern was the potential confusion that users might encounter in differentiating OPC from UGC and vice versa. In other words, how could the sources of the content be clearly indicated, so that users were clearly informed? One of the initiatives to address these concerns was to create different color schemes, one for OPC and one for UGC. By using different colors for each type of content, users were able to differentiate between the two types of content and adjust their expectations about the quality of the sources of information. Even though this segregation was deployed intentionally to allow employees to differentiate between content that had been curated and checked by editorial systems, and information posted by users without moderation the platform was designed to provide a coherent and consistent user experience across the two types of content areas. Indeed, some UGC services were developed to respond to and complement OPC services, for instance, YourViews with TRTToday and TRTpedia with TRTHelp.

Next we consider the distinctive organizational culture that formed over time to support TRT’s development of a communicational ambidexterity capability to manage efficiently the conflicting demands from the two modes of communication.

## Nurturing a communication culture for OPC and UGC

TRT came to realize that they needed to segregate the two modes of communication and create separate governance structures to manage OPC and UGC services and content. We found that one of the main driving forces to enable efficient management of both UGC and OPC was a shift in the communication culture of TRT, an initiative started in 2010 as shown in Fig. 2. As explained earlier the emphasis on the use of social media was part of a wider process of transformation in the organization. One of the pillars of this strategy was to enable remote work through digital tools, and the second was to change the culture of the company to encourage behaviors that enabled the company to appropriate the new ways of working now based on a more mobile working model. In particular, the ability to mentally shift from being passive receivers of OPC to participants in the UGC environment was important in building the organization-wide capability in the new environment. Encouraging this new mindset was achieved by constantly reminding people that ‘‘everyone is both a user and a pub lisher now.’’ This is expressed in the following quotation by a senior leader:

‘‘The intranet is a key strategic enabler. It is about communicating with the employees. It is extensively used. It is not just a case of pushing pages. It is used to gain feedback, create new ways of working. It is very powerful tool to help set strategic direction.’’

More specifically, the intranet with its social media features was perceived to be a key part of a more ambitious program of cultural and strategic change within TRT. The company saw internal communication as a key enabler to modernize TRT in terms of attracting and retaining talent, creating a new culture of flexible and remote work by providing a platform for employees to work from anywhere, and reducing the dependency on office based work. The intranet was therefore integral to the strategy of TRT, and used both to communicate strategic information but also as a medium to keep its workforce engaged given that as an organization TRT was becoming much more geographically dispersed.

To support this transformation, TRT created a programme to promote four core new values for the organization: ‘‘open’’, ‘‘inspiring’’, ‘‘straightforward’’ and ‘‘helpful’’. Openness was an important value closely related to the adoption of social media. The organization was keen to encourage employees to feel that they ‘‘had a voice’’ in the organization and share information and ideas in a more open and transparent environment. However they were also keen to highlight that with this power came responsibility and that all comments and views should be attributable to ensure that people do not misuse this opportunity. An interviewee in the internal communications department explained:

‘‘We are trying to engender a culture of free speech, in the sense that anyone can say what they want, not anonymously, and you are accountable for everything you say.’

TRT was very serious in creating an ‘‘open’’ culture and added many features to encourage participation and interactivity. This came through in our interviews with users, for example one stated:

‘‘We have a fairly popular blogging platform. Anyone can share opinions. . . Anyone can write in comments. Depending on the discussion, anyone, up to and including the CEO level, can get involved in providing views or add to the debate.’

The second organizational value was to be ‘‘inspiring’’. This value was also closely related to the use of the new social media platform. It was an important driver for adoption and for producing useful content and contributions. To be inspiring was key to ensure that the ideas shared would be relevant and important to the users. This was seen to address a concern shared in an internal review that TRT’s intranet has provided a platform to share knowledge and good practice, yet its strategic value for ‘‘encouraging, facilitating and showcasing innovation’’ was yet to be realized.

By providing users with services to voice their concerns and ideas openly, TRT was simultaneously opening itself up to possible abuse of this new power by its users. However, from our interviews we identified only one incident where one user showed disrespect to the senior team and in a ‘‘particularly strong and forceful way’’. After some discussion between this user and the Intranet team, the user agreed to withdraw the comments made. Abuse was minimal at TRT and this was seen to be linked to the strong values that were promoted alongside the development of the social media strategy.

The last value that contributed to a new culture of use of social media was to be ‘‘helpful’’. It referred to two aspects. On the one hand, it refers to the service orientation of TRT’s intranet. For instance, two internal reports state the following as guiding principles for new developments: to ‘‘enhance user experience’’, ‘‘users find what they need’’, ‘‘information built around users’ needs’’ and ‘‘users control information consumption’’. On the other hand, the other element strives to foster users’ sense of ownership and shared responsibilities when communicating in TRT’s intranet.

These four core values were introduced during our data collection period, and some interviewees indicated that they had started to witness their effects on users in 2011, for instance, in the growing participation in different UGC channels and short, precise and accessible content in OPC. Nevertheless, some interviewees also acknowledged the additional time required to fully incorporate these values into TRT’s ‘‘DNA’’. More generally, our findings indicate that the new values enabled TRT to develop the skills and ability to cope with the two distinct communication modes by promoting four new corporate values, closely linked to the use of social media. We review how each of these values of being ‘‘open’’, ‘‘inspiring’’, ‘‘straightforward’’ and ‘‘helpful’’ created the conditions that reinforced the intended behaviors of being both accurate and factual when producing and digesting OPC (straightforward and helpful) and simultaneously open and transparent linked to UGC (‘‘open’’ and ‘‘inspiring’’). These values contributed to creating the cultural context necessary for the effective use of the two modes of communication in TRT, so that employees engaged in online discussions for different purposes and were able to distinguish between personal viewpoints published by users (UGC) and facts checked by experts (OPC).

The following section outlines our theorization of these findings, and further develops the idea of ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’ as a capability that develops over time as the organization learns to accommodate the two opposing modes of communication simultaneously.

## Analysis and discussion: communicational ambidexterity

We now further develop the concept of ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’ to theorize the ongoing development of this distinctive capability which enabled TRT to simultaneously support univocality (associated with OPC) and multivocality (associated with UGC) by creating complementarity between these two distinct and potentially contradictory modes of organizational communication. We present a framework that conceptualizes this capability and we also offer a process view of its development. However instead of applying the structural and contextual distinction, our analysis emphasizes how these mechanisms individually and collectively contribute to the development of communicational ambidexterity. This is then reflected in the efficient and complementary use of distinct modes of communication within organizations. Fig. 3 below captures the essence of this. We believe that this framework is a useful way to understand the complex dynamics of social media as a communicational platform within organizations.

Our analysis suggests that a communicational ambidexterity capability involves the use and blending of new communication tools through establishing distinctive, yet complementary, governance structures and a supportive organizational culture. We saw that the blending of these different dimensions in the day-to-day communication of employees (Cooren et al., 2011) enabled complementarity to be established between univocality promoted by OPC communication and multivocality by UGC communication. As shown in the case, complementarity was reflected in how the two modes of communication co-existed to form a balanced whole. For instance, there was a focus on how much communication space is devoted to each mode of content so that users do not feel that the voice of top management in OPC communication is too strong and over-powering UGC communication. Equally important, complementarity is created as a distributed sense-making process (Cao et al., 2009), based on a gradual development of shared understanding and acceptance between users and managers of how to use diverse modes of communication in a more open communication environment.

Another important analytical finding is the emergent nature of this new capability and the emphasis on this capability as a process rather than an outcome. To be ambidextrous and cope with conflicting demands, as reflected in the case of TRT, required creating and recombining tools, governance structures, culture and practices to effectively respond and adapt to new demands. For instance, the engagement of users in debating, revising and, on some occasions, co-producing corporate strategic blueprints, formed vibrant, interactive and participative communication spaces which evolved alongside more formal spaces and content. For example the ‘‘Your Views’’ and Executive Q&As encouraging user participation were closely linked to more formal sites and areas of the intranet published by central teams.

The above conditions underpinned the development of ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’ as a new organizational capability at TRT. Next we elaborate the mechanisms that enable the development and functioning of this new capability. We first depict how the ability to architect the communication environment in a way that segregates the two modes of communication played a role in forming different expectations for each mode. Secondly, we review how the ability to formally regulate different modes of communication worked in conjunction with the nurturing of self-regulated usage behaviors in creating a language that users were able to refer to when adopting each mode. Thirdly we review how the ability to adapt organizational culture was also central in fostering the new capability. We use Fig. 3 as the underlying structure to discuss each element in turn.

## Architecting communication

‘‘Communicational ambidexterity’’ involved the ability to architect content and services by setting boundaries between the two distinct modes of communication. Table 1 provides a useful and structured overview of this by detailing how these services functioned and their distinct governance and content management systems. By looking at these services as a whole, it is clear that the mechanism of architecting communication was grounded in the possibilities afforded by the technical platform that allowed the creation of new terminology, layout and color schemes to distinguish the different needs and expectations of OPC and UGC. For instance, while blogging was mainly ‘‘conversational’’ and was used to engage and exchange viewpoints, wiki-based tools were used for knowledge sharing and collaboration purposes. By contrast, ‘‘TRT Today’’ was setup as an online news desk used to broadcast headlines from senior leaders.

By setting apart different tools to address different communication needs, the underlying logic behind the architecture of TRT’s communication platform mirrors the concept of ‘‘partitioning’’ (Adler et al., 1999). In other words, each tool is used to enable and develop a rather distinctive space across the platform and training is provided to ensure that individuals understand how to utilize the various publishing tools available to them. By partitioning these spaces, different communication dynamics and processes can be individually catered for. In this way, multivocality can be concurrently developed alongside univocality. For instance, encouraging each user to embrace his/her ‘voice’ affords some of these communicational spaces to permit the principle of ‘‘multivocality’’ (Haslam et al., 2003; McGriff, 2012). However, it does not mean that ‘‘univocality’’ – the voice from the organization (Balmer, 2001) conveyed through ‘‘TRT Today’’ for example – has to be compromised. Through partitioning, these two can be nurtured side-by-side. We suggest that the process and ability to partition TRT’s communication platform according to different modes of communication was an important element in developing the new capability of ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’.

## Governing communication

However, structurally separating each service/communicative space as shown in Table 1, may not warrant sufficient synergy to develop complementary between univocality and multivocality. We saw that the ability to adjust governance of different modes of communication by adjusting roles and expectations of use was also a key mechanism to support effective use of each. What became evident was that the ability to change and adapt governance and policy to reflect new requirements was as important as the actual roles and policies established. Creating conditions for discussing and agreeing new ways to communicate and for establishing associated roles enabled the rebalancing of expectations.

One major trend underpinning the changes in governance was the growing belief that “TRT's intranet is for evervone and owned by everyone’’, therefore also looked after and supported by everyone. New governance systems were increasingly based on developing policies and roles that take account of benefits for the collective rather than maximizing the interests of specific individuals or sub-communities. We found that univocality and multivocality were fostered in parallel to the development of shared values and goals, which was accompanied by adjustments to governance mechanisms that allowed this synthesis to emerge. The idea of ‘‘stewardship’’ came through strongly in our empirical analysis. Stewardship is, in fact, an established concept in governance literature, and given its insurgence in our analysis we now extend it as a concept that can help theorise social media governance as it affects communicational ambidexterity within organizations. The concept of ‘‘stewardship’’ was originally proposed to challenge the dominance of agency theory. In that context, ‘‘stewardship’’ referred to ‘‘situations in which managers are not motivated by individual goals, but rather are stewards whose motives are aligned with the objectives of their principals’’ (Davis et al., 1997; p. 21). One of the underlying ethos of stewardship is a rejection of the traditional over-generalization about ‘‘instrumentality’’ within corporations, providing in its place an alternative theoretical explanation in terms of how sustainability is achieved through shared responsibility (Hernandez, 2008). One important aspect of this concept that is relevant to our study is that governance based on ‘‘stewardship’’ can coexist with more centralized control systems. In other words, it is not an ‘‘either-or’’ choice that a management team has to commit to. As Sundaramurthy and Lewis (2003) point out, the either-or approach does not fully address the paradoxical nature of governance. This debate is also captured by Davis et al. (1997) and the ‘Dialogues’ published in Academy of Management Review (Albanese et al., 1997; Preston et al., 1998). What is relevant for us is that our empirical evidence indicates that the two governance models – based on central control and individual responsibility – can be individually developed and enacted, yet orchestrated simultaneously.

This approach shows that a sense of stewardship not only encourages users to take initiative and to share responsibilities, but also encourages a different kind of mindset and behaviors. This new mindset was supported by the final mechanism we identified, related to changes in the culture of internal communication.

## Dynamic culture of communication

Another important mechanism contributing to the emergence of communicational ambidexterity that we found was the effort to reenergise a new value system within the culture of the organization which was more consistent with the two modes of communication based on both OPC and UGC. We suggest that the introduction of these new corporate values was a crucial mechanism to reinforce the growing distributed nature of internal communication and the use of the new platform within TRT. As covered in the findings section we found that TRT actively encouraged changes in the culture of the organization at the same time as it deployed the new digital tools that supported the strategic move toward mobile working. Their effort to convey the new culture was visible at different levels, and even in the tone of voice and style used to write new social media policies. For instance, the new social media policy specifically stated that personal blogs support the organization by ‘‘providing a mechanism for building communities through which the collective power of the community can solve problems and issues individuals face’’ and by ‘‘encouraging innovation and inspiring and engaging TRT colleagues’’.

As seen previously, there were four distinctive yet interrelated corporate values actively promoted and nurtured by the central team – to be ‘‘open’’, ‘‘inspiring’’, ‘‘straightforward’’ and ‘‘helpful’’. When comparing these corporate values with the four enabling mechanisms, in particular ‘‘trust’’ and ‘‘support’’, by Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) we notice a significant overlap in the emphasis on creating a collective and facilitating organizational context.

## Review of mechanisms of communicational ambidexterity

The above shows the mechanisms that underpin the new capability of “communicational ambidexterity". Even though each mechanism provides its distinctive contribution to the development of communicational ambidexterity, their influence has to be considered and developed more cohesively. For instance, while the mechanisms of ‘‘architecting communication’ and ‘‘governing communication’’ might provide structural definition and clarity to the users, the mechanisms of ‘‘dynamic culture of communication’’ and ‘‘governing communication’’, in particular through stewardship, are crucial for creating an organizational context where both multivocality and univocality grow simultaneously. Furthermore, the ‘‘governing communication’’ mechanism reinforces the architecture of communication while simultaneously helping to support the more dynamic organizational culture that can concurrently cater for OPC and UGC. From our analysis we also found that the distinction between structural and contextual mechanisms presented in the ambidexterity literature was not sufficient in explaining how these mechanisms interrelated in the case of TRT. For instance, ‘‘governing communication’’ is, on the one hand, a structural mechanism because of the dual structure design, which includes central control and a user centric approach to governance. On the other hand, the ‘‘governing communication’’ mechanism is equally important for nurturing a communication culture and an organizational context by distributing the governance of TRT’s internal communication among its users. ## Complementary outcome of internal communication

Based on the previous discussion we suggest that the three mechanisms, individually and concurrently, produced over time a new capability in TRT, which we conceptualize as ‘‘communicational ambidexterity’’. We highlight the dynamic nature of this new capability and emphasize that these mechanisms were not static, but rather continuously fed off each other, so they were simultaneously inputs into the communicational ambidexterity capability but also outcomes of the emerging capability.

We noticed that this new capability was reflected in three types of new behaviors or practices emergent in TRT: ownership, service orientation and self-regulation. Ownership relates to new roles and formal demands to keep content and information up-to-date and maintain the quality of both OPC and UGC content. This was also aimed at fostering users’ sense of responsibility toward the way they use and engage via TRT’s intranet. Service orientation reflects the new focus on creating content to be shared and utilized by all users, both in respect of UGC and OPC content. So users felt more like engaged participants with a sense of service to the community. Self-regulation refers to how users form a community, which is more in charge of itself by co-creating shared norms and functions, and a governing body. It also relates to the way in which the community self-regulated itself through online open rating and commenting on other postings. These behaviors and practices emerged in response to the new challenges and show a good degree of complementarity between univocality and multivocality.

## Conclusion and implications

Our research examines the strategic use of social media as a distinct form of communication within organizations. We are aware of the potential limitations of a single case study, in particular its ability for generalization (Denzin, 1997). Furthermore, even though the phenomenon of communicational ambidexterity is reported to represent the uniqueness of the case, we do not intend to project TRT’s internal communication as completely unproblematic. Instead, we have outlined the importance of seeing communicational ambidexterity as something that is constantly in the making. Despite these limitations, our account has also yielded several valuable theoretical and practical contributions. We elaborate each in turn.

Our first contribution is to theorize the new capability of communicational ambidexterity. While there is growing attention to ambidexterity in the IS research community, the emphasis has been mainly on the context of IT projects/products (e.g. Napier et al., 2011: Tiwana, 2010: Vinekar et al., 2006) and IT companies (Du et al., 2013: Gulati and Puranam. 2009) Surprisingly, few have examined how IT enabled communication, in this case primarily via social media, can itself be considered an ambidextrous capability. In this regard, our account contributes to the IS literature with an integrative conceptual foundation to explain how strategic use of social media can provide valuable means to address divergent communication demands, and at the same time facilitate the development of complementarity between these demands. Furthermore, despite growing interest on the strategic potential of social media, the focus is placed mainly on how organizations use it for external purposes, rather than for internal communication (Huang et al., 2013; Majchrzak et al., 2013). Our account represents an attempt to theorize this relatively under-explored terrain.

Our second theoretical contribution lies in the theorization of communicational ambidexterity by elaborating how univocality associated with OPC can be developed concurrently with multivocality associated with UGC through three different enabling mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 3. Despite the newness of our offering, the concept of communicational ambidexterity draws on prior accounts of ambidexterity. For instance, the need to develop complementarity between conflicting activities pinpointed by Cao et al. (2009) is well illustrated in how univocality and multivocality are developed and managed at TRT. Echoing several prior studies (e.g. Gulati and Puranam, 2009; Napier et al., 2011; Tiwana, 2010), our findings also indicate the importance of using multiple mechanisms in order to foster ambidexterity, rather than the earlier conceptualizations that assumed one type of mechanism (dual structures) fits all. Also, our account reinforces the view stressed by Jansen et al. (2009) that different enabling mechanisms have to work in synchronization. Our data provides some insights on these phenomena and suggests that TRT developed a governance approach that enabled the organization to oper ate based on a centralized control governance model mainly designed to manage and maintain OPC, and simultaneously operate based on a decentralised system designed to support the publishing of UGC. Furthermore, instead of analytically separating structural mechanisms from contextual ones, we present a process view of the capability of communicational ambidexterity and its enabling mechanisms to show that these mechanisms do not function in isolation but rather complement each other

Our third theoretical contribution is to theorize an important governance principle for the development of communicational ambidexterity – stewardship (Sundaramurthy and Lewis, 2003) and a growing focus on individuals taking responsibility to produce content by developing new practices reflecting a new sense of self-ownership, service orientation and self-regulation, Our account has elaborated not simply the role of stewardship and how it was developed through adopting different governance approaches for different intranet services, but also how it was related to the nurturing of a participative, open and interactive communication culture. Even though theorizing stewardship is not our main research focus, we believe that future studies can examine its relevance and implications in IS-related phenomena.

Our fourth theoretical contribution is to emphasize how a capability like communicational ambidexterity is always emergent. In this sense, the mechanisms that help to produce this capability are not static but rather are themselves shaped by the

Following the call for more practice-based studies by Whittington (2014) we suggest that additional research on this topic may draw on the perspective of practice theory to examine how social media shapes and is shaped by the day-to-day taken-for-granted practices that it affords.

In addition to these four theoretical contributions, our study also has two practical contributions. Firstly, our study shows how, with the continuous expansion of remote and mobile working arrangements, organizations face shifts in the underlying processes of internal communication. Many organizations have perceived social media as a communication panacea to this situation. However, we have shown that there are tensions associated with the introduction of social media so that careful adoption is needed to ensure that social media can support multiple voices without drowning out a unified corporate message. This then raises the issue of how social media can best be incorporated into the existing communicational landscape, which relates to our second practical contribution. In this respect we show how organizations might foster the concurrent development of multivocality and univocality through the enabling mechanisms outlined above. While the architecture of the communication platform and different governance policies can help organizations consider how different communication tools and spaces can be partitioned and integrated, the mechanism of communication culture can lead to the maturing of an organizational context where a diverse range of communication practices can be simultaneously enacted.
