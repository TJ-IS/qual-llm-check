# Digitally enabling social networks: resolving – fl

Hope Koch,\* Dorothy E. Leidner\* & Ester S. Gonzalez<sup>†</sup>

Information Systems Program, Baylor University, Waco, TX USA, \*email: Hope\_Koch@baylor.edu, and \*email: Dorothy\_Leidner@baylor.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Information Systems Program, California State University at Fullerton, Fullerton, CA USA, email: esgonzalez@fullerton.edu

Abstract. Digitally enabled social networks, encompassing social media applications such as wikis, blogs and social networking sites, have the potential to dramatically change organizational culture by building communities, promoting information sharing and fostering collaboration. Although organizational social media site (hereinafter SMS) implementations are proliferating, these technologies often do not harmoniously combine with traditional organizational cultures. To shed light on social media’s impact on organizational life, this paper draws on an interpretive case study to investigate a global security company’s efforts to implement a digitally enabled social network as part of a cultural change effort. Conceptually, based on the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict, this paper underscores the interplay between organizational culture and technology. Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that con<sup>fl</sup>icts can arise between employees’ workplace values and the values they ascribe to social media. This can result in an IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict, which organizations can address using policy-based, socialization-based and leadership-based mechanisms aimed at bringing cultural values and social media site values into alignment.

Keywords: social media, social networking, IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict, interpretive case study, IT ambassador

## INTRODUCTION

Digitally enabled social networks, which use electronic media to link groups of people, have proliferated in the last few years with the rising popularity of social media. As a form of electronic media, social media refers to Internet-based applications that enable users to create and exchange content (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010, p. 61). Today’s social media applications include: collaborative projects (e.g., wikis), blogs, content communities (e.g., YouTube), social networking sites (e.g., Facebook), virtual game worlds (e.g., World of Warcraft) and virtual socia worlds (e.g., Second Life) (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010).

Aspiring to leverage the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts social media elicits for individuals (Ellison et al., 2007; Shang et al., 2011), organizations are incorporating social media sites (hereinafter SMS), which encompass an array of social media applications, into their digital networks and business processes (Li & Bernoff, 2008; McAfee, 2009; Deans, 2011). Whereas these efforts were mostly experimental until 2011, social media has now become a key element of both business and IT strategies (Rozwell, 2011). In fact, Gartner predicts that as our societal behaviours and work cultures evolve, social media will transform companies, industries and even governments (Bradley, 2011).

Organizations are using social media applications to manage both external (i.e., customer, sup plier and partner) and internal (i.e., employee, department and division) relationships (McAfee, 2009; Deans, 2011). For example, Dell, Wal-Mart and Starbucks use social media to brand, sell directly, provide customer service and support, develop products and recruit employees (Culnan et al., 2010; Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010). Increasingly organizations such as USAA (Leidne et al., 2010) and IBM (DiMicco et al., 2008; Majchrzak et al., 2009b) are replacing their intranets with SMSs that connect employees, facilitate mass collaboration and improve communication.

Whereas some organizations are experiencing success with their SMS implementations (Li & Bernoff, 2008; Majchrzak et al., 2009a; McAfee, 2009), digitally enabled social networks and traditional organizational networks do not necessarily combine harmoniously (Deans, 2011). The executive is labouring to decide how employees should and should not use social media (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Leidner et al., 2010; Deans, 2011). Organizational struggles with social media are evident in their social media policies that re<sup>fl</sup>ect a limited understanding of social media’s value creation potential and a strong concern for managing its risks (Kaganer Vaast, 2010). Despite evidence linking employee social media use to productivity increases (Gaskell, 2011), engagement (Leidner et al., 2010) and innovativeness (Gray et al., 2011), for several years now, many organizations have banned popular external social media sites like Facebook, Twitter, iTunes and YouTube in the workplace (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Cain et al., 2011)

Management concerns about SMSs centred around productivity, security and control (Kaplan & Haenlein, $2 0 1 0 ;$ Cain et al., 2011). Although some managers feel that SMSs may lead to unbridled socialization (Leidner et al., 2010; Denyer et $a I .$ , 2011), line managers, responsible for meeting performance targets, do not want employees distracted by collaboration efforts tangential to meeting performance objectives (Li, 2010). With regard to security and control (Bonabeau, 2009; Skeels & Grudin, 2009; Andriole, 2010), managers are concerned about employee posts harming morale, damaging the organization’s reputation or disclosing sensitive information such as intellectual property and proprietary knowledge.

Although organizations show concern about socialization, security and control if employees are allowed to use social media at work, employees are concerned about privacy and inequity in digitally enabled social networks. Both Telco and USAA’s employees were worried about privacy. Telco’s employees thought the SMS, which was introduced as a tool to encourage open communication, was a management surveillance tool (Denyer et al., 2011). And at USAA, some new hires feared negative rami<sup>fi</sup>cations if co-workers saw them use the SMS even though USAA encouraged its new hires to use the SMS (Leidner et al., 2010). Equity concerns over digitally enabled social networks have arisen in traditional–virtual worker relationships (Akkirman & Harris, 2008) and in middle-manager–new-hire relationships (Leidner et al., 2010). In both cases, one group of employees became dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed and felt underappreciated when they perceived that the digitally enabled social network provided more support to the other group.

If both employers and employees perceive dangers in SMS use, one might think that SMS implementations in organizations would be few and far between. Yet, this is not the case. Gartner Group, Forrester Research, IDC, and the Cutter Consortium report that SMSs are making their ways into corporate technology infrastructures (Andriole, 2010; Rozwell, 2011). In spite of this increased pervasiveness of SMS in organizations, organizations are concurrently maintaining, or tightening, their policies of employee SMS use (Gaudin, 2009) in an attempt to control the outcomes of SMS use (Kaganer & Vaast, 2010). However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, if no impossible, to fully embed policies into any IT use or to control the outcomes (Orlikowski, 2000). Rather, individuals can choose to enact IT in such a way as to conform to their pre-existing way of work, to re<sup>fi</sup>ne their way of work or to radically alter their way of work (Orlikowski, 2000). To which end a user enacts his/her IT use depends to some extent on the user’s values (Alavi et al., 2005–2006).

Using an interpretivist philosophy, our work seeks to understand organizational implementations of digitally enabled social networks, both why organizations pursue such initiatives in light of concerns over the outcomes of SMS use and how individuals’ values in<sup>fl</sup>uence digitally enabled social network initiatives. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we explore the con<sup>fl</sup>icts in values that emerge as organizations pursue digitally enabled social networks and how these implementation con<sup>fl</sup>icts are resolved. Toward this end, we draw upon the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict to interpret how a global security company dealt with the challenges of implementing a digital network that generated a culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Following the presentation of our theory in the next section, we then present the method, a description of the case and the analysis. The pape concludes by discussing our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and drawing theoretical and practical implications.

## THE THEORY OF IT–CULTURE CONFLICT

Various theories exist to help understand how humans make sense of, use and incorporate IT into their work practices. At the organizational level, theories such as structuration theory (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Jones & Karsten, 2008), innovation theory (Swanson, 1994; Swanson & Ramiller, 2004), organizational learning theory (Huber, 1991) and organizational change theory (Markus & Robey, 1988) have aided our understanding of how and why organizations incorporate IT into organizational structure and processes. Human agency theory and theories of IT practice-in-use have likewise provided valuable insights into how individuals respond to and enact IT in their everyday work, sometimes leading to anticipated and sometimes, unanticipated, consequences (Orlikowski, 2000; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Vaast & Walsham, 2005). These latter theories are largely oriented to understanding the changes that occur through the situated use of IT by individuals, who choose to appropriate features of an IT that <sup>fi</sup>t their situation while ignoring others features of the IT (Vaast & Walsham, 2005). Research that seeks to understand the situated use of IT recognizes that artefacts, such as IT, are not culturally neutral and may come to symbolize a host of different values driven by underlying assumptions and their meaning, use and consequences (Robey & Markus, 1984). The non-neutrality of IT and the different values that it symbolizes to different user groups forms the core of the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). This theory is particularly helpful in studies, such as ours, that seek to understand the values that individuals ascribe to a technology and how they respond when such values con<sup>fl</sup>ict with their work values.

## IT and group member values

Taking a value-based perspective of culture (Schein, 1985b; Schein, 1985a), the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict identi<sup>fi</sup>es three potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts that may arise during IT implementations: system, vision and contribution con<sup>fl</sup>icts. These three forms of con<sup>fl</sup>ict emerge from inconsistency across two, or more, sets of values that include group member values, values embedded in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of technology and IT culture. Group member values comprise the values shared by members of a group that re<sup>fl</sup>ect what is important to the group. For example, a group of IT developers might share as a value a willingness to work long hours to meet a deadline. Values embedded in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology refer to values that are embedded in a work practice that an IT feature is designed to support or enable. For example, smartphones enable anytime–anywhere work. Although the particular features enabled by a technology may never be appropriated (e.g., a smartphone user may choose not to work at certain times), the values embedded in the technology are less concerned with whether, or not, the values are realized by users but that the technology makes these values possible. IT values are then the values that individuals assign to IT in general. IT is symbolic and IT values form over time as people use technology to communicate, standardize processes and share information (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). IT values may include equality, progressivism, community, sympathy, competency, legitimacy, rationality, hierarchy, order and control (Feldman & March, 1981; Robey & Markus, 1984; Scholz, 1990). Table 1 summarizes these three sets of values that are central to the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

When disparity exists during an IT implementation between two of these sets of values, the result is a values’ con<sup>fl</sup>ict. There are three forms of values’ con<sup>fl</sup>ict: system, vision and contribution. System con<sup>fl</sup>ict refers to the tension that arises when group member values are unsupported, or run contrary to, the values embedded in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT. For example, a group of IT consultants that value the power that their knowledge affords them may not embrace a system that is designed to encourage them to share their knowledge. Vision con<sup>fl</sup>ict is the tension created when a group’s IT values are inconsistent with the values embedded in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT. For example, if a group of IT designers believes that IT is inherently enjoyable but then a new development tool proves particularly cumbersome, the designers will experience a vision con<sup>fl</sup>ict: their belief about IT in general is being challenged by a speci<sup>fi</sup>c instantiation of IT. Contribution con<sup>fl</sup>ict refers to the tension that resulted from a disconnection between group member values and the values that the group attributes to technology in general. For example, a group of social workers who value human interaction might perceive ITas impersonal and fail to realize the contribution that speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of IT might offer their social campaigns.

## System con<sup>fl</sup>ict in digitally enabled social network implementations

Because we are interested in how different user groups within a single organization respond to the implementation of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT (i.e., an SMS), system con<sup>fl</sup>ict, represented graphically in Figure 1, is the most relevant form for our investigation. The diagram shows three group member sets. Group A’s values overlap the values embedded in the IT, so system con<sup>fl</sup>ict is low. Group B has fewer values in common with the IT, so system con<sup>fl</sup>ict is moderate. In group C’s case there are no shared values with the IT, leading to high system con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

System con<sup>fl</sup>ict is largely unavoidable for organization-wide implementations of IT (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). As a system spreads beyond an initial user group or champion group, the likelihood of encountering user groups whose values do not coincide with those in the system increases. A review of organizational research investigating digitally enabled social networks highlights situations where organizational values con<sup>fl</sup>ict with SMS values. Table 2 highlights the con<sup>fl</sup>icting values and shows that they fall into three categories: information (e.g., security vs. information sharing), employee relationships (e.g., work life integration vs. separation) and work (e.g., control vs. experimentation).

Several cases explicate SMS values con<sup>fl</sup>icting with workplace values. For example, at Telco, the workplace value of control trumped the SMS value of ceding control, resulting in employees feeling that posting their real opinions on the SMS was unsafe and would result in management shutting them down (Denyer et al., 2011). This resulted in management using the SMS to promote authority by posting corporate policies and procedures. In another example, USAA’s employees struggled with how its SMS encouraged employees to integrate their personal lives into the workplace (Leidner et al., 2010). Tension arose when middle managers, with high performance targets, perceived that the new hires were using the SMS to make friends at work. In the Epsilon case (da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008), management struggled to control information <sup>fl</sup>ow when employees began using its digitally enabled social network, which was designed for technical issue discussions, to instead express frustrations about organizational changes that were threatening their identity and decreasing their value. Management addressed these negative posts by suing and suspending one employee and warning the others about SMS use.

The previous examples highlight the contradictory nature of workplace and SMS values, which makes IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict likely in workplace SMS implementations. In line with the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict, the examples earlier illustrate that the system con<sup>fl</sup>ict between workplace values and SMS values may inhibit employees from exploring how and when SMS features such as discussion forums, blogs and wikis may be appropriate to use within the workplace. The theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict suggests that implementation problems are likely when the values embedded in IT contradict an organization’s values. In cases where the con<sup>fl</sup>ict goes unrecognized, the likely results are that user groups will resist using the technology or will <sup>fi</sup>nd unanticipated ways to use the system to support their values (Carlsson et al., 1996; Gamble & Gibson, 1999; Chau et al., 2002). This is consistent with the human agency perspective of use that demonstrates how users will appropriate uses of a technology that <sup>fi</sup>t their situation (Vaast & Walshem, 2005) and will modify the intended use of technology to better <sup>fi</sup>t with their assumptions about what the technology should do (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). However, although theory and research suggest that users can, and do, modify their use of a technology to reduce con<sup>fl</sup>icts with their values and/or their work practices, little research exists on how organizations can intervene to actively reduce the system con<sup>fl</sup>ict. This leads to our research question: How do organizations resolve IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict in digitally enabled socia network implementations?

## METHOD

## Research context

Our study was conducted in a large global security company, which we refer to as GloSecure, a pseudonym. GloSecure operates in all 50 US states and 75 countries. GloSecure’s primary business units include aeronautics, electronic systems, global services and space systems. Its workforce encompasses nearly 136 000 worldwide employees who are primarily engineers, scientists and IT professionals. With annual sales exceeding \$40bn, GloSecure’s primary customer is the US government.

We chose GloSecure for theoretical sampling reasons (Patton, 1990). GloSecure was implementing a digitally enabled social network that consisted of an SMS. Its history of Internet use restrictions combined with its culture characterized by security, information hoarding and control made IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict likely in the digitally enabled social network implementation. GloSecure’s SMS was organized into team spaces and personal spaces that could utilize activity feeds, blogs, discussion boards, social bookmarking and wikis.

## Data collection

A vice president of one of GloSecure’s largest divisions provided us entry to conduct <sup>fi</sup>eldwork at the company. This gave us immediate legitimacy and credibility with the interviewees (Patton, 1990). The vice president provided internal documents (i.e., Internet use policy, social media policy and screen shots of the SMS) and arranged most of our interviews, which were conducted on site at GloSecure’s largest division. Prior to entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld, we spent two weeks reviewing relevant external documents (i.e., corporate website, annual reports, trade press articles and public presentations) about the company and its SMS. While most data collection occurred during the summer of 2010, we made initial contact in February 2010 and continued to conduct interviews through March 2012.

Table 3 provides interview details. To gain a broad understanding, we interviewed people involved with implementing the SMS and target SMS users. Thirteen of the interviews lasted 45 min and were tape recorded and transcribed. The other interviews ranged between 1 h and 3 h. To allay interview fears, we provided signed informed consent forms assuring anonymity. The interview questions were tailored to the informant’s role and were open ended and exploratory. We wrote extensive <sup>fi</sup>eld notes that described our other interactions with GloSecure’s employees during lunches and dinners (6), facility tours (2), a top-secret GloSecure’s manufacturing plant tour and an IS security and business conduct panel presentation.

## Interpretivist perspective and data analysis

Recognizing the nascent stage of knowledge on organizational implementations of digitally enabled social networks (Whelan et al., 2011) and our how question, we rely on an interpretive case study method (Klein & Myers, 1999) and a contextualist approach, grounded in the accounts of the relevant stakeholders (Madill et al., 2000). This approach allowed us to gain rich and deep insight about digitally enabled social networks in their organizational context and to investigate the meaning employees assigned to the SMS (Walsham, 1995b; Walsham, 1995a).

Consistent with guidelines on conducting interpretive case studies, we did not seek to impose an a priori theory on our data or test a theoretical framework (Walsham, 1995b; Walsham, 2006). Rather, we examined and made sense of our data using constant comparative analysis (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). This allowed us to identify initial concepts and link the evolving set of concepts to higher level categories.

Our initial data analysis involved an open, inductive coding approach (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Vaast & Walsham, 2011). By using QSR NVivo $8 ^ { 1 }$ to organize our emerging coding scheme, we read and coded data chunks into categories and subcategories suggested by the data. This resulted in 12 categories and nearly 50 subcategories. In the process of data coding, memo writing and reviewing the coding level (i.e., the number of sources and references) of categories and subcategories, two themes emerged. These were: (1) the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between employees’ perceptions of the SMS values and GloSecure’s culture and (2) GloSecure’s efforts to resolve the con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

After numerous iterations between the initial set of categories and the organizational literature, we decided that the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006) was a good lens through which to analyse the data. Upon choosing this theory, we analyzed the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes yet again. This process resulted in four broad categories: the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between GloSecure’s culture and the values of the SMS, the strategies GloSecure used to resolve these con<sup>fl</sup>icts, employees’ perceptions of GloSecure’s culture (before and after the con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution interventions) and employees’ perceptions of the SMS (before and after the con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution interventions). The case section, which follows, elaborates on the categories by including sample quotes that add rich insight to the issues GloSecure’s employees faced. To assess our research approach, we relied on Klein & Myers’ (1999) principles of interpretive research, which have become the standard for evaluating interpretive case studies in IS.

## GloSecure’s culture (Figure 2: circle 1)

In 2007, GloSecure embarked on efforts to change employees’ perceptions of its information practices from a ‘need to know’ to a ‘need to share’<sup>3</sup> culture. Characterized by employees who tended to ‘hold their knowledge close to their vest’ rather than share it, GloSecure knew that maintaining its government certi<sup>fi</sup>cation required swift cultural changes in the areas of information, employee relationships and work. A GloSecure’s enterprise system champion commented, ‘High dependence on email has led to budget overruns, missed deadlines, knowledge sharing issues, and repeated responses to the same inquiries.

Two dynamics contributed to GloSecure’s information hoarding culture. First, GloSecure’s history of hiring freezes, acquisitions, mergers and government cutbacks created employees who guard knowledge in efforts to prove their indispensability and abate lay-offs. Second, as the primary provider of security, intelligence and advanced weaponry for the US government, the very nature of GloSecure’s business requires high security. This is evident throughout the organization. Cameras and recording devices are prohibited; guests require security clearances and constant escorts, and employee badges don stars denoting security access levels. Furthermore, people relinquish some privacy when GloSecure hires them. An engineering planner who started his career with GloSecure in February 2011 explained GloSecure’s extensive background checks and searches:

Hierarchy, authority, seniority and thick boundaries separating employees’ work lives and their social lives describe GloSecure’s employee relations’ culture. GloSecure’s workforce is comprised primarily of baby boomer engineers who have spent their careers climbing to the top of GloSecure’s organizational ladder. With each promotion, employees gain authority, security access and privileges. For example, an employee’s rank determines the information that she/he can access, the buildings they can enter and even their parking space.

Because most of GloSecure’s contracts are cost-plus government contracts, meaning that GloSecure passes its cost along with a pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin to the government, the governmen constantly audits GloSecure. This creates a culture in which GloSecure’s employees separate their work lives and their social lives. Interviewees consistently commented,

All we do is work. There is no socializing within the organization or on company time. That is all done outside. We could be dismissed and face additional legal rami<sup>fi</sup>cations for engaging in activities that are unrelated to the government contract.

Driven by its research and production of advanced military weaponry that requires years to design and produce, GloSecure’s work culture values long-term planning, measurement and order. GloSecure subscribes heavily to earned value management, a project management technique for measuring project progress and performance. Gantt charts measuring ITand engineering projec progress line GloSecure’s walls. During our tour of a GloSecure’s manufacturing plant, a property management lead commented, ‘Every pillar, bin and item is tagged and accounted for.

Unfortunately, GloSecure’s culture had several negative rami<sup>fi</sup>cations. First, work duplication was commonplace. Knowledge workers responded to the same requests repeatedly. A GloSecure’s SMS designer’s comment is telling. ‘It is very likely that employees in the aeronautics division and employees in the space division could be working on the same project without either group knowing.’ Indeed employees across functional areas (e.g., engineering and <sup>fi</sup>nance), business units (e.g., aeronautics and space) and locations (e.g., Texas and California) did no know what projects their counterparts were working on nor did they have a means of collaborating with one another. Second, information <sup>fl</sup>owed hierarchically. Employees emailed weekly activity reports to the supervisors while co-workers had to ‘run team members down’ to determine how they were progressing on a project. Third, knowledge transfer was unsystematic. GloSecure needed a system to transfer tacit knowledge from its retiring baby boomers to its newly hired millennials.

To address these negative outcomes of GloSecure’s information hoarding culture, GloSecure held a ‘cultural summit – a meeting of the minds at the higher executive level’. The purpose of the summit was to seek ways to promote information sharing, improve communication and collaboration between employees and reduce redundant work. To facilitate these cultural shifts, GloSecure began implementing many different systems including a digitally enabled social network that encompassed a SMS.

## SMS implementation encounters con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Figure 2: circle 2)

Organized into personal spaces and team spaces, GloSecure’s SMS included: activity feeds, blogs, discussion boards, social bookmarking and wikis. GloSecure hoped that the SMS’s knowledge-sharing capabilities would ‘unify the corporation’ by reducing corporate silos and improving everyone’s ability to leverage collective knowledge. Furthermore, it wanted to leverage the SMS’s collaboration capabilities and knowledge repositories to transfer and preserve the retiring baby boomers’ knowledge to their millennial replacements.

Needless to say, implementing the SMS created some con<sup>fl</sup>ict between how GloSecure’s employees perceived the SMS in relation to GloSecure’s culture. During our initial interview, the SMS enterprise system champion immediately exclaimed, ‘This is not Facebook! It is for work only!’ Despite this, most of GloSecure’s baby boomer employees perceived that the SMS was Facebook and viewed it as a tool for recent college graduates who wanted to play at work. A senior IT staff specialist who did not use the SMS commented:

I think it’s just a big time waster, a distraction from my responsibilities. It’s Facebook for the kids coming out of college. They want Facebook but we can’t give them Facebook because we need the security. They say ‘I want my iTunes, I want my Facebook.’ They’re not looking for jobs or real work. The SMS is for people who don’t have enough work to do. I don’t use it!

In fact, the perception of the SMS as a tool for millennial new hires was partly true. Recognizing that ‘60% of its workforce is baby boomers who will be eligible for retirement in the next <sup>fi</sup>ve years,

GloSecure implemented the SMS as a way to attract recent college graduates and leverage the technologies they are accustomed to. The comments of a baby boomer vice president and a millennial new hire are telling:

The hiring freezes of the 80s and 90s have created a bathtub organization. We have lots of baby boomers and few Gen Xers. We now need millennials to replace the retiring baby boomers. [Baby boomer vice president]

One of the main problems we have is that there is no one in between the young people and the older people. We see how they do things and are like ‘you should do it this way’ and they are like ‘we’ve always done it this way and we don’t want to change’. So we just try to rely on facts to get them to change how they do things. [Millennial new hire]

The con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the fun, collaborative and sharing values that baby boomer employees associated with the digital culture surrounding SMSs and GloSecure’s secure, work-focused culture manifested itself in few users adopting the SMS. A SMS enterprise system champion commented:

User adoption has been a struggle for us. Many of our employees are not technically savvy and they are stuck on email. I’ve had people get upset and leave the room during the training sessions. People are sensitive, they are used to things a certain way and change isn’t easy for everyone.

With SMS use optional and email readily available, employees had a variety of justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for their non-use of the SMS:

We have a new quality initiative coming in here about every 6 months. They come and go and it’s hard to tell if it’s something we should invest our time in. There is so much to subscribe to that you can get overwhelmed by so much information and you can’t get anything done. I can’t put the time and investment in to learn another tool. Not enough people are using it to make it valuable. The system is too slow. [Property management lead]

Given that GloSecure’s employees spend most of their time adhering to government contracts that ‘prohibit using corporate resources for anything unrelated to the contract’, accessing the SMS, much less creating a pro<sup>fi</sup>le with pictures and hobbies on GloSecure’s SMS, seemed risky.

## Culture–SMS value integration (Figure 2: circles 4 and 5)

Given the resistance to the SMS and GloSecure’s desire for cultural change, GloSecure had to address certain elements of its culture to encourage employees to adopt and use the SMS. As such, GloSecure implemented several changes to its work practices and policies that helped resolve the system con<sup>fl</sup>ict that emerged between the SMS and GloSecure’s culture. GloSecure began promoting information sharing by incorporating collaboration into employee performance objectives and decreasing hierarchy in its security levels and work arrangements. With reduced information security levels, employees could access previously classi<sup>fi</sup>ed information thus increasing the information that could be posted on the SMS. GloSecure reduced hierarchy in work arrangements by placing engineers on the shop <sup>fl</sup>oor with the factory workers to enable ongoing communication.

GloSecure took a few additional steps to change its culture. In addition to hiring more millennial generation college graduates known for their use of collaborative technologies, it began allowing telecommuting, expanded workday Internet privileges and hosted events to foster personal relationships between employees. A senior IT staff specialist describes one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst events held in spring 2010:

They wanted us to send in a picture that showed something personal about ourselves. It took place in the cafeteria at lunch. I sent in a picture of myself on a motorcycle.

Combined, these cultural changes fostered an environment that made SMS use more likely by re<sup>fl</sup>ecting increased tolerance for taking breaks from work, increased trust that employees will only charge for work-related activities and an increased understanding of the value of personal and virtual relationships for accomplishing work tasks.

In conjunction with these cultural shifts, the SMS implementation team adapted the SMS, which initially imbued networking, information sharing, collaboration and communication values to better align with GloSecure’s cultural values of work, security, hierarchy and control. Buil using Microsoft SharePoint with Active Directory and a Google search appliance, the SMS offered many features and capabilities that GloSecure’s employees rarely used. When the SMS implementation team rolled out interventions to increase understanding of how the SMS features could assist employees with their daily routines, employees began adapting the SMS into their work practices and culture. This case study sought to reveal how an organization can resolve IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict in digitally enabled social network implementations. The GloSecure’s case suggests that resolving IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict in digitally enabled social network implementations is a two-step process. First, organization must recognize the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between employees’ perceptions of the values that comprise the organization’s culture and their perceptions of the values embedded within the SMS. Second, organizations must work to implement integration mechanisms to reduce the disconnection between the existing cultural values and the values employees ascribe to the SMS. When GloSecure decided to deploy the SMS corporate-wide, it quickly recognized that many employees associated SMSs with Facebook and that the values in these digital cultures con<sup>fl</sup>icted with GloSecure’s organizational culture. As such, GloSecure embarked on a concerted effort to resolve the IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict. This effort involved seven integration mechanisms, as summarized in Table 6. In general terms, these integration mechanisms can be categorized as policy based, socialization based and leadership based.

In this way, the distance between the employees’ values and the values ascribed to the SMS was reduced.

The policy-based mechanisms are largely centred around shifting from the need to know to the need to share information culture and communicating the criticality of increased information sharing and collaboration to GloSecure’s survival. These policies served both as a signal to employees that the culture needed changing and as a means of encouraging behaviours in line with the desired culture. By developing speci<sup>fi</sup>c policies that underscored the importance of this shift, GloSecure transcended the initial con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the SMS and its culture. However, policy mechanisms in and of themselves might have had marginal impact. Instead, GloSecure reinforced these policy mechanisms with socialization-based mechanisms designed to change not just the behaviours of employees, something that the policies would do, but the relationships among employees. By fostering closer personal relationships and bringing in new employees who valued the use of social media to foster relationships, GloSecure created an additional layer of cultural support. By this, we mean that the encouragement of social relationships served as a reinforcement of the behaviours, such as collaboration and information sharing, being espoused in the policy-based mechanisms.

Finally, the leadership-based mechanisms were crucial in getting the SMS project on the radar of upper executives and key users from various departments. Two things were key: (1) building a value proposition that tied directly to GloSecure’s future in the industry and (2) relying on ambassadors to promote the link between the SMS and GloSecure’s future. Selected from their workgroups because of their relationships and understanding of workgroup processes and SMSs, the ambassadors were charged with <sup>fi</sup>nding productive ways for their workgroup to use the SMS. Because the ambassadors were respected, productive employees, their use of the SMS helped change co-workers’ SMS perceptions and helped co-workers begin to associate the system with knowledge sharing. After determining how work practices could be reengineered with the SMS and how the SMS could be customized to support work practices, the ambassadors engaged in information and training sessions to promote the SMS. With the policy mechanisms in place to encourage employees to explore the SMS, the training sessions developed behavioural expectations regarding SMS use: the sessions explained the SMS, how employees could use it and how use would reduce an employee’s workload and improve their effectiveness.

Ultimately, these three sets of integration mechanisms helped to resolve the system con<sup>fl</sup>ict experienced at GloSecure. The three worked in conjunction to help change employee behaviours, help change employee values (GloSecure’s culture) and help change employees perceptions of the values embedded in the SMS.

## IMPLICATIONS

In addressing the research question set forth in this paper, our study makes several contributions to research on digitally enabled social networks and to the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict. First, our interpretive approach (Walsham, 2006) allowed us to consider how organizational context and the meaning employees assigned to the SMS affect the SMS implementation. As a result, this study offers rich and deep insight into the system con<sup>fl</sup>ict associated with workplace SMS implementations and strategies for overcoming such con<sup>fl</sup>ict. These insights add other dimensions to the digitally enabled social network research (Anjana et al., 2012, among others), which has been dominated by quantitative methods and a positivist philosophy (Whelan et al., 2011). Showing that organizational culture and employee misperceptions can lead to SMS resistance, this interpretive case study suggests that IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict can be reduced in digitally enabled social network implementations by reducing cultural distance between the group championing the SMS and its intended users, allowing users to modify the system to support their values and introducing cultural change mechanisms.

Second, this study demonstrates that system con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006) may not necessarily arise because of the values the designers’ embedded in the IT, but because of people’s perceptions of the values embedded in IT. In the case of Web 2.0 technologies, people may associate the technology with the digital cultures of popular social media applications that they use to socialize in their personal lives and not recognize the work-related features such tools can provide. Although people can use social media tools to socialize, these tools have many more uses. Thus, it is as important to consider employee perceptions, as well as misperceptions, of the values embedded in an IT as it is to consider the actual values that have been embedded in the design. Future research might look further at this discrepancy between perceived values embedded in a system and actual values inculcated in the design by drawing upon theories of stereotyping: individuals might develop technology stereotypes based upon popular uses of a technology. Research could consider how to overcome such technology stereotyping.

Thirdly, this study extends the theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict by identifying speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms to resolve system con<sup>fl</sup>ict. The theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict proposes that managerial interventions can reduce all three forms of con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006) but stops short of proposing speci<sup>fi</sup>c interventions to respond to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Our study identi<sup>fi</sup>es three mechanisms – policy based, socialization based and leadership based – that work in tandem to create changes in employee behaviour, employee values and employee perceptions of the values embedded in a technology. Future research can investigate appropriate mechanisms to respond to the other forms of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict (i.e., vision and contribution con<sup>fl</sup>ict).

Fourthly, this study has implications for work on ITand culture. Whereas much of the work on culture focuses on organizational culture and includes technology as a mere artefact in the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of culture (Schein, 1985a), our work shows that in addition to organizational culture, digital cultures can also exist. Both cultures are based on assumptions, values, artefacts and creations (Schein, 1985a). In GloSecure’s case, the digital culture surrounding employees interactions with social media in their personal lives differed from employees perceptions o the values central to GloSecure’s workplace culture. Moreover, the culture that employees initially ascribed to digitally enabled social networks appeared to be based upon their percep tion of the popular use of such networks rather than upon their perception of IT more generally. Their belief that digitally enabled social networks encouraged distraction from work, idle chatter and irresponsible behaviour suggests a rather un<sup>fl</sup>attering view of a digital culture even if the culture is not applicable to all users. Future research can explore how such culture associations emerge, how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence the use and outcome of systems and how users shape cultural attributes.

Furthermore, most work on organizational culture assumes that cultural change is slow and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (Schein, 1985a). Organizational culture is often treated as a variable that must be taken in account during IT implementations as having an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on how IT will be appropriated or as a force that might lead to unintended cultural change. In addition, much of the work on organizational culture assumes that the impact of culture is one directional in that organizational culture impacts some aspect of IT, without considering how IT might potentially play a role in rede<sup>fi</sup>ning or transforming culture (Scholz, 1990; Weick, 1990; Coombs et al., 1992; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). Rarely is IT considered the force behind wilful organizational culture change. Our case suggests that organizations can use IT as a major tool in an organizational culture change effort.

In the GloSecure’s case, the SMS was a major element in the effort to change GloSecure’s culture. The effort to integrate the SMS at GloSecure was not motivated by a desire to successfully implement a system per se, but by the desire to lay the ground for GloSecure’s future. It is possible that absent such an important cultural motivation to resolve the system con<sup>fl</sup>ict, that the three mechanisms might have been less effective. Future work can conside the preconditions necessary for effective IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution.

## CONCLUSION

This research has examined digitally enabled social networks by identifying the value con<sup>fl</sup>icts that arise when organizations begin incorporating SMSs into organizational life and how organizations can resolve these con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Relying on an interpretive case study of a global security company’s SMS implementation efforts, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the interplay between organizational culture and digital culture. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that SMS implementations can be hampered by employee perceptions of SMS values that con<sup>fl</sup>ict with their perceptions of workplace culture. The theory of IT–culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict offered a framework through which to examine these con<sup>fl</sup>icts and how to resolve them. Theoretically, our understanding of SMS– organizational value con<sup>fl</sup>icts can be based on the notion of a system con<sup>fl</sup>ict, whereas ou understanding of con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution is based on the notion of integrating organizational and SMS values.

This paper has several limitations. First. this research explored the data from a single conceptual perspective and other explanations such as the technology acceptance model are possible. Second, most of the interviews were conducted during the early stages of the SMS implementation, and the interview set has more system users than non-users. We supplemented our non-user interview data with system implementer’s accounts regarding initia

SMS non-use, and we conducted follow-up interviews in 2012, which indicated that the SMS has achieved widespread adoption. Third, although the case highlights the presence of generational (i.e., baby boomers vs. millennials) subcultures within GloSecure’s organization, this research focused on what appeared to be GloSecure’s dominant cultural values, which emanated from baby boomers working in its aeronautics division. Future research could advance our understanding of IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict by examining the interplay of various organizational cultures (e.g., digital, divisional and functional) and their impact on IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Despite these limitations, our research is generalizable to theories (Lee & Baskerville, 2003) on digitally enabled social network implementations and IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

Given the rising popularity of Internet-based applications such as wikis, blogs and social networking that enable the creation and exchange of user-generated content, organizational adoption of these technologies will only accelerate. Managers embarking on digitally enabled social network implementations should devise implementation plans that address both employee misperceptions and organizational culture. Employees, who use social media and participate in digital cultures in their personal lives, may misperceive social media’s value in organizational settings. When on the surface, the perception of the technology contradicts organizational values; organizations need to work on overcoming IT–culture system con<sup>fl</sup>ict by instituting policy-based, leadership-based and socialization-based mechanisms. These mechanisms can help align the organization’s culture and the SMS’s values. Digitally enabled social networks are <sup>fl</sup>uid systems that can both change and adapt to an organization’s culture. We hope that this research will increase managers’ awareness of the impact that organizational culture and digitally enabled social networks have on each other and devise plans for leveraging both in bene<sup>fi</sup>cial ways.
