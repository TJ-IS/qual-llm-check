# Scaling of HIS in a global context: Same, same, but different

Sundeep Sahay ⁎, Johan Sæbø, Jørn Braa

Department of Informatics, University of Oslo, Norway

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 10 September 2012 Received in revised form 4 August 2013 Accepted 5 August 2013 Available online 12 October 2013

Keywords: Scaling Health information systems Technology transfer Translation Circulating references India Kenya Sierra Leone

## a b s t r a c t

Scaling of information systems is a <sup>fi</sup>eld of research with growing importance. This paper presents the story of scaling of an artifact (called District Health Information Software — DHIS) and associated principles and practices around a health information system that has taken place over 15 years, both within and between multiple developing countries. Through the lens of the story of the artifact over its trajectory of development and implementation in multiple contexts and time, we develop insights that challenge traditional thinking around scaling. Scaling is not about constant gains and expansion, as is often assumed, but involves a dichotomy of losses and gains, associated with each step or translation in its process of movement. We draw upon Latour's insights on circulating references to analyze this dichotomy of loss and gains, conceptualizing the process as circulating translations. We contribute to the technology transfer literature in arguing that the process of transfer is not about a “parachuting” from point A to B, or a “design from nowhere” but something which occurs in a series of small steps, where with each step new socio-technical con<sup>fi</sup>gurations are created which not only shape subsequent steps, but also rede<sup>fi</sup>ne the content of the artifact. In this way, we are in line with <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the social studies of technology, but differ in that our artifact of study – software – is more “virtually immaterial” than machines which had been primarily earlier objects of study. This property of software, coupled with growth of web-based and mobile infrastructure, allows relative ease of circulation across contexts, where it gets rede<sup>fi</sup>ned and embedded at the same time at the interconnected levels of the global and local. We conceptualize this process of global scaling as being “same, same, but different”. We discuss both the characteristics of this process of global scaling, and the channels and mechanisms through which it takes place. Four overlapping conditions that shape this process include the software itself, the infrastructure, institutional practices, and ideas — these form the basis for a general framework to understand global scaling of health information systems. Empirically, the story of DHIS is told from its birth in the mid-nineties in South Africa developed on a Microsoft platform to its transformation to a web-based platform, built using Java based open-source frameworks, and now moving through multiple countries. We focus on these dynamics primarily within three countries namely India, Sierra Leone and Kenya.

## 1. Introduction

Scaling, or the process of expanding the scope, spread and size of an information system, is a topic which is gaining in importance in contemporary contexts. Amongst other things, this is fuelled by the demands of globalization that requires the same system to be spread to multiple contexts (Monteiro & Rolland, 2002). The same is also the case with health information systems (HIS), where global agencies like the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Global Fund promote HIS strengthening in multiple developing country contexts. The increasing use of open source applications for HIS (Braa & Sahay, 2012) which carries with it the potential for customization and reuse, heightens this process of expansion of systems, both geographically and functionally.

In information systems research, studies have primarily focused on projects within local settings and described scaling to follow a “replication” logic, mimicking as in a natural system where the various branches have the same structure as the whole plant (Eoyang, 1996). Similarly, the technology transfer literature often describes the movement of technology across countries to also follow a replication logic as the “same” system — System A is sent from place x to place y where the box is received, unpacked and A is installed. However, within our perspective of global scaling, technology A when moved from place x to y, would retain some of the “sameness” but through the very process of movement across different socio-technical domains, will also acquire elements of “differences” — leading to the scaling process being “same, same, but different”<sup>1</sup>. This represents a process of technology “translation” (as contrasted to transfer), exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by Akrich's (1992) study of how technology gets “translated” in every step of its movement across countries.

Our analysis concerns “global scaling” of HISs across multiple developing countries. This has two key points of departure from Akrich's work. While Akrich described the story of a forest pulping machine, our focus is on software, surely “less material” than machinery, a property we denote as “virtual immateriality”. While this higher degree of immateriality enables easy transportability across contexts, it also makes it vulnerable to greater “interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility” (Orlikowski, 1992; Pinch & Bijker, 1984), as different social groups have the opportunity to engage with the software and adapt it to their local context. The second point of departure is that unlike Akrich's story of a movement of technology from one country (Sweden) to a second (Nicaragua), our analysis focuses on understanding scaling processes in global, implying multiple country contexts, and also within the same country in different settings and spanning different domains of use. These points of departure make global scaling of (H)IS to be qualitatively different from technology transfer of a machinery involving two countries. While the latter focuses primarily on the dimension of geography or institutions, our analysis spans multiple dimensions such as system functionality, its inherent complexity, user maturity, and processes of learning and adaptation in new contexts (Braa, 1997; Sahay & Walsham, 2006). The global dimension necessarily shifts the perspective of scaling from a bilateral movement from country A to B, to a networked view involving multiple locations at the same time, and also at different points of time.

The networked view allows for an analysis of scaling not just as a linear process of expansion and gain, but also helps to simultaneously examine processes of “loss” and associated non-linearity. This arises through feedback and feed-forward mechanisms, from users to designers, or from local to global designers, which stimulate processes that may lead to non-linear and unpredictable movements. Furthermore, scaling is not treated as one large step of expansion and movement from point A to point B, often described as a form of “parachuting” (Braa & Sahay, 2012) or “design from nowhere” (Suchman, 2002), but instead as consisting of a series of small steps or translations. Each such step involves the creating of new forms of socio-technica networks which also in<sup>fl</sup>uences the content of the technology itself (Akrich, 1992).

Scaling of HIS is intertwined with challenges of the health programs itself, for which the systems are being designed to provide informational support. The drive to provide anti-retroviral therapy to AIDS patients globally from the late 90s and the adoption of the Millennium Development Goals in 2000 led to a sharp increase in international aid for health, and consequently, a similar increase in the interest in ‘scaling up’ in the international health literature (Hanson, Cleary, Schneider, Tantivess, & Gilson, 2010; Mangham & Hanson, 2010; World Health Organization, 2007; Yamey, 2011). Scaling up is not clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the health literature but tends to be used to describe the objective or process of increasing the coverage of health interventions that have typically <sup>fi</sup>rst been tested in pilot projects (Mangham & Hanson, 2010). As HIS are part of the support, monitoring and evaluation structure of the global health interventions, global scaling of HIS is inevitable. UN agencies such as WHO, Health Metrics Network (HMN) and UNICEF and donor agencies such as PEPFAR, GAVI and Global Fund are circulating global systems, standards, software, and best practices in different partner countries. The HIV/AIDS pandemic, for example, has triggered world-wide responses, necessarily with global dimensions, including the HIS to monitor the spread and inform response based interventions.

Despite being backed by huge <sup>fi</sup>nancial strength, scaling of health interventions has met with problems and an increasing realization that ‘scaling up is not just about technology transfer and information dissemination, but instead is essentially a learning process in which the adaptation of the innovation to local realities is vital’ (Gilson & Schneider, 2010, p 97). Related to this, there is the realization that clinical research <sup>fi</sup>ndings cannot ‘be immediately translated into public health impact by issuing “one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all” clinical guidelines without engaging in systematic study of how health outcomes vary across community settings’ (Madon, Hofman, Kupfer, & Glass, 2007, p 1728). Furthermore, in the pursuit of global health, the challenge of achieving rapid spread of effective changes seems to be greater than the challenge to actually create technologies and practices that are better able to reduce mortality and mortality (McCannon, Berwick, & Massoud. 2007). In order to be able to scale, therefore. health innovations will need to be able to adapt to the local context, that is to enable differences, while at the same time include and maintain the essence of the innovation, that is to in some areas remain the same. A similar ‘same, same, but different’ characteristic also apply to the HIS: software and procedures will need to both change and adapt and to carry out essential functionality in order to scale and effectively support health programs.

HIS thus needs to be designed in a manner that they scale globally, implying the possibility to circulate from one context to another, while also simultaneously being well embedded in local contexts. Such movement is never friction-less, and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by various conditions such as culture (Lind, 1990; Nhampossa, 2005), infrastructure, human resources (Shaw, Mengiste, & Braa, 2007) and various others (Heeks, Mundy, & Salazar, 1999). Roger's classical “S” shaped curve of diffusion (Rogers, 1962) has wide popularity despite various critiques that diffusion processes do not follow universal trajectories, taking various forms and shapes depending on the contexts of the source and origin of the movement process (Nhampossa, 2005).

There are important differences between scaling of the health interventions referred to above and scaling of HIS. First, while the health information will be supported by massive funding, the funding for HIS has traditionally been negligible. Second, compared with the actual health services, drugs and other needed commodities, the HIS is not similarly critical to the rendering of services. As a consequence, the scaling of HIS must to larger extent than for the scaling of health interventions, which are supported by massive 'push', rely on 'pull' and demand from users and organizations. Scaling of HIS will therefore to a large extent depend on the creation of ‘attractors’, solutions that are seen as immediately useful for many, which through their success, enroll more users, and thereby create more success, or demand and ‘pull (Braa, Hanseth, Heywood, Mohammed, & Shaw, 2007).

From a “global scaling” perspective, while geography is indeed fundamental, various other dimensions are scaled, including system functionality, user maturity, and political complexity (Sahay & Walsham,

2006). A key argument explored in this paper, is that scaling does not lead only to differences, but also carries with it a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degree of sameness as some of the old always continues. Our focus is on understanding how global scaling takes on a character that is same, same but different, and why this is the case. The research questions this paper focuses on include:

a. What are elements of sameness and difference that can be inferred during the process of global scaling of HIS?

b. Why is this the case, and how can this be best managed in order to have positive consequences of HIS scaling?

Empirically, the analysis is carried out within the framework of the Health Information Systems Programme (HISP), a research and development initiative coordinated through the University of Oslo, Norway and ongoing since 1994 in multiple countries of Asia and Africa. In the map that follows, the current presence of DHIS2 is pointed out to provide a sense of the geographical scale involved (Fig. 1).

Activities under HISP include the design, development and implementation of an open source HIS application called DHIS (District Health Information Software, hereafter DHIS). In Section 3, we provide a box each for DHIS and HISP to describe more concretely what they are constituted of. A key focus in this paper is on the artifact – DHIS – and the functionalities, ideologies and practices that are embedded in its design and use. Given that, the “same” DHIS is being developed and used in multiple countries, it provides an interesting perspective to understand global scaling, on how the artifact circulates, and what remains the same while simultaneously as new inscriptions are developed, what becomes different. Geographically, we focus on three regions of the South — South Asia, West and East Africa for our empirical analysis. While in South Asia, there is the focus on India and the spread of DHIS to Bangladesh, in West Africa the focus is on Sierra Leone and Ghana, with interesting inter-linkages between these two regions through developments in the East African country of Kenya. This empirical design sets up the analysis to understand how DHIS translates to being “same, same but different.”

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we discuss related literature including on scaling and technology transfer. Taking the points of departure for our analysis as outlined above, we present a theoretical framework that helps to guide our analysis. The research methodology, basically involving the large scale multi-country action research called HISP is then described, followed by the case studies. Next, the cases are analyzed, within the perspective of “same, same but different” scaling. Discussions and conclusions follow

## 2. Global scaling of IS/HIS

## 2.1. Traditional perspectives: technology transfer and diffusion

Various strands of literature are relevant in understanding the progression of thinking in the conceptualization of scaling. Early thinking in this domain was informed largely by the technology transfer paradigm, dominant from the 1960s. Technology transfer refers to the process of transferring skills, knowledge, technologies, methods of manufacturing, products and services, and facilities amongst governments or universities, and other institutions to ensure that scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technological developments are scaled and made accessible to a wider range of users. The primary focus on “transfer” assumes a center and a periphery to where these different elements are moved. In the context of technology transfer involving countries, the core was then the developed, and the periphery included the developing countries. In the sixties, the dominant technology transferred related to agriculture and other manufacturing machinery, while since the nineties, information technology has also become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant object of focus and transfer.

This model of center–periphery movement was typi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Rogers' popularly used “S” shaped curve, which sought to explain how, why, and at what rate new ideas and technology spread through cultures (Rogers, 1962). Rogers proposed 4 main elements that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the spread of a new idea: the innovation itself, communication channels, time, and a social system. The diffusion process, of how an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time amongst members of a social system, was described by Rogers to progress through 5 stages: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation.

Rogers' ideas have also found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant application in the IS research domain through the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and its subsequent extensions (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh, Morris, Gordon, & Davis, 2003). TAM seeks to model how users come to accept and use a technology, which is shaped by two key in<sup>fl</sup>uences: perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. While the former is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the “degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance”, the latter re<sup>fl</sup>ects the “degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free from effort” (Davis, 1989).

While Rogers' ideas have been quite in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the understanding of how innovations spread, and similarly the TAM in describing acceptance of new ISs, they have also been subject to criticism. For example, Nhampossa argues how the cognitive basis of these models tends to limit our understanding of the system (Nhampossa, 2005) in its broader context. Furthermore, the assumption of a one way <sup>fl</sup>ow of an innovation tends to rule out new ideas emerging from the periphery, and of it being treated as a relatively static event, excluding the broader and multiple interactions taking place in context. Per Lind has argued how the promise of (information) technology transfer from the Western world to developing countries has not been fully materialized due to challenges that are cultural as well as technological (Lind, 1990). While we do not subscribe to Lind's extreme position that computer programs are a product of a developed culture, and as such, are not suited to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c problems of Third World reality, we argue for an approach that emphasizes mutual learning and innovation within a framework of “translation” rather than “transfer.”

Braa, Monteiro, and Reinert (1995, p 15) argue for speaking of technology learning rather than transfer, as “technology is not just an isolated machinery or artefact”, but involves “the social and cultural context of use of a technical artefact”. Subsequently, Reinert conducted an impressive analysis of technology transfer from an economic point of view, arguing that successful economies are based on industry protection that facilitates learning of technologies, i.e. that only by being given time for learning and adapting technology, can industries stand a chance of being sustainable in a competitive setting (Reinert, 2007). Evocative of the expression “teach a man to <sup>fi</sup>sh…”, the core is that it is not the technology alone, the <sup>fi</sup>shing gear, that feeds a man for a lifetime, but learning how to use it in his waters.

## 2.2. Reconceptualization of scaling: as translations

An alternative perspective to technology transfer, which we argue has interesting implications to study global scaling, is Akrich's (1992) notion of technology translation. Akrich describes the physical, economic, and institutional modifications taking place around the introduction of a Swedish forestry machine to Nicaraguan cotton farming. Through successive alterations to the technology, one small step at a time, it is slowly translated into a useful device for the local context, quite different from that originally used in Sweden and also from that intended to be used in Nicaragua. These translations not only affect the technology itself; it is a mutual alignment of the altered technology to a changing industry, in the end resulting in the development of a new economic niche; selling cotton stalk briquettes as fuel to local bakeries. The essence of technology translation then, as compared to transfer, is the contextual forces working upon the technology help alter parts of its materiality, its potential and eventual use, and also the networks around these different processes.

Similarly, Nhampossa studied the introduction of a HIS developed originally in South Africa to Mozambique, and describes the translations that took place at different stages of the process. For example, the technology being <sup>fi</sup>rst tested at the national level of the ministry, followed by its deployment to provinces, and then to districts (Nhampossa, 2005). Like Akrich, Nhampossa too traces the translations involved at each of these stages, and how these lead to changes in the technology itself. He uses the example of the birth of the multi-language support in the DHIS because of the demands of the local context to have the system in Portuguese. He argues that “technology is developed as a result of the interaction of culture (manner or way of thinking, talking and acting), context or environment (e.g. country, organization or department), work practices, and the material characteristics of the technology itself” (page 139). This translation is not a one-off event, but characterized as a successive chain of smaller translations, each involving different socio-technical con<sup>fi</sup>gurations (Law, 1996) and their respective multiplicity of in<sup>fl</sup>uences.

Another important aspect in re-conceptualizing technology transfer as translation is the higher degree of “interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility” (Orlikowski, 1992; Pinch & Bijker, 1984) associated with information technology as contrasted with the more <sup>fi</sup>xed materiality of technologies like machines and equipment. Interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility implies the potential of the same technology being perceived by different social groups in varying ways leading to different forms of appropriations and associated unexpected consequences. Barley described how the same CT scanners were interpreted differently by the radiologists and doctors in two hospitals in Massachusetts, leading to different consequences (Barley, 1986). Sahay and Robey similarly described different consequences of the same Geographic Information Systems (GIS) technology in two county government organizations in South Florida, and attributed these to how geographers and computer scientists interpreted technology in varying ways in these two settings (Sahay & Robey, 1992). Aware of the potential of being viewed as materially deterministic (Leonardi & Barley, 2008), our use of the term materiality above focuses on the constraints and affordances, rather than mere physical attributes, of objects. The level of materiality, and the durability of this, becomes important in this regard (Law, 2009; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). By virtue of its “virtual materiality”, information systems can relatively easily circulate across different settings, thus becoming vulnerable to varying social constructions and associated translations. As a result, scaling processes around a IS tend to have the potential to be non-linear, and involving unanticipated consequences.

## 2.3. Our perspective on global scaling

Firstly, we see scaling to involve change in a range of dimensions, including geography, functionality, user maturity (Sahay & Walsham, 2006). Successful scaling, and the related concept of scalability, has often been attributed to the more technological aspects, like architecture (Lemley & Lessig, 2001), generativity (Zittrain, 2006), and standards (Braa et al., 2007; Monteiro & Rolland, 2002); including how standards need to be <sup>fl</sup>exible to enable change. In recent times, there also has been a focus on the organizational aspects that shape scaling. For example, in a comparative study of Nigeria and Ethiopia, Shaw et al. present a framework for analyzing scale in relation to organizational capacities in the health sector (Shaw et al., 2007). They propose a strategy of mindful innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004), balancing available human resources, access to technology and the type and volume of data collected. An important aspect of our perspective on scaling as involving multiple dimensions, is the need to establish trade-offs between the different dimensions and the ability and need to scale, and also the direction of scaling since expansion in one dimension can lead to losses in another (Latour, 1999).

Secondly, we emphasize non-linearity in the process of scaling, including the rather unexplored simultaneous dual process of loss and gain. As with language, any translation runs the risk of deviating from the original meaning. In Akrich's example, the modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the technology to handle cotton stalks instead of Swedish forestry waste signi<sup>fi</sup>ed a physical change that compromised the ability to function as intended back in Sweden. So while there is a gain in better suitability to the Nicaraguan context, at the same time there is a loss in terms of the original functionality in the technology.

This gain/loss dichotomy has been beautifully illustrated by Latour's model of circulating references (Latour, 1999). He has looked at the sequential transformation of a set of empirical data, in his case specimens of <sup>fl</sup>ora and soil samples from a Brazilian savannah, via coding in note-books, to academic papers. Latour identi<sup>fi</sup>es a chain of translations not unlike what is described by Akrich. Each step signi<sup>fi</sup>es a modi<sup>fi</sup>cation for a different use, in Latour's case: a methodological abstraction from a living piece of soil towards a coded piece of data to aid research. What Latour also notes, as shown in Fig. 2, is the simultaneous loss that takes place in this movement. At each step of translation in his example (from left to right in the <sup>fi</sup>gure), what was particular about the soil sample was reduced, and what was general was ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed. There was a drift towards more universality, accompanied by a similar drift away from the locality. We <sup>fi</sup>nd this model appropriate to study processes of global scaling, seen as involving consecutive steps, each representing a transformation from the local to the global (and also sometimes the other way), from the particularity of a node in the system, to the generality of the wider system (or vice-versa), involving different time and space locales. ## 2.4. Bringing the conceptual pieces together: the perspective of scaling as circulating translations

As discussed above, we propose three key concepts to study global scaling. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is on multi-dimensionality, emphasizing the various facets that scaling encompasses. The second concerns non-linearity, which brings our attention to the back and forth movement and the multi-linkages which accompany scaling, and it is not just a push in one direction of growth and expansion. Thirdly, the concept of translation, which emphasizes how scaling takes place in small and incremental steps, and with each step new socio-technical con<sup>fi</sup>gurations are formed, which not only shape the process of scaling, but also help to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne the constitutive base of the technology. We take these three concepts together to propose the notion of “circulating translations” to understand global scaling. Like Latour, the notion of circulating emphasizes a move forward, but always in relation or reference to what exists and what is left behind. The successive chain of translations through learning, imply processes of gain and loss, which accumulates over time to depict an ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of generality, and a simultaneous reduction of particularity. Going back to Akrich's example, the changes in Nicaragua goes perhaps unnoticed back in Sweden, but any supplier of the machine, or spare parts, could potentially extend his business by taking both implementations of the technology into account. The scaling from Sweden to Nicaragua ampli<sup>fi</sup>es the commonalities of the technology, the wider the scale, the stronger the emphasis on commonalities across actors.

The health sector, which is the focus of this study, presents a unique requirement around scaling of HIS. The primary health sector seeks to provide “universal” access to health care to the people, implying “all” must receive care. Scaling of health programs too has its particular logics, and time-space frameworks. The implication of this for HIS is that they need to be able to register and provide data for all facilities and also individuals in the region covered, and be able to adapt to the health program needs. It does not help the manager of the immunization program for example to know whether children within a pilot area of his/her district have got vaccines, but requires the status for all children in the region — given the aim of universal coverage. The “scaling” problem is thus of “all or nothing” which has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications on the scaling strategy where loss and gain is on-going, and the need to ensure the gains outweigh the losses. The inherent nature of health information is to be dependent on a critical mass to be useful for management (Braa, 2005), implying the need to scale rapidly and extensively enough to achieve utility, in a context of constrained resources. Given the political and unpredictable nature of the health sector, protecting against losses is a non-trivial task, and requires a constant and on-going vigil. We illustrate these dynamics through the story of scaling of the DHIS artifact described in the case study. But <sup>fi</sup>rst we describe the research methods.

## 3. Research methods

This paper builds on research carried out within the Health Information Systems Programme (HISP) project since over more than a decade. HISP is an R&D network of health and IT with a focus on developing countries. HISP includes various individuals and institutions (universities, government departments and ministries, NGOs etc.) that have been involved in strengthening processes around the design, development, implementation and use of HIS including the software called DHIS in a number of countries in Africa and Asia In the two boxes below, we introduce the DHIS software and HISP network in some detail.

Box 1 DHIS2.

The District Health Information Software (DHIS) is a free and open source platform for management of routine health information, with a primary focus on health statistics. Since the launch of DHIS version 2 in 2005, it has been web-enabled and platform independent, and currently functionalities are expanding also to cover individual patient data. It is released on the BSD license, based on standard Java technology, and can run on any major relational database, such as PostgreSQL, MySQL, and H2. It is in use in over 30 countries, both by national governments and by some international organizations. The development is coordinated by a core team at the University of Oslo, but there is a growing number of individuals contributing around the world, especially related to translation. It is fully translated, along with documentation and various implementation guides, to French and Spanish, and supports the use of other alphabets and scripts. The development of DHIS2 has now been formalized with a publicly available roadmap and four new releases annually.

Box 2 The HISP network.

HISP is a loose network of individuals and institutions involved in HIS strengthening. HISP is collectively responsible for the development of the DHIS2 software, and in supporting implementation of it in collaboration with ministries of health. With several implementing partners around the world, including entities like HISP South Africa, HISP India, and HISP West Africa, most R&D activities are done by affiliated universities. HISP also collaborates with international organizations such as WHO, The Global Fund, and PEPFAR. In addition to software and system development and implementation, HISP is particularly involved in capacity building, both PhD and Master's programs in health informatics, and short courses and on-site training for relevant practitioners. One particularly important institution is the DHIS2 Academy, which HISP organizes annually for four regions of the world (East and West Africa, Asia, and Latin America) together with local partners, offering 10 days of intensive training and experience sharing.

HISP was initiated in 1994 in South Africa, and today spreads over more than 30 countries. While the network, especially the research and funding related activities, are coordinated from the University of Oslo, increasingly a regional focus is being promoted including the creation and strengthening of nodes in HISP India (for South Asia), HISP West Africa (for the West African region) and HISP East Africa (for the East African region). The research activities in HISP are characterized by the following three features:

– Action Research, carried out in

## 3.1. Interpretive and critical approach

An interpretive perspective has informed our research, which builds on a long tradition of research that seeks to make change. The origins of the HISP tradition of participatory action research can be traced back to the Scandinavian research projects that centered on industrial democracy, starting with the Norwegian Iron and Metal Workers Union project (Nygaard, 1979). This project looked at planning, control and computerization from a trade union perspective in the 1970s (Bødker, Ehn, Knudsen, Kyng, & Madsen, 1988), emphasizing aspects of user-participation and emancipation. In HISP, this was also the point of entry for development of a new HIS in South Africa after the fall of apartheid (Braa & Hedberg, 2002). Emphasis on user-participation and decentralization was key in subsequent work, and our research can thus also be labeled as critical, containing elements of critique of existing structures of power, and the aim to change them (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). The participatory design principles were operationalized into approaches of rapid prototyping and real-life testing of the DHIS software, contributing to its evolution over the years. Participatory design has not only been a method for design and development within HISP, but has also served as an important object of research (Braa & Sahay, 2012).

## 3.2. The framework: Action research

The overall methodological framework for this research and for the HISP network more broadly, has been of action research. While the <sup>fl</sup>avor and form in which the action research is organized and carried out by different members and nodes of the network has a lot of variations, some common design principles can be discerned as underlying the efforts. The <sup>fi</sup>rst principle relates to the use of a participatory design form of research where the researcher is engaged with change processes in an organization (typically health departments in developing countries), actively collaborating with them to solve an accepted problem (Avison, Lau, Myers, & Nielsen, 1999; Checkland & Holwell, 1998) related broadly to HIS strengthening. This approach holds that an understanding of social processes is best achieved by being engaged in collaborative efforts towards the introduction of change related interventions and observing the effects (Baskerville, 1999; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 2002; Davison, Martinsons, & Kock, 2004).

A recurring critique of action research stems from the tension between researcher and client (Davison, Martinsons, & Ou, 2012), and how to produce relevant outcomes for both research and practice. Strict adherence to principles of so-called canonical action research (Davison et al., 2004; Susman & Evered, 1978) has been promoted as a strategy to achieve this relevance (Lindgren. Henfridsson. & Schultze. 2004). Principal in this is a cyclical approach of problem stating, action planning, action taking, evaluation, and specifying and disseminating learning.

There have been concurrent cycles of action research applied within HISP, re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the articulation of learning and dissemination that have followed. As such, reports and conference papers have emerged from cycles of a shorter time span, while theses, policy documents, and books have emerged from longer cycles that span years and now even decades. The client–researcher relationships have typically been formulated as Memorandum of Understandings (MoU) between the University in Oslo (UiO) and Health Ministries/Departments, providing scope for both research and practical work.

## 3.3. Networks of action

Another principle underlying the action research approach has been of networks, which has provided the setting in which both the action and research components of the HISP initiative have been undertaken. In 2004, Braa et al. articulated the networks of action approach in HISP, as addressing the need to manage risk and increase sustainability of the networks. Key to this has been the circulation of key personnel, technology and knowledge between various nodes of the project. This helps to increase learning and the basis fo generalization by conducting comparative cases rather than work in isolated instances (Braa, Monteiro, & Sahay, 2004). This also increases the likelihood of following a single instance over time, thus answering an articulated de<sup>fi</sup>ciency in IS studies (Pollock & Williams, 2010). The point of departure for this articulation was that earlier studies of action research had argued for the need to situate the action within networks rather than in singular units of isolated action research projects which did not create enough momentum and scale to attain sustainability. Therefore, the focus needed to be shifted from single organizations and workplaces, to networks (Engelstad & Gustavsen, 1993). This networked based approach tends to be more pragmatic and <sup>fl</sup>exible towards understanding and managing processes of change in the research setting. Further, it enables longitudinal and multi-site action research which forms the empirical basis of this paper.

Our empirical story description builds on three cases of scaling; a) within and from India, to Bangladesh where also we span domains from health to food security, b) within and from Sierra Leone, to West Africa, representing a quantum leap in scale from a single country to a region including 15 countries; and c) within and from Kenya, to East Africa, with implications for both South Asia and West Africa. In each of these cases, the activities are still ongoing, starting in 1999 (India), 2007 (Sierra Leone), and 2011 (Kenya). ## 4. Case study: Tracking the DHIS

This case study tracks over time and space the movement of the open source DHIS application within the framework of the HISP global network. While as a network, HISP is relatively diffused and fuzzy, the DHIS, however, is a material artifact with concrete implementations, and clear trajectories of its scaling across dimensions of technology, geography and time. Through the lens of the artifact, we can see the inscribed public health practices, policies, architectural visions, and depict the networks and political compulsions driving the process. We focus our scaling description to the following dimensions:

1. Technological — to describe the growth of the DHIS through technological changes over time; from MS Access based standalone application to the contemporary cloud computing.

2. Geographical — to trace how the DHIS and HISP activities have progressed over geography, from the start in South Africa in the 90s to India and from there to Bangladesh, and back to Africa, West Africa and East Africa. Given our focus on scaling within a global context, we also discuss how DHIS grows within a country; new functionalities get developed which trigger interest also in other countries.

3. Institutional — this includes various formal and informal elements, such as the growth of ideology, legitimacy, ideas, political compulsions, new organizations, people, and related processes.

DHIS development has evolved over two versions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst – DHIS v1 – was present since 1997 by HISP in South Africa on the platform of MS Access. Since then the DHIS v1 has spread to multiple countries in Africa and Asia and it is still in use in South Africa, although now in the process of transition to v2. The second – DHIS v2 – building on the v1 data model is a modular web-based software package built with free and open source Java frameworks, developed since 2004 and implemented for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in India since 2006 and now spread to multiple countries in Asia, Africa and in recent times also to Latin America.

## 4.1. DHIS initiation in South Africa — “Information for decentralized action”

HISP and the DHIS was born out of the post-apartheid South Africa, initiated in 1994 as a part of the new ANC government's reconstruction and development program (RDP), which emphasized a decentralized system of health districts including their supporting HIS. HISP started out as collaboration between, on the one hand, public health researchers and activists with background from the anti-apartheid struggle, and on the other hand, informatics researchers with background from the Scandinavian participatory design and action research tradition (see Braa & Hedberg, 2002, for a detailed presentation of HISP in South Africa).

DHIS v1 was developed and implemented <sup>fi</sup>rst in pilot districts in the Cape Town area and then in two provinces of Western and Eastern Cape in 2008. The aim was to create a <sup>fl</sup>exible DHIS which would capable of integrating data from different programs through a shared essential data set linked to indicators. As other provinces followed the Western and Eastern Cape provinces, DHIS slowly evolved into the national standard from 2001 and remains also currently so. While in the past data <sup>fl</sup>ow from facilities in the district would tend to go out of the district, using DHIS this situation changed, as users were given more direct access to their own information. There was an increasing focus on collecting data that would lead to the generation of indicators.

DHIS was developed during periods of constant changes in data requirements and organizational structures and boundaries, the old ‘homelands’ where merged into new provinces, and districts boundaries were changed. DHIS was therefore developed with a design of a <sup>fl</sup>exible meta-data structure that enabled making these changes and <sup>fi</sup>tting the application to different use contacts. Changes in the metadata, such as adding new district boundaries, new facilities, or new data sets could be done through the user interface without programming intervention. In this way, being developed to serve the users' needs in a constantly changing environment – a worst case in terms of stability – DHIS developed into a <sup>fl</sup>exible platform for developing applications and tools.

## 4.2. Web based DHIS2 and implementation in India — scaling in South Asia

DHIS2<sup>2</sup> was <sup>fi</sup>rst implemented in Kerala, India in January 2006 by the HISP India team which had prior experience of working with DHIS v1. Since 2006, the use DHIS2 has evolved as it has been implemented in several states in India and from 2010, also in Bangladesh. We start with the Indian account, and its spread further to Bangladesh.

## 4.2.1. Taking the DHIS 2 to the web and to the Indian sub-continent

Re-developing DHIS as a modular web application represented a radical break in technologies, which created new challenges to the Indian technical team. While the core development took place in Oslo, customization and implementation was in India. When DHIS v2 was <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced, the HISP India team members, who had several years of experience with v1, found it hard to use the new tools provided to get the data out. The database model now incorporated a database abstraction layer, requiring the use of a Java API instead of directly accessing the database as in v1. As a consequence, the Indian team chose to bypass the API in order to be able to obtain quick results and thereby providing “hard-coded” but well-performing reports.

In response to immediate user needs, the Indians implemented a number of solutions for data analysis and presentation, such as the Dashboard module developed in collaboration with health managers, enabling graphical analysis of data and indicators in charts. This module became a key vehicle for scaling DHIS in India across multiple states as state health authorities found the graphical interface to view indicators and data status to be an effective medium. A GIS module was developed in collaboration with Vietnamese developers, giving further impetus for scaling in India. By 2010, the Indian team could scale the DHIS to about 20 states in India during 2008–2010, which Lancet described as one the largest open source implementations in the developing world (Webster, 2011). However, post-2011, a politically driven process of de-scaling took place, which we describe later.

For quite some time, the Dashboard module developed locally by the Indian team could not be included in the global core code repository due to the many local workarounds used, such as directly accessing the database. Given this local setting, global core developers could not help the Indian team with their installations, and the Indians could not upgrade to new DHIS versions released by Global, shutting of the sharing of new innovations. As the DHIS was implemented in other countries, similar local workarounds started to proliferate as add-ons were created to the core, raising challenges for synchronization of multiple local developments with the global code base.

The approach to solve the lack of compatibility between the Indian and global DHIS development was to circulate developers, the Indians went for a few months to Oslo and core developers from Oslo went to India for shorter periods. The Dashboard module was reprogrammed and committed to the globa repository during this period. Shortly after that, the Dashboard was used with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant success at meetings with WHO in Geneva, resulting in further scaling of the DHIS.

## 4.2.2. HISP and DHIS in India: Scaling as a response to a constant pressure to de-scale

HISP India, a not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t NGO, is a small group of public health informatics people who have been since 2000 struggling to keep a<sup>fl</sup>oat as a small start-up out<sup>fi</sup>t in a large country where the formal ministry dominates, and large vendors hold sway. Being proponents of open source software, the HISP approach was often at odds with the Ministry's centralized structures based on proprietary systems, having to constantly struggle with the pressure to “de-scale”.

HISP <sup>fi</sup>rst came together as an informal group of interested people, supported by the HISP global initiative of Oslo, to initiate small HIS pilots in some clinics in the states of Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh. In early 2002, they got permission to work in Andhra State, in 12 <sup>fi</sup>eld level clinics, after having made an approach to the Chief Minister, through his IT Advisor. Entry through the political rather than the health department channel made HISP throughout their 5 year stay in the State, to be viewed as “outsiders”. Despite these perceptions, HISP worked in the state for 5 years, expanding <sup>fi</sup>rst from 12 clinics to a full district and then on to multiple districts and also to the state capital. When in 2005, the political party in power was ruled out with the elections, the HISP India contract with the state was also terminated.

Following this, HISP made initial visits to Kerala, a state which was reputed as favoring open source software, and decentralized planning. In 2005, HISP was given permission to pilot efforts in one clinic in the capital district of Trivandrum. Around the same time, Oslo started the development of DHIS2, and released in early 2006, the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance of DHIS2 which was installed in one clinic in Kerala. By 2008– 2009, DHIS2 evolved with much new functionality, and became the state standard for routine reporting. State wide scaling was enabled through a MoU agreement between the State and HISP. In 2010, the state elections resulted in a new party being voted into power, with consequent changes also in the health department. By this time, the MoU period was over, and the project was not extended. However, the use of DHIS2 was continued in the state, even till today supported through an Annual Maintenance Contract between the state and HISP India.

While the use of DHIS was evolving in Kerala, at least three other states (Gujarat, Jharkhand and Madhya Pradesh) invited HISP to start implementing DHIS. Implementation progressed to different stages in each of these three states, and in all cases was limited by administrative issues. In Gujarat, from 2006 to 2008, under the guidance of an extremely visionary Commissioner, DHIS was extended to all facilities in the state, and also involved the development of various new functionalities, such as the Dashboard module and integration with GIS. However, with the transfer of the Commissioner, the new incumbent decided to scrap DHIS and start a new initiative. Similar political struggles halted the project half way through in

Jharkhand, and also in Madhya Pradesh. The initiated pilots did not scale as the authorities behind these efforts had been moved and new of<sup>fi</sup>cers came with new visions.

Around the same time in 2007, a national think tank (National Health Systems Resource Centre — NHSRC) was established in Delhi to provide technical assistance to the Ministry of Health in 6 areas, including HIS. HISP India, because of its extensive <sup>fi</sup>eld experience, was invited by the NHSRC to serve as an actor in the system reform process. The DHIS was presented to states as an open source solution that could be used at the state level, technically supported by HISP India, and covered through NHSRC central funds. This proposal was taken up positively by about 20 states given DHIS's features of <sup>fl</sup>exibility and analysis. However, this uptake of DHIS was at odds with the Ministry's efforts of implementing the national web portal developed as a proprietary solution. By 2012, only half the states continued with DHIS — the scaling of 2008–2009 was accompanied with an equally rapid de-scaling in 2010–2011.

## 4.2.3. From India to Bangladesh — regional and functional scaling

In 2008, a consultant from EPOS, a German group providing technical support to the Ministry of Health, Bangladesh, on a visit to India came to meet HISP India and see the DHIS. This led to EPOS creating a MoU with HISP India to provide technical support in the development of a national data warehouse. This effort fed into the global Health Metrics Network's (HMN) framework of creating a national data warehouse. During the period 2008–2010, the MoU was successfully implemented, leading to the national ministry taking to decision to adopt DHIS nationally, a process ongoing in 2013.

In parallel, the Bangladesh FPMU (Food Programme Monitoring Unit, Ministry of Food) faced challenges with their system (being developed by Rome UN FAO) for management of food security and nutrition and scouted the web for alternative solutions. After a selection process, they opted for DHIS. FPMU was also given con<sup>fi</sup>dence by the success of DHIS in the health ministry of their own country. While the collection, management and presentation of statistical data in health and agriculture share many similarities, there are also key differences. For example, policy making in agriculture relies upon extensive statistical analysis, raising the need for integration with statistical packages.

## 4.3. Implementation in Sierra Leone and working with HMN and WAHO

DHIS2 was <sup>fi</sup>rst implemented in Africa in Sierra Leone, West Africa from early 2008 in a high pro<sup>fi</sup>le HMN project where Sierra Leone was selected as a ‘Wave one’ pilot country for HIS strengthening. A situation analysis and strategic planning process was carried out during 2006–07, and the plan was to start implementing a central data repository according to the HMN Technical framework, for which DHIS2 was selected. The positive results on DHIS2 use from India contributed to this selection decision. We now provide an overview of the Sierra Leone implementation story, both intra-country and across in the WAHO (West African Health Organization) region spanning 16 countries.

## 4.3.1. Sierra Leone — the first implementation of DHIS2 in Africa

Sierra Leone is a small country in West Africa, with approximately 5 million inhabitants, and only as recently as 2001 has come out of a decade-long civil war. Many international organizations have been present in the rebuilding efforts, and in 2006 Sierra Leone was selected by the HMN, a Geneva-based partnership with the WHO to strengthen their HIS based on a data warehouse approach — the HMN technical framework. Since the HMN were active in about 80 countries, their choice to pilot DHIS in Sierra Leone provided a strong impetus for DHIS scaling, both as a software application and a HISP process.

The rebuilding of health services after the civil war led to rapid growth of various health initiatives, but within a fragmented framework as various donors nurtured their own systems. Each health facility used about 15–20 different paper based reporting tools which were not harmonized with huge overlaps and with implications on quality. The agreed way forward was to use DHIS to integrate the various data <sup>fl</sup>ows and data sources and to work towards an integrated framework. A two-phased strategy was chosen. First, implement the existing fragmented data reporting forms in the DHIS, and second, revise, harmonize and integrate the forms using the DHIS as a central data warehouse. One data element in the database was related to a <sup>fi</sup>eld in several data collection forms, thus doing a “behind the scenes” integration. In January 2008, this integrated system was implemented, accompanied with intensive training, <sup>fi</sup>rst in four pilot districts, and six months later in more districts. The district databases were then integrated in the national database, with data sometimes moving on memory sticks, and a rather complete national data set was available for analysis during the last half of 2008. By January 2009, all stakeholders agreed on new harmonized shared data collection forms to be implemented from March 2009, and implement the DHIS in all districts immediately in order not to lose momentum and data.

There is no power grid in Sierra Leone, and the project applied two approaches to address these constraints: Linux servers and low-voltage computers. All districts were equipped with dedicated Linux servers and DHIS was accessed over WIFI giving protection from the virus problem. The WIFI was used by multiple users to both capture and access data using their web-browser. The setup with low-voltage computers was less successful due to problems of low capacity and frequent failures which required spare parts from the US. The low luminous screens were also not well received by the users. Given these technical problems, the project moved to normal computers.

## 4.3.2. Sierra Leone, HMN and WAHO: legitimating the DHIS and help further scaling

Interoperability was a central part of the data warehouse approach promoted by the HMN Technical Framework, including the use of WHO's SDMX-HD protocol for statistical data exchange. A pilot was set up in a clinic in Freetown where the medical record software OpenMRS was used for managing HIV/AIDS patients and aggregate data was sent to DHIS using the SDMX-HD. The establishment of this interoperability, even though only at a test basis, led to regional interest in “the Sierra Leone architecture”. WAHO then took the initiative to include also in the architecture the iHRIS application for human resource management, developed by the US Capacity plus project. The gathering of interest for these different software led to the organization of a workshop on training on these applications in Accra, Ghana, September 2010. The WHO organized a ‘Connectathon’ meeting, which led to the of<sup>fi</sup>cial launch of the SDMX-HD standard. The participation of WAHO helped provide the impetus to the rest of the region. Following this, an assessment of the HIS in the region was carried out and based on this a HIS policy and strategy document was developed and endorsed by the 16 health ministers of the region in their annual meeting in 2012. This gave further impetus to the scaling efforts in the region

We next discuss the move to the cloud infrastructure, driven by a key challenge of managing and synchronizing multiple standalone versions of the DHIS running in different districts. For example, Gambia which implemented DHIS in 2009 using this architecture, encountered this problem with adverse implications on scaling.

## 4.4. DHIS going online — Cloud infrastructure in Kenya

In<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the achievements of the DHIS as promoted by the HMN, the Ministry of Health in Kenya decided to use the DHIS in October the same year. While the decision was to implement a web based system, the national Internet connectivity was not seen as being good enough to allow for a central server solution nationally. The plan was therefore to implement standalone instances in districts and hospitals around the country, but a <sup>fi</sup>eld visit to Machako in October 2010, a district and hospital not far from Nairobi, changed the course of action. Testing the DHIS online server in the information of<sup>fi</sup>ce, everything went well until a power cut left the line dead. As it was a hospital, the generator started and power came back, but the Internet was gone. The team was just about to conclude that Internet is still not there, when a staff suggested to use his “dongle”, which worked <sup>fi</sup>ne, as mobile networks are not affected by power cuts. Immediately after this discovery, Internet modems were tested around the country with positive results. This prompted the decision to go for a central server solution, probably a <sup>fi</sup>rst for a country-wide public sector project in Africa. However, the server in the Ministry of Health could not be used, as the connectivity was poor and unreliable in the building. As a temporary solution, therefore, a commercial server was rented — through a London based company, meaning cloud computing for real, although politically it was not seen yet as acceptable to locate national health data outside the country. The Coast province was selected as a pilot, and the system was implemented in January 2011 to all districts and hospitals. The pilot was successful and the rest of the country was covered during April–October.

All users were provided a modem and a budget for airtime. The network was working, but the bandwidth was limited in many places, and the cost of airtime restricted the amount of time online. In order to address a multitude of problems, the DHIS lab was literally moved to Kenya; the DHIS2 lead developer and others took part in building a local team and to address numerous user requests in rapid prototyping cycles on site with the users. Working on an online server meant that changes made as responses to requests from users could be implemented for all users immediately or overnight. This would not have been possible with standalone instances where changes would need to be installed individually in all instances. New functionalities were developed in response to user requests. Three such examples were the following:

i) Online messaging to strengthen developer-user communication

ii) Semi-online feature using HTML – 5 standard

iii) A super-lightweight DHIS application installed locally

These different new developments could be made available to users through the cloud based deployment of DHIS, enabling cycles of user feedback and developer based revisions.

## 4.5. Tracker in Uganda and Ghana — new ways of using DHIS

The successful “online” implementation in Kenya led other countries in the region, Rwanda, Uganda and Tanzania, and also Ghana in West Africa to adopt similar approaches based on the cloud starting 2011–2012. Ghana and Uganda adopted different approaches to further develop two different DHIS features addressing individual patient data <sup>fi</sup>rst developed in India. While the <sup>fi</sup>rst feature represented anonymous non-trackable line listing of cases, the other represented more of a maximum requirement: the ability to track a pregnant mother through the services of ante natal care, delivery and post natal care and immunization of babies. The line-listing system had earlier been developed in India as a local solution and had not been incorporated in the global DHIS core. While compulsory registration and tracking of all pregnant mothers was introduced in India in 2009, it mandated the use of a centralized government software, the development of the tracker module could not go very far in India. As additional requirements to the DHIS implementation in Uganda and Ghana, both these features (line listing and tracker) from India were brought forward. While in Ghana, the requirement was to include a line listing module for individual anonymous cases following the ICD10 codes, in Uganda the requirement was of complete pregnancy tracking. Ghana used the workaround where the tracker was only used for registration, and dismissed the tracking functionality where patients were assumed to pass through the stages of a health program. The use case in Uganda was similar to the originally intended use in India and this project became the driver for further development on this module.

Both in Uganda and in Ghana, it was the large national DHIS project that provided the framework for development of the tracker module. The circulation of HISP facilitators between India, Kenya, Ghana and Uganda enabled the merging of requirements and use cases into a more comprehensive framework, providing the basis to develop a generic module in the DHIS which potentially could be localized to other countries. Already, as example, the Ghana module was customized for reporting of morbidity and mortality cases using the ICD10 codes in Kenya. The line listing prototype system developed in India illustrates movement as a use case and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation to Ghana where it is implemented anew, now including ICD10 codes through customization carried out earlier in Kenya

The case analysis is now presented.

## 5. Case analysis

The case analysis is presented in two broad parts. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst part, we discuss the nature of translations observed across spatial, temporal, material, and the associated loss and gain involved in the respective cases. In the second part of the analysis, we theorize the underlying mechanisms driving these translations to develop our model for circulating translations.

## 5.1. Nature and types of translations

Broadly, we conceptualize the process of translations to unfold over three dimensions of spatial, temporal and material. Translation represents every small step of change coming from the artifact moving across time and space, which results in and also is a consequence of different forms of socio-political networks which get created around the technology, and their different interests and expectations of the technology. These socio-technical con<sup>fi</sup>gurations then contribute to changes in the material basis of technology, implying the software itself, and also the new kinds of IT infrastructure that enables the changes and its deployment. In the second part of the analysis we discuss some of the mechanisms that drive these translations.

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of this analysis section, we start with describing the different kinds of translations that we have empirically observed. Each of these translations are conceptualized over three dimensions: spatial, temporal, and material. While the spatial dimension describes the movement of the artifact and associated practices within and across countries, the temporal dimension represents changes over time. With respect to the material dimension, we trace changes in the DHIS software itself, or the perceptions of it, and also the supporting infrastructure of servers, networks, hardware, etc. In each of these translations we try to trace how different socio-technical networks get created and how these changes not only in<sup>fl</sup>uence the movement of the technology, but also the constitution of it. Based on our empirical work, we discuss the following <sup>fi</sup>ve sets of translations.

Translation 3, DHIS2 moves from Kenya and India to Ghana in 2012. Translation 4, DHIS2 moves from Kenya and India to Uganda in 2012. Translation 1: Involved the move from Kerala, India, to national level in Sierra Leone, from Asia to Africa, from the state level to national level. Further, enabled through a collaboration between HISP India and their national partner NHSRC (National Health Systems Resource Centre), DHIS was also taken to multiple states in India. In Sierra Leone, because of the engagement of the HMN and their desire to identify a data warehouse solution to support their framework of integrated HIS, DHIS2 was selected and positioned as this solution. It got customized accordingly, so that the different forms could be harmonized and presented as a single integrated format for data entry and reporting. This customization took place in the particular context of Sierra Leone, where there existed poor infrastructure, poor internet connectivity, and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult communications. These conditions implied that the application was customized as a stand-alone district data warehouse, as contrasted to a state-wide single reporting tool deployed on a server in Kerala. In Sierra Leone, the customization was driven by the socio-technical network of HMN, University of Oslo, Ministry of Health, and other donors. While the HMN was interested in <sup>fi</sup>nding a technical solution to support their conceptual integration framework, the UiO was interested in positioning DHIS2 as that particular solution. This translation implied a gain in global political legitimacy for DHIS2 through the HMN network, while it lost some of the detailed engagement that was possible within the more limited environment of a state in India, moving into a production environment within a global framework. Within Indian states, the national level had mandated that states report on district consolidated <sup>fi</sup>gures to a national web portal. But states wanted also a system for their local state analysis which included more data than wanted by the national level. To enable this reporting, states had HISP India to con<sup>fi</sup>gure DHIS as their state application, with interoperability linkages with the national level for reporting the district consolidated <sup>fi</sup>gures. The network which enabled this was the NHSRC–HISP India collaboration technically supported by UiO.

Translation 2: Sierra Leone to Kenya: The translation from Sierra Leone to Kenya involved moving from standalone district architecture to a national cloud-based architecture. This involved a movement from West Africa to East Africa, and required identifying appropriate hosting solutions which was found in “the cloud”. The decision to adopt DHIS2 in Kenya was largely fueled by the success in Sierra Leone, and through the active development in Sierra Leone, and ongoing improvements in India now dealing with scaling issues around 20 states. All these contributed to the maturing of the software. The cloud-based architecture meant shorter cycles of interaction between developers and users leading to new functionalities and innovations, such as relating to messaging and of<sup>fl</sup>ine data entry. The software gained tremendously in terms of functionalities, however, it lost in terms of the process of harmonization of data entry forms which was a precursor for the implementation in Sierra Leone. Furthermore, the process in Kenya was driven primarily by the technologists, which marginalized to a large extent the public health inputs into the system design process which was a highlight of DHIS1 implementation. Enabling these changes were the networks of technologists from Oslo and the Ministry of Health. Increasingly, district level users were becoming active members in this process, and driving the need for new functionalities. The exclusion of the public health professional from this network was a loss, reducing the health relevance of the systems developed.

Translation 3: Kenya/India — Ghana: Success in Kenya inspired Ghana to also adopt DHIS2 as a cloud-based infrastructure. This change was also inspired by the failure of an earlier effort which had relied on a proprietary platform. They had additional requirements of line listing of births and deaths. The line listing module had earlier been developed in India, but because of its hard coded nature could not be translated to Ghana. In the meanwhile, the tracking system had better evolved through developments in Kenya, and Ghana then decided to use that for line listing, utilizing only the registration functionality. In this way they created a hybrid solution arising from the line listing and tracking functionalities with origins in two different use contexts. The gain in this translation was arising from the development of a working solution for dealing with birth and death registration. However, what was lost was the original line listing functionalities developed in India, which had found extensive use there, but could not by design be scaled out of the country. The networks enabling these changes were the IT staff from the Ghana Health Services, UiO represented by a PhD student who spent many months in the country, and the experience from Kenya. Within the same IT infrastructure of the central server as in Kenya, a new functionality was developed.

Translation 4: Kenya/India — Uganda: Around the same time as Ghana, Uganda also decided to adopt DHIS2 as a cloud-based system. Their additional requirement was in terms of the mother and child tracking system, which had been initiated in India, but had been largely discontinued due to political reasons. The Oslo team then engaged in an intensive development process of evolving the original mother and child tracking system to support the requirements in Uganda. The gain came in terms of a more fully functional mother and child tracking system to support Uganda's needs, while the loss was in terms of the original application which was originated in India

Translation 5: Sierra Leone — West Africa. As Sierra Leone was within the framework of WAHO, as a regional body responsible for strengthening HIS in the region, they could observe the successes of Sierra Leone and suggest the model to the other countries. In the meanwhile, some of the countries in the region such as Gambia and Ghana had independently already initiated processes to implement DHIS2, thus providing impetus to the regional spread. A major modi<sup>fi</sup>cation to the DHIS2 came in terms of language translations, as the region included French, Portuguese and English speaking countries. Translations were then gradually carried out in these three languages, and as they became available, other countries, such as Togo for French and Guinea Bissau for Portuguese, who also came on board and started actively engaging in implementing processes. The network which enabled these changes was the WAHO working with UiO as their technical partner, and various donor agencies that were supporting HIS strengthening in the region. Further, other open source networks such as around iHRIS and OpenMRS were also engaged in developing integrated HIS solutions as the interoperability standard of SDMX-SD was established and gained credibility. The software got transformed in terms of availability in different languages, moving more into the form of an interoperable architecture, opening the doors for others to also get on board. While the software gained tremendously in geographical scope and coverage, it lost out on the intimacy of implementation which is possible in single country contexts.

## 5.2. Mechanism for translations

Looking across these translations we <sup>fi</sup>nd different mechanisms at play which are driving these changes. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst case, while the impetus from the translation comes from HMN seeking their data warehousing solution, within the country there were various institutional factors, for example their routines of data collection, which needed to be changed to support an integrated framework. This process was driven by the ministry but strongly supported by some of the donors. In India, the impetus came from NHSRC who were supporting state speci<sup>fi</sup>c open source solutions, and HISP India as their technical partner could drive these solutions. In both cases, HMN and NHSRC provided respective political legitimacy to these efforts, which an external university or NGO could ever have gained.

The Kenya decision was based on a ministry survey of best practice solutions, which led to the selection of DHIS2. The internal scaling process was driven by the technical team in the ministry supported through donor funding. The geographical size of Kenya made it all but impossible to go for standalone implementation like in Sierra Leone. Furthermore, the discovery of how the mobile internet could support online data entry provided the technical basis to go for a national cloud-based architecture. The Oslo technical team, eager to create a <sup>fi</sup>rst solution for a web-based architecture in Africa, then put in all their resources to ensure this cloud based solution is created and sustained, while in the process also developing new functionalities.

Ghana had experienced previous failures of proprietary software, and was actively looking for open source solutions. Their engagement in the West African community, where they had seen the Sierra Leone solution, provided their impetus to move to DHIS2. Their demand for both the aggregate system and the line listing was unique, and DHIS2 provided a potential solution through their recent incorporation of the tracker module in the application, based on the earlier implementation in India. Prior experiences from India, Kenya and Sierra Leone had provided a lot of learning to the Oslo team, and the Ghana systems could be rolled out nationally in record time. Mechanisms in Uganda were similar to those in Ghana, with their impetus for DHIS2 coming from the need for a mother and child tracker system. Both Ghana and Uganda had similar infrastructure to enable cloud based deployment like in Kenya.

After outlining the nature and mechanisms for translations, we now move to describing our inductively derived model on global scaling which we term as “circulating translations.”

## 5.3. Model for global scaling: Circulating translations

Moving from the analysis of the nature and mechanisms for translations, at a higher level of abstraction and generalization, we discuss theoretical implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings with respect to the phenomenon of global scaling of HIS. By following the trajectory of the DHIS artifact for over 15 years, we bring in a strong temporal perspective into the analysis of this process of scaling. We take a view on scaling that is similar to how the Buddhists see reincarnation: taking a candle to light another candle, so that the <sup>fl</sup>ame of the old burns on in the new. Similarly, we see that even though the DHIS which was originally born in South Africa as a standalone, Access based system, has now been transformed into a Java based web application being used in multiple contexts, there are elements of the “old” which live on with the “new”. Thus as global scaling takes place, we see that there are elements both of sameness and difference with respect to what was left behind — as Latour would describe this as a “circulating reference.” We conceptualize this process as “circulating translation” as the movement of the artifact over the years takes place in many small steps or translations, and at each step we see “same, same, but different.”

To help unpack the above dynamics around the process of circulating translations, we start by providing an overall schematic of our theoretical conceptualization of global scaling, and then discuss it (Fig. 4).

The <sup>fi</sup>gure is composed of the following components:

Three key dimensions of global scaling including infrastructure, software, and institutions. These dimensions re<sup>fl</sup>ect diminishing degrees of “immateriality” — while infrastructure is mostly material, institutions can be least so. Software lies in between. These three dimensions are brie<sup>fl</sup>y described:

Infrastructure: This can be understood in terms of different types of infrastructure that are required for the operation of the DHIS: physical relating to buildings, roads, distances and similar conditions of electricity supply; hardware including computers, servers, printers, networks, cables and others. In this paper, the focus has primarily been on hardware, though as the Sierra Leone case demonstrates the importance of electricity supply, infusion of viruses, and in India we have the huge challenge of geographical scale.

Software: Represents the DHIS2 itself, in addition to all the other software required for running the application on the server (such as Linux operating system), and others such as for virus protection. Software represents a form of “virtual immateriality”, as it enables with relative ease (as compared to physical artifacts like machines) the circulation across space and time. As the software circulates, it potentially becomes exposed to different groups of people, and thus their comments, feedback and local adaptations. As the software becomes more generic, it can link up with other solutions, thus increasing the functional scaling of the application.

Institutions: Institutions are both formal and informal rules and traditions of how people carry out their everyday work. For example, the routines of how data is captured, registered and transmitted, or practices of supervision. Ideas also comprise institutions, representing underlying ideologies, logics and philosophies. Such ideas may represent perspectives on the role of information, centralized vs. decentralized decision making, and open source versus proprietary software. There are further institutional arrangements that shape scaling processes, such as systems of procurement. If there are not existing systems of procurement of open source software, that would serve as a deterrent to scale.

These three sets of conditions can be conceptualized at acting at two levels of intra and inter context. These dynamics are brie<sup>fl</sup>y described, and then two examples are provided to illustrate them.

1. Intra-context dynamics — which we term as “interaction effects” refers to how these three dimensions mutually in<sup>fl</sup>uence or not each other. Within a context, while in our example, relates to countries (India, Sierra Leone, etc.), they could very well involve provinces or districts within a country, or different health programs within a particular health system. Interaction effects, for example, could represent how improvements in infrastructure can change how software is designed and applied, and the practices and ideas surrounding this. For example, our case study describes how the “discovery” of the application of mobile internet in Kenya, helped to revolutionize the entire implementation process bringing in new practices.

2. Inter-context dynamics — which we term as “constellation effects” representing how different contexts, including its constellation of the three dimensions, in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other. For example, how new software functionalities developed in India, in addition to rede<sup>fi</sup>ning interaction effects within its context, also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced software use in Kenya and Sierra Leone – constellation effects – with each of them also triggering respective interaction effects in their local settings.

The above de<sup>fi</sup>ned interaction and constellation effects take place in contexts of respective countries and the global respectively. We argue that these taken together create “circulating translations” implying that changes or translations within or across countries take place in small steps, and with each is associated a simultaneous process of gain and loss, every move forward points to something left behind — scaling is about a circulating translation. We illustrate this with two examples:

1. The translation from standalone deployment of DHIS to increasing degrees of the “cloud”.

2. The birth of the “information for action” ideology in South Africa, and its steady dissipation over the years.

5.3.1. Example 1 to illustrate the model: Translations from standalone installations to increasing degrees of the “cloud”

Microsoft Of<sup>fi</sup>ce was the defacto standard of available infrastructure in post-apartheid South Africa, shaping the decision of DHIS v1 to be developed as a standalone system on Access platform. The development was shaped by strong ideology and ideas related to “empowerment of the coal face” and participatory design (Braa & Hedberg, 2002). Practices of participatory design implied a strong involvement of public health practitioners (nurses and doctors) in shaping the development trajectory of DHIS, and led to strong inscriptions of “information for action” in the software — i.e. to collect and process only those essential data that leads to the generation of indicators and action. The rapid uptake of the software led to the DHIS becoming a national standard in South Africa by 2001, and its proliferation to all facilities — “physically installed” in the computers there. The interaction effects between hardware software. and institutions were clearly evident

In 2004, when the DHIS2 development was initiated on Java platform, the DHIS v1 was taken as the basis for requirement — ensuring “the old lives on in the new”. However, the sameness was tempered with a great deal of difference as the development platform, methodology and practices changed – going for more distributed development – and very different software development practices. The new software approach had implications on the infrastructure (server based deployment), and different practices for training, such as strengthening capacities in server administration. The ideas too were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced, as participatory design took on a new form, and driven more signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by the technologists than the public health people as was the case in South Africa. The DHIS2 developed and deployed for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in India had constellation effects in Sierra Leone, where through the HMN, the DHIS2 was introduced. However, there were rather different interaction effects there, as the absence of a power grid and supply in Sierra Leone meant that local district level systems had to be deployed. The HMN placed the DHIS2 within a different ideology — i.e. of an integrated technical framework being promoted from HMN Geneva, giving it a global dimension.

When DHIS2 was later introduced in Kenya, a way was found to achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst real online deployment using mobile modems and the cellular network. This had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications on the management and logistics of the system, as it also solved the problems of keeping tens and hundreds of database deployments in sync. The work on the software to allow this to function even when temporarily of<sup>fl</sup>ine opened up possibilities of use in yet other countries, with Ghana and Uganda adopting the same setup. Coming full circle, with the software translated in Kenya within the new platform, the new developments (for example, the introduction of messaging services) are now coming back to India and Sierra Leone. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects our point about the back and forth movement that is necessarily inherent in global scaling.

Even as the DHIS2 evolved, and has moved to multiple countries, in South Africa, where it all began, they have not yet been able to make the transition to DHIS2. The existing installed base – both materially (its physical installations) and ideologically (“Africa is not ready for the Web”) – have impeded this transition. Maybe there is a loss there? However, in the DHIS2 network more broadly, there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loss in terms of the public health driven information for action agenda that was so strong in South Africa, as the technologists hold more sway. We discuss this loss and gain also in our next example.

## 5.3.2. Example 2: The birth of the “information for action” ideology in South Africa, and its steady dissipation over the years

A driving force from the very start of HISP was “information for action”, giving primacy to public health management needs over technical ones. South Africa successfully, through a long and political process, established this with a minimum, essential health data set, and a strong focus on regular data analysis for decentralized evaluation and action planning. Only what was needed for regular management should be covered by the system, data needed for “peripheral” activities should not clog the papers, computers, charts and minds of the health staff. The focus was on “essential” and not “useful to know” or “useless to know” data.

Through a cycle of translations, we can track how this concept has taken more of a background position in other countries. Firstly, post 2005, the informatics people at Oslo became responsible for de<sup>fi</sup>ning the trajectory of the artifact, naturally making the public health inputs relatively marginal, although the original DHIS1 design was the foundation for DHIS2. The interaction effects between the three identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dimensions quickly started to erode the information for action concept in countries like Cuba and Tajikistan, where it found little room in the centralized, socialist environments. DHIS was largely instead translated to serve the existing ideological paradigm. Furthermore, in many of the countries – example Cuba, Tajikistan, India – the statisticians dominated in the respective national ministries of health. They favored the ideology of “more data is better” (Sahay and Lewis, 2011) helping to further erode the focus on local action. The introduction of powerful servers into the infrastructure landscape lifted constraints on data handling, encouraging more and more data to be sent to the national level, making local action an increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult task.

In various other countries, the primary focus was to focus data supply to the information needs of the international donors. The notion of “essential data” was thus transformed from what was required for local action to that what was going out of the country, with implications for continued money <sup>fl</sup>ows. This shift had implications on practices around training, as material started to be developed on “how to make reports for donor X”. Finally, the centralization of the software development to Oslo, geographically and culturally distant from local contexts of use, reduced the regular and intimate interaction of the developers with the end-users including the doctors and nurses as was the case in South Africa, While the information for action paradigm was well known by core HISP members, it was not so well understood and practiced by the second generation HISP members, where the technical focus was greater. It was not any longer what drove data management improvement, to the degree it had in South Africa.

Lately this concept has faced some force from changing constellations originating in the global context. With increased attention on measuring effects of aid (declarations, HMN) and pay-for-performance schemes, the focus is again on actionable information, albeit in a top–down manner. With the constellation and interaction effects again towards global and national information use, information for action has been translated towards a hybridization of its earlier phases.

## 6. Discussions

The aim of this paper was to theorize global scaling around HIS, including the nature and mechanisms of the process, and some management implications. Based on an empirical analysis of the circulation of an artifact – DHIS – across time, space and different material conditions, we have articulated the model of circulating translations, which is based on the intra and inter context dynamics of software, hardware and institutions. In this section, we discuss some broader implications of the model.

## 6.1. Global scaling: One step back and two or many steps forward — cyclic development

The model emphasizes how the global scaling of an artifact like DHIS can be conceptualized as taking place at two inter-connected levels of the global (or “shared”) and the local. Of analytical interest is the generalized process of how such an artifact can be introduced and embedded in a particular country context, and at the same time, the learning and innovations which such an embedding should give rise to, should feed back to the global and become part of the shared repository. The more these innovations are shared globally, the greater is the potential for the artifact to be introduced to new contexts both in terms of geography and functional areas — global scaling. Absence of local embedding and innovations, or a non-responsive global development group, can both impede global scaling processes.

Innovations are carried out as cyclic processes within and between the two distinct levels of the global and local. In terms of software, the global ‘core’ application platform is being continuously evolved based on feedback from countries and users. At the next level, a country or use organization will take this empty ‘shell’ and populate it with metadata, and customize a local instance of the DHIS application. Features are ideally developed locally as plug-ins to the global core which then enables the upgrade to new versions. In other cases, new requirements may be extensive requiring modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the core application. This process of change should be based on user requirements shaping the global ‘roadmap’ for development. The current deployment of DHIS for the new domain of food security in Bangladesh has raised demand for new functionalities, such as inferential statistics. This creates a new demand in the roadmap of DHIS2 development for integration with an open source statistical language called R. For effective scaling, this integration should be done in a generic manner. to enable its use in other countries and functional areas and also feeding back to the use context of health.

This interactive innovative scaling process may thus be seen as a process of going one step ahead (example, creating the dashboard locally in and for India), but then taking a step back and redeveloping it as a global generic tool. Thereafter the development can take two or many steps ahead. if the dashboard developed in India is directly ready to be used not only in India but also in other countries. Our model emphasizes the process of loss and gain in circulating translations, while there is gain through innovations, there may be loss while making it general (India's loss in redoing the dashboard), but gain as the general becomes more widely available and can be adapted to multiple local contexts and used over time. This is due to the double ‘dimension’ of the DHIS; it is both a local adaptation to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context and a generic tool to be localized to multiple contexts. The aim is to not lose the local speci<sup>fi</sup>cs in the process of being generalized, rather to enable it to become even better localized and adapted to the local context and also in more sites. This represents a gain, then loss. but then again double gains. Or one step back and then two or many steps forward. At any point in time, there are several concurrent steps being taken back and forward throughout the HISP network; local speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovations that will in turn lead to a generalized re-make of the core software to allow other users to adopt new features, contributing to greater innovation. Thus, the generi<sup>fi</sup>cation processes apparent in these scaling trajectories are more unintended, and come as a by-product of increasing scale, rather than an intended strategy to achieve scale which has been the focus in the generi<sup>fi</sup>cation literature (Johannessen & Ellingsen, 2009; Pollock, Williams, & D'Adderio, 2007).

## 6.2. Chance and unintended consequences

No matter how much we may plan for scaling and the shaping of its trajectory, the process is full of coincidences and unintended consequences. The DHIS scaling trajectory may appear arbitrary as a stone being kicked around at both the global and local levels, as it moves from South Africa to India and from there to West Africa, then to Kenya and East Africa, before it goes back to Ghana and then India. Within each of these countries there are some similar sets of processes, such as movements between and within states in India or districts in Sierra Leone, and some of the processes are quite unique to the country. Part of this meandering trajectory arises from our perspectives as story tellers and the case material which we have selectively chosen to present. However, coincidences and unintended effects also make up a big part of it, as the HISP developers and network seek support and funding to survive and grow. Opportunism is thus very much the order of the day, chance becomes of crucial importance over planned strategies. For example, that the DHIS and HMN teams happened to be in Sierra Leone at the same time was a chance event which led to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant scaling implications. There was an unintended match here between two sets of processes — HMN's search for a technical data warehouse solution and the maturing of DHIS in India, which provided the scaling impetus. This match became a major driver of the DHIS scaling processes.

In the past, coincidences have also had adverse implications, like in Ethiopia and Mozambique. “David and Goliath” kind of battles against international powers have often resulted in DHIS also being ‘thrown’ out due to political pressure. But these cases, as failures more generally, are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to account for because they do not materialize and history is ‘written by the victors’, at least those left to tell the story, and the story they want to tell.

## 6.3. Politics as the driver of scaling, de-scaling and then sometimes re-scaling

Political legitimacy, or lack thereof, is a key issue driving scaling processes of both the DHIS as an artifact and the HISP as a network including concrete legal entities such as HISP Vietnam, South Africa and India. Political struggles ensue on multiple dimensions, including relating to open source versus proprietary software. Another dimension concerns issues of centralization and decentralization, and in what end of the spectrum does HISP locate itself. For example, HISP India started to work in local clinics, moved to district, state, and in 2008 to the national levels. At different levels, there are varying political pressures that exist, and the natural trajectory is that as the efforts move more to the center, the greater is the political spotlight. From 2009, when DHIS was introduced in about 25 states because of a visionary bureaucrat, it was de-scaled to 10 states as the federal level mandated the use of a different, national system. However, in 2012, states were <sup>fi</sup>nding it increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use the centralized system for their local analysis, and some opted to use DHIS to enable that. A process of re-scaling was initiated, as DHIS gained ground to about 12 states. Further, a process of geographical and functional scaling was also initiated, as the Ministry of Health in Bangladesh adopted DHIS2, and the DHIS2 was also adapted by HISP India to the new domain of Food Security. De-scaling of DHIS2 in one context contributed to proving the impetus to <sup>fi</sup>nd different geographical and functional domains to innovate in, and stand up to the scaling challenge. As a result, HISP India could survive and are able to tell the story. In contrast, in Ethiopia and Mozambique, from where HISP was thrown out, the story is fading, existing only as memory traces in the minds of some of the HISP members who were then present — a decade ago.

As the case of Sierra Leone demonstrates, political support is a key driver, especially when its legitimacy is derived from global entities like HMN. But political support on its own cannot sustain scaling, and it is only garnered from being able to respond to user needs, and <sup>fi</sup>nding acceptance in the “local” context. In Sierra Leone, it was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant achievement to be able to join all stakeholders in a process to revise and integrate the previously fragmented data reporting coming from multiple sources using the DHIS as the data warehouse. Success in this process helped to convince both local stakeholders and regional authorities (WAHO) to support DHIS as the broad integration approach. The erosion of the HMN as a global force indeed slowed down efforts of global scaling, but DHIS may have reached a stage of “irreversibility” to be able to have a life of its own even without the previously existing global impetus. While the legitimacy in the region now comes from association with WAHO, new global actors such as the Global Fund and PEPFAR are now (in 2013) making arrangements to support development and scaling of the DHIS.

It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to describe a case which ended long ago and it is only natural that the stories told are from ‘surviving' cases, like the success in West Africa, or the ongoing (last 15 vears) successful story of the DHISy1 in South Africa, and the struggles of India. What must be emphasized is that in all these surviving cases, political standing and legitimacy has accumulated, and the greater the survival and success, the more political legitimacy is obtained. This is an important ingredient for scaling at both levels of the local and global.

## 6.4. User driven innovations: cause and effect of scaling

We take a practical approach to understand innovation — from a user perspective of being able to do existing work more effectively, and to carry out new tasks with the innovation supporting user demands. In the early days of the DHIS in India we saw how several solutions, such as the Dashboard being developed to provide users with their data at “a push of a button”. The struggle to give users direct access to their own data has been a driver of innovations in the DHIS since early days in South Africa, where specially designed Excel pivot tables were used as the tool to present data. In Kenya, providing end-users access to their own data has led to both online access to tools to visualize and use local data in an of<sup>fl</sup>ine mode. All these developments have been driven by user needs and involved a close process of user participation. Technical developments, such as the implementation of the DHIS on a central server, has both created the need for and also facilitated the spread of the innovation. The central server approach has facilitated rapid cycles of interaction between the users, their expression of needs, its action by the development team, and its spreading back to the user community. Such a cycle would have been very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult if not impossible to implement in a standalone mode of deployment. In Kenya, users in multiple locations could test out new functionality ‘live’ once installed at the server. New functionalities, bug-<sup>fi</sup>xing and other improvements can be carried out 'on the fly' or overnight. The online messaging implemented in DHIS enabling users and developers to communicate and feed back to each other, represents yet another innovation enhancing such user participation, and impetus for functional scaling.

An important aspect of our perspective is that innovations scale only when they circulate and are “transferable” across multiple contexts of use — from the local to the global. While the Indian dashboard was a success in India, it could not be shared and integrated with the global repository because of the hard-coded nature of its design. As a next step, however, it was redeveloped and included in the global repository, and could thereby be shared by all users globally. This example illustrates a typical DHIS design approach, where new features and solutions addressing particular use cases emerge as local innovations, which may or may not be of interest to users globally. In the cases where the local innovation is regarded as being important also for other contexts, the local innovations, such as the Indian dashboard, serve as a prototype for its redesign as a generic functionality in the global core, which is shared by all DHIS installations. Scaling of the DHIS globally depends on the interplay between the generation and spread of innovations, which again, through the exposure of new functionalities to a large and increasing user base will lead to the innovation of new features and functionalities. The critical issue for a global software project such as the DHIS, is therefore its ability to include changes, make these changes available in the entire network and the speed with which it is happening. The DHIS2 has adopted a 3-month cycle of new releases as an effort to address the challenge of speed and agility. While the speed with which innovations are adopted in the software is an important precondition for scaling, the way in which these new solutions are actively spread and taken into use through local champions, learning and adaptation to the local context in multiple contexts, represent the critical condition for the ability to scale.

## 6.5. Global scaling of health interventions as compared with DHIS and HIS

There have been multi-billion dollar investments by international donors in global health interventions since 20o0 and the adoption of the MDG. such as to fight HIV/AIDS or to provide vaccination to all children These developments have led to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest in effective methodologies and approaches to scaling (Yamey, 2011). The ability to effectively spread health interventions is increasingly being seen as more critical than the actual development of these interventions through new technologies and practices (McCannon et al. 2007). The “one-size-<sup>fi</sup>t-all” of health interventions is being criticized and the need to engage in systematic studies of how health outcomes vary across settings is seen as critical (Madon et al., 2007). Key components of effective approaches to scaling of health interventions are to not regard them as not just being about ‘technology transfer and information dissemination’, but rather about a ‘process of learning where the adaptation of the innovation to the local realities are vital’ (Gilson & Schneider, 2010). We see that the issues on scaling being discussed to be relevant to the global health literature, such as the need for learning and local adaption, and in line with our discussion on the scaling of DHIS and information systems. Also action oriented approaches to scaling quite similar to our action research approach are now appearing in the global health literature, such as being concluded by Hanson et al., 2010, p 3: “Perhaps the most critical is the recognition that that any scaling up process must include both opportunities to learn through action and a way to feed the lessons of experience into strategies to strengthen implementation.”

Although the global health literature and experiences on scaling of health interventions in many areas are aligning with the lessons from scaling of DHIS and information systems, we will highlight two important differences between our cases of scaling information systems and the cases of scaling health interventions in the global health literature; <sup>fi</sup>rst, while the global health interventions in questions are backed by big funding, or we can say being pushed by big money in order to emphasize the point, the scaling of the DHIS up to this point has been poorly funded and rather driven forward by activism and champions than by funds. Second, despite our view that quality information is crucial for management and health delivery, it is nevertheless obvious that information is not critical for health interventions as are drugs, vaccines and nurses and doctors. A hospital may perform somehow despite having a poor information system, but without quali<sup>fi</sup>ed health staff, it will not perform well, despite the quality of the information system. A consequence of these two differences is that, while the scaling of health interventions is happening in a push mode. scaling of the DHIS is basically about the creation of a pull situation where scaling is dependant of success on the ground that eventually will create a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient pull for the scaling to gain momentum. Of course, scaling of health interventions will also need to achieve success and a sense of usefulness in order to be able to scale, but solid funds will make it possible to continue despite an initial clumsy implementation or poor adaptation. Furthermore, many health interventions, such as to provide anti retroviral treatment (ART) to AIDS/HIV patients, are able to prove their worth quite immediately and intuitively, and thereby help create a momentum that spur further scaling. To prove the usefulness of the information system is at the outset a much more subtle and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult task. We have seen in the DHIS case that the creation of a sense of success by focusing on achievable tasks has been important in order to create a momentum for the scaling. In Kenya, the ability to provide local users in even remote rural areas direct access to their own data through modems to the Internet, helped creating enthusiasm that further spurred the scaling. The creation of attractors has proven to be important in attracting the interest of more users, aligning more actors and thereby creating further momentum (Braa et al., 2007). In the successful cases of scaling the DHIS, such as for example in Kenya and Ghana, we have seen that being able to provide good results for the initial health programs led others to join forces, which again resulted in further momentum.

## 6.6. What is being scaled at the level of ideas and principles?

DHIS2 scaling involves the circulation of a range of ‘things’, from the software, to concrete organizations such as HISP India to more abstract principles such as the HMN technical framework. Two sets of health information principles are key to the DHIS story: 1) the principles of information for action and local management developed by the HISP South Africa; and, 2) the HIS architecture principles of integration and interoperability as laid out in the HMN Technical Framework.

The set of principles of ‘information for action’ and ‘local empowerment’ represents a broader ideology of ‘decentralized management’ which has been translated into design inscriptions in the DHIS. This includes the principles of essential data and the use of indicators rather than raw data. User friendly ways to analyze and present information using maps and graphs represent another line of development of software features. Local empowerment is more generally pursued through the <sup>fl</sup>exible metadata structure making it easy to modify and extend the system locally. We have seen that local users in rural Kenva are excited by the fact that they can access their own data, and compare it with neighboring facilities and districts – or country wide – online using their modem to the Internet over the mobile network, literally at any time and from anywhere. Before DHIS, this data was barely available at all and only in paper based annual reports.

The other set of principles relate to architecture, and DHIS scaling has involved the circulation of these principles. In Sierra Leone, through the HMN framework the architecture principles were materialized into a concrete implementation on the ground. This materialization was quite fundamental as the original architecture conceptually had a clear ‘Western’ bias and assumed a computerized environment where all data were available in digital format. This was translated to the reality in Sierra Leone and other countries in Africa where data sources were paper based, and required data warehousing principles to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly amended. Where, for example the Western data warehouse architecture prescribes ‘Extract Transform and Load’ digital data, the African translation will include harmonization of paper based forms and procedures and technical solutions for capturing these different data sources from paper and others. This learning was abstracted, re<sup>fi</sup>ned and turned into concrete principles and written up as, for example, in the WAHO HIS policy document covering 16 countries.

## 7. Conclusions

We have in this paper described the scaling of software and best practices between several countries, and within these countries. This paper contributes to move forward our thinking in the domains of scaling and technology transfer. The process of circulation is described as being non-linear, with gains and losses, taking place in small steps and involving an ongoing rede<sup>fi</sup>nition of socio-technical con<sup>fi</sup>guration. Such a conceptualization can inform our thinking of scaling and technology transfer, for HIS particularly, and information systems more generally.

We have compared the scaling of DHIS and HIS with the global health literature on scaling of health interventions more generally and we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed similarities and differences. The similarities are linked to the need in both cases for learning and local adaptation to take place in order for the scaling to succeed. Differences identi<sup>fi</sup>ed are that while the health interventions are backed by big funding and basically operate in a push modus, DHIS and HIS have generally poorer funding and are less critical to the success of a health program than drugs and nurses and rely much more on creating a pull in order for the scaling to succeed. Creating early success and the creation of attractors are therefore critical for DHIS and HIS scaling to succeed.

Global businesses often use the slogan of “think global, act local” to illustrate their scaling strategy. The DHIS story helps to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne this slogan to “think and act global and local” at the same time. This rede<sup>fi</sup>nition also helps to emphasize that the global and local mutually reinforce each other, and one needs to be simultaneously doing both.
