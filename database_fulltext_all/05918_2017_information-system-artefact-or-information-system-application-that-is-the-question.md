# Information system artefact or information system application: that is the question Juhani Iivari\*

Department of Information Processing Science, University of Oulu, Oulu, Finland

Abstract. Lee et al. (2015) suggest the concept of information system (IS) artefact as an alternative to information technology (IT) artefact, de<sup>fi</sup>ning the former as a sys tem of an IT artefact, an information artefact and a social artefact. This paper as sesses this suggestion, contrasting it with the concepts of IT artefact and IS application as introduced in the prior IS literature. After this conceptual analysis, the paper discusses the relationships between an IS artefact and design and between an IS artefact and design science. The analysis concludes that an IS artefact is problematic as a unit of design because of the great differences in the designability of its constituent parts. Instead, the paper maintains that an IS application is more ap propriate as a unit of design, but an IS artefact may be fruitful as an analytical concept to be used in behavioural science research on the interplay of ITartefact, information artefact and social artefact, or – especially if including IS application design – in ac tion research-oriented design science research. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

Keywords: IS artefact, IS application, IT artefact, information artefact, social artefact, unit of design, unit of analysis

## INTRODUCTION

Lee et al. (2015) suggest the concept of information system (IS) artefact as an alternative to in formation technology (IT) artefact, de<sup>fi</sup>ning the former as a system of an IT artefact, an informa tion artefact and a social artefact. The explicit inclusion of an information artefact is an important addition when one considers mainstream IS research that has tended to omit information as sets (Schryen 2013, Väyrynen & Iivari, 2015). The wide conception of IS artefact also illustrates the potential scope of IS design – i.e. that when designing an information systems, we deal not only with the technical solution but also with the information content and the social/organisational system making use of the designed system.

At the same time, Lee et al. (2015) are quite unclear in many points in their argumentation. First, they do not properly recognise the variety of meanings associated with IT artefacts and the rationales for introducing the concept. It seems that they simply interpret IT artefacts as purely technical ones. This interpretation differs from that of the in<sup>fl</sup>uential IS literature (March & Smith, 1995; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Hevner et al., 2004).

Second, Lee et al. omit ‘IS applications’ (Davis, 1982) as ITartefacts (Appendix A). In addition to application software, IS applications comprise information content (e.g. data in databases), the latter resembling ‘information artefacts’ (Väyrynen & Iivari, 2015). IS applications also encapsulate ‘the structures, routines, norms and values implicit in the rich contexts within which the artefact is embedded’ (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003, p. 186), implying that they encapsulate something social. Thus, they are not socially and informationally empty as Lee et al. (2015) assumes all IT artefacts to be.

Third, when omitting IS applications, Lee et al. (2015) forget their role as units of design and development in much of IS development (Davis, 1982). It is not clear that the concept of IS artefact provides a better unit of design than the concept of IS application does. Actually, this pa per suggests that an IS artefact is problematic as a unit of design because of the great differences in the designability of its constituent parts. Instead, the paper maintains that an IS application is a more appropriate unit of design, while an IS artefact may be fruitful as an ana lytical concept to be used in behavioural science research (BSR) on the interplay of IT artefact, information artefact and social artefact.

Finally, Lee et al. (2015) associate their work with design science but do not explain what they mean by it. Is it synonymous in some sense to design science research as interpreted by Hevner et al. (2004), or to something else?

This note is quite personal by nature – my attempt to digest the central ideas of Lee et al. (2015). The following sections elaborate the four points identi<sup>fi</sup>ed earlier. Appendix A provides a concise glossary of the major concepts used in this paper.

## A BRIEF HISTORY OF THE CONCEPT OF IT ARTEFACT

Two, quite distinct traditions have suggested the concept of ‘IT artefact’ in IS for quite different reasons.<sup>1,2</sup> March & Smith (1995) introduced it to denote the outputs of design science research (DSR), distinguishing constructs, models, methods and instantiations, with the <sup>fi</sup>rst three being strongly affected by conceptual/semantic information/data modelling (related to information artefacts in Lee et al., 2015) and instantiations ‘operationalising constructs, models and methods’ (p. 258). This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was directly adopted by Hevner et al. (2004). One should note that it includes methods (e.g. IS development methods) among ITartefacts, differing in this respect from the focus of Lee et al. (2015).

It was Orlikowski & Iacono (2001), however, who popularised the phrase ‘IT artefact’ within the IS research community. The purpose of their paper was to pay attention to the fact that IS research (as represented by Information Systems Research journal) had largely neglected ‘IT artefact’. They de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT artefacts as ‘bundles of material and cultural properties packaged in some socially recognizable form such as hardware and/or software’ (p. 121). As this de<sup>fi</sup>nition implies, they do not interpret IT artefacts to be purely technical.

Orlikowski and Iacano’s idea of the IT artefact was adopted by Benbasat & Zmud (2003), who characterise it (p. 186) ‘as the application of IT to enable or support some task(s) embed ded within structure(s) that itself is embedded within context(s). Here, the hardware/software design of the IT artefact encapsulates the structures, routines, norms and values implicit in the rich contexts within which the artefact is embedded’. Again, Benbasat and Zmud do not interpret IT artefacts to be purely technical, but they do encapsulate various organisationa (social) aspects.

In view of the seeming design orientation of Lee et al. (2015), quite interestingly, they focus only on ‘instantiations’, following in that sense Orlikowksi and Iacono (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) and Benbasat & Zmud (2003) rather than Hevner et al. (2004). However, when reading Orlikowksi and Iacono’s (2001) and Benbasat & Zmud’s (2003) de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of ‘ITartefacts’, I have a feeling that they have ‘ITapplications’ and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally ‘IS applications’ in mind rather than all sorts of ‘ITartefacts’. By ‘IS applications’, I mean a subset of ITapplications that have informa tion content, while all IT applications do not necessarily have any information content as exem pli<sup>fi</sup>ed by computer games, some digital media applications (e.g. adult-entertainment pages) or the digital fantasy world (e.g. Second Life) (Appendix A). In the following, I will focus on IS applications only and leave other types of IT applications outside of the scope of this paper.

Lee et al. (2015) wish to ‘unpack’ what has been called the ‘IT artefact’ into an ‘information artefact’ and a ‘technology artefact’, which, together with a ‘social artefact’, interact to form the ‘IS artefact’ (pp. 7–8). This causes them to interpret the term ‘IT artefact’ so that it has a purely technical meaning.

## IS APPLICATIONS AS COMBINATIONS OF TECHNICAL, INFORMATIONAL AND SOCIAL ARTEFACTS

Lee et al. (2015) omit IS applications totally. At the same time, they lose sight of prior DSR research on IS development methods, methodologies and approaches that have IS applications as units of design and comprise quite similar ideas as theirs. There is a long and rich tradition of IS research on IS development methods, methodologies and approaches with ‘information systems' as units of design and development (Davis. 1982: livari, 1991: livari et al., 1998: Sidorova et al., 2008). Because Lee et al. (2015) use the concepts of ‘IS artefact’ and ‘information system’ interchangeably (most clearly on page 15, when writing ‘IS or IS artefact’, they equate the two), I will use the term ‘IS application’ rather than ‘information system’ in the sequel to avoid confusion with their terminology. The purpose of this section is to contrast the concept of ‘IS artefact’ of Lee et al. (2015) with the concept of ‘IS application’ as the traditional unit of design in IS development (Davis, 1982).

Lyytinen (1987) identi<sup>fi</sup>es the technology context, language context and organisational context of information systems (IS application). The organisational context refers to the nature and content of organisational roles through which organisational activities are carried out; the language context refers to the nature, form, content and use of messages recorded and ex changed in the organisation; and the technology context refers to the physical means and technical knowhow by which organisational tasks are carried out (Lyytinen et al., 1991). If the organisational context is interpreted as corresponding to the social (as Lee et al. do in their <sup>fi</sup>rs exemplifying case), a clear similarity – even though not necessarily a full equivalence – exists between Lyytinen’s contexts and the three categories of artefacts that Lee et al. (2015) suggest.

According to Iivari & Lyytinen (1998), Lyytinen’s contexts were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the idea of levels of abstraction suggested in the PSC/PIOCO model for IS applications as units of IS develop ment (Kerola & Järvinen, 1975; Iivari, 1983): the pragmatic/organisational level (≈ social level), semantic/conceptual/infological level and constructive/datalogical/technical level. The names of these levels of abstraction have varied, but their meanings have largely remained the same. Brie<sup>fl</sup>y stated, the pragmatic/organisational level views the IS application in its – possibly redesigned – organisational context; the semantic/conceptual/infological level focuses on the semantics of the information content of the IS application; and the constructive/datalogical/tech nical level on the technical implementation of the application.

Iivari (1989) summarises the relationship between contexts and levels of abstraction using Figure 1 with slight modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. The major modi<sup>fi</sup>cation is that Figure 1 explicitly conceptualises an IS application as a system of application software and information content (Väyrynen & Iivari, 2015). The point is that any application software (e.g. Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) software) is not an IS application without information content (data/information/knowledge). <sup>3</sup>

Iivari (1989) justi<sup>fi</sup>es the hierarchy of levels of abstraction, referring to Lyytinen’s (1987) contexts (p. 11): ‘Technology, or in general, the physical world, is the basis for the language context, because language is always represented in some material carrier. On the other hand language is necessary for any organizational social action that comes into focus in the organization context’.<sup>4</sup> Figure 1 also reminds us that the organisational and language contexts are largely emergent (Tichy, 1980) and only partially prescribed and designable.

To illustrate the framework of Figure 1, let me take a familiar example of an accounting IS rather than esoteric examples as in Lee et al. (2015). Table 1 maps the six components of accounting information systems (people, procedures and instructions, data, software, IT infrastructure and internal controls and security measures Romney et al., 2013, p. 16) according to the three levels of abstraction. It should be noted that people – mentioned at the pragmatic/organisational level – who are making use of the accounting IS are not a part o the IS application. They are simply users belonging to the environment of the IS application (even though the application likely includes information about its intended users).

The point of Figure 1 and Table 1 is that an IS application is not organisationally (≈ socially) empty but rather typically encapsulates various ‘structures, routines and norms implicit in the organisational contexts within which it is embedded’ (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003), corresponding to the pragmatic/organisational level in Figure 1. Similarly, an IS application is not informationally empty but instead comprises information content (data/information/knowledge) corresponding to the semantic/conceptual/infological level in Figure 1.

This model leads to several questions about Lee et al. (2015). Because they do not recognise IS applications as artefacts, the most crucial one is whether their ‘IS artefact’ can be equated with ‘IS application’: the ‘social artefact’, ‘information artefact’ and ‘IT artefact’ corresponding to the three levels of abstraction in Figure 1. If not, is it reasonable to forget ‘IS application’ totally as an irrelevant concept and to focus on ‘IS artefacts’ as Lee et al. (2015) implicitly suggest? The rest of this section addresses the former question and the next section the latter question.

To understand the position of Lee et al. (2015), it is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to make a distinction between levels of abstraction and hierarchical decomposition (Appendix A). Lee et al. (2015) conceptu alise ‘IS artefact’ as a system consisting of subsystems, including (1) a technology artefact, (2) an information artefact and (3) a social artefact, where the whole (the IS artefact) is greater than the sum of its parts (the three constituent artefacts as subsystems). This suggests that, at the general levels, they see an IS artefact as a composite of the three subsystems rather than as three levels of abstraction from the IS artefact.

The three concrete examples in Lee et al. (2015) help with determining the position of the social artefact more concretely. The <sup>fi</sup>rst case is the classical sociotechnical study of the longwall method of coal mining; the second is a case of Chinese immigrant restaurant workers distrib uted around the USA, who use mobile phones and especially their conference capability to par ticipate in church activity remotely; and the third example is a case of communication and information sharing to facilitate the reporting and tracking of health issues among women living in a particular community in India. Appendix B analyses in detail the IS artefact and its constituents in the three cases. The analyses lead me to interpret that the social artefact, as a component of the IS artefact, is something emergent, even though it is prescribed (designed) to some degree in the organisational context (≈ social context) of IS applications in Figure 1. Especially, the second example indicates that the information artefacts in Lee et al. (2015) may comprise all kinds of speech-act utterances in the language context, being largely emergent. Thus, I interpret the concept of IS artefact to be a broader concept than IS application is, with the social artefact largely belonging to the organisational context in Lyytinen (1987), and with the information artefact belonging to the language context.

## DESIGNING IS ARTEFACTS

Lee et al. (2015) seemingly emphasise design but address it quite thinly. One reason is that they focus only on ‘instantiations’ and totally omit design artefacts such as constructs, models and methods (March & Smith, 1995; Hevner et al., 2004), which provide concepts and notations for design drawing and sketching. Nevertheless, one can identify some problematic issues in their design thinking at a more general level. I will discuss four of them:

• Design of an IS artefact as an emergent system.

• Different degrees of designability of subsystems of the IS artefact.

• IS application as the design nexus of the IS artefact.

• The role of IS professionals as developers of the IS artefact.

## Design of an IS artefact as an emergent system

Lee et al. (2015) do not make it clear how they perceive the design of the IS artefact as a totality to take place. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst example of coal mining, one may construe that technology and the two different forms of division labour were designed in the spirit of the sociotechnical design, but de signing the information artefact is not explained. In the second example, it seems that the IT artefact was designed – even though not by professional designers – while the other two subsystems of the IS artefact emerged. In the third case, likely all of the three subsystems were designed to some degree, even though Lee et al. (2015) does not explain it in detail.

Irrespective of the desians of the three subsystems. it seems the whole IS artefact emergec in the three cases, evidenced in particular by the second case of designing an IS (or an IS artefact) for Chinese restaurant workers in the USA: ‘The result can be considered to be an IS, emerging from the mutually enabling interactions of a technology artefact, an information artefact and a social artefact’ (Lee et al., 2015, p. 14). As a consequence, they seem to see the de sign of IS artefacts as ‘design by emergence’ rather than by deliberate design.

‘Design by emergence’ is an interesting idea, but the concept of ‘emergence’ is also a contested concept (Appendix A). I <sup>fi</sup>nd Abbott’s (2006, 2007) proposal for distinguishing static emergence and dynamic emergence to be useful. By static emergence, I mean emergence where the emergent properties of the whole are static and usually predictable, while in the case of dynamic emergence, the outcome is dependent on time and is often dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict (Appendix A). Simple emergence in the case of chemical compounds illustrates the former (e.g. a water molecule is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen), while dynamic emergence is typica in the case of complex, open systems with autonomous agents.

Although Lee et al. (2015) use chemical compounds to exemplify emergence, I assume that IS artefacts exhibit dynamic rather than static emergence, the outcome of which may be highly unpredictable. A question is if it is meaningful to talk about the design of a dynamically emergent, unpredictable phenomenon. It naturally depends on what we mean by ‘design’. The Oxford English Dictionary includes a number of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of ‘design’ as a noun. Two of them seem relevant here. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one states that design is a ‘plan or scheme conceived in the mind and intended for subsequent execution; the preliminary conception of an idea that is to be carried into effect by action; a project’, and the second one states that it is a ‘preliminary drawing or sketch; a plan, outline, or model produced to show the look or function of a building, machine, or other object before it is made or built’. Thus, ‘design’ in ordinary English covers both plans and designs, the latter in a narrow meaning. The idea of combining planning and emergence in dynamic environments has been proposed (Minzberg, 1994; Andersen, 2004), while the idea of combining design and emergence seems to be rarer.<sup>5</sup> Minzberg (1994) distinguishes ‘deliberate strategy’ and ‘emergent strategy’. Deliberate strategy refers to the parts of an intended strategy that were fully implemented, whereas emergent strategy is where the realised patterns were not expressly intended but rather emerged as patterns of action. In an analogous way, one might distinguish ‘emergent plan’ and ‘emergent design’.

It is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss the ideas of ‘design by emergence’ and ‘emergent design’ in more detail. <sup>6</sup> I contend that the unpredictability of dynamic emergence imposes limits on the possibilities of design, i.e. the designability of a phenomenon, and therefore at least makes it necessary to have a design approach that considerably differs from the dominant deliberate-design approach. To avoid the connotation of deliberate design, I prefer the word ‘development’ when speaking about in<sup>fl</sup>uencing a dynamically emergent phenomenon, recognising that development comprises both design and emergence. Thus, I see ‘design and ‘emergence’ not as mutually exclusive but rather as complementary approaches (Figure 1) so that the same system (e.g. an organisation) may be simultaneously governed by both (cf. prescribed vs. emergent organisation in Tichy, 1980).

## Different degrees of designability of subsystems of the IS artefac

It seems to me that Lee et al. (2015) underestimate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of technical design in the context of IT artefacts, overestimate the chances of meaningfully designing the social artefact and do not really address the nature of designing the information artefact. As for the IT artefact, Lee et al. explain the second case of an IS artefact with an IT artefact but without IT designers by the observation that the IT artefact (in this case, the mobile phone/conference call technology) was already built and therefore already available (p. 20). I do not think the availability of technology (IT artefacts) eliminates the need for technical design, as usually somebody must <sup>fi</sup>gure out how to use the available technology artefacts in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS artefact, which often requires tailoring, con<sup>fi</sup>guration and programming.

As for information artefacts, one should remember that, in the context of traditional IS appli cations, designing information artefacts usually takes place at the meta-level (using, e.g. ER modelling, designing the schema for the database and designing the report layouts), but the ac tual information objects (data) are created during the use process.

The idea of designing the social artefact is quite problematic as illustrated by the suspicious reputation of the idea of ‘social engineering’ in sociology, although there are also supporters too (Duff, 2005; Turner, 2005). It is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss this topic, but in my view, ‘engineering’ is not a particularly good metaphor in the case of social development, as social artefacts to a large extent dynamically emerge, being only partially designed. Thus, traditional blueprint-oriented (social) engineering (Duff, 2005) does not work in their case. Second, as Petroski (1982) claims, failure is central to understanding engineering, and lessons learned from failures can advance engineering knowledge more than successful cases can. It does sound very good in the case of social artefacts to consciously experiment with social systems.

It may be that for these reasons, organisational design in IS development methods (e.g. Mumford, 1983 and Lundeberg et al., 1981) is quite limited, concerning work arrangements and the division of work as well as the redesign of business activities. If social design is done more broadly, I think it should be conducted very cautiously as incremental interventions, making use of the expertise of various behavioural scientists, such as psychologists, organisational theorists, sociologists and so on, and above all in a participatory way.

To sum up, I do not see that the IS artefact is necessarily an appropriate unit of design. The major reason is that the IS artefact and its constituents – the ITartefact, information artefact and social artefact – are quite different from the viewpoint of design, especially in terms of their designability. On the other hand, I understand the point of Lee et al. (2015) that the IS artefact should be the focus. Perhaps it would be more appropriate to speak about the development or cultivation of the IS artefact as a continuous process that includes design and intervention, the emergent processes of mutual adjustment and alignment, subsequent redesign and interven tion and so on.

In the following, I will outline a model for the development of the IS artefact with an IS appli cation as the design nexus.

## IS application as a design nexus of the IS artefact

Even though Lee et al. (2015) do not discuss this issue, good reasons also exist for the sepa ration of the social artefact, information artefact and IT artefact from IS applications. In the case of IT artefacts, two reasons exist for the separation. First, IT artefacts are available on the market to be used, Second, usually IS applications share common infrastructural IT artefacts, It is a similar situation in the case of persistent (stored) information artefacts too. Public data are increasingly becoming available on the market, and different IS applications may share the same information artefacts. It has also been argued that in some application domains, information artefacts may have higher longevity than any IS application does, so their lifespans are different (Tyrväinen et al., 2006). One should note, however, that these persistent information artefacts cannot exist without some technology. Nowadays, they are increasingly in the digital form.

One notable difference between Lee et al. (2015) and the model outlined in Figure 2 is that while Lee et al. ## The role of IS professionals as developers of the IS artefact

It is not a new observation that the competent design of IS applications requires expertise in several domains and of different stakeholder groups. Taking into consideration that an IS artefact is broader in its scope than an IS application is, it seems still more likely that its design (or development) is a cooperative effort of people with different competences. The question is what role and identity can IS professionals have in this effort?

Iivari et al. (2004) identify the knowledge of <sup>fi</sup>ve domains relevant to IS application development: the (1) technology domain, (2) application domain, (3) domain of the intra-organisationa and inter-organisational contexts, (4) domain of systems development processes and (5) domain of IS applications. The <sup>fi</sup>rst three domains are close to the three constituent artefacts of the IS artefact (when the third domain is interpreted more broadly to cover the social world), while the latter two do not have a direct correspondence.

Iivari et al. (2004) ends with the conclusion that the distinctive knowledge of IS professionals lies in the last two domains, i.e. in the domain of IS applications (i.e. in the knowledge of the typical applications, their structure, functionality, behaviour and use in a given application domain) and in the domain of systems development processes (i.e. knowledge of tools, methods, approaches and principles used in systems development), which do not have a clear correspondence to the IS artefact. Their argumentation is that experts in the sister disciplines (Computer Science and Software Engineering) are usually more knowledgeable of the technology domain than are IS professionals, experts in the behavioural sciences of the organisational domain and experts in business functions (such as accounting, marketing, hu man resources, logistics and manufacturing), medicine, geography, governance and so on of the application domains.

The reasoning of Iivari et al. (2004) is based on the view that IS applications form the nexus of the competence of IS professionals. Contrasting IS applications with IS artefacts lead to severa questions:

1 Is the concept of IS artefact more appropriate as a nexus of IS professionals’ competence than the concept of IS applications is?

2 If the answer to question 1 is ‘yes’, should IS professionals be educated as generalists who have reasonable expertise in design in all three subsystems, implied by the concept of the IT artefact and their interactions? (In my view, this seems impossible, especially in the case of the information artefact, because of the variety of application domains with their own informa tion artefacts.)

3 Or, should we accept that the design (or rather the development) of the IS artefact is coop erative work that requires expertise in different domains as well as the participation of different groups?

4 If the answer to question 3 is ‘yes’, what is the distinctive domain of expertise of IS profes sionals in this cooperative process?

## IS ARTEFACT AND DESIGN SCIENCE

Lee et al. (2015) makes frequent references to design science. I have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty following many of them, possibly because I am not sure of what concept of design science they have in mind. According to my interpretation, DSR has artefacts as its core contribution, and they ‘must be in novative, solving heretofore unsolved problem or solving a known problem in a more effective or ef<sup>fi</sup>cient manner’ (Hevner et al., 2004, p. 82).

The recent DSR discourse has, however, become a hodgepodge of various ideas (Baskerville et al., 2015). One reason is that DSR includes ‘design’, a topic of extensive ‘design research’ (Cross, 1993, 2001), covering ‘design science’ with the Simonian ambition of basing the design process on ‘an explicitly, organized, rational and wholly systematic approach to design’ (Cross, 2001, p. 53, Huppatz, 2015); ‘scienti<sup>fi</sup>c design’, claiming that design (as a noun) should be based on scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge (cf. kernel theories in Walls et al., 1992); and ‘science of design’, referring to BSR into the design practice.<sup>8</sup> When one combines all of this with ‘science’ and in particular with the ‘theory obsession’ of IS research, which attempts to interpret all artefacts as some sort of design theory (Walls et al., 1992; Gregor & Jones, 2007; Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2010), the hodgepodge is ready.

Thus, it is almost impossible to condemn any idea on design, science and research irrelevant to the DSR discourse. However, somehow, I feel that Lee et al. (2015) confuse ordinary design – that of commercial IT artefacts available on the market or the design of speci<sup>fi</sup>c (‘instantiated’) IS applications – and design in the DSR context. For example, in the case of Chinese restaurant workers in the USA, they state (p. 15): ‘The innovative use of mobile phones, conference call features and, presumably, free evening and weekend minutes occurred spontaneously and haphazardly among “users”, without the participation of IT designers using design science to design new ITartefacts’. I am not sure that this application of using mobile phones is innovative enough in terms of what is expected from a DSR contribution.

Furthermore, I wonder in what sense these designers use design science to design new IT artefacts? If DSR is considered a design method, it is a research method rather than an ordinary systems development method. If it means the designers of those commercial IT artefacts have made use of some research results of DSR when designing those IT artefacts, then I un derstand.<sup>9</sup> However, it is not obvious that they do so. In any case, I do not interpret that the design of those commercial IT artefacts is DSR in itself.

If one looks at the relationship between IS artefact and DSR in the IS/IT <sup>fi</sup>elds in more detail, it seems that the distances between DSR and the three subsystems of IS artefacts are quite dif ferent. DSR has made direct contributions in the case of IT artefacts. The contributions of DSR in the area of information artefacts have been more indirect. Computer scientists (and to some extent IS researchers) have suggested innovative modelling and the representation formalism of information artefacts, such as data modelling (e.g. the relational model of data), semantic/conceptual modelling (e.g. ER modelling formalism), hypertext and multimedia representations and animation and visualisation techniques, but these are at the meta-level or meta– meta-level if the database schemas and report layouts are considered to be meta-data. Yet, in some IS application domains, such as computer-aided education/learning, for example, perhaps DSR could design information artefacts (education/learning material in this case) in speci<sup>fi</sup>c disciplines (e.g. physics) that are innovative enough and make it possible to solve heretofore unsolved problems (e.g. how to get every citizen to fully understand the theory of relativity, for example). However, it is likely that such information artefacts are products of physicists rather than of experts in IS artefacts.

Although usually not framed as DSR, traditional BSR in IS on the interaction between IT and social systems at different levels (groups, organisations and communities, for example) may very well include prescriptive recommendations that can be regarded as DSR contributions related to the social artefact or to the relationships between the ITartefact and the social artefact. Perhaps BSR into virtuality enabled by IT might provide the most promising research area for testing whether BSR in IS has managed to provide notable DSR contributions, even though not introduced as such.

## FINAL COMMENTS

It seems to me that Lee et al. (2015) ambitiously aim for the high generality of their ideas. One should note that this is not necessarily fruitful. For example, they start their discussion of artefacts with Simon’s (1969/1981/1996) seminal work on design science. This leads them to adopt a very wide interpretation of the concept of artefact. When extending the scope of any concept, one easily loses the point. For example, the word ‘artefact’ can be interpreted to comprise all World 3 entities in Popper’s (1978) ontology (Iivari, 2007). If we regard all theories as artefacts, all theory building would be DSR. Similarly, all measurement instruments (e.g. how to measure perceived usefulness) are artefacts, and therefore, all research developing measurement instruments would be DSR. As a consequence of this extension, much of the so-called BSR would suddenly become DSR, and the latter would not imply any substantial reorientation of IS research.

As a second example, if analysed together, the <sup>fi</sup>rst two cases in Lee et al. (2015) lead to the conclusion that normal face-to-face communication – ad hoc or planned/organised – by a group of people in the same space is also an IS artefact, even though it does not include any modern IT artefact (as in the <sup>fi</sup>rst case). It has the information artefact (just as in the second case) and the social artefact of participants with their social relationships. This extreme case indicates that their argumentation logically leads to so broad of a scope of the concept of IS artefact that it in principle covers all social life based on linguistic communication. I do not think it is meaningful.

The key concept – IS artefact – in Lee et al. (2015) is a possible way of structuring our world. As in the case of any new concept, a crucial question is how useful and fruitful it is from the viewpoint of research and practice. Even though Lee et al. (2015) emphasise the association among the concepts of IS artefact, design and design science, my analysis earlier suggests that the IS artefact is not an appropriate unit of design, as it is a dynamically emergent system, and because the designability of its components is so different. Partly because of this, its rela tionship between DSR is still more complicated.

Contrary to Lee et al., I think the IS artefact may be more useful as an analytical concept, which can be used to investigate the interplay of ITartefacts (including IS applications), information artefacts and the social artefact. This kind of research can be framed as BSR or alterna tively as action research-oriented DSR (Markus et al., 2002; Sein et al., 2011; Iivari, 2015). Possibly, this research line will lead to insights and prescriptive recommendations concerning the phenomenon called ‘IS artefact’ in Lee et al., which can be framed as DSR contributions (e.g. design principles).

I see that this research strategy has at least two advantages. First, the inclusion of the IS artefact provides the IS scholar with some comparative advantage when compared with sociologists, organisation theorists and other behavioural scientists specialising in analysing organisations and other social artefacts and computer scientists specialising in ITartefacts only, usually at quite a technical level. The comparative advantage of IS scholars may be that they are best prepared to analyse all three together, recognising their interactions.

Second, as noted in the ‘Introduction’ section, the information artefact has largely been neglected in mainstream IS research. I am concerned that this omission continues in the future as well. For example, I am afraid of that the sociomaterial perspective, which has recently achieved popularity in IS research (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Jones, 2014), does not di rect attention to the information/data artefact.<sup>10</sup> Among the six papers on sociomateriality in the special issue of MIS Quarterly, only Jones (2014) and Mazmanian et al. (2014) really pay attention to information/data, but both with some unease. Jones (2014) is puzzled with the physical or material nature of digital material (data), and Mazmanian et al. (2014) write ‘we call attention to the ways in which current sociomaterial perspectives have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty articulating the shifting, <sup>fi</sup>gural, asymmetric and dynamic negotiations between people, social structures, information technologies and representational objects’, with the representational objects clearly being infor mation artefacts. Perhaps to underline the physicality or materiality, both Jones (2014) and Mazmanian et al. (2014) prefer to speak about ‘data’ rather than about ‘information’ because the latter – if interpreted as the meaning or semantics of data – may still be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to interpret in the sociomaterial perspective than ‘data’ is. That is an interesting terminological return to the 1970s.

Thus, the explicit inclusion of ‘information artefact’ would help to expand IS research to take information more seriously so that it is not (information) technology alone and not even (information) technology and social together but rather all three as Lee et al. (2015) propose.

## A.2 Auxiliary concepts

Table A.2 introduces three auxiliary concepts used in the paper. Levels of abstraction as a gen eral modelling principle have been conceptualised in a number of references (e.g. Mesarovic et al., 1970; Wymore, 1976; Iivari, 1989). For example, a human being can be described at dif ferent levels of abstraction, such as the physical level, biochemical level, physiological level and psychological level. It is clear that an upper level does not exist without the lower levels and ultimately the physical level.

This strati<sup>fi</sup>ed model can be contrasted with the hierarchical decomposition or levels of detail. To illustrate, the human body can be decomposed into body parts or different subsystems, such as the bone structure, muscular system, digestive system, vascular system, nerve system and so on, in which the components (subsystems) can exist independently of one another (even though not in a living body). Each of the subsystems can further be divided into smaller subsystems. The muscular system can be decomposed into separate muscles and muscles into indi vidual cells, for example, and so on.

Even though conceptualizations of levels of abstraction do not necessarily refer to the concept of emergence, the idea that each level – while being dependent on the lower levels – has its own concepts and properties implicitly assumes ‘emergence’. This concept has been widely discussed in biology, psychology, physics, systems theory, philosophy and so on (e.g. Stephan, 1999; Chalmers, 2006; Clayton, 2006; Johnson, 2006; Kim, 2006; Corning, 2012). Quite interestingly, it has also been of considerable interest in Computer Science and Software Engineering (e.g. Holland, 1999; Abbot, 2006; Müller-Schloer & Sick, 2006; Abbot, 2007) but not so much in the IS literature, Merali (2004); Nan (2011) and Hovorka & Germonprez (2013) being the most notable exceptions. The concept has been used in different meaning, however. According to Merali (2004), ‘emergence refers the phenomenon whereby the macroscopic properties of a system arise from the microscopic properties (interactions, relationships, structures and behaviours) of its constituents’ (p. 422).

Following Abbott (2006, 2007), I <sup>fi</sup>nd it useful to distinguish static emergence and dynamic emergence. Static emergence means emergent property does not depend on time. A wate molecule as a compound of hydrogen and oxygen is one example and wetness of water – a property not owned by singular water molecules – is another. In the case of dynamic emergence, emergent properties (emergent behaviour) change over time. Emergent dynamic behaviour may be expected (predictable) when it is deliberately designed into the system (e.g. designed functionality of an IS application) or when it is known based on the earlier experience that the system will exhibit a certain dynamic behaviour. Alternatively, an emergent dynamic behaviour may be unexpected (unpredictable). In both cases, the emergent behaviour may be desirable or undesirable (Ferreira et al., 2013).

Dynamic emergence typically occurs in complex systems, which interact with the environment, learn and adapt and do not have central control (Holland, 1999). Social systems are prime examples of such complex systems even though they may have some centralised control, but usually only partial. Because of the complexity of such a system and its interactions, the predictability of dynamic emergence is often low, as the state space of the system is very large, the system rarely returns to a state already visited and the system is able to learn and adapt (Holland, 1999).

## IS Artefacts in the Three Cases of Lee et al. (2015)

The <sup>fi</sup>rst example borrowed from a classical sociotechnical study identi<sup>fi</sup>es mining coal as a technological artefact and the division of labour as a social artefact. The case illustrates how the division of labour in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the information requirements of individual coal miners. Unfortunately. Lee et al. (2015) does not describe the information artefact itself and how it satisfiec the information requirements, and even less the medium (≈ technology) used as the material carrier of the information artefact.<sup>11</sup>

The second example illustrates a case of immigrant restaurant workers – coming from Fujian district in China – distributed around the USA, who use mobile phones and especially their conference capability to participate in church activity remotely (with the church being physically located in Chinatown in New York). The IT artefact in this case consists of mobile phone technology in combination with conference call lines at the church for receiving calls and the information artefact of the words spoken during the conference call sessions. According to Lee et al. (2015), the social artefact ‘refers to the relationships newly emerging among restaurant workers (where, through these relationships, they pursue the goals of ending their isolation and forming a community) as well as relationships already existing among them as members of the Fujianese community’ (p. 25). This case suggests that the social artefact – consisting of the emerging community using the technology and the social relationships among them – is largely outside of the IT application (consisting of the mobile technology, allowing the confer ence call sessions and possibly encapsulating some of the social relationships).

Lee et al. (2015) introduce the aforementioned example to illustrate a case of an ‘IS artefact with IT artefact, but without IT designers’. Lee et al. (2015) note that this may not be a typica situation. Contrary to it, I would claim that it is quite typical. For example, users may design IS applications, using the spreadsheet software on their computers. Perhaps the most striking aspect in this example – even though not unprecedented in the research literature (e.g. Syrjänen, 2007) – is that the system was not designed for personal use only, as is usually done in the end-user development of IS applications, but for a community of users or participants. Ac tually, this is quite common nowadays, when different communities develop Web-based IS ap plications for themselves.

The social artefact in this case – the community of women, healthcare workers and medical of<sup>fi</sup>cers in government-operated health centres, with their relationships and health-related activities – lies in the context of the IS application in question (an IT artefact consisting of the mobile phones available for women in the community, with the text messaging feature and various health-related information artefacts).
