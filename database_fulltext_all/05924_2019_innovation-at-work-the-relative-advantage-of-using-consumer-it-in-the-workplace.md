## Abstract

Employees' personal devices are increasingly evident in the workplace; the use of non‐enterprise sanctioned hardware and software is now commonplace. This phenomenon, frequently referred to as IT consumerization, is gaining momentum. Employees increasingly are using their own devices and choosing their own software (eg, Google Apps, Skype or Dropbox) in addition to—or instead of—enterprise IT. Employees are turning from consumers of enterprise IT to IT deciders, bypassingtheISdepartmenttousewhatcriticscall“rogueIT.”Whilediscouragedinsomecontexts,theinfluxofconsumerIT intotheworkplace has been suggested to influence innovative behaviours among employees. Although the phenomenon is very prevalent, research lags in the operationalization of an IT consumerization model. In this paper, we take a close look at the antecedents and consequences of consumerization behaviours. We examine to what extent an individual's level of satisfaction with enterprise IT in juxtaposition with the level of perceived relative advantage of consumer IT over enterprise IT influences an individual's usage of consumer IT in the workplace; we also examine how organizational mandates and IT empowerment influences IT consumerization behaviours. Finally, we investigate the influence of IT consumerization on innovative behaviours at work.

## KEYWORDS

consumer IT, consumerization, empowerment, innovative work behaviour, relative advantage, satisfaction

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

IT consumerization, or the usage of personal technologies in the workplace, is now a well‐documented phenomenon. Influenced by their personal experiences, employees perceive their personal devices as easier to use and more useful, up‐to‐date, and enjoyable than the enterprise tools provided by the organization (eg, Harris, Ives, & Junglas, 2012 Niehaves, Köffer, & Ortbach, 2012; Weeger, Wang, & Gewald, 2016; Weiss & Leimeister, 2012). Studies have found that half to 90% of employees harness the capabilities of consumer gadgets for business purposes (eg, Columbus 2014; Harris et al., 2012; IDG, 2014). File sharing and collaboration tools (such as Dropbox, Box, Egnyte, Google Apps or Skype) are some of the mostly used applications (79%), followed by social networking sites (57%), such as LinkedIn or Facebook, and CRM tools (53%) (Columbus, 2014; IDG, 2014). Accessible through the Web, or downloadable as “apps,” consumer software applications have achieved a level of maturity that signify a genuine alternative to existing enterprise IT.

IT consumerization has been predicted to impact various organizational outcomes from boosting personal productivity and strengthening social and business relationships, to empowering virtual communications (eg, Köffer, Ortbach, & Niehaves, 2014; Leclercq‐Vandelannoitte, 2015; Meske, Stieglitz, Brockmann, & Ross, 2017). The organizational impacts of IT consumerization can be attributed to the shift in the role of an employee, who instead of being solely a consumer of enterprise IT, becomes an active participant in IT decision‐making, thus influencing business pro cesses (Jarrahi, Crowston, Bondar, & Katzy, 2017). Employees may no longer tolerate long development or approva cycles, often choosing instead to provide their own IT support; they feel “empowered” (Bernoff & Schadler, 2010; Junglas, Goel, Ives, & Harris, 2014; Schadler, 2011) by the freedom of choice, and are willing, often gladly, to mak their own IT decisions (Harris et al., 2012; Junglas & Harris, 2013).

The employee's new role as an active IT decision‐maker may also change how innovation takes place within th organization (Gregory, Ruch, Kaganer, & Henfridsson, 2014). Traditionally, IT innovation emanated from the IS organization. IT consumerization may constitute a trigger that shifts the role of the IS organization from the source of IT innovation related business value to a mere bystander in the phenomenon. With employee users driving th requirements for IT and the IS department, innovative behaviours will increasingly include the employee user, creating new opportunities for “intrapreneurship” (Antoncic & Hisrich, 2001) or IT‐empowered business value creation. IT consumerization, purposefully or not, has spurred an IT revolution that seems to provide employees with an increased role not only in IT decision‐making but also in innovation. While prior research and anecdotes from practice suggest that there is a relationship between IT consumerization and innovative work behaviours, empirical evidence remains scant.

Further, there is little research that synthesizes drivers of IT consumerization. Often, the phenomenon is attributed to market characteristics, such as the supply and ease of access to mobile devices and cloud‐based apps (Columbus, 2014; IDG, 2014). However, it is conceivable that workplace, along with personal factors, influences an employee's decision of whether to use his own personal devices at work or not (Köffer, Junglas, Ortbach, & Niehaves, 2014). Workplace factors may include the degree to which “rogue IT” is permitted or tolerated in the workplace. Whil in some cases the decision to allow personal IT is clear, more often company norms and culture dictate the level to which an employee perceives such usage as permissible (Harris et al., 2012). An employee may also evaluate the relative advantage of using his own IT instead of, or in addition to, enterprise systems for work, and consider his leve of satisfaction with the enterprise IT. Finally, an employee's decision to use consumer IT may be driven by persona factors. including how empowered he feels making IT decisions

Accordingly, this paper is concerned with developing a model of IT consumerization in the workplace. We identify and study 4 antecedents of IT consumerization, including relative advantage, IT empowerment, IT satisfaction, and permission levels. We examine the effect of IT consumerization on innovative work behaviours. Our research mode is tested with a sample of 254 employees across a variety of industries. Our analysis provides recommendations for research in IT consumerization and insights for practice

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

We define IT consumerization behaviour as the decision by individuals to use personal IT, including hardware and software, for work purposes, irrespective of organizational policies that may prohibit or promote their usage. Th availability of consumer IT offers autonomy; it allows individuals who voluntary adopt enterprise‐grade IT in a nonwork setting to consequently apply it at work. Today, employees have a choice: using consumer IT in lieu of, or i addition to, enterprise IT for work purposes. Increasingly, this choice favours consumer IT. While IT consumerization is now a well‐known phenomenon, the predominant view is that it is driven by market factors, such as smart phone adoption rates and increased ease of use of cloud‐based apps (Columbus, 2014; IDG, 2014). There remains a gap i research that examines personal and workplace factors that influence an employee's choice to use personal IT

## 2.1 | The relative advantage of consumer IT

Traditionally, IS research has been dominated by the study of a single system, or a class of systems, in a particular context. For example, research has often looked exclusively at organizational or personal settings; and mandatory or voluntary adoption. This is evident in models of IS usage or adoption that focus on factors of user perception of a system in a specific context. For example, researchers have pointed out that mobile devices have the ability t infringe and even transcend organizational boundaries due to their versatility for use for many purposes—in bot personal and work contexts (eg, Middleton & Cukier, 2006; Prasopoulou, Pouloudi, & Panteli, 2006; Scheepers, Scheepers, & Ngwenyama, 2006). Often, IS researchers have focused on studying 1 system, or 1 application, at a time; they have looked at Word processors (eg, Davis, Bagozzi, & Warshaw, 1989), email (eg, Straub, Keil, & Brenner, 1997) spreadsheets (eg, Mathieson, 1991), or ERP systems (eg, Amoako‐Gyampah & Salam, 2004). Methodologically, this makes sense as it rules out confounding effects that may have been introduced by the existence of other systems Researchers, as part of studying the structurational characteristics of technology, have also acknowledged that “the interpretive flexibility of any given technology is not finite” (p. 409, Orlikowski, 1992), or that technology can be appropriated if the right support is given (Dennis, Wixom, & Vandenberg, 2001).

With IT consumerization gaining momentum, however, the limitations of extant research models are apparent. The existence of alternative technologies does have an influential effect on the pattern of usage. An individual does not necessarily have to appropriate a technology but can choose a consumer IT in addition to—or in lieu of—an existing enterprise technology that might do an equal or even better job. Hence, in the context of IT consumerization, traditional demarcation lines blur (Köffer et al., 2014). The distinguishing difference in the context of IT consumerization is that an individual can now choose between IT alternatives and does not have to put in the effort to make a system fit the work task. For the first time, many employees have a technological choice: using consumer IT or enterprise IT for work purposes. While the choice is not mutually exclusive, the plethora of inexpensive devices and applications available in the market allows individuals to selectively pick those tools they believe best fit their work needs. This perceived advantage of 1 technology over another is what the IS discipline has described as relative advantage (Choudhury & Karahanna, 2008; Moore & Benbasat, 1991). In the context of IT consumerization, this choice is captured as the preference of using consumer tools over enterprise‐provided systems. In that sense, relative advantage can be viewed as a construct that has a personal as well as a workplace component as its underlying driver (see Figure 1).

## 2.2 | Psychological IT empowerment

Choosing 1 technology over another is only possible if employees feel autonomous and able to bypass or even counter the decisions of the IS department. While the sharing of decision powers between employers and employees has been promoted by theories of participative management in organizational behaviour literature (eg, Conger & Kanungo, 1988: Wagner, 1994: Wilkinson, Godfrey, & Marchington, 1997), participative management has vet to be applied to decision making in the realm of the IT function in organizations. Cognitive theories advocate the role of self‐efficacy in bringing decision powers into action (Bandura, 1989; Conger & Kanungo, 1988). Rooted in such theories is the notion of employee empowerment, capturing a psychological state of an individual's sense of control in relation to his or her work (Spreitzer, 1995b; 1996). It describes a state of cognition in which an individual wishes and feels in control in relation to his work. This heightened level of control is due to an individual's positive assessment of the work task at hand (Thomas & Velthouse 1990)

Organizational behaviour research recognized that the classical bureaucratic perspective, in which work was assumed to have only functional value to an employee, had to be substituted by a perspective in which work carries meaning (eg, Block, 1987) and is used as a means of self‐identification (eg, Bennis & Nanus, 1985), self‐expression (eg, Shamir, House, & Arthur, 1989), and self‐concordance (eg, Bono & Judge, 2003). Such empowerment has been found to be generalizable across a variety of work contexts (eg, Kirkman, Rosen, Tesluk, & Gibson, 2004; Seibert Silver, & Randolph, 2004; Spreitzer, 1996) and cultural settings (eg, Avolio, Zhu, Koh, & Bhatia, 2004; Laschinger, Finegan, Shamian, & Wilk, 2004).

Likewise, IS studies have more recently introduced the notion of IT empowerment (Junglas et al., 2014). It captures an employee's tendency toward controlling IT decisions as they relate to his job (eg, Bernoff & Schadler, 2010; Junglas et al., 2014; Schadler, 2011). IT empowerment takes into account an employee's perceptions about himself in relation to his work, as well as in relation to the technology that is available to get the work done. For example, in a state of IT empowerment, an employee may feel that he is comfortable and willing to make technologica choices and that he is, in fact, a good, if not the best, judge for making this call by virtue of his ability to pair business insight with available consumer IT. The sense of control experienced when provided with the opportunity to mak technological choices leads to a sense of IT empowerment

Intrinsic to the concept of empowerment is the cognitive assessment of choice (Thomas & Velthouse. 1990). The Latin root of power, “potere”, also means the ability to choose (Labonte, 1989). Another related concept is that of selfefficacy in bringing choices into action (Bandura, 1989; Conger & Kanungo, 1988). Both the organizational behaviour and IS literature have alluded to the multi‐faceted nature of empowerment. Empowerment comprises components of meaning (or the value an individual places on work), self‐determination (or an individual's propensity to choose how work is executed), competence (or an individual's belief that he is able to effectively do a job), as well as impact (or an individual's belief that his choice of how to do work makes a difference for the organization or group), which could all influence the decision to adopt consumer IT. In that sense, IT empowerment is an inherently personal factor that is reflective of a psychological state an individual forms toward a technology, or a set thereof (see Figure 1).

## 2.3 | Satisfaction with enterprise IT

Apart from an individual's perception about the relative advantage of 1 technology over the other and his sense of empowerment, his level of satisfaction with existing enterprise IT has also been found to be essential in technology adoption decisions (eg, Bailey & Pearson, 1983: Baroudi & Orlikowski 1988: Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988 1991: Doll

Xia, & Torkzadeh, 1994; Ives, Olson, & Baroudi, 1983). Satisfaction represents the degree of favorability or unfavorability that an individual expresses toward an information system and captures to what extent the system exceeded, met, or fell short in providing a service (eg, Bhattacherjee, 2001; Khalifa & Liu, 2003; Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004).

Traditionally, an information system has been provided by the IS department; it might even have been designed and built by the IS department in the hopes of achieving a strategic advantage over its competitors. Today, the proportion of purchased or customized enterprise applications is increasing, and so is the scope of choice an employee has. Armed with the possibility of using alternatives, employees may or may not feel satisfied with the IT provided at their workplace.

While satisfaction is usually studied as an outcome variable (eg, Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988; Brown, Venkatesh, Kuruzovich, & Massey, 2008; Oliver, 1980; Westbrook & Reilly, 1983), its predictive effect on behaviours has also shown to be vital. In fact, satisfaction with a product or service is considered one of the predominant predictors for re‐purchase behaviours (Oliver, 1999, 2009) and technology usage intentions (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Satisfaction with enterprise IT is driven by the organization that is either able to provide technological tools that fit an individual's work, or not. In that sense, it can be classified as a workplace factor as shown in Figure 1.

## 2.4 | Innovative work behaviours when using consumer IT

Empirical evidence remains scant about the benefits an organization is able to reap when employees chose to mak their own decisions about technology. One such proposed organizational benefit of consumer IT usage is engaging in innovative work behaviours (Harris et al., 2012).

Innovative work behaviours have been cited as the backbone of organizational innovation, which, in turn, is essential for the long‐term survival of any organization (Kanter, 1988). Innovative work behaviours are defined as the “intentional generation, promotion, and realization of new ideas within a work role, group or organization, in order to benefit role performance, the group or the organization” (Janssen, Van der Vliert, & West, 2004, p. 130). An underlying assumption across the management and intrepreneurship (entrepreneurship within existing organizations literature is that such behaviours benefit the organization, group or individual.

Organizational innovation is rooted in innovative work behaviours of the employee. After all, it is the individua employee who explores, develops, promotes and ultimately puts an idea into action, and produces what is referred to as innovation (De Jong & Den Hartog, 2010; Janssen, 2005; Janssen et al., 2004; Van der Ven, 1986). At the individual level, the focal point of this paper, benefits can stem from performing a job task more effectively and efficiently changing the way traditional work processes are done, or introducing new methods altogether (De Jong & De Hartog, 2010).

As it is difficult to formally recognize innovative work behaviours as part of institutionalized reward systems (Organ, 1988), they are rarely an explicit part of an employee's job description (Janssen, 2005; Janssen et al., 2004) As such, innovative work behaviours are discretionary. In other words, it is up to an employee to perform the behaviours in question or to refrain from them.

Despite the difficulty of implementing a formal process for innovation within the organization, innovative work behaviours are deemed vital to a firm's business prospects (Anderson, De Dreu, & Nijstad, 2004)—not least because innovative work behaviours contribute to a firm's value. This assumption also holds true in the case of IT enabled innovative work behaviours. Prior research on the relationship between IT resources and business value has recognized that while IT is valuable, its extent depends upon internal and external factors, such as a firm's complementarity of organizational resources and its competitive and macro environment (Melville, Kraemer, & Gurbaxani, 2004). Process improvement and innovation as measures of business value have been identified as desirable outcomes for IT investments (Davenport, 1993; Mooney, Gurbaxani, & Kraemer, 1996). Based on this research, it can be argued that managing IT consumerization, due to its potential role in changing how employees do work and pursue innovative behaviours, will be a central task of the IS department when seeking to provide business value to the enterprise.

Management studies describe innovative work behaviours as a set of multi-dimensional activities that an individual performs before innovation can take place (eg, De Jong & Den Hartog, 2010; Janssen et al., 2004; Kanter, 1988; Scott & Bruce, 1994). Even though the exact number of dimensions is still under debate in the current literature its existence is not. For example, Janssen (2005) and Janssen et al. (2004) proposed a 3‐dimensional set of idea generation, promotion, and realization, whereas De Jong and Den Hartog (2010) favour, and empirically validate, a 4‐dimensional model, inclusive of idea exploration, idea generation, idea championing, and idea implementation. In this paper, we follow the more recent model that starts with the exploration of an idea. It primarily entails the aware ness and openness to search for an opportune idea with the potential of innovation. The second dimension, idea generation, aims at identifying and recognizing an opportunity to be pursued from a set of workable ideas (Kanter, 1988). Considerable time can be spent on these two; it can happen in a structured way, or entirely serendipitously (Drucker, 1985). The third dimension entails idea promotion, which includes championing the idea among colleagues and creating favourable support. The last dimension, idea implementation, puts the idea into action

A sizeable body of research, particularly in the organizational behaviour realm, has concentrated on identifying personal and contextual antecedents for innovative work behaviours for organizations (Anderson et al., 2004; Janssen et al., 2004; Van der Panne, Van der Beers, & Kleinknecht, 2003). For example, job features, such as autonomy and pay, or personality traits, such as flexibility, have been found to have a positive direct effect on the likelihood that an employee changes his work processes in pursuit of better efficiency and effectiveness (eg, Anderson et al., 2004; Janssen et al., 2004; Ramamoorthy, Flood, Slattery, & Sardessai, 2005; Van Der Panne et al., 2003).

In the context of IT consumerization, innovative work behaviours are achieved, among other things, through the availability and use of personal consumer technologies. Individuals assume the authority to apply capabilities of con sumer technologies to the work task at hand with the ultimate goal of achieving operational efficiencies.\* In othe words, we contend that being exposed to an alternative tool set causes an individual to re‐evaluate how the task at hand can be done better and to apply a chosen tool to harness its effect of performing better at work

In a Canadian hospital, for example, consumer IT proved instrumental in improving work operations when a nurse decided to re‐design the wound care process simply by taking a picture of a patient's healing lesion with his mobile phone before re‐bandaging the wound (Harris et al., 2012). In the past, doctors often arrived after a wound had been re‐bandaged to check on the patient's progress. Nurses would have to reopen the dressing, wasting time, and ban dages, and further inconveniencing the patient. With the nurse's innovative work behaviour, doctors could view the time‐stamped photos and assess the healing, without disturbing the freshly applied dressing. Later, the IS organization helped to promote this innovation by institutionalizing a new process that provided a wizard for uploading and storing the image securely on the corporate server. In another example, a civil engineer found and used a cloud application for file storage and sharing in order to exchange building blueprints, typically large in size, with his clients (Harris et al., 2012). The alternative, email, was far more cumbersome due to limitations caused by the size of attachments. Later, the IS organization approved the corporate‐wide deployment of the consumer storage tool for work purposes. In a third example, a consumer products company had to relax a long standing policy against taking photographs in the workplace, when employees began routinely photographing the content of white boards with the cameras in their phones to provide an enduring summary of team meetings.

Innovative work behaviours driven by personal consumer IT capture the intentional exploration, generation, promotion, and realization of ideas with the help of consumer IT in order to benefit an individual's role performance, his group, or organization. As seen in the first 2 vignettes, benefits initially drawn at the individual level were later turned into an organizational advantage.

## 3 | HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

Predicting the usage of an information system has a long research tradition in the IS field. While factors such as the availability, ease‐of‐use, and usefulness of consumer IT may indeed play a role in their adoption, we believe that the decision to bring personal IT into the workplace may also be influenced by relevant personal and workplace factors. In particular, personal factors such as the level of IT empowerment an individual feels, and workplace factor such as the perceived relative advantage of personal IT over enterprise IT, satisfaction with enterprise IT, and permis sion to use personal IT may sway an individual's decision to use consumer IT at work, or not (see also Figure 1)

## 3.1 | The relationship between relative advantage of IT and IT consumerization

Conceptually, relative advantage (Choudhury & Karahanna, 2008) is rooted in models of attractiveness of alternative (Jones, Mothersbaugh, & Beatty, 2000). Empirical evidence in the retailing realm suggests that the degree to which viable alternatives exist in the marketplace impacts a consumer's switching decisions (Jones et al., 2000). With n alternatives available, the perceived benefits are low, causing a consumer to stay with his extant choice. This preference of 1 alternative over another has also been shown to be important in employee turnover (Farrell & Rusbult 1981), romantic relationships (Rusbult, 1980), and electronic channel providers (Choudhury & Karahanna, 2008).

We argue that being exposed to an alternative technological tool set causes an individual to re‐evaluate how the task at hand can be done better and to apply a chosen tool in an attempt to perform better at work. A perceived superiority of consumer IT, we speculate, should not only lead to its adoption, but primarily to a change in an employee's work behaviours. Inversely, if an employee perceives his provisioned enterprise IT to be more advantageous to hi work, he is less likely to adopt consumer IT and thus also less likely to switch his work behaviour in an innovative way. We therefore propose:

H1. The relative advantage of consumer IT over enterprise IT positively impacts IT consumerization behaviours.

## 3.2 | The relationship between IT empowerment and IT consumerization

We argue that IT empowered employees are more likely to choose to make IT decisions related to their jobs and show a greater likelihood of using personal devices. A state of heightened IT empowerment therefore entails an individual's heightened sense of meaning for IT, his heightened need to choose IT that fits his job, his heightened ability to do so, and his heightened belief that his choice can make a difference for the organization. We therefore expect that empowered individuals choose to use consumer IT at work. Inversely, employees who experience low degrees of IT empowerment are less likely to choose their own technological alternatives for work, preferring enterprise‐sanctione systems. Accordingly, we state:

H2. IT empowerment positively impacts IT consumerization behaviours.

## 3.3 | The relationship between satisfaction with enterprise IT and IT consumerization

An employee displeased with his enterprise‐provisioned IT is more likely to seek out alternatives in the consume market. By contrast, an emplovee who is satisfied with his enterprise provisioned IT is less inclined to choose alterna: tives in the consumer IT realm and accordingly less likely to use his personal IT. The obvious explanation would be that employees feel dissatisfaction toward the existing enterprise IT, and thus decide to switch to more favourable (and com mercially available) alternatives (Enderle, 2012). When faced with alternatives, for instance in the case of a purchasing decision, an individual typically chooses an option that he is more satisfied with, considering, among other things, variables including cost or convenience (Choudhury & Karahanna, 2008). Analogously, we expect that an employee is more willing to choose personal consumer IT over enterprise IT if it is deemed unsatisfactory, We hence predict that

H3. Satisfaction with enterprise IT has a negative impact on IT consumerization behaviours.

## 3.4 | The relationship between IT Consumerization and innovative work behaviours

Leaving a positive impact is one of the key aspects of innovative work behaviours in general, and those associated with consumer IT in particular. Without the aspirations of a benefit, individuals would not pursue IT alternatives. And whil their intentions might be good, their behaviour might not be perceived this way by management or the IS organization

The 4‐dimensional model of innovative work behaviour is observable when employees use their personal IT for work purposes. During exploration, employees might scout for the availability of technology in the consumer market They might spend considerable time sifting through numerous consumer devices and applications with the potential for productive use at work. During generation, employees identify tools that seem most promising and suitable to streamline their work processes. They might use the newly found tools as a base to generate new ideas for advancing their work. As part of promoting the technology, employees might discuss the newly found consumer technology wit their colleagues, perhaps portraying its usage in a favourable light. And last, during realization, employees might put consumer technology into action by adopting it as part of their routine work processes, or even developing tools on their own. The latter is possible with the newly found degree of IT savviness found among employees, particularly among the millennial generation (King, 2012). Hence, we hypothesize that:

H4. IT consumerization behaviours positively impact innovative work behaviours (including exploration, generation, promotion and implementation)

## 3.5 | Permission to use consumer IT in the workplace

Studies have shown that organizations take different stances on tackling the IT consumerization phenomenon. Organizations can either embrace the IT consumerization phenomenon by encouraging employees to bring their own I into the workplace, or they can do the opposite by either discouraging or prohibiting an employee from doing so. About a third of organizations vehemently restrict the use of consumer IT and issue policies not to use consumer IT in the workplace due to reasons of security and compliance (Harris et al., 2012; IDG, 2014; Niehaves et al., 2012). Another third of organizations embrace the IT consumerization trend and allow its usage within the corporate firewall, hoping it will lead to organizational benefits, including innovation and process efficiencies (Harris et al., 2012 Niehaves et al., 2012; Weeger et al., 2016). The last third fall between these 2 extremes, neither explicitly prohibiting nor embracing consumer IT in the workplace, but tolerating the fact that employees increasingly bring consumer IT t work. For the most part, these organizations either have not recognized the need to issue policies for or against the use of consumer IT, or simply do not see a need to do so.

In the absence of an explicit mandate for or against using consumer IT, employees differ in their perceptions of how permissible consumer IT usage is within organizational boundaries. One study, for example, has found that one‐third of sampled employees admitted to using consumer IT in the workplace, regardless of the organizationa mandate (Harris et al., 2012; IDG, 2014). Perceptions may be influenced by industry norms, organizational culture, or the prevalence of consumer IT among fellow employees. Overall, we propose that the higher the level of perceived permission to use consumer IT in the workplace, the more likely an employee will choose to do so:

H5. Permissions to use consumer IT in the workplace positively impact IT consumerization behaviours

## 4 | RESEARCH METHOD

The study was conducted as part of a comprehensive global research project on IT consumerization, spanning a 5‐year period. The project's overarching goal was to understand the phenomenon of IT consumerization, its drivers, and effects

The project included 3 methodological phases: (1) a qualitative phase in which we conducted in‐depth phone interviews with 47 business and IT executives representing a wide range of industries, (2) a quantitative phase that included a survey of over 4000 full‐time employees from 16 different countries and a survey of more than 300 busi ness and IT executives, and (3) a refinement phase in which prevalent constructs as identified by the previous phase were refined, re‐adjusted, and re‐measured via a questionnaire. The data for the present study, collected in the US was part of the refinement phase.

Overall, we followed a comprehensive, yet detailed, research design process. Each phase contributed to a better understanding of the relevant constructs, their role within a nomological network of constructs, and their measure ments. Measurements were subjected to a rigorous scale development process, following principles as outlined fo example by Moore and Benbasat (1991). The initial set of survey items was developed based on existing literature it then underwent 3 iterations of data collection and item analysis resulting in the items presented in this study. Psy chometric assessments were conducted at each stage; some items were re‐worded and others discarded based on the discriminant, convergent, and nomological validity of items. A complete list of items that were used as part of th questionnaire can be found in the Appendix (see Table 4).

A total of 350 subjects were recruited, out of which 254 provided usable data points. Table 1 provides a summary of the demographics. Subjects are either full‐time (68%) or part‐time (32%) employees; the majority (87%) has been with their respective company for more than 2 years, spanning a variety of industries. In order to account for the extent to which technology plays a major role in an employee's job, his overall technology usage per day was measured. As Table 1 shows, the majority (71%) of employees work with technology for more than 5 hours per day.

All questionnaire items were measured on a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), Measures for innovative work behaviours were adopted from Janssen et al. (2004: Janssen, 2005), De Jong and Der Hartog (2010) and refined for the context of consumer IT. Because innovative work behaviour has been conceptualized as 4 dimensions (ie, exploration, generation, promotion, and implementation) that additively contribute to an overall mea sure, innovative work behaviour was operationalized as a reflective second‐order construct. Measures for relative advan tage were developed based on measures adopted from Choudhury and Karahanna (2008) and Moore and Benbasat (1991). Measures that capture the psychological state of IT empowerment were adopted from Junglas et al. (2014) who based their initial measurements on Spreitzer (1995a; 1996). Like innovative work behaviours, IT empowerment was operationalized as a second‐order construct with meaning, self‐determination, autonomy, and impact as its 4 facets

In order to measure permission levels of consumer IT within the organization, items asked about the extent t which an organization permits use of personal devices (eg, smartphone or laptop) and/or personal applications (eg, Skype, Twitter, Facebook, text messaging, etc.) for getting work done and was measured on a categorical scale including “not allowed,” “tolerated,” and “allowed.”

Note that the questionnaire was prefaced with a description of consumer technologies as follows: “Personal consumer technologies are technologies that you, the consumer, have chosen to use for work. They include hardware devices such as your personal laptops, smartphones or iPads; and they also include software applications, such as Facebook Skype, Twitter, or Instant Messaging.” The questionnaire also stated that: “The objective of this study is to understand how employees are using personal consumer technologies in their jobs.” All descriptive statistics are shown inTable 2.

The research model was tested using SmartPLS (developed by Ringle, Wende, & Will, 2005). PLS was favoured over covariance‐based SEM for several reasons. ## 5 | DISCUSSION

We find that IT consumerization necessitates a new paradigm of IT innovation where the focus of the decision‐making for IS use shifts to the employee, and the organizational boundaries extend the corporate walls to include employees households and personal experiences. We discuss here what our results mean in the context of such a paradigm shift.

## 5.1 | Innovative work behaviours as part of future IS adoption models

In order to answer the ever‐pertinent question “so what?”, it is important to tie a workplace IS phenomenon to organi zational outcomes. Managers seek to understand and optimize how systems and users interact in order to influence organizational impacts (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). Organizational impacts are often assessed as economic measures, such as performance (Delone & McLean, 2003). However, support for the link between IS factors and firm performance remains mixed at best (Mithas & Rust, 2016; Sabherwal & Jeyaraj, 2015). One possible reason for the lack of unequiv ocal support for IS' contribution to firm performance is the dependence on various other internal and external factors (Melville et al., 2004) as well as lag effects (Bardhan, Krishnan, & Lin, 2013). The effect of IS phenomena may be more clearly observed on other organizational outcomes, such as work innovation. A related concept, intrepreneurship, which refers to “emergent behavioral intentions and behaviors that are related to departures from the customary ways of doing business in existing organizations,” (Antoncic & Hisrich, 2003) emphasizes the importance of innovative work behaviours in large corporations as well as small and medium‐sized enterprise (Ireland, Reutzel, & Webb, 2005). Innovation at the individual level is initiated by emplovees, bypassing their reguired iob functions or reguirements (Antoncie & Hisrich, 2003). Intrapreneurship has been suggested to require autonomous, as opposed to induced, behaviour b employees (Hart, 1992). While intrapreneurship and its possible antecedents have been thoroughly studied in the management literature, there is little about the role of employees as IS users that is linked to innovative behaviours. Given that employees, irrespective of their role in an organization, are more and more reliant on IS, this linkage is important. By capturing IT consumerization behaviours, along with its determinants, and finding strong support for it as an antecedent to innovative work behaviours, we are also able to contribute to the literature in intrapreneurship

The freedom to choose consumer IT in the workplace, as we have shown, is linked with self-reported innovative work behaviours at the individual level. Research has shown that an individual's innovative work behaviours are asso ciated not only with his future propensity to innovate in new job roles (West, 1987), but also with changing cultura values of the organization (Damanpour. 1991: King, 1992). Studies have also demonstrated that an organization's innovativeness and capacity to innovate is an influential variable for organizational learning, development, and participative decision‐making (Hurley & Hult, 1998). Innovativeness, as part of a firm's culture, has also been found to be critical to the firm's ability to adapt to environments and to develop a competitive advantage (Hurley & Hult, 1998; Swanson, 1994). These studies give reason to believe that allowing employees to choose technologies that they deem most appropriate fosters their potential for innovative work behaviours, thus creating value for the organization.

## 5.2 | IT empowerment is vital for IT consumerization

Our results also reveal the value of harnessing an individual's sense of IT empowerment. Acting on their sense of empowerment, employees keenly choose to use their own IT. Such employees have the potential to play a major role in changing their work practices. A newly found sense of IT savviness—manifested in personal consumer IT usage— turns employees into innovators. This sense of tech‐savviness is unprecedented and is only likely to increase as the millennial generation and their affinity for technology and electronic exchanges prospers (Oxford Economics, 2011) In the past, IS research has looked at individual facets of IT empowerment separately. For example, studies have looked at computer self‐efficacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995), or one's “judgment of one's capability to use a computer” (p. 192); they have looked at computer playfulness (Webster & Martocchio, 1992), or “the degree of cognitive spontaneity in microcomputer interactions” (p. 201); and have also looked at personal innovativeness (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998), an individual's “willingness ... to try out any new information technology” (p. 206). While each is an important ingredient for determining use, their combined effect truly comes into play with the emergence of the IT consumerization phenomenon

## 5.3 | Enterprise IT does not matter

The fact that satisfaction with extant enterprise IT has no effect on IT consumerization behaviour is contrary to wha existing theories would predict (eg, Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988; Doll et al., 1994; Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988, 1991; Ives et al., 1983). Based on extant literature, we expected that if an individual is satisfied with the IT solution provided by the organization, he is less inclined to switch to another product. However, as our data show, this is not the case, Satisfaction with existing enterprise IT neither contributes nor hinders an individual from utilizing consumer IT in the workplace. What is even more surprising is the fact that employees perceive enterpris IT to be very satisfactory on average; in numerical terms, they give their enterprise IT a 3.6 on a scale of 1 through 5. This indicates that IS departments surveved have done a good job in providing emplovees with the right tool for getting their job done.

The insignificance of the relationship, on the other hand, may be indicative of the fact that enterprise IT possibl serves a different role than consumer IT. It seems plausible that enterprise IT is regarded highly because it does certain aspects well (eg, transaction processing) while other aspects, such as communication or collaboration particularly as part of knowledge‐related work, are typically less supported by enterprise IT. Consumer IT might be used to fill this void by providing capabilities that are closer in nature to what an employee does every day.

## 5.4 | The attractiveness of alternatives

IT consumerization behaviour stems from the choice an individual exercises for work. IS researchers have sought to describe patterns of usage with respect to a single, organization‐specific system (eg, Amoako‐Gyampah & Salam, 2004; Davis et al., 1989; Mathieson, 1991; Straub et al., 1997). Similarly, IS theories have focused on behaviours with respect to a single system, seeking to predict the degree of intentionality to use the system. However, users now have a choice in what hardware and software to use to achieve a given objective. This resonates with the equifinality char acteristic of systems, where a goal can be achieved by multiple means (von Bertalanffy, 1968). With the technologica alternatives readily available in the market today, an individual can make a choice about what to use to achieve the goal, no longer relying on enterprise‐sanctioned technology. However, with the option to choose what system to use, it is likely that employees weigh their options in making that decision. As our study has shown, the relative advan tage of 1 technology over another is just one of the influencing factors in predicting IT consumerization behaviours

Our study also demonstrates that the dichotomy between voluntary and mandatory use prevalent in traditional IS utilization models is becoming increasingly blurry (eg, Agarwal & Prasad, 1997; Brown, Massey, Montoya‐Weiss, & Burkman, 2002; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). In organizations where usage is mandated, an individual unwilling to comply was, up to now, only left with a few choices: he either underutilizes, obstructs, or sabotages the systems (Hartwick & Barki, 1994). The influx of personal consumer IT offers alternatives. In fact, it offers the voluntary adoption of enter prise‐quality IT in the workplace, initiated by the employee user, not the IS department

## 6 | CONTRIBUTIONS

Empowered employees choose what systems and applications to use for getting their work done—and at what time and situation. They choose to use their own devices, applications, and IT services and undermine the centralized con trols that CIOs have carefully erected. As the effectiveness of an organization's IS function is shifting, so too are th fundamentals of creating business value through information systems. We identify implications for research and prac tice of this new paradigm of IT consumerization based on our results.

## 6.1 | Implications for research

Predicting the usage of an information system has been a key stream of research in the IS field. As individuals decide to bring personal IT into the workplace, the boundaries between personal and enterprise IT blur. New factors that recognize the role of an individual as a consumer and an employee need to be considered by IS research. Personal factors, such as the level of IT empowerment an individual feels, and workplace factors, such as the perceived relative advan tage of personal IT over enterprise IT, satisfaction with enterprise IT, and permission to use personal IT, may sway an individual's decision to use consumer IT at work, or not.

In the same vein, IS research should consider the availability of alternatives and move away from the paradigm of studying IT adoption and usage from the perspective of a single system, single application, or a specific context Finally, incorporation of organizational outcomes in adoption models, such as innovative work behaviours, might be able to some extent address the productivity paradox

## 6.2 | Implications for practice

Our results also show that organizations pursuing innovation as part of their agenda might want to pay close attention to the individual user and his individual work habits. Realizing that no matter how well‐chosen the enterprise IT, empowered users will act autonomously. Encouraging such empowerment might therefore be vital and leads to innovative behaviours at work

If indeed employees become an active part of the IT decision‐making process, the role of the IS department needs to be re‐defined. The “individualization of IS” might become reality (Baskerville, 2011) where individuals, based on their idiosyncratic needs and preferences, would be able and motivated to produce information products and/or services for themselves and others

Interestingly, employees vary considerably in the way they innovate with consumer IT. For example, and as part of our overarching research study, we found that 21.3% of employees worldwide use their personal social network to find solutions to work problems; 31.9% admit to creating their own technological “workaround.” In the future, we might find more of the latter—not unlike Jonathan Springer, a Captain in the US Army, who invested 26 000 USD to develop a smart phone application for soldiers that was able to locate the enemy, direct artillery fire, and summon helicopters for evacuating wounded soldiers (Adams, Ives, & Junglas, 2013).

The term power‐users, or super‐users, is frequently applied to a group of employees selected to pilot a new system and to help in rallying other groups in the organization (Mabert, Soni, & Venkataramanan, 2003). This group is externally empowered to lead the charge of actuating change (Wagner & Piccoli, 2007). Similarly, we suggest that organizations acknowledge intrinsically empowered employees, those that are most likely to make their own IT decisions and think of innovative ways to perform their jobs. For the IS department, IT empowerment means experimenting with new ways to nurture and channel employees' energy and enthusiasm for consumer technology. Building consumer IT sandboxes, identifying such power users, and providing a non‐punitive approach for consume IT usage should be part of a strategy that embraces the adoption of consumer IT for organizational innovation. To sim ply tolerate its usage is not sufficient; instead, organizations should proactively foster a culture of allowing IT empowered employees to choose their personal IT, if they so desire.

## 7 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

This study has several limitations. The focus was to define and explicate innovative work behaviours among employees using personally owned, commercially available consumer IT. In narrowing our focus to our research model, we restrict attention to user‐centric concepts that were most salient as part of our qualitative pre‐study: innovative work behaviours, IT consumerization behaviours, relative advantage, satisfaction with enterprise IT, IT empowerment and permission to use consumer IT in the workplace. In doing so, we sacrificed the opportunity to arrive at a more comprehensive model. Future research may identify other factors, particularly in the realm of organizational behav iour, that may influence our model. Future research might also benefit by considering other outcome variables, suc as productivity gains or employee satisfaction, that might be affected.

Also, our reliance on survey methodology provides no evidence of causality. Even though the extant literatur supports our assumptions presented in this paper, future studies might consider deploying either longitudinal or experimental methodologies. Also inherent in surveys is the limitation of relying on self‐reported measures for constructs. An avenue for future research might be linking employees' innovative work behaviours to firm‐level factors such as performance.

Finally, the consumerization of IT can be viewed from multiple perspectives. Our study is restricted to a user centric model, ignoring the influence of industry and firm‐level factors. Future research may look at expanding, or inte grating, our model into a nomological net of a larger set of factors.

## 8 | CONCLUSION

Empowered users mark the next‐generation of employees. Executives and IS departments alike need to understand that innovating in the workplace is a phenomenon that is driven, among other things, by the relative advantage an employee experiences when exposed to the set of powerful tools available in his personal realm, by a sense of IT empowerment that he acquires over time, and the scope of permission levels he is granted to use personal IT in the workplace. Consumer IT will play a growing role at work as the capabilities of consumer devices and software out pace those provided by enterprise IT. Innovation, and with it business value, will less and less stem from the IS department, but instead be kindled by employees and their IT. Consumer IT, so we believe, will fundamentally—and irreversibly—transform how enterprise IT is consumed, delivered, and used for innovation.
