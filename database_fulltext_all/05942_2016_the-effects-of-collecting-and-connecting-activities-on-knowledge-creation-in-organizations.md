# The effects of collecting and connecting activities on knowledge creation in organizations

Andreas Kaschig, Ronald Maier ⇑, Alexander Sandow

University of Innsbruck, Department of Information Systems, Production and Logistics Management, Universitaetsstrasse 15, A-6020 Innsbruck, Austria

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 27 May 2014 Received in revised form 29 June 2016 Accepted 1 August 2016 Available online 12 August 2016

Keywords: Knowledge activity Knowledge creation Knowledge development Knowledge management Knowledge work Structural equation model

## a b s t r a c t

Knowledge management in organizations aims to create an environment that facilitates knowledge work in order to improve knowledge creation and organizational performance. Since social software has been added to the portfolio of technologies for knowledge management, the emphasis of support for knowledge work has shifted from collecting manifestations of knowledge to connecting people. While this strategic move tremendously increased the connectivity of people, there is little evidence on its effects on knowledge creation. Building on the concept of knowledge activities and theories of organizational knowledge creation, we investigate how organizational and IT mechanisms that support collecting and connecting activities affect knowledge creation using a stratified sample of 126 European organizations. The paper offers two contributions to explain knowledge creation in organizations: (1) we empirically validate the claim that individual knowledge creation is a prerequisite of collective knowledge creation; (2) we differentiate between support for collecting and connecting activities and explain the link between organizational and IT mechanisms as input and knowledge creation as output. Organizations can promote knowledge creation by a strategy that combines support for collecting and connecting activities, and designs mechanisms for collecting activities targeted at individuals plus mechanisms for connecting activities targeted at individuals and collectives.

## Introduction

An organization’s success is increasingly dependent on its strategic ability to manage knowledge effectively (Galliers, 1999) as the share of semi- or unstructured knowledge work (Drucker, 1957) has continuously risen during the last decades (Wolff, 2005). The way knowledge is handled in organizations is seen as a key differentiator between organizations and as a source for competitive advantage advocated by the resource-based view (Grant, 1991; Wernerfelt, 1984) and the knowledge-based theory of the firm (Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Spender, 1996). Organizations apply organizational and IT mechanisms to establish an environment that is conducive to knowledge work (Davis, 2002) and to address the need for constant communication and acquisition of knowledge dispersed among employees (Hayek, 1945). With such managerial interventions, organizations enact conditions that enable, encourage, or facilitate knowledge creation to improve innovation, learning (Nambisan et al., 1999; Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka et al., 2006), and, ultimately, an organization’s performance (Holsapple and Wu, 2011; Zack et al., 2009).

Information systems are key to support processes of knowledge creation, transfer, or retention (Argote et al., 2003) in organizations (Newell and Galliers, 2006). Researchers distinguish between information systems support for collecting manifestations of knowledge and information systems support for connecting people (Bloodgood and Salisbury, 2001; Hansen et al., 1999; Zack, 1999). Collecting activities gather externalized knowledge that is represented in digital resources and stored in a knowledge repository (Markus, 2001). Individuals and collectives jointly handle these representations of knowledge in intertwining activities (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Kane and Alavi, 2008). Connecting activities link individuals and collectives by facilitating material, informational, or social exchange (Kolb, 2008). These links enable gaining timely access to valued knowledge of others (Borgatti and Cross, 2003) and encourage people to collaborate (Gold et al., 2001; Haefliger et al., 2011). Recently, online communities and social software have been added to the portfolio of technologies for knowledge work in organizations (Faraj et al., 2011; von Krogh, 2012). We observe that social software’s strategic imperative for more openness (Gold et al., 2001; Haefliger et al., 2011) boosts connectivity (Dery et al., 2014; Mazmanian et al., 2013) and knowledge creation (Razmerita et al., 2014).

The differentiation into collecting and connecting activities is important for organizations to decide on what activities to target when they intend to promote knowledge creation. Specifically, investigating the effects of support for collecting and connecting activities is relevant for managers, teams, and individuals who are concerned with the selection of mechanisms to facilitate knowledge creation. While researchers found that organizational mechanisms (Nambisan et al., 1999) and IT mechanisms (Alavi and Leidner, 2001) are positively related with organizational knowledge creation, they have not yet distinguished between the effects of support mechanisms for collecting and connecting activities on organizational knowledge creation. Assessing these effects is difficult due to the consequences ranging from individual to organizational levels of analysis (Gray, 2000) and the corresponding ambiguity around individuals versus collectives as the locus of knowledge (von Krogh, 2009). Thus, we need to study knowledge creation at the individual and the collective level (Allard, 2004; Crossan et al., 2011; Kane and Alavi, 2007; Merali, 2000; Nonaka, 1994) considering individual knowledge creation as a prerequisite of collective knowledge creation (von Krogh, 2009).

We address this gap by investigating the relationship between support mechanisms and knowledge creation with the concept of knowledge activities (Davenport et al., 1996; Kelloway and Barling, 2000; Meyer and Zack, 1996). We build on the input-process-output model (Hackman and Morris, 1978; McGrath, 1964) and the integrative framework for studying knowledge management enablers, processes, and organizational performance (Lee and Choi, 2003). The research question in this paper is: what are the effects of supporting knowledge activities on knowledge creation in organizations? To answer this research question, we performed a survey with a sample of 126 European organizations stratified according to size, sector and knowledge intensity. We analysed the data collected with a structural equation model to assess the effects of support for collecting and connecting activities on individual and collective knowledge creation.

## Theoretical background and hypotheses

We first discuss knowledge creation in organizations and the concept of knowledge activities before we turn to the effects of support for collecting and connecting activities on knowledge creation.

## Knowledge creation in organizations

Organizational knowledge creation theory explains the process of making available and amplifying knowledge created by individuals as well as crystallizing and connecting it to an organization’s knowledge system (Nonaka et al., 2006). This process aims at improving an organization’s capabilities to solve problems and to achieve goals (Li and Kettinger, 2006). Organizational knowledge creation is not envisioned as a sequential process, but as continuous upgrades of the organizational knowledge base transcending boundaries between individuals and between knowledge of varying epistemological forms, such as tacit and explicit knowledge (Lyles and Schwenk, 1992). Knowledge creation in organizations has been investigated at the individual level and the collective level (Allard, 2004; Crossan et al., 2011; Kane and Alavi, 2007; Merali, 2000; Nonaka 1994).

Individual knowledge creation represents the starting point for organizational knowledge creation (Crossan et al., 1999; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2006; Salisbury, 2008). Theories on individual knowledge creation focus on personal knowledge that is the contribution that individuals bring to situations, which enables them to think, interact, and perform, and on the handling of personal knowledge in organizational contexts (Eraut, 2004; Simon, 1991). Individuals innovate (a) by observing and interpreting new phenomena, creating abstractions, and implementing them for applications to situations or (b) by giving new meaning to previously observed phenomena and connecting them to situations at hand (Amar and Juneja, 2008). Individuals as locus of knowledge (von Krogh, 2009) generate and apply knowledge when working with col leagues who have different backgrounds and different kinds of expertise (Engeström. 1994).

Collective knowledge creation takes place when individuals team up, for example in communities (Brown and Duguid, 1991; Lave and Wenger, 1991) or informal networks (Krackhardt and Hanson, 1993). Theories on collective knowledge creation focus on organizing principles (Kogut and Zander, 1992) that allow formal or informal groups of employees to build and maintain a knowledge system (Nonaka et al., 2006), to coordinate their reflection, to formulate, and to solve problems relevant to the social unit (von Krogh, 2009). Both individual and collective knowledge creation are to a large extent informa and based on a continuous negotiation of meaning (Wenger, 1998). This negotiation captures the way in which individuals in the community make sense of their experiences and provides an important context for individual knowledge creation ‘‘Emergent knowledge” can be created by the interaction between the individual’s knowledge and the organizational knowl edge base in an act of collective knowledge creation by an individual (Cress and Kimmerle, 2008).

A number of theories connect individual and collective knowledge creation (Crossan et al., 2011). Individual knowledge creation is seen as a prerequisite of collective knowledge creation (von Krogh, 2009). The better individuals create knowledge in an organization, the more they increase the variety of knowledge (Ashby, 1958) in the organizational knowledge base that is available for collective knowledge creation. The spiral model of organizational knowledge creation (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995) explains the link between individual and collective knowledge creation as a social process moving and transforming knowledge from individuals into ever larger communities of interaction. The SECI (Socialization, Externalization, Combination, Internalization) model describes this social process as interactions between tacit and explicit knowledge These interactions increase (in scale and speed) as knowledge is distributed in the organization (Nonaka, 1994). The 4I framework (Crossan et al., 1999) details the two levels of individual and collective knowledge creation into four categories of psychological and social processes: intuiting and interpreting (individual level) plus integrating and institutionalizing (collective level). Thus, knowledge creation can be described as moving knowledge through phases extending from the individual level to the collective level of groups, communities, and the organization (Allard, 2004; Crossan et al., 1999; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). The phases are not passed in a strictly linear way, though, and organizational knowledge creation is influenced by individual knowledge as much as by collective context so that individuals need organizational and IT mechanisms for contributing their knowledge to organizations (Garud and Kumaraswamy, 2005; von Krogh. 2009) or, in other words, the better individuals create knowledge the better their organizations will perform collec: tive knowledge creation. We therefore expect:

H1. The more successfully organizations perform individual knowledge creation, the more successfully organizations perform collective knowledge creation.

## Knowledge activities

A focus on activities, that is on what people do rather than on what they know (Blackler, 1993), has been repeatedly used to study knowledge work (Leonardi and Bailey, 2008). Conceptualizing knowledge work as activities (Kelloway and Barling, 2000) resonates with a practice perspective (Orlikowski, 2000; Schultze, 2000) and focuses on employees acting knowledgably (Newell, 2015) or, in other words, on their behaviour as they deliberately pursue knowledge activities (Davenport et al., 1996; Davis, 2002).

Knowledge activities have been studied as explicit, goal-oriented knowledge processes (Davenport et al., 1996), as episodes representing knowledge manipulation activities (Holsapple and Joshi, 2002), as a means of creating useful knowledge (Fowler, 2000), as activity systems describing the settings in which productive activities take place (Blackler, 1993), and as patterns or practices of individual information- or knowledge-oriented activities (Schultze, 2000). Researchers have listed numerous knowledge activities (Davenport et al., 1996; Holsapple and Jones, 2004; Kelloway and Barling, 2000; Levitan, 1982; Meyer and Zack, 1996). In line with the delineation of information systems support for collecting manifestations of knowledge and for connecting people (Bloodgood and Salisbury, 2001; Hansen et al., 1999; Zack, 1999), we distinguish collecting activities from connecting activities.

Collecting activities manifest knowledge in collections of digital resources in knowledge repositories (Markus, 2001) that enable knowledge to be stored and shared (Newell, 2015). Repositories provide IT capabilities for codifying knowledge (Bloodgood and Salisbury, 2001) that enable organizations to implement a codification strategy for knowledge management (Hansen et al., 1999). Digital resources in a knowledge repository possess key attributes, such as editability, interactivity, openness and distributedness, that build a basis for intertwining activities of individuals and collectives (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Kane and Alavi, 2008; Markus, 2001). We understand collecting activities as explicit, goal-oriented processes to gather externalized knowledge that is represented in documents and systems and to store the knowledge in dedicated containers. Collecting activities mediate the joint handling of knowledge by artefacts and the sharing of knowledge by making use of its representations.

Connecting activities aim for an open culture which encourages people to interact and collaborate and has the potential for creating knowledge (Gold et al., 2001; Haefliger et al., 2011). Such relations between people who jointly seek, provide and generate knowledge are enabled by IT capabilities for creating networks (Bloodgood and Salisbury, 2001) that help organizations to implement a personalization strategy for knowledge management (Hansen et al., 1999). Catalogues of experts enable knowledge seekers to locate expertise and forge connections between communities and their knowledge bases (Garud and Kumaraswamy. 2005), Support for connecting activities describes mechanisms that link individuals and collectives by facilitating material, informational, or social exchange (Kolb, 2008) and enable gaining timely access to valued knowledge of others (Borgatti and Cross, 2003). For example, individuals control the contributions in Blogs and social networking sites while collectives control the contributions in community forums and Wikis in order to support connecting activities (Razmerita et al., 2014). We understand connecting activities as explicit, goal-oriented processes to link people, to evaluate, restructure, and integrate knowledge and to negotiate the relations between people and knowledge. Connecting activities mediate the joint handling of knowledge by discourses among people taking into account potentially contradictory interpretations.

Conducting knowledge activities is considered pivotal in order to promote knowledge creation (Cress and Kimmerle, 2008; Holsapple and Joshi, 2000, 2002). While many initiatives supporting knowledge activities in organizations involve IT support (Davenport et al., 1996), termed knowledge management systems (Alavi and Leidner, 2001), organizational sup port also needs to be considered (Lee and Kim, 2001). Organizations create shared spaces for employees to participate in knowledge conversions and therefore to engage in knowledge creation (Nonaka and Konno, 1998). Moreover, organizations facilitate employees to form high-care relationships and establish specific instruments such as project debriefings for collecting knowledge or mentoring for connecting people (Higgins and Kram, 2001; Schindler and Eppler, 2003; Swap et al., 2001). We concentrate on knowledge activities which are performed, enabled, or facilitated by organizational and IT mechanisms (Holsapple and Jones, 2004) for assessing effects on knowledge creation (Glazer, 1998; Schultze and Boland, 2000). The relationships between support for knowledge activities and knowledge creation echo the input (support) - process (activities) - output (knowledge creation) model (Hackman and Morris, 1978; McGrath, 1964) and the enabler (support) - knowledge process (activities) - intermediate output (knowledge creation) model (Lee and Choi, 2003). Support captures organizational and IT mechanisms (input) according to their ability to facilitate knowledge activities (process) which enable knowledge creation (output) (Nambisan et al., 1999; von Krogh et al., 2000). For example, using knowledge management systems (support) assumedly improves the efficiency of knowledge searches that lead to more effective problem analysis (activities) which eventually improves the effectiveness of solutions (knowledge creation) (Gray, 2000). Hence, in order to stimulate the performance of knowledge creation, organizations concentrate on knowledge activities, and implement organizational and IT mechanisms to support them. Therefore, we expect that a higher support for collecting and connecting knowledge activities helps organizations to more successfully perform individual and collective knowledge creation. In the following, we discuss these relationships in more detail.

## Effects of support for collecting activities on knowledge creation

Organizational and IT support for collecting activities helps individuals on the one hand to externalize and represent their knowledge and on the other hand to access the organizational knowledge base and internalize the explicit knowledge created by other members of the organization. For example, knowledge repositories offer organizational and IT mechanisms to create and to embed digital resources and facilitate expressing and contributing knowledge to an organizational knowledge base. Search engines operating on knowledge repositories offer mechanisms to find digital resources and foster individual usage of organizational knowledge previously made explicit. Therefore, the more effective individuals handle representations of organizational knowledge and the easier this process is, the better organizations will perform individual knowledge creation. Knowledge emerges through the subjectivity of individuals who are embedded in the context of an organization (Nonaka and Toyama, 2005) and collect knowledge by accumulation and storage of knowledge manifestations (Weiss, 1999). Collecting activities aid this process by helping individuals to articulate a part of their knowledge into information and represent the information in the form of explicit digital resources (Tuomi, 1999). Using such collections of knowledge in an organizational knowledge base increases the information exposure (Alavi and Leidner, 2001) and the variety of knowledge (Ashby, 1958) that an individual can consider for a more complete problem analysis and for finding effective solutions (Gray, 2000) and thus for creating knowledge. Therefore, we expect:

H2. The higher the support for collecting activities, the more successfully organizations perform individual knowledge creation.

Organizational and IT support for collecting activities helps groups of people to coordinate the representation, sharing, and joint sense making of explicit knowledge contributed to an organizational knowledge base. For example, knowledge repositories offer organizational and IT mechanisms to create, to embed, and to share digital resources and facilitate the building of trusted shared spaces for co-developing codified knowledge among the members of a collective. Therefore, the more effective collectives are and the easier it is for collectives to handle representations of the organizational knowledge, the better they will perform with respect to collective knowledge creation. Support for collecting activities helps to establish organizing principles to codify knowledge in a form that is accessible to a wider circle of employees beyond the boundaries of groups (Kogut and Zander, 1992). Making knowledge explicit creates a shared language and shared understanding that ease communication (Berger and Luckmann, 1967). Moreover, shared language and understanding enable employees to span the boundaries between different social practices, to expand knowledge and therefore facilitate knowledge creation (Wenger, 1998). People make sense of knowledge that has been articulated by others (Tuomi, 1999), thus increasing the variety of knowledge (Ashby, 1958) available to a work group, a project team, or a community for a more complete problem analysis and for finding effective solutions (Gray, 2000). Collecting activities aid social processes that objectify individual knowledge and are therefore essential for collective knowledge creation (Nonaka and Toyama, 2005). Thus, we expect:

H3. The higher the support for collecting activities, the more successfully organizations perform collective knowledge creation.

## Effects of support for connecting activities on knowledge creation

Organizational and IT support for connecting activities helps individuals to link and evaluate their knowledge. For example, online communities and social media offer organizational and IT mechanisms to find people and to communicate and, thus, allow for the activation of personal networks. The discourse in these networks helps individuals to assess, reflect upon, and reorganize personal knowledge. Therefore, the more effective individuals establish social relations and reflect upon their personal knowledge and the easier this process is, the better organizations will perform individual knowledge creation. Support for connecting activities helps individual members of an organization to create knowledge by justifying their beliefs in a process embedded in a social context (Nonaka and von Krogh, 2009). Social relationships provide individuals with opportunities to create knowledge and with the incentives to participate in this process (Argote et al., 2003). Employees can exchange knowledge directly and, thus, maintain awareness of developments in a domain, evaluate sources, extract, and verify useful knowledge (Ellis and Haugan, 1997) as well as classify and integrate their knowledge into personal knowledge bases (Marchionini, 1995). Support for connecting activities provides an extended field for interaction among individuals (Alavi and Leidner, 2001) and helps bringing together different biographies so that individuals can generate new ideas, insights, and experiences (von Krogh et al., 2000). Therefore, we expect:

H4. The higher the support for connecting activities, the more successfully organizations perform individual knowledge creation.

Organizational and IT support for connecting activities helps groups of people to relate and to integrate their shared knowledge. For example, online communities and social media offer organizational and IT mechanisms to set up and manage groups, to jointly reflect on knowledge and to collaboratively make sense of phenomena. Connecting activities help groups to build shared understanding, that is mental representations of organized knowledge structures shared by group members (Mathieu et al., 2000). Shared understanding eases communication (Berger and Luckmann, 1967), drives group interactions and fosters a group’s performance of knowledge creation (Mathieu et al., 2008). Therefore, the more effective collectives are and the easier it is for collectives to establish groups and to reflect upon their shared knowledge, the better they will perform with respect to knowledge creation. Support for connecting activities helps to establish groups linking knowledge seekers and knowledge providers in order to improve knowledge identification and retrieval (Weiss, 1999). These groups bring together different knowledge representing social practices as a source of creativity relevant for collective knowledge creation (Nonaka and von Krogh, 2009). Interactions within an existing social practice use a shared language and understanding while interactions between diverse social practices offer more options contributed by the group members’ different interpretive schemes (Brown and Duguid, 1991). These interactions help to expand knowledge in an organization by aiding the synthesis of knowledge and the social process of justification, during which new, useful, practical, valid, and important knowledge is created (Nonaka et al., 2006). Finally, we expect:

H5. The higher the support for connecting activities, the more successfully organizations perform collective knowledge creation.

Thereby, we gained insights into the support for knowledge activities in organizational settings. In two rounds of discussions with experts of our research project consortium and building on existent sets of knowledge activities (Davenport et al., 1996; Holsapple and Jones, 2004; Kelloway and Barling, 2000; Levitan, 1982; Meyer and Zack, 1996), we clustered the 155 knowledge activities and then identified those twelve knowledge activities representing the clusters that were supported by organizational and IT mechanisms. In this paper, we moved beyond an isolated investigation of single knowledge activities and aimed to explain the relationship between support mechanisms and knowledge creation with the concepts of connecting and collecting knowledge activities. To this end, we grouped the twelve knowledge activities into five collecting and seven connecting activities and created items that represent the two constructs support for collecting activities and support for connecting activities. The item sets can be found in Appendix A.

Additionally, we created items for measuring the performance of knowledge creation. We selected two items that represent individual knowledge creation and four items that represent collective knowledge creation by relying on phases of individual and collective knowledge creation taken from the literature (Allard, 2004; Crossan et al., 1999; Kaschig et al., 2013; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). We understand performance as on-going and continuous upgrades of the organizational knowledge base transcending boundaries between individuals and between knowledge of varying epistemological forms (Lyles and Schwenk, 1992). We developed the two constructs performance of individual knowledge creation and performance of collective knowledge creation. Again, the item sets can be found in Appendix A.

For data collection, we conducted a survey in fall and winter 2009/2010 and employed structured telephone interviews (Bryman and Bell, 2007) with representatives of European organizations to test our five hypotheses. The questionnaire was evaluated in a pilot study with six selected individuals who worked in different organizations and represented our target group. The pilot study allowed us to test and improve wording and understandability, and to further enhance content validity (Straub, 1989). Interviewers conducted appreciative critical incident interviews (Flanagan, 1954) using example instances of knowledge creation that the participants had experienced in their organizations. These examples provided opportunities to create a common understanding of knowledge creation between interviewers and participants. We collected perceptions about the performance of knowledge creation and about support for knowledge activities on a 7-point Likert scale.

The organizations were selected using two sampling approaches: stratified random sampling and purposive sampling (Bryman and Bell, 2007). In stratified random sampling, lists for randomly selecting organizations were obtained from organizations like Creditreform (www.creditreform.com). In purposive sampling, potential participants were contacted because they were thought to provide valuable contributions to the understanding of knowledge creation or could act as key informant helping to establish a contact to a representative of the organization. The sample was stratified based on size (medium vs. large), sector (industry vs. service) and knowledge intensity (low vs. high) of the organization. We relied on the NACE code for statistical classification of economic activities in the European Community (Eurostat, 2009; OECD and Eurostat, 2005) to determine sector and knowledge intensity. We used the number of employees to classify organizations according to their sizes (OECD and Eurostat, 2005).

We allocated substantial efforts to attract participants to the study with responsibility and knowledge in the domain of knowledge creation as well as a good overview of knowledge handling in the organization or organizational unit they represented. Our target group of participants needed work experience of at least three years, had been employed in the organization for at least one year, were responsible for fields of work such as change management, innovation, knowledge management, or human resources, and ideally held an executive position. Finally, we assured confidentiality to all participants.

For data analysis, we performed exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses to test reliability and validity (Churchill 1979; Hair et al., 2010) as well as a covariance-based structural equation model technique (Bollen, 1989; Hair et al., 2010) to test our research model using SPSS 19 and Amos 19.

## Results

We first describe our sample, then the reliability and validity tests performed and finally the structural model we used to test our hypotheses.

## Sample

We contacted 939 organizations of which 139 participated in our study (14.8% response rate). A total of 128 respondents fulfilled our selection criteria described in the ‘Methodology’ section. In spite of the large number of questions about complex matters, only 16 cases had missing data among the twelve variables concerning support for knowledge activities, and six variables regarding performance of knowledge creation. Two cases with more than 50% of missing data (participants had to abort the survey) were excluded (Hair et al., 2010). None of the variables had more than 4.0% of missing data and there were no significant patterns of missing data in the reduced sample of 126 cases. We relied on an all-available data approach for the statistical analyses in SPSS 19 (Hair et al., 2010). We analysed outliers with univariate and multivariate methods. All potential outliers were investigated in detail, based on participants’ comments and were not excluded from our sample

The final sample contains 126 organizations from 13 European countries, namely Austria, Belgium, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. The following three tables describe the organizations in the sample and the participants who represent them (Kaschig et al., 2013). The sample of participants matched the target group we aimed for. Based on their extensive experience and relatively high-ranked positions, all participants were capable of speaking for large parts or the entire organization they represented in the survey.

## Reliability and validity

To assess reliability and validity, we followed the common iterative approach. including the assessment of corrected item-total correlations, Cronbach’s alpha values, and results of exploratory factor analyses (EFA) (Churchill, 1979; Hair et al., 2010). For assessing validity, we adopted the two-step approach proposed by Anderson and Gerbing (1988).

For investigating the factor structure of the dimension of support for knowledge activities, we performed an EFA including all twelve knowledge activities. We relied on principal component analysis as extraction method, oblimin (with Kaiser normalization) as rotation method and eigenvalues greater than 1.0 to determine the number of factors (Hair et al., 2010; Parasuraman et al., 2005). A series of iterations (Hair et al., 2010) resulted in seven items representing the two factors: collecting activities and connecting activities. The analysis for the dimension of performance of knowledge creation in organizations resulted in a two-factor solution that separates performance of individual knowledge creation from performance of collective knowledge creation (all factors and items are listed in Table 4).

## Discussion

Despite the widespread acknowledgement of how important knowledge creation is for driving organizational performance and the substantial advances in knowledge creation research, we lack insight into how organizations can support knowledge creation. Our findings offer several theoretical as well as practical contributions.

The paper advances our understanding of organizational knowledge creation (Nonaka et al., 2006) with two contributions. (1) We confirm that individual knowledge creation is a prerequisite of collective knowledge creation. (2) We differentiate between support for collecting and connecting activities and explain the link between organizational and IT mechanisms as input and knowledge creation as output. Thus, our findings posit that the success of an organizational initiative to promote knowledge creation can be attributed to the organization’s ability to offer organizational and IT mechanisms that support collecting and connecting activities.

Concerning our first contribution, individuals have long been seen as starting organizational knowledge creation (Crossan et al., 1999; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2006; Salisbury, 2008). Our study validates this claim empirically on a stratified sample of European organizations. Moreover, there has also been a debate between a collectivist and an individualist stance on the locus of knowledge (von Krogh, 2009). We add to this debate by explaining not only upward causation of knowledge creation from individuals to collectives, but also downward causation of knowledge creation from collectives to individuals because individuals collect and connect knowledge from an organizational knowledge base that has been previously established.

Concerning our second contribution, our findings suggest that the performance of individual and collective knowledge creation is affected by support for connecting activities. While support for collecting activities facilitates individual knowledge creation directly, the effect on collective knowledge creation is mediated by individual knowledge creation. One explanation for this finding is that collecting activities facilitate only sharing and development of rationalized knowledge, while connecting activities facilitate sharing and development of both, rationalized and embedded knowledge (Weiss, 1999). While individuals are effective at internalizing rationalized and embedded knowledge, teams need to contextualize knowledge and thus operate most effectively on embedded knowledge. Collectives, such as work groups or project teams, need to share and jointly develop both knowledge types, though, Therefore, individuals drive the social processes of knowledge cre: ation (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995) by identifying and internalizing valuable rationalized knowledge and by transforming the knowledge into embedded knowledge so that the team can build shared mental models. Organizations can even extend the rationalized knowledge offered to individual employees beyond the organizational boundaries to benefit from open knowledge repositories (McLure Wasko and Faraj, 2000) that enrich the variety of knowledge available in the organizational knowledge base (Ashby, 1958).

Organizations can influence the ability, motives, and opportunities to create knowledge (Argote et al., 2003). In this view, organizations can support collecting activities to enhance an individual’s ability to create knowledge, while organizations can support connecting activities to create opportunities for both individuals and teams to create knowledge. Put in a nutshell, teams need support for connecting activities, while individual team members need support for collecting and connecting activities.

Our findings indicate that merely concentrating on collecting activities may not be sufficient, despite the commonly held view that IT support was most amenable for gathering knowledge in repositories and typical for a knowledge management strategy aiming at knowledge creation (Bloodgood and Salisbury, 2001). Connecting activities seem more important helping individual and collective knowledge creation alike. This is consistent with the findings that personalization-oriented support had a significantly higher impact on innovation capability than codification-oriented support (Rhodes et al., 2008) and that organizations should concentrate on providing opportunities and spaces for individuals to engage in knowledge creation, rather than focusing on capturing, locating, transferring and sharing existing knowledge (von Krogh et al., 20o0). Consequently, organizations can support collecting and connecting activities which corroborates the finding that organizations target both a codification and a personalization strategy for knowledge management (Hansen et al., 1999) at the same time (Andreeva and Kianto, 2012). Researchers found that interaction effects from such a combination of knowledge codification and knowledge sharing in networks benefit organizations particularly if the potential of knowledge sharing between employees is high (Liu et al., 2009).

From a managerial point of view, our findings shed light on how knowledge creation can be improved. As individual knowledge creation positively affects collective knowledge creation, employees need to be individually provided with work spaces conducive to performing knowledge activities as well as with organizational processes through which they can engage in knowledge creation (von Krogh, 2009). The organizations represented in our sample support collecting activities by providing employees with a centralized Intranet (organization-internal) browser-based search mechanisms, network shares plus email exchange of documents. The organizations in our sample support connecting activities by email, Microsoft Sharepoint, audio/video conferencing, face-to-face meetings, jour fixes, RSS feeds, newsticker, training courses, full-text search, wikis and document management systems.

Supporting knowledge creation is not considered a matter of IT mechanisms alone, but needs to be coordinated with organizational mechanisms to facilitate knowledge processes (Newell and Galliers, 2006). For example, in order to reap beneficial effects of social software on knowledge creation, organizations need to design such systems to fit the context and encourage employees to engage and build ties (Jarvenpaa et al., 2015) which resonates with our findings that such organizational and IT mechanisms support connecting activities that positively affect knowledge creation.

Understanding the effects of support for collecting and connecting activities is relevant for managers who are concerned with the strategic selection of mechanisms to facilitate organizational knowledge creation, such as for project managers work group managers, and managers of organizational IT infrastructures and processes. For example, project managers need to provide support for collecting activities of individual team members and support for connecting activities on the team level. The teams need to be enabled to embed knowledge in their context in order to foster team performance (Choi et al., 2010). Consequently, providers of knowledge management systems can focus individuals as target groups for the design of mechanisms to support collecting activities plus individuals and teams as target groups for the design of mechanisms to support connecting activities. Individual team members need to be aware of their organization’s support for collecting and connecting activities in order to establish their personal view on the organizational knowledge base and for sharing the views of teams they participate in. Managers continue to be responsible for providing an environment conducive to knowledge handling, but at the same time can help to establish a climate of autonomy for employees to engage in creative behaviours (Durcikova et al., 2011). Concretely, managers provide mechanisms to support collecting and connecting rationalized and embedded knowledge by individuals and teams. However, there are also potential shifts in control and responsibility to individuals and teams that go along with the handling of personal and team views on the organizational knowledge base (Razmerita et al., 2014).

As knowledge creation in organizations is performed in collectives which are complex and dynamic interlacings of social constellations, support for connecting activities and thus effective use of social software for knowledge management has become increasingly important for knowledge creation (von Krogh, 2012). However, the strategic shift from collecting to connecting also carries risks. First, indeterminate and uncertain reactions of employees using social software might oppose management’s strategic decisions (Haefliger et al., 2011). For example, employees might understand the introduction of social software as a signal that their connections are valued as important resources and might therefore manipulate the system to build symbolic rather than real social capital (Karoui et al., 2015). As a consequence, the organization might fail to accrue the potential benefits for knowledge creation that are entailed in real social capital. Our findings encourage managers to make explicit that their employees’ engagement in connecting activities is not an end by itself, but the means that help them to generate new knowledge and thus improve individual and collective knowledge creation. Such a clarification might help to align the organization’s strategic interests with the employees’ ‘‘individual-based information systems strategies” of how to use social software (Ward, 2012). This intervention needs to acknowledge the defining characteristic of social software that managers have little control of what is communicated (Newell, 2015).

Second, employees were found to use social software for fulfilling social, cognitive and hedonic needs with mixed effects on job performance (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015). Such engagement with social software boosts connectivity by promoting communication media as integral to knowledge work (Dery et al., 2014; Wajcman and Rose, 2011), but shifts norms towards continuous availability which can have detrimental effects on performance (Kolb et al., 2008; Mazmanian et al., 2013). However, our findings suggest that engagement in connecting activities facilitates both individual and organizational knowledge creation which is in line with research results claiming that the organization-internal use of social software strengthens both productivity and innovation (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015; Kuegler et al., 2015).

Third, organizations might find it difficult to recreate organizational boundaries, to appropriate and to protect knowledge (Trkman and Desouza, 2012; von Krogh, 2012). For example, the use of social software can enrich, extend the reach and change the dynamics of organization-internal communication (Huang et al., 2013). Organizations also increasingly engage in the strategic use of publicly accessible social media for interactions with customers and stakeholders and for understanding their needs (Benthaus et al., 2016) as well as in the strategic use of crowdsourcing platforms to develop capabilities for open innovation (Bayus, 2013; Dong and Wu, 2015; Majchrzak and Malhotra, 2013). This challenge to encourage and constrain the employees’ engagement in connecting activities entails the need for coordination and control configurations that strike a balance between the paradoxical requirements of knowledge sharing and knowledge protecting (Loebbecke et al. 2016). Managers enact organizational and IT mechanisms to foster the development of ‘‘communicational ambidexterity”, a capability to simultaneously address such conflicting communication needs (Huang et al., 2013). In addition to such organization level interventions to sustain inter-organizational knowledge creation, managers can help to develop individual level capabilities that allow employees who represent the organization in collaborations beyond their boundaries to selfregulate the tension between sharing and protecting knowledge (Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak, 2016).

Although this study uses well-established research methods, some limitations have to be acknowledged. First, a single respondent was asked to rate both predictor and dependent variables which might result in common rater effects (Podsakoff et al., 2003). To compensate for this, we first asked respondents to rate items of dependent constructs and then, after a temporal separation achieved by explaining knowledge activities, to rate items of independent constructs. We also assured confidentiality to all participants. Second, a single respondent represented the whole or a large part of an organization. While this is common practice in business and management surveys (Bryman and Bell, 2007), we ensured that the respondents held positions with good command of knowledge handling and that they were with the current organization for a period of at least one year. Third, performing a study that involved organizations in 13 European countries was only possible through sharing the effort of conducting the survey among a group of researchers and required the translation of the questionnaire. This might have resulted in differences in the responses (Groves, 2004). To compensate for this, the same individuals, native speakers of the target language, carried out translations and interviews to transfer the intended meaning of questions into the respective target language. Interviewers were carefully selected and strongly involved in the study design. The jointly created definitions, explanations and examples facilitated creating an appropriate understanding between interviewers and participants. We found no significant differences between responses collected by different interviewers The comments gathered during the study also make us confident that this potential bias was mitigated. We partially employed purposive sampling which limits the generalizability of results (Bryman and Bell, 2007). Small organizations, that is organizations with fewer than 50 employees, were not part of the study. This was based on the assumption that medium and large organizations support knowledge activities more systematically, potentially with designated roles that can serve as respondents and engage in qualified reflections in the study. Additionally, we selected organizations according to size, sector and knowledge intensity and found no significant differences between these strata. While our findings show strong content and construct validity, the results would benefit from confirmation on an additional sample.

## Conclusion

This paper presents the results of an investigation into the antecedents of knowledge creation. Following up on the call for transdisciplinary information systems research (Galliers, 2003), we intend to enrich the discourse on the dynamics of knowledge activities in organizations. Next to the empirical validation of the relationship between individual and collective knowledge creation, we explain the effects of support for collecting and connecting activities on knowledge creation. Specifically, we contribute to the understanding of the effects of organizational and IT mechanisms on the two levels of individual and collective knowledge creation. The differentiation into collecting and connecting activities as antecedents of individual and collective knowledge creation is in line with the argument that theory development on organizational knowledge creation is positioned at the nexus of context- and artefact-oriented information systems research plus the individualist and collectivist perspective on the locus of knowledge (von Krogh, 2009).

Future research could extend our model by investigating organizational and IT mechanisms as antecedents of the conduct of collecting and connecting activities plus the presumed moderating effects of the potential of knowledge sharing between employees on these relationships (Liu et al., 2009). Moreover, effectiveness, flexibility, innovation, and organizational performance could be included into our research model as outcomes of organizational knowledge creation (Lee and Choi, 2003). Such extensions help to investigate how organizations can benefit from the strategic use of social software to improve organizational performance while mitigating risks of negative effects by inadequate use (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015), escalating engagement (Mazmanian et al., 2013), symbolic acting (Karoui et al., 2015) or a lack of knowledge protection (Loebbecke et al., 2016). Another avenue for future work could differentiate the collective level into the group and organizational level of knowledge creation (Crossan et al., 2011; Garud and Kumaraswamy, 2005) to enhance our understanding of the contingencies among work groups, project teams, or communities as social units that perform knowledge creation. Finally, as boundaries of organizations have become increasingly fluid, future work could investigate the role of collecting and connecting activities for absorbing strategically relevant organization-external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Roberts et al., 2012), for sense making of large-scale data (Chen et al., 2012; Newell, 2015) and for facilitating open innovation or crowdsourcing initiatives (Bayus, 2013; Dong and Wu, 2015; Leimeister et al., 2009; Majchrzak and Malhotra, 2013) with a specific focus on inter-organizational, open and user-driven knowledge creation.
