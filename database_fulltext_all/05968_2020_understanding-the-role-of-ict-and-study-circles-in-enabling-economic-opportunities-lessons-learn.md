# Understanding the role of ICT and study circles in enabling economic opportunities: Lessons learned from an educational project in Kenya

## Abstract

In this paper, we explore how the combination of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) and selforganised study circles improves rural communities' ability to enhance their economic opportunities. We present the case study of an adult study-circle project in the Kwale County of Kenya with the objective of providing rural communities with viable and sustainable livelihoods. Study circles can be seen as a democratic and emancipatory method for learning, where community groups regularly meet for participatory discussions to educate themselves on a variety of topics of their own interest. The main goal of the studycircle members was to increase economic opportunities using ICT and, more specifically, to start micro-businesses, improve production efficacy and methods, and obtain employment. Using the capability approach and affordances as theoretical lenses, our analysis of the explorative case study shows that the outcomes of the study circle were dependent on various conversion factors and facilitating conditions, such as resources, skills, socio-cultural norms, and infrastructure. The main contributions from our work are represented by the five practical lessons learned from the study-circle members' goals (starting micro-businesses, product efficiency, and obtaining employment) from the self-organised training approach (study circles) and from our theoretical approach (combining the capability approach and affordances).

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

How can Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) be introduced in rural communities in such a way that they enhance economic opportunities? This question provides the motivation for the research introduced here which focuses on how study-circle groups and ICT are introduced to improve the economic opportunities in rura county districts in Kenya. An economic opportunity can be described as “the opportunity to freely exchange good and services as well as enjoy consumption” (Diaz Andrade & Urquhart, 2012, p. 285). However, economic opportuni ties not only involve profit maximisation but also relate to what a person can do or be with the increased economic opportunities (Sen, 1999). Employment, for example, can increase the ability of an individual to provide for his or her family, increase the individual's self-worth, and improve the general well-being of, and agency for, the individua (Sen, 1999). Increasing the number of economic opportunities is important for the sustainability of ICT4D projects (Thapa, Sein, & Sæbø, 2012). In ICT4D, a common approach for enabling economic opportunities is the use of infor mal education in, for example, telecentres, which can provide an underserved population with access to ICT and community-based training (Mat Aji, Affendi, Affendi, Sheik Osman, & Yusop, 2010). For example, women who attended computer training in telecentres were able to apply for jobs in their own or neighbouring communities and may use the telecentres to improve their businesses (Alao, Lwoga, & Chigona, 2017). There is also a need for skil training, where telecentres play a role in providing training for individuals, which will increase their employabilit (Bailey, 2009). Hence, education and training to increase human capital are to be considered as prerequisites for indi viduals to participate in economic activities (Bada & Madon, 2006)

While access to formal education has increased in most countries since the start of the millennium, there is still a divide between urban and rural areas. For example, in Kenya, the enrolment rate in primary education is 84% (United Nations Children's Fund, 2013), while in Kwale County (the study site), 39% of the people still have no formal education (Kenya National Bureau of Statistics, 2013). The adult literacy rate in Kenya is 78.7% (The Worl Bank, 2014); however, comparatively, the rate is around 20% lower in Kwale County (Ministry of Agriculture Livestock and Fisheries, 2016). To address the inequality of education in Kwale County and increase the rural com munities' sustainable livelihoods, a study-circle project was initiated in 2007 (Wamala, 2012). Study circles can b defined as a “democratic and emancipatory method for learning” (Bjerkaker, 2014, p. 260), where community groups regularly meet for participatory discussions to educate themselves on a variety of topics. Study circles as a form of informal liberal adult education are common in Scandinavia and especially in Sweden (Bierkaker. 2014). Study circles in Sweden follow the tradition of folkbildning, which can be translated as education and cultivation of the people. Other examples include Great Britain, where study circles have been used by Muslim women to engage with Islam (Bhimji, 2009); and Namiba, where they were used to attract young people to attend schools (Theunis, 2011), allowing for discussions on topics such as teen pregnancies, hygiene at the schools, and after school activities.

What differentiates study circles from other forms of informal education, such as training in telecentres, is their democratic and participatory approach to education and skill development. Study circles are, by their nature bottom-up initiatives where the study-circle group members decide their topics of study. In the implementation phase of the project presented in this paper, many study-circle groups received their own computers, printers, and Internet access, which they appropriated to achieve their goals

In this paper, we explore how self-organised study circles using ICT improve rural communities' ability to enhance their economic opportunities. The study circles helped groups in generating economic opportunities b identifying and actualising the action possibilities (affordances) of ICTs. In this regard, we derived lessons learned b harnessing the benefits of ICT and study circles. Moreover, we identified the contextual factors that can enable o inhibit the actualisation of the identified affordances vis-à-vis the economic opportunities associated with them.

In the subsequent section, we introduce the theoretical premises: capability approach (CA) and affordances Thereafter, we describe the research context and method used in the study. This is followed by the findings and th discussion focusing on the lessons derived from the project.

## 2 | THEORETICAL PREMISES

This project observed how the introduced ICT influences, and is influenced by, social arrangements and the context and obtained an understanding of the objectives within such projects. Here, we introduce the theoretical premise to gain such insights.

We understand the ICT4D project as a socio-technical phenomenon. By so doing, we pay equal attention to th technical (such as devices, tools, and techniques) and social parts (such as the participants and their knowledge, skills attitudes and values, and existing structures) to understand the interdependency between these parts and their influence on the results of the project<sup>1</sup>. Following a socio-technical tradition, we first derive our notion of economic development from the CA by considering economic opportunities as a way for individuals to make relevant choice so as to improve their agency and well-being (Diaz Andrade & Urquhart, 2012; Sen, 1999). Second, we argue that the technological components in the CA are often presented as a “black box” within ICT4D projects (Sein, Thapa Hatakka, & Sæbø, 2019). Therefore, we complement the CA using affordances, as suggested by Thapa and Zheng (2019). While both theories have been used in ICT4D research (see, eg, Zheng & Walsham, 2008; Diaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Thapa & Sein, 2018; Zheng & Yu, 2016), they are seldom combined in the same study. This study com bined both theories to gain a holistic understanding of the ICT4D project at hand, as suggested by Sein et al. (2019).

The CA originated from the field of development economics with a focus on the agency and well-being of indi viduals and a concern for social arrangements that can provide an individual with the freedom to live the kind of lif that he or she has reason to value (Sen, 1999). Central to the CA are capabilities, which are related to an individual's freedom to achieve well-being, representing the various combinations of functionings from which the individual can choose (Sen, 1992). A functioning is an individual's doing or being (such as being well-fed) and is the realised achievement resulting from an individual's choice to act on a capability. The choices, however, depend on the context of th chooser. The context is represented by conversion factors, which enable or restrict individuals' ability to gain new capabilities and make choices. The conversion factors can be of three types (Robeyns, 2005; Sen, 1992): (a) personal, such as a person's age, gender, or level of education; (b) social, including rules and norms and cultural traditions; and (c) environmental, including the geographic location, climate, and infrastructures, such as roads, electricity, and th Internet. The combination of the three types of contextual factors may enable or inhibit individuals' ability to enhance their freedom (Robeyns, 2005).

The CA does not explicitly include ICT. In ICT4D studies using the CA, ICT is often seen as something neutra that may or may not lead to positive outcomes (Zheng & Stahl, 2011). Here, we argue that the affordances perspec tive can explain the gap between the ICT and capabilities because the result is dependent on the interactio between an object (such as ICT) and a goal-oriented actor and that various actors will differently perceive and actualise affordances with the ICT (Volkoff & Strong, 2013). Consequently, this combined perspective adds value to explore how and why different actors perceived various results from their use of ICT.

The concept of affordance originated from the field of ecological psychology. According to Gibson (1979 p. 127), “[t]he affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill. It implies the complementarity of the animal and the environment.” The theory of affordances made its way from human computer interaction (Gaver, 1991; Norman, 1999) to information systems (Leonardi, 2011; Markus & Silver, 2008) and ICT4D (Sein et al., 2019; Thapa & Hatakka, 2017; Thapa & Zheng, 2019). Building on a socio-technical perspective, the theory of affordances occupies the middle ground between technological determin ism and social constructivism (Faraj & Azad, 2012). Affordances are defined as “the possibilities for goal-oriented action afforded to specified user groups by technical objects” (Markus & Silver, 2008, p. 622).

While there is an ongoing discourse on the perspectives of affordances, for a more detailed discussion on vari ous perspectives, see Lanamäki, Thapa, and Stendal (2016), our view resonates with Volkoff and Strong's (2013) view that considers affordances as the potential for behaviours associated with achieving an outcome, where the potentia arises from the relationship between the object (eg, an ICT artefact) and the goal-oriented actors. Affordances represent a potential for action, where the specific actualisation is dependent on the context (Volkoff & Strong, 2013) Affordances, however, pay only limited attention to broader social contexts in which the actors are situated and to how their actualisation may give rise to any socio-economic development (Thapa & Zheng, 2019).

Summarising our theoretical premises, we argue that while the CA and affordances have their limitations (as argued above), they complement each other in explaining the ICT4D phenomena (Thapa & Zheng, 2019). The theoretical lenses provide us with a way to analyse the outcomes of the project and organise our findings (see Figure 1) The affordances of ICT are not directly perceptible: They need facilitating conditions (such as training, appropriate information, awareness and skill development) to make them perceptible and available. Therefore, we argue that making affordances available does not automatically enhance individuals' capabilities because the proper arrangement of conversion factors is needed. Hence, to ensure that the affordances of ICT result in capabilities, close atten tion must be paid to the facilitating conditions, and the limiting conversion factors must be addressed. Furthermore capabilities are not automatically converted into functionings (Sen, 1999). To exemplify, although school educatio could be made available to everyone in remote villages, if villagers have to work instead of going to school or if socia norms prevent certain groups (eg, girls) from attending school, the resulting outcome will be minimal. In such instances, for villagers to act properly on existing capabilities, conversion factors such as personal motivation, chang ing social norms, or creating environments with job opportunities in the village need to be addressed. The actualisation of an affordance may also reveal new affordances that an individual can act upon (Thapa & Sein, 2018)

## 3 | RESEARCH CONTEXT

The project studied here introduces study circles to improve the livelihoods of rural communities in Kwale County on the South Coast of Kenya (south of the main coastal city of Mombasa; see Figure 2). The area is diverse, wit most of the population sustaining their livelihood through fishing, agriculture, forestry, or tourism. Wage employ ment in the county is low and only contributes, on average, to 8.6% of household incomes; the unemployment o underemployment rates are estimated to be around 30% of the labour force (people aged 15-64 years; Kwale County Government, 2016). While most of the villages included in the project are close to the main road to Tanzania some are more remote and only accessible by dirt roads.

The project is coordinated by Coastal Oceans Research and Development—Indian Ocean (CORDIO) East Afric and aims to address the educational needs of the rural poor to help support their livelihoods and other incomegenerating activities (Wamala, 2012). The overall objectives of the project are to support environmentally sustainable livelihoods, introduce adult education, support activities to empower villagers, reduce poverty and increase the standard of living by introducing alternative livelihoods, and develop collaborations among project partners to alleviat poverty, and reduce economic vulnerability in these coastal communities. In this study, we specifically focus on th effects that the project had on the economic opportunities of individuals.

The groups selected to be part of the project were recommended by community leaders and the district officer of the Ministry of Social Services in Kenya. In 2007, groups from four locations in the Kwale County (Tiwi, Diani Kinondo, and Msambweni) were invited to express an interest in participating in the project. The groups alread existed as community groups, and all suggested groups were visited by the project team. In the initial phase, thre groups were selected, and after the first year, more groups were continuously added to the project, resulting in 10 groups being investigated in total. At the time of the evaluation (in 2012), nine of the groups were still active. The groups that did not already have facilities to host their meetings were helped by the project and local provincia administrators to find a location, and the project provided some groups with furniture and electricity. Three of th groups, however, were unable to find a location or office to host the meetings and had to have their meetings out in the open. To help offset the shortage of access to ICT, the project also provided most of the groups with a deskto or One Laptop per Child (OLPC) XO computers and mobile Internet (where available). The aim was to support educa tion through learning resources, provide the study-circle groups with access to computers (in areas where computer were not previously available), and increase ICT literacy among participants through IT training.

Education in the study circles was based on the folkbildning concept and allowed community members to learn a variety of skills in a self-organised manner. Participants came up with topics that they want to focus on in the stud circles. The topics discussed could vary greatly and included, for example, learning computer skills, reading and writ ing, issues related to agriculture, health, and women's rights, and budgeting in relation to their income-generating activities. While most of the circles were organised and run by the groups themselves, different instructors visite the groups to teach the skills needed, depending on the topic being studied. The instructors include, for example staff from CORDIO, who teach computer skills; private companies such as M-Pesa, who teach about mobile services; or government officers, who teach agricultural or fishing skills. Moreover, the groups learn new skills by teachin each other. For example, when one of the study-circle participants had skills in basic computer use, he or she held classes and discussions with other participants during the meetings. The study-circle participants live in remot areas where the literacy and education levels are low. For many, the study circles are the only way to gain access to adult education.

In addition to the study-circle sessions, the groups conduct various projects through the study circles, such as agriculture, fishing, soap production, or ecotourism. The aim of the projects is to generate income or increase food production to improve group members' livelihoods. There are, however, challenges related to the region's infrastruc ture. Kwale County has a weak infrastructure in regard to Internet and a shortage of ICT equipment (Ministry of ICT 2015), and most rural communities do not have Internet access. The larger cities have public-access points, such as cybercafés, whereas most of the smaller and more remote villages do not have access to public computers. Conse quently, if community members want to access computers and the Internet, they must travel to one of the larger cit ies or villages in the region. ## 4 | RESEARCH METHOD

The study focuses on exploring how the study circles and ICT can enable economic opportunities for individuals in rural areas and how the context enables or limits these opportunities. Given this study's emphasis on understanding the phenomena investigated within a real-life context through a rich description of particular instances (Yin, 2009) adopting an exploratory case study approach is appropriate (Kirsch & Beath, 1996). Exploratory case studies typicall address the how and why questions concerning the dynamics present within a particular contextual setting with th objective of developing initial understandings (Eisenhardt, 1989; Kapland & Maxwell, 2005; Yin, 2009). Our study is based on a single project, and we aim to establish patterns of relationships among the constructs and identify thei underlying logical arguments (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007).

## 4.1 | Data collection

Data were collected during two field visits to the Kenyan South Coast in May and October 2012 (see Table B1). Th fieldwork was performed in the rural areas by visiting study-circle groups, local governments, and other ICT actor (eg, people in local cybercafés). The main source of data for the study is the focus group discussions (FGDs) with study-circle participants (12 FGDs with 109 participants: 74 females and 35 males). Of the nine active study-circl groups, eight were included in the study (six groups were included during the first field visit, four of the groups were revisited during the second field trip, and two additional groups were included). For two of the FGDs, we had more participants (24 and 21) than we found suitable. However, owing to the participants' limited available time, we were unable to divide them into smaller groups. Hence, those two FGDs resembled meetings, where not all the participants were active in the discussions. In addition to the study-circle participants, we conducted FGDs with gov ernment officers (two FGDs with six respondents: one female and five males) and project managers (one FGD with six members of the CORDIO staff: three females and three males). For FGDs with study circles during our visits, w designed a discussion guide with topics to capture the different aspects of how the study circles had impacted th participants' lives

The discussion guide was exploratory and focused on four topics related to the CA and affordances. First, we asked about the participants' opinions on and experiences with the study circles and the use of ICT to increase our understanding of their general backgrounds and experiences. Second, to explore which capabilities ICT had enabled for them and which affordances, they perceived to be associated with the capabilities, we asked what the partici pants specifically valued about ICT and the study circles. Third, we asked what the study circles and ICT had enabled the participants to do, focusing on the functionings that the study circles and ICT provided for them. Finally, we asked them to identify any factors that enabled and inhibited their ICT use and study-circle participation, including any conversion factors that facilitated or restricted their opportunities. Hence, the topics discussed were broader than only economic opportunities. However, because the main aim of the projects was to improve the groups' liveli hood and economic situation, most of the discussions evolved around the potential economic opportunities that computers, the Internet, and study circles had enabled. The FGDs were held in Swahili (with one exception), and one member of the research team who could speak both English and Swahili translated the discussions

In addition to the FGDs, individual interviews were conducted with nine people (four government officers, three public access providers, one IT support worker, and one content developer). The interviews with the government officers aimed to gain a more in-depth understanding of the groups' context. All FGDs and interviews were recorded, except the ones with government officials, for which notes were taken because the officials were reluctant to allow the interviews to be recorded. Moreover, the activities of five study-circle groups were observed to explore how they ran their sessions and how they interacted with ICT and for what purposes. The data collected for this stud include 12.5 h of recorded FGDs, 4 h of recorded individual interviews, 12 pages of notes from interviews where the persons did not want to be recorded, and seven pages of observation notes

## 4.2 | Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using the thematic analysis approach (Braun, Clarke, Hayfield, & Terry, 2019). While following this approach, we familiarised ourselves with the data, generated initial codes, searched for themes reviewed themes, named these themes, and finally built the constructs. The process of familiarising ourselves with the data in the context was based on direct observation by one of the authors and interpretation of the notes and the transcribed FGDs and interviews. The analysis included different cycles of coding and categorising. Focusing on economic opportunities, in the analysis process, we first identified the goals that the individuals in the groups set for their ICT use in the study circles. For example, one group stated: “With the computer, they [users] must pay. That's how you can make some money if you have a computer.” The inferred goal was to start a micro-business to generat income by offering valued services (in this case, a printing service) to the community.

After identifying the groups' key goals, we identified the affordances perceived and actualised (or not actualised to achieve their goals, ie, economic opportunities. In this step, we analysed the statements in the transcribed dat and the observation notes related to ICT use. The statements were categorised based on the ways in which th study circles and ICT could aid the groups to achieve their goals (see example in Figure C1). Thereafter, we explored how the groups had actualised the affordances as well as the outcomes of the actualisation. If the groups actualisation did not match their goals or if the resulting outcome did not positively impact the groups' economi opportunities, we looked for conversion factors that negatively affected the income-generation process. The conver sion factors were categorised as personal, social or environmental (see example in Figure D1). For example, in th statement “We have problems with electricity, and the difference in the power current makes the computers break,” the limiting conversion factor relates to the poorly developed infrastructure. The subsequent section details the find ings of the data analysis

The support provided to the groups to start their micro-businesses could vary between the groups and type of activities the groups focused on. For example, CORDIO provided the groups with their own computers, Internet service, printers (SC1-SC6), electricity for their meeting locations (SC2, SC6, and SC7), and furniture and renovation of office spaces (SC2 and SC6). Only one study circle group (SC8) had not started any new income-generating activities. Instead, they focused their study-circl meeting on learning how to read, write, and use mobile phones and on discussing health-related issues

In the analysis, we will focus on cybercafés because this was the most common micro-business that the group started, and it was the activity that has the clearest connection to the introduction of ICT into the communities.

Of the eight study-circle groups included in the study, six of them started a cybercafé using the ICT provided by the project; starting a cybercafé was a goal of one additional group (SC7), but they lacked the facilitating condition (they did not have their own computer and no indoor meeting space) to achieve this goal. While one of the aims of the project was to support and encourage the study-circle groups in starting new micro-businesses, startin cybercafés were not planned by the project management. However, the groups perceived that they could make an income by allowing community members to use their computers, printers, and the Internet for a small fee. Hence, the initiative to launch cybercafés was a bottom-up approach that originated from the groups themselves and wa not initially intended by the project.

To start a cybercafé, the groups perceived several affordances of the ICT. For example, their communities required access to computers and the Internet, and the groups could generate income by providing the required ser vices. We label this affordance as technology and information accessibility<sup>2</sup>. Since many of the groups were in remote areas, where communities had no prior access to public computers or the Internet, the groups believed that the would be able to fulfil a need

There are a lot of people that would want to use that [the Internet]. We have primary schools and secondary schools, and they [the teachers] would come. Because this is becoming a big centre here, instead of going to another village, they can come here and make use of this office. ( Study-circle leader in SC6)

The study-circle group in the above example identifies public-school teachers as the most frequent customers at their cybercafé. The teachers visited the cybercafé to print teaching materials and handouts for their students because they lacked access to computers or printers at their own schools. However, different communities could have different needs that the groups' cybercafés could provide solutions for. For example, Kenya has initiated th digitalisation of public services, resulting in a need to electronically access services via the Internet because the services previously performed offline are now exclusively conducted online. As a result, the need for public Internet access in communities has increased. An employee at a commercial cybercafé noted:

Of the seven groups whose goal was to start a cybercafé (see Table 2), six were able to actualise it. One group (SC7) did not have the ICT or the facilities to realise their goal. The six groups that started a cybercafé had the ICT and facilities needed to start the cybercafés, and the study circles provided them with the computer training that they needed. However, all groups struggled to make the cybercafés sustainable. For example, three of the group struggled to pay for the costs of operating the cybercafé (SC1, SC3, and SC6), and one group only made sufficient money to generate a small income for the group member that acted as a computer trainer in the café (SC2). Note that although the groups (with the exception of SC1 and SC3) were able to make a small profit from the cybercafés, the profit was not nearly enough for them to be able to only rely on the cybercafé as their main source of income. The main reasons the groups struggled can be divided into three broadly defined conversion factors:

lack of understanding of the community needs, poorly developed infrastructure, and operational cost of the services provided.

The groups that struggled the most with making an income from the cybercafés (SC1 and SC3) had difficultie attracting community members to visit their cybercafés. For example, in one study circle, it was mainly the childre of the women in the study circle who visited their cybercafé:

There are persons that use the computers, but there are mainly children of the members of the study circle that use the computer. ( Study-circle member in SC3

Another major issue with the cybercafés was the poorly developed infrastructure in the area in terms of bot access to the Internet and reliable access to electricity. One group, for example, tried to access the Internet using a mobile Internet service provided by Safaricom, but there were no mobile towers nearby. Therefore, the grou was unable to connect to the Internet. They considered using satellite Internet, which they had been promise would work:

No (we don't have access to the Internet). The network here with Safaricom is a problem, so we should be looking at other service providers. I don't know what works here. However, the group did not have the financial resources to acquire a satellite Internet service, which would hav required external funding. Indeed, the poorly developed infrastructure was mentioned as a major hurdle for al groups: “There are problems with the computers. The problems are with powering the computers” (SC5) and “Th computers do not have access to the Internet” (SC1) were reported by two study-circle members

The poorly developed infrastructure also resulted in problems with the power current, which caused issues with the reliability of the computers themselves

We have problems with electricity, and the differences in the power current makes the computers break. ( Cybercafé owner in Kwale)

During our first visit, several groups had computers that were broken owing to electricity problems or misuse of the computers, and there were no social agreements made to service the computers. The groups were meant to be self-sufficient and self-organised to a large degree, but they lacked the financial resources to cope with th operational cost of maintaining the ICT themselves. Many of the more remote groups were also unaware of where to find someone who could service the computers for them; therefore, even if they could afford the main tenance cost, they would have difficulties. However, when this was identified as a problem in the project, an agreement was signed with a local service provider and funded by the project; thereafter, the computers wer serviced on a regular basis

The main concern with the electricity was not only that the infrastructure was unreliable but also that paying for the electricity to run the computers was challenging for the groups. They either needed a steady stream of customers or had to seek other funding to sustain their businesses:

The printer cartridges are very expensive, the printers are very slow and the cartridge can only produce very few copies. So, if they use it for a business, they see that the cartridge is finished, but whatever they have paid, they cannot pay for the cost of the printing. ## 5.2 | Goal 2: improving the production efficiency and methods

The study circles and ICT helped the members to improve existing businesses and livelihoods by adopting more effi cient methods or by marketing their businesses.

All groups are situated in areas where the main sources of livelihoods are agriculture, forestry, or fishing, and th groups identified the opportunity to improve their income-generating activities. Their livelihood activities are based on traditional practices, and they wanted to improve the efficiency of their production. By gaining access to educa tion and information, the communities can potentially challenge these traditions and find more efficient production methods to sustain their livelihoods.

To improve production efficiency, the study-circle groups (SC1, SC3, SC4, SC6, SC7, and SC8) perceived two dif ferent affordances. For example, one study-circle member in a group with both a cybercafé and camp for ecotourism stated the following:

The Internet provides access to information, and since we have a camp in the village, we want to interact with those people (potential customers) and get them to visit us. We have to contact them via the Internet. And to do that, we have to travel to where there is Internet, and that is costly in terms of travelling and time. ( Study-circle member in SC6)

Another member in the same group also recognised that they could create a website to further interact and market their activities:

If we have a website, we can post information there about the camp and the projects that we have so that donors can access and find the information there. So, it is very important. We can post what we are doing and market our activities. ( Study-circle member in SC6)

However, the groups had neither the skills to develop a website by themselves nor the funds needed to employ someone else to do it. Using a social media platform, such as Facebook, instead of developing a websit was not something that the group had thought about, despite most of them frequently using different socia media.

Most of the discussion in the study-circle meetings revolved around their livelihoods, and all groups saw a potential for using the study circles and the computers to improve their livelihood practices. However, not all groups were able to actualise potential benefits even though they had it as a goal, and they perceived the affordances. Th groups had difficulties actualising the affordances because of three limiting conversion factors: the infrastructure, poor information literacy, and restrictive cultural norms. The first limitation is attributable to the poorly developed infrastructure. Similar to the case when the infrastructure caused a problem for the groups when they wanted to start a cybercafé, the poorly developed infrastructure also meant that groups had difficulties accessing relevant infor mation on the Internet. The groups that did not have a computer with Internet access (SC1, SC6, SC7, and SC8) ha to rely on using their mobile phones to access the information or content that was either installed on their computers or provided offline. This severely limited their access to information.

The second limitation was that the group members had low information literacy. Most of the members were new to using computers and the Internet and had very limited skills in how to search for relevant information. How ever, the information literacy requirement is aided by the study-circle groups' structure, in which one member of the group can help those who do not have the skills needed. A study-circle member of a youth group that used mobil phones to search for information noted the following:

We manage. I know how to search for that. But I am the only one. ( Study-circle member in SC7)

Another member in the same group stated:

Yes, he helps us very much because he knows how to do it. Yes, we can learn from each other. ( Study-circle member in SC7)

Hence, based on the structure of the study circles, one member having the relevant skills is sufficient to benefit the rest of the group members. However, gaining access to understanding and acting on the information can be problematic because the members need to be able to assess the validity of the information in their specific context.

Another challenge mentioned by one of the women's group (SC2) was the strict cultural norms that affecte most parts of the group members' lives. The group could perceive affordances of accessing information on the Inter net, such as access to context-specific information (eg, about agriculture) to improve their practices or access to information on women's rights. Although they perceived the affordances and had the facilitating conditions, such a ICT, required skills and information, the group could not actualise it:

The problem with accessing the Internet for a woman, where we are (in the town where the group is located), is that ladies are not allowed to browse for their husbands. ( Study-circle member and ICT trainer in SC2)

The reason women were not allowed to access the Internet (according to local males) was that the men were worried that the women would be exposed to inappropriate material (eg, pornography) or become fraud victims (eg, of phishing). The reason provided by the women was that the men were worried that the women woul empower themselves and become more independent. This illustrates that for an individual's capabilities to increase, it is not always enough that the ICT supports his or her capabilities and that the individual can perceive its affordances. Here, the community's male-dominated hierarchy prevented women from actualising a perceived affordance.

The new skills that the groups learned also benefitted the entire communities because they shared the knowl edge they gained with the rest of the community.

Sometimes, when the others come and buy [their products], they ask what we have been taught and we spread the information to the community. ( Study-circle member in SC7)

The circle will help both the group and the community because the group members are from the community and they share what they get here with each other. ( Study-circle member in SC5)

## 5.3 | Goal 3: getting employment

The third goal of the study-circle members was to get employment in the offices in towns. Members in two of the groups expressed this as a goal (SC4 and SC6). One of the members stated:

I expect a lot of things from the study of computers. Maybe, in the future, I will have a job in an office where computers are used. If I learn computer skills, it will be easy for me to go. ( Study-circle mem ber in SC6)

However, to actualise the possibilities of job searching using ICT, the members needed basic computer skills and the study-circle groups provided an environment to obtain these skills. The study circle also facilitated access to ICT so that the members could apply their skills in finding relevant job information. One of the study-circle leaders stated:

Currently, we have a technological world. Anyone without computer knowledge, even if he/she has a degree, doesn't have anywhere to be or a way to get employed. So, the project is very viable for the community members as such. (…) They realise that computers are important for lifetime employment and will join the project. They get a lot more opportunities. ( Study-circle leader in SC6)

This statement shows the importance of learning computer skills to get employment.

The analysis in the study shows that the members identified three affordances related to realising their goal of getting employed. The first affordance was educability. Through the study circles, the members could learn compute skills using the ICT tools that they had been provided with. Basic computer skills were a necessary condition for get ting a job. One study-circle member expressed this situation as follows:

In fact, I am one of those that have that problem (being rejected for a job). I went to an interview, and they asked if I knew how to use the computer, so I didn't get the job. ( Study-circle member in SC4)

The other two affordances are directly related to each other and are conceptualised as job information reachability and information disseminability. These affordances are related to the use of ICT to find available jobs an to apply for the jobs. Two study-circle members stated:

Some (…) advertise on the computer, and you can find jobs online. ( Study-circle member in SC6)

We can find more information (on the Internet), job information and opportunities. It is important to know about computers when you search for a job. ( Study-circle member in SC4)

The actualisation of the identified affordances, however, was not straightforward. For example, although ther were computer colleges in the area, the majority of people did not have the funds and time to attend. This is where the study circles provided facilitating conditions, such as affordable (or free) computer training in the groups' loca vicinity, so that the members could attend the training sessions while continuing their current work activities. Th analysis also shows that different conversion factors can put constraints on actualising the affordances, which in turn inhibits the members' ability to actualise their capabilities. For example, the training in the study-circle groups was not a formal education; therefore, the participants did not get a certificate after completing their training. In the job market, a certificate was often needed, such as proof of their computer skills, when they applied for positions. A study-circle member said:

Another challenge with computer studies is certificates. (We) did not get a certificate. […] A certificate is important for employment to be able to show something. ( Study-circle member in SC6)

CORDIO, as an environmental and non-government organisation, could not issue educational certificates. How ever, there were discussions between CORDIO and the computer colleges to allow the study-circle members to tak exams at the colleges to get a certificate. But, at the time of the study, no such agreement had been reached. The unskilled trainer was another constraining factor. For example, the computer trainers in SC6 were self-taught and had only basic computer skills. One study-circle leader stated:

The teacher has no proper computer training, and she learned from the other person before her. So, she feels limited in what she offers the students. She wants computer training. ( Study-circle leader in SC6)

Likewise, another member of the same group added:

The teacher had only a few skills, so the problem I faced is that I only learned the skills that she has. ( Study-circle member in SC4)

The analysis also shows that the affordances do not always lead to enhanced capabilities. Other conversion fac tors that can inhibit capabilities are present, such as poor Internet infrastructure in the region. While it was possible to look for jobs in the newspapers or by visiting the companies directly, most job information was published on th Internet. Therefore, computer skills were important for individuals to be able to search and apply for the jobs. Thi required access to the Internet. If the groups did not have Internet access on the computers (which SC6 did not have), they either visited a public Internet access place or, more commonly, they could use their mobile phones to access the Internet when looking for jobs. However, to apply for jobs, using mobile phones is more difficult than using computers because it usually requires attaching files and filling extensive forms. ## 6 | DISCUSSION

The overall goal for the study-circle project was to address the educational needs of the rural communities to sup port their livelihoods and other income-generating activities (Wamala, 2012). While most ICT4D studies on economic benefits focus on specific applications of ICT (see, eg, Jensen, 2007; Islam & Grönlund, 2011), we have take a broader approach and looked at which goals and benefits the groups perceived with the study circles and ICT use how they appropriated the ICT to fit their goals and how the context enabled or restricted the groups from achievin their goals. We continue the discussion by presenting the key lessons learned from the project.

The first lesson we can draw from this project is that the self-organised learning approach allows for an inclusive bottom-up identification of goals and needs. As a practical guidance, we argue for the importance of providing ade quate support for participants to identify the questions that they need to ask rather than providing the answers on what to achieve. Groups or individuals, based on the facilitating conditions available to them, will innovate an appropriate their use of ICT to fit with their goals and needs. This approach is in line with Rangaswamy and Nai (2012), who argue for the importance of introducing bottom-up approaches in ICT4D projects. The most apparent example from the project was the initiation of cybercafés. The organisation of the study circles, where participant can decide what to focus on and how to organise their activities, proved useful for identifying valuable economi activities that were not initially intended by the project.

Our findings relate to the argument made by Avgerou (2003) that people in organisations “[…] make sense of the potential of ICTs in their work context and seek to appropriate it” (p. 142). Hence, this lesson could be seen a an already established common knowledge within the field. Our contribution relates to the exploration of how th pedagogical approach within the project, the self-organised study-circle groups, proved valuable for the empower ment of the groups to find their own ways. We argue that the self-organisation of the groups strengthen the unplanned characteristics of the project. In practice, our findings imply that introducing self-organised study groups may increase the value of the ICT interventions by favouring unplanned processes over more predefined manage ment and design, thereby being more likely valuable to the individuals because these processes are based on th actual needs in the communities.

We argue that the self-organised study-circle approach is more beneficial compared with other similar approaches. As mentioned in the introduction, the most common form of informal adult education in ICT4D relate to the use of telecentres. Training in telecentres is found to be beneficial in improving individuals' chances of getting employment, improving business practices, and so on (see, eg, Mat Aji et al., 2010; Alao et al., 2017). However, such training is pre-determined and often limited to computer training. Study circles have a broader approach and focu on the needs based on the current knowledge level of the members. Another benefit relates to the training of women. A gender-bias computer culture and the women's role in the families may result in women being reluctant to attend computer training (Alao et al., 2017; Kleine, 2011). Study circles are inclusive community groups started b the members themselves, and the members are comfortable in discussing most topics even in gender-mixed groups An exception to this is the issues related to sexual health and reproductive rights, which only the women's groups discussed.

However, our study also shows that a risk with such unplanned processes is that the groups fail to account for the limiting contextual factors. Regarding the activity of starting cybercafés, the variety of outcomes for the groups may be explained by the differences in both the facilitating conditions and the conversion factors from one group to the next, such as limited access to the Internet or an overestimation of the communities' willingness or ability to pay for the services. Our findings suggest that several of these factors, including social conversion factors, and not adequately dealt with within the study-circle groups. Moreover, the potential value of the study-circle approac heavily depends on the presence of individual skills within each group. Without someone in the group being knowl edgeable on the discussed topics or on the potential provided by the ICT and how to benefit from such potential, it seems almost impossible for the groups to succeed. Consequently, a lesson learned from our work is the importance of identifying the needs within the project and reflecting on the strengths and weaknesses of the study-circl approach to decide whether such an approach is sensible.

The second lesson learned is that positive economic outcomes from the project were not a result of either the ICT or the study circle but rather the combination of the two. Furthermore, the combinations should be backed-up with conducive socio-cultural norms. It is argued that a main priority for the poor is to obtain employment (Heeks, 2008) to increase their income as well as self-worth (Sen, 1999). This was evident in our project with members having the goa of getting employment to lead a better life. This goal was facilitated by two factors. First was the use of ICT as a too for learning computer skills and searching and applying for jobs online, and second was the structure of the study cir cles that acted as a facilitating condition to gain access to adult education and computer training needed for the jobs In addition, the structure of the study circles implies that skills needed to use the ICT could be aggregated from indi viduals into groups. It was often sufficient that one or a few of the group members had the skills needed for th groups to gain new opportunities because the structure of the groups allows for the transfer of knowledge from one individual to the entire group. The mutually reinforcing interplay between the ICT and study circles relates to the need to unravel the interplay between the social and technological needs (Heeks, 2012). The study circles and ICT both provide the essential facilitating conditions that mutually reinforce each other. By removing either of them, the positive outcomes would have been more difficult to obtain. Moreover, within this frame of reference, we argue that our theoretical premises proved their value. Affordances and the CA together help us to focus on both the social an technical needs. The CA is, arguably, strong on the social aspects of development but weaker on the technical ones Affordances, however, are built on a socio-technical perspective and provide us with a necessary link to examine the interplay between the technical object and a goal-oriented actor without being either technological determinism or social constructivism (Faraj & Azad, 2012). Combining the technical and social aspects in the project proved essentia in achieving positive outcomes.

The third lesson learned was related to the importance of understanding what could be achieved by the use of ICT as well as providing individuals with the competence on how to achieve potential benefits. In more practical terms, there is a need to focus on overall goals and reflect on how ICT may play a part in achieving them before providing guid ance on how to increase the competence needed to do so. Our theoretical premises prove valuable for identifying this practical lesson learned by seeing affordances as a precondition for the development of capabilities. For ICT implementations to contribute to economic development, people need to be able to perceive and actualise the affordances of the ICT because people do not use ICT if they do not see any possible actions that could help them achieve their goals (Leonardi, 2011). We identified two reasons explaining why the potential values were not perceptible in our project. First, the potential value was not always identified. The groups' low information literacy meant that they were unaware of the vast source of information available on the Internet in regard to, for example, efficient production methods for agriculture. However, once they were educated in how to use the Internet and search for information, the affordance was made perceptible to them. Therefore, it is essential that the projects provide training and/or awareness programmes. Second, if individuals do not have a goal that can be achieved by interacting with ICT, they will not perceive the affordances. For example, only young males had the goal of using the ICT to get employed in an office in a city; no females mentioned the affordance of using the ICT for that purpose. While we did not explicitly explore why no females had this as a goal, we may speculate about the reasons. In the culture of the rural communities participating in the project, women have a large role in the families, where they are supposed to take care of the household and children. Owing to this, they are often seen as not being flexible in partaking in activi ties away from their homes for extended time periods (Andersson & Hatakka, 2017), which meant that they did not have the goal of moving away from the rural communities. Hence, they did not perceive the affordance of the ICT as enabling them to do so. While ensuring that the affordances are perceptible is necessary—via, for example, design or education—for capabilities to be enabled, individuals will not always actualise the affordances; this may simply reflect the fact that the ICT does not correspond with the users' goals

The fourth lesson learned was related to the importance of understanding the context and its effect on the ICT interventions aiming to enable economic opportunities. Our findings suggest that the groups' adoption of ICT and its impact on their economic opportunities depend on both their goals and the context (facilitating conditions and con version factors). To understand the realised and potential impact of ICT on rural communities, we need to under stand the context and its effect on the ability to perceive and actualise different affordances. To quote Davison and Martinsons (2016), “Context is king.” Exploring the context is key to understanding the differences between groups, both in terms of which possible actions they identify as perceived affordances when ICT is introduced and the rela tive success of their actualisations. The CA gives us good guidance on how to categorise the conversion factors as personal, social, and environmental and how conversion factors influence the conversion of goods and service (eg, ICT) into capabilities and realised achievements (Sen, 1992; Zheng & Walsham, 2008). Our analysis shows the sam pattern where conversion factors have an effect on the conversion of an affordance into a capability and on th actualisation process (when people chose to act on the capabilities). For example, one of the women's groups had facilitating conditions (computers, the Internet, and the support of the study circle) to use the Internet to search for information to improve their agricultural practices. They also had improving agricultural practices as a goal and had perceived the affordances. However, it seems like the social pressure in a male-dominated society that prohibited them from using the Internet prevented them from actualising the capabilities. Hence, the potential benefit from the ICT intervention did not materialise owing to social norms, despite otherwise facilitating conditions. Therefore, a ke lesson learned is to not limit the evaluation of the achievement of positive results purely based on the provision of ICT and educational opportunities within rural communities. The context determines the outcomes. We argue that our theoretical premises provide analytical lenses to identify the contextual characteristics to expand the possibilities of adequately dealing with these within similar projects

Finally, we argue that ICT4D projects often have a very narrow focus on individual skills (individual capacities) and do not identify real needs (capabilities), which may lead to a design-reality gap. For example, focusing on developing individuals' capacities such as computer skills by providing computer training is clearly needed. However, if the project does not take a wider perspective, ie, identifying capabilities (what people actually want), there is a risk of a design-reality gap (Heeks, 2002), where the developed capacities do not lead to any broader development goals. This is apparent in our project in terms of the training of the members in using computers and the Internet. What is important from a development perspective is not that they have learned to use a computer and the Internet (capaci ties) but rather what they can do with their skills and which opportunities (capabilities) have been enabled for them (eg, using the computers to apply for jobs or to search for relevant information on agricultural practices). For future projects within our domain, we therefore argue for the importance of understanding the difference between capacit building and capabilities enhancement, which we derive here from the combined lenses of affordances and CA.

## 6.2 | A critical reflection and concluding remarks

We acknowledge a limitation of our work based on the fact that our study design does not allow for showing long term impacts for the study-circle members' livelihoods. The various field visits took place in a rather short period of time, allowing us to only discuss short-term effects. Hence, future research ventures include revisiting the project groups to evaluate the potential long-term impacts from the project

We started this study by asking how ICT may be introduced in rural communities in a way that will enhance eco nomic opportunities. The case study being discussed is on how study-circle groups organised education activities to increase economic opportunities by the use of ICT. While we argue that the self-organised approach in the study cir cles has proved to be valuable for the members involved, our analysis also identified factors that may challenge th outcome of such projects.

Three main goals that the study-circle participants associated with the ICT intervention in regard to improving their economic opportunities were identified: starting a micro-business, obtaining employment, and improving production efficacy and methods. The study-circle groups saw a need in their communities, and they perceived how the study circles and ICT could aid them in achieving their goals. We argue that while the study circles have been successful in many areas—members have learned how to read and write, they have learned how to use a computer, thei income-generating activities have become more successful, and so on—they have not been without problems that need to be dealt with. While self-organised groups allow participants to focus on their real needs, it seems challeng ing for them to avoid factors that negatively influence the projects' outcomes. The identification of personal, social and environmental factors that affect the groups' economic opportunities allows us to reflect on what changes are needed to increase the impact of the initiatives. Some of the changes may be as small as informing users, whereas others may require more efforts, such as changes related to social structures or societal norms. For instance, the lac of competence within the groups and issues related to infrastructure seem to be poorly managed owing to the self organised approach employed in the projects. Consequently, there is a need for balance between self-organisation and control, which reflects not only on what is achieved but also on what is not achieved and the reasons for this, so as to be able to adjust and increase the practical influence of such projects.

Our study contributes to a better understanding on how to introduce ICT in rural communities to enhance their economic opportunities, emphasising on the role of emancipatory learning methods in the study-circle groups. W provide a practical base for similar projects to learn from by providing lessons learned related to the project's objec tives, the pedagogical approach, and our theoretical lenses. Moreover, we provide a theoretical base for the further explanation of the relationship between the development goals and the role of ICT by combining the lenses of th CA and affordances. We argue that the introduction of the study-circles approach in rural contexts has proved its potential here. Following Avgerou's (2008) argument that “Information Systems innovation in developing countries is about constructing new techno-organisational structures within a given local social context” (p. 135), the project ha been successful in adopting a new form of informal adult education in a region where educational opportunities ar few. Our study has revealed that the use of ICT in self-organised study circles may be a way forward to educate an underserved population and enable economic opportunities for them. In communities with few educational opportu nities, study circles can provide individuals with an opportunity for an adult education that is based on their needs and goals.
