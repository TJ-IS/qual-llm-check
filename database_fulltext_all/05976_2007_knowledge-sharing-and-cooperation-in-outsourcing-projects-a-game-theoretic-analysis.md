# Knowledge sharing and cooperation in outsourcing projects — A game theoretic analysis

Subhajyoti Bandyopadhyay <sup>⁎</sup>, Praveen Pathak

University of Florida, Gainesville, FL, 364 Stuzin Hall, 32611-1310, United States

Received 19 January 2005; received in revised form 21 September 2006; accepted 7 October 2006 Available online 30 November 2006

## Abstract

The popular notion of outsourcing is that it helps organizations cut down costs of operation. In reality, however, firms often outsource to organizations that possess complex, complementary skills. We model the interaction between employees of the “host” firm and the outsourcing firm, who have to share their knowledge and skill sets in order to work effectively as a team, but might be naturally antagonistic towards each other. The analysis shows that when the degree of complementarity of knowledge between the employees is high enough, better payoffs can be achieved if the top management enforces cooperation between the employees. In these situations, the involvement of the top management extends far beyond negotiating the contract to make the outsourcing successful.

## 1. Introduction

Recent advances in technology and management expertise have substantially changed our thinking about how the global businesses are organized. The changes are driven mostly by two factors [1]: first, the cost structure enabled by new technologies, and second, the complexities in business products and processes that are the result of companies exploiting new technologies. With the advent of abundant and cheap telecommunication bandwidth and the open standards of the internet, businesses can lower their cost structure by reorganizing their work and handing over significant amounts of white-collar work to outside suppliers. In other words, by ‘outsourcing’ parts of their white-collar work businesses can gain substantial cost advantages. Some studies [27] estimate that by outsourcing part of their work, multinational firms hope to lower their costs by as much as 70%. Consulting firm Gartner estimates that cross-border Business Process Outsourcing (or BPO for short) will grow into a \$178.5 billion business by 2005 from \$123.6 billion in 2001 [16]. Other estimates suggest that the off-shore BPO industry will grow to over \$230 billion in 2015 [15].

While most of the discussion on outsourcing seems to focus on the cost-cutting initiatives of firms, what is often overlooked is that many firms are increasingly looking at outsourcing as a means of benefiting from complementary skill-sets of outside firms. This is in direct response to the increasing complexity of the products that the firm offers and the complexity of the processes used within the firm. The complexity has manifested in many industries, like banking and insurance and corporate functions such as human resources and administration and information systems development and support [1]. More and more outside organizations possess skills that are either not readily available within a firm or have become prohibitively expensive. The ‘host’ firms will typically have in-house teams to collaborate, monitor and control the outsourced work. These in-house teams have to co-operate with the teams from the outside firm in order to ensure common understanding of the tasks, handover of work and monitor the quality of the work. For example, some firms are nowadays outsourcing parts of their product design [1]. The internal team (from the ‘host’ firm) and the external team (from the ‘outsourcing’ firm) have to collaborate on the specifications of the products, the existing know-how about the technologies available in the company, know-how about the characteristics of similar products available within the company, etc.

In this paper, we model the interaction between two teams in two separate firms, where one firm (the “host”) is outsourcing some of its work to the other firm (the “outsourcing firm”). For the purposes of our model, we stylize this interaction between the teams as a strategic game between two employees in these firms. The employees, who possess different expertise, need to share their knowledge to effectively work with each other (apart from the fact that in the beginning, there is a significant amount of training and handholding involved), but the degree of sharing will be affected by the fact that the employees of the host firm might have to collaborate with employees of the outsourcing firm in projects that might have been handled completely inhouse (by the host firm) earlier. Many IT outsourcing projects show characteristics of this nature. Many prominent IT outsourcing firms like Wipro Technologies, TCS and Infosys Technologies often collaborate with consulting firms like Andersen Consulting (the host firm), whereby the latter provides the structured system requirements of an IS project, and the former converts those requirements to actual computer code. The employees in the host firm might be antagonistic to the employees in the outsourcing firm, perceiving them as a threat or at least as people who have displaced or might displace their erstwhile co-employees.

Thus, this is not merely an academic exercise, but a very real and emerging situation — workers in a firm have to collaborate in real time or even face-to-face with other workers in another firm who might be one day replacing them from their jobs. While CIOs have increasingly embraced outsourcing, they have to deal with the rising “backlash” of the nervous employees. Several recent trade articles point out (see for example [31] (and perhaps equally important, the nearly 90 comments that followed from various readers of that article), [30] and [5]) how workers of the host firm become hostile in such environments. Many such workers have come together formally and informally in order to raise public awareness and mobilize political pressure.

All these developments lead to the question as to whether such outsourcing becomes beneficial to the host firm. Specifically, what kind of projects could potentially benefit from outsourcing? What are the steps that the management of the host firm needs to take to ensure that the outsourcing exercise is successful? We attempt to answer these questions by modeling the environment in a stylized framework. We model the interaction between two employees – one in the host firm, the other in the outsourcing firm – as a game, and interpret the equilibrium of their behavior in light of the aforementioned questions. Our results indicate that depending on the nature of the outsourcing project, the benefit to the firms when the employees mutually decide on the level of cooperation between them will be higher than when the employees arrive at a non-cooperative equilibrium. It needs to be emphasized that the type of outsourcing projects that aim primarily at reducing costs is not modeled in this paper. The strategic interaction between employees of two firms that we discuss in this paper becomes germane only when the outsourcing is carried out to leverage the complementarities of knowledge inventories between the two firms.<sup>2</sup>

Our paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review some of the related work in the areas of knowledge sharing (with specific consideration to the objectives of this paper), economics (dealing with the boundary of the firm) and outsourcing. In Section 3, we present our model for the interaction between the two employees. Section 4 analyzes this model and draws implications from it while section five concludes the paper.

## 2. Related work

Traditionally, knowledge sharing literature has been concerned with issues like people's attitude to knowledge sharing, incentive alignment problems, knowledge management styles within organizations and the role of trust in knowledge sharing. Knowledge sharing literature has typically appeared in the overall context of knowledge management [12,17,19].

Constant et al. [10] explored people's attitude towards information sharing, and especially with an individual who has been unhelpful with them previously. They conducted three experiments to study attitudes about sharing technical work and expertise. They found that greater self-interest reduces support for sharing. Constant et al.'s work advances the Kelly and Thibaut's [21] interdependence theory (that deals with exchanges between two individuals in a social context) by explicitly addressing information exchange in organizational settings. When the individuals act alone then self interest and reciprocity predict behavior. But when there is a strong organizational context within which the individuals operate then good citizenship norms and altruistic behavior dominate the primitive self-interest instinct. Reasons for altruistic behavior include thinking for the organization as a whole [28,32] and reputation enhancement that can lead to future potential benefits [12].

Knowledge in organizations needs to be treated as a public good if it is to be available to everyone in the organization. But any public good is faced with the freerider problem eventually leading to individuals undercontributing to the public good (thereby sharing less than required knowledge). Ba et al. [4] contend that to deal with such a free-rider problem in knowledge sharing there has to be proper incentive-aligned mechanism that induces people to reveal their true valuation of knowledge. To justify the necessity for incentive alignment, they have discussed obstacles to knowledge sharing. For example, consultants sometimes avoid knowledge sharing as they can not charge that time to the clients as a valid billable activity [29]. They note [4]: ‘Knowledge sharing is both constrained and difficult without proper and necessary incentives for doing so. Economic and monetary incentives should be explored in a knowledge-sharing context where, for example, people may be willing to give away certain knowledge when they perceive value in return for having done so’. In another paper [3] the same authors discuss that to alleviate the free-rider problems in knowledge sharing, a market mechanism with properly aligned incentives could be created.

Several papers [6,11,13,14,20,23,25,26,33] have explored the role played by trust and conflict within inter-organizational alliances on the level and quality of knowledge sharing. Knowledge sharing and knowledge creation are treated as a social processes. High levels of trust between the teams are considered essential for effective communication to improve the quality of discussion between teams and to facilitate knowledge sharing. Trust is also seen as a necessary element in alleviating the risks associated with the opportunistic behavior of teams, and in dealing with uncertainty, and incomplete information. Pantelli and Sockalingam [34] have developed a framework for understanding the dynamics of trust and conflict. They have utilized such framework to identify three distinct structural forms of virtual alliances, and developed strategies for minimizing conflict and enhancing trust between the players in the virtual alliances.

Apte and Mason [2] analyzed the outsourcing of information-intensive services, and developed a framework for identifying criteria and guidelines for selecting services that can be globally “disaggregated”, while Bozarth et al. [7] studied the various stages of the global outsourcing strategy evolution for 55 different manufacturers. Cachon and Harker [8] analyzed outsourcing under scale economies within a game-theoretic framework, and concluded that scale economies provided a strong incentive for outsourcing even without cost advantages, and Gunasekaran and Ngai [18] looked at the build-to-order supply chain management strategy that has improved the competitiveness of many organizations by leveraging the advantages of outsourcing and information technology (IT).

From the standpoint of extant literature in economics, outsourcing is interesting in the sense it changes the boundary of the firm. It was Coase [9] who first challenged the prevailing notion that the natural boundaries of the firm were determined by technology, technological non-separabilities and economies of scale. Coase held that the firm and the market were alternatives for organizing the very same set of transactions. The firm-as-production-function approach and applied price theory combined with technological determinism do not entirely explain all the transactions that take place within the firm [38]. Klein et al. [22] extended Coase [9] by addressing the issue of moral hazard involved in postcontractual opportunistic behavior. Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) was developed to justify the firm as economizing on transaction cost, i.e., to identify the most economically efficient governance structure [37].

A related problem that arises in this context is identifying an expert's true expertise. Wolinksy [39] analyzes the problem under a setting where customers search for expertise in a market with multiple expertise and reputation considerations might lead to disciplining the experts.

Sakakibara [35,36] has looked at the role of learning in Research and Development (R&D) consortia. She finds that when R&D consortia consist of firms with complementary technological knowledge then these firms realize knowledge sharing opportunities. This leads to higher endogenous spillover rates among participants resulting in more intensified effort of the participating firms to learn from other members of the consortia. She observes that higher learning among the participants makes the R&D consortia welfare enhancing, thus justifying government support for such projects. In this paper we use the modeling construct of [36] for our analysis.

To the best of our knowledge, no paper has looked at the phenomenon of knowledge sharing as a strategic interaction between two employees, and analyzed it as a game. Game theory analyzes multi-person decision problems, and a central feature of multi-person interaction is the presence of strategic interdependence [24]. Inherent in the concept of knowledge sharing between two individuals is the same idea of strategic interdependence — the individuals gain from each others' knowledge, but incur a positive cost in sharing their own knowledge with the other person.

## 3. Model

Our formulation is closest in spirit to Sakakibara [36], who analyzes the competition among knowledgeintensive firms. We use the construct of Ref. [36], as it closely matches the environment and nature of competition that we envisage in this paper. This has allowed us to use what are essentially very similar abstractions for the purposes of analysis, and thus many of the results mirror closely that of Ref. [36]. While our model draws upon some of the basic constructs of Ref. [36], we get around the problem of embedding the game in a Cournot setting by implementing a different mechanism of knowledge sharing. We model the interaction between an employee from the host firm and an employee from the outsourcing firm. The employees know that sharing each other's knowledge will result in higher payoffs to either of them (in terms of increased knowledge). However, sharing one's own knowledge with someone else involves investment of one's time and effort. These two opposing “forces” therefore limit the amount of sharing, and move the two employees towards a possible (Nash) equilibrium. We analyze this equilibrium in Section 4, after explaining the modalities of the interaction in the current section.

Consider two employees i and j, one from the “host” firm and the other from the “outsourcing” firm, interacting with each other in order to make a new product. The “product” in question could be an actual end product (physical or intellectual) to a customer, or something like an internal report that can be used further within the organizations. We assume that the marginal cost to an employee to produce new products, $m _ { i } ,$ which is a function of her knowledge $\tau _ { i } ,$ decreases with the amount of her knowledge (a summary of the variables used appears in the Appendix):

$$
m _ {i} = m _ {i} (\tau_ {i}) \text { and } m _ {i \tau i} <   0 ^ {3}\tag{1}
$$

An employee's knowledge depends on her own investment in getting that knowledge, $I _ { i } ,$ as well as the amount of knowledge she gathers from the other employee when the latter shares some of his knowledge with her. We model this knowledge as

$$
\tau_ {i} = \tau_ {i} (I _ {i}, I _ {j}, \sigma_ {j}, \kappa)\tag{2}
$$

where $I _ { j }$ is the other employee's investment in his knowledge, $\sigma _ { j }$ is the degree the other employee shares his knowledge with this employee, and κ is the degree of complementarity of the knowledge between the two employees. When two individuals collaborate on a single project, ideally they would bring separate skill sets to the table. We define complementarity of knowledge of two such individuals as the extent to which their skill sets are dissimilar. For example, if two individuals are collaborating in a software development project, with the host firm's employee being a skilled business analyst, while the outsourcing firm's employee is a skilled application programmer in Java, the two employees would possess knowledge with a high degree of complementarity. Before discussing the nature of these parameters in greater detail, we provide the functional form of $\tau _ { i \cdot } ^ { \cdot }$

$$
\tau_ {i} = I _ {i} + \alpha_ {i} (I _ {i}, \kappa) \sigma_ {j} I _ {j} ^ {4}\tag{3}
$$

Here, $\mathsf { \Omega } \mathsf { \Omega } \mathsf { \Omega } \mathsf { \Omega }$ is the fraction of the other employee's knowledge that this employee is able to assimilate (i.e.

her learning capability), and this depends on her own investment in knowledge $I _ { i } ,$ as well as the complementarity of knowledge κ.

We assume that $0 \leq \sigma \leq 1 , 0 \leq \alpha \leq 1$ and $0 \le \kappa \le 1$ , so that the contribution of the shared knowledge, although almost always contributing to the employee's knowledge, is never greater than the employee's own knowledge. Also implicit in the functional form – by the additive nature of the other employee's contribution – is that there is no overlap in the two employees' knowledge as far as their collaboration is concerned, which is justifiable if the employees possess different expertise.

An employee's learning capability $\alpha _ { i }$ depends on her own investment in knowledge, $I _ { i } ,$ and complementarity of her knowledge with that of the other employee, κ. As discussed earlier, if the complementarity is high (κ → 1), the employees have very disparate expertise areas, which would imply that it would be increasingly difficult for one employee to assimilate the other employee's knowledge as complementarity increases, since it would be difficult to absorb such disparate knowledge. In other words, $\alpha _ { \kappa } { < } 0 ^ { 5 }$

On the other hand, investment in one's own knowledge makes an employee increase her learning capability, so $\alpha _ { I } { > } 0$

We assume that as one employee shares more of his knowledge, the learning capability of the other employee increases, as she gets a better understanding of the other employee's expertise, i.e. $\alpha _ { i \sigma j } { > } 0$

We can think of the degree of sharing, σ, as a parameter that we can measure in real life in terms of the number of (formal or informal) meetings, the number of hours spent by one employee with the other employee to share her expertise (often referred to colloquially as “bringing him up to speed”), etc.

The increased knowledge through sharing comes at a cost — it is assumed that there is a cost for knowledge sharing, $s ( \sigma _ { i } )$ for the employee. We assume that this cost increases with the degree of sharing, i.e. $s ^ { \prime } ( \sigma ) { > } 0$

We model the collaboration between the two employees as a two-stage game. In stage one, the employees decide – cooperatively or non-cooperatively – on their respective knowledge sharing rates, $( \sigma _ { i } , \sigma _ { j } )$ . Cooperative sharing means that the employees share the knowledge after a mutual understanding on the degree of sharing, while non-cooperative sharing means that the employees do not mutually agree on their degree of sharing, but arrive at their respective $\sigma _ { i }$ as a best response to each other's action.<sup>6</sup> In stage two, the employees decide on their own investment in knowledge, $( I _ { i } , I _ { j } ) . ^ { 7 }$ To understand the mechanism modeled here, consider a real-life example. Pipal Research is an Indian BPO company headquartered in Chicago started by ICICI, a leading banking organization in India. Pipal Research has as its clients several leading international banking and financial firms, and one of its principal business activities is to carry out custom research work for its clients. On its web site, Pipal Research defines it work in terms of first “identifying what is known and what has been done already” (i.e. the knowledge that the client has already gathered for the current project) and then “determining the deliverables and deadlines”. In effect therefore, the client and Pipal Research come together for the project, undertake some effort to gather some knowledge about the project, and then share that knowledge between each other for the successful completion of the project.

We look at the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, and analyze our results for symmetric equilibria — i.e. in the non-cooperative equilibrium, we analyze the case where the two employees decide on their best response by assuming that the equilibrium is based on the players choosing the same σ, and in the cooperative case, both firms agree on the same σ. As is standard in such analysis, we will solve for the equilibria in the two cases using backward induction.

The payoff for the employee i in this game is given by

$$
\pi_ {i} = \tau_ {i} - m _ {i} - I _ {i} - s (\sigma_ {i})\tag{4}
$$

Thus, while the employee gains from the collaboration in terms of increased knowledge (the implicit assumption being that more knowledgeable employees are more valuable to the firm and therefore command some forms of higher compensation, and that the employees of the host and outsourcing firms get compensated in some form for delivering the cost savings)<sup>8</sup>, the accompanied cost of the sharing her knowledge (which in turn will induce the other employee to share his knowledge with her) means that she will limit her sharing. For the sake of simplicity, we assumed that the value of the knowledge-based product is the value of the knowledge itself. In our formulation, the employee intends to maximize her payoff in a two-stage game.

## 4. Analysis and discussion

As discussed in the previous section, we analyze the interaction as a two-stage game. In the first stage, the employees can decide on their knowledge sharing rates either by cooperating with each other, or by not cooperating with each other, and this will result in two different equilibria for the game. We first analyze the non-cooperative equilibrium.

4.1. Equilibrium when agents do not cooperate with each other

As is standard in such analysis, we use backward induction, and solve first for the equilibrium in Stage 2, given the decisions in Stage 1.

## 4.1.1. Stage 2

In the non-cooperative case, the employees choose their degree of cooperation σ, and their own investment in knowledge I, independently. Using backward induction, employee i's problem at stage two, given $\sigma _ { i } , \sigma _ { j } , I _ { j } ,$ κ is

$$
\underset {I i} {\text { Max }} g _ {i} = \tau_ {i} - m _ {i} - I _ {i} - s (\sigma_ {i})\tag{5}
$$

where $g _ { i } { = } \alpha _ { i } ( I _ { i } , \kappa ) I _ { j } \sigma _ { j } { - } m _ { i } ( \tau _ { i } ) { - } s ( \sigma _ { i } ) , I _ { i } { = } I _ { i } ( \sigma _ { i } , \sigma _ { j } , \kappa )$ and $I _ { j } { = }$ $I _ { j } ( \sigma _ { i } , \sigma _ { j } , \kappa )$ since the investment in knowledge will be dependent on the degree of cooperation (that is derived in Stage 2), and the complementarity of knowledge.

The first order conditions yield

$$
\frac {\partial g _ {i}}{\partial I _ {i}} = \alpha_ {i l i} I _ {j} \sigma_ {j} - m _ {i \tau i} [ 1 + \alpha_ {i l i} I _ {j} \sigma_ {j} ] = 0\tag{6}
$$

The above equation implicitly defines employee $i \mathrm { { ^ { \circ } s } }$ reaction function $I _ { i } ^ { * } ( I _ { j } )$ as the best response to employee $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ investment in his own knowledge $I _ { j } ,$ and similarly we can get employee $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ reaction function $I _ { j } ^ { * } ( I _ { i } )$ . The intersection of the two reaction functions gives us the Nash equilibrium at stage two, $( I _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { N C } } , I _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { N C } } )$ , where the superscript NC refers to the non-cooperative equilibrium.

## 4.1.2. Stage 1

At stage one the employee maximizes her payoff by choosing an appropriate $\sigma _ { i } \mathbf { : }$

$$

$$

Note that $\tau _ { i } = I _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { N C } } ( \sigma _ { i } , \sigma _ { j } , \kappa ) + \alpha _ { i } ( I _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { N C } } ( \sigma _ { i } , \sigma _ { j } , \kappa ) , \kappa ) \sigma _ { j } I _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { N C } } ( \sigma _ { i } ,$ $\sigma _ { j } , \kappa )$ . $$

$$

As before, we get the reaction functions for both employees that define their best response at stage one, $( \sigma _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { { N C } } } , \bar { \sigma } _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { { N C } } } )$

The equilibrium non-cooperative payoff for the employees can therefore be found by substituting the equilibrium values of $\sigma _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { N C } }$ and $\sigma _ { j } ^ { \mathrm { { \check { N C } } } }$ in the payoff function:

$$
h _ {i} = h _ {i} (\sigma_ {i} ^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \sigma_ {j} ^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \kappa)\tag{9}
$$

As discussed before, we are considering only symmetric equilibria, and therefore

$$
\sigma_ {i} ^ {\mathrm{NC}} = \sigma_ {j} ^ {\mathrm{NC}} = \sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}\tag{10}
$$

which implies that

$$
I _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{NC}} = I _ {j} ^ {\mathrm{NC}} = I ^ {\mathrm{NC}}\tag{11}
$$

4.2. Equilibrium when agents cooperate with each other

In the cooperative game, the employees choose their own investment I independently, but choose the same knowledge sharing rate σ jointly. This decision can be decided by the employees themselves, or enforced by higher management. This might involve establishing some metrics for quantifying the degree of sharing, e.g. the number of meetings, the number of hours spent by an employee with the other employee, and a credible means of enforcing the agreement in terms of “punishment”. Note that this σ is different from the symmetric equilibrium in the non-cooperative case, since the latter is derived as a best response to the other employee's $\sigma ,$ while in this case, it is a joint decision that maximizes the payoffs of both employees.

However, we can prove that when the complementarity of the knowledge between the employees is large (i.e. there is sufficient disparity in knowledge), the equilibrium degree of cooperation, and the payoffs to the employees, is larger when the employees cooperate and decide on a common degree of sharing. When an organization outsources some of its functions to another firm, the employees of the two firms need to share their expertise to effectively work together, in addition to the initial significant amount of cross-departmental training and handholding that is expected of the employees of the host firm. The employees of the host firm might often look at the changes with suspicion and hostility, and as a result, without proper intervention by upper management, they might not cooperate with the employees of the other firm. In our analysis, we prove that when the complementarity of the knowledge between the two employees is high, it makes sense for the management of the two firms to enforce cooperation, so that the employees decide on the degree of sharing.

We note that the case is not that clear-cut when the degree of complementarity is low. In real life, the latter situation might mean that the employee in the host organization views the employee in the other organization as a credible threat who might usurp her role when he learns enough about her current expertise.

## 5. Conclusion

Most of the current discussion on outsourcing revolves around organizations that purportedly outsource their operations in order to cut down cost. While cost-cutting is definitely one of the objectives, organizations are increasingly outsourcing their operations in order to benefit from the complementary skills that are available in the external firm. In such situations, it is instructive to analyze the interaction between the employees of the two firms, who might be naturally hostile to each other.

Our paper models the interaction between the employees of the two firms as a two-stage game, using the analytical construct of [36]. We analyze the model under two assumptions — one, when the employees arrive at a non-cooperative equilibrium, and second, when the employees mutually decide on the extent of knowledge sharing. The results show that if the complementarity of the knowledge of the two firms (and by extension, the employees) is high enough, the payoff is higher when the two employees cooperate than when they decide not to.

The results have important implications for the success of outsourcing projects. When a firm outsources in order to benefit from a set of complementary skills, the firm's management will have to be involved not only in the negotiation of the outsourcing contract, but also in modalities of operation and interaction between the two firms. Since the employees of the two firms would probably be mutually antagonistic, the management would have to lay down the guidelines of interaction between the firms. These guidelines might include specifics like the number of hours that the employees would have to spend with each other to share each other's knowledge (and simultaneously set up effective monitoring of that interaction), frequency of meetings between managers, etc. Such mutually agreed upon degree of knowledge sharing will result in better payoffs to the employees, and by extension, to the firm. Of course prescribing a solution is easier said than done, but the results do emphasize that fact that organizations which outsource their knowledge-based projects for a quick return on investment might be in for a rude shock. There are significant management challenges that need to be addressed before any such project can become a success.

If the degree of complementarity of knowledge between the firms is low, the results might not be this prescriptive. When the knowledge of the two employees are similar enough, sharing does not lead to a significant increase in each other's knowledge, but comes with its associated costs — and this is especially acute when much of the time in the meetings might be spent in determining the exact knowledge which is dissimilar between the two employees (and therefore worth sharing). Thus, forcing a higher level of cooperation would add to the costs but not deliver commensurate benefits.

The results of our analysis bring to light some interesting operational issues for the host firm that opens several avenues for future research. For example, once the host firm decides to outsource a particular project, how does it ascertain the degree of knowledge complementarity and what does it need to do in operational terms to ensure its success? An interesting extension to our model is to analyze the composition of effective teams. Employees within the organizations can be classified in two dimensions — in their ability to share their knowledge (i.e. in terms of our current model, they might have different costs of sharing their knowledge, $s ( \sigma _ { i } ) )$ and in their ability to utilize the knowledge of others for their own work (α ). Depending on the characteristics of the employees, we can come up with the composition of the two-member teams that give the best payoffs to the firm. Another way to extend this research would be to model the antagonism of the employee of the host firm towards the employee of the outsourcing firm into the game, rather than treat it as an exogenous variable as we did in this paper.

Some other extensions of the model include considering the exercise as a repeated game rather than a oneshot exercise. In such an exercise, the motivation of the problem – uncertainty of the employee of the host firm regarding his future employment – will be central to the analysis. Since the true information of either agent is hidden from the other, at the heart of the problem there is a double-sided moral hazard issue, which can be subject of a separate analysis. Finally, one could relax the assumption of considering only symmetric equilibria, since the host firm and the outsourcing firm's behavior could be different.

## Appendix B. Proof of proposition

From Eq. (9), and by definition of a symmetric Nash equilibrium,

$$
h _ {i} (\sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \kappa) \geq h _ {i} (\sigma^ {\mathrm{C}}, \sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \kappa)\tag{A1}
$$

By choosing their degree of sharing σ cooperatively, the employees can do at least as well as in the noncooperative case, i.e.

$$
h _ {i} (\sigma^ {\mathrm{C}}, \sigma^ {\mathrm{C}}, \kappa) \geq h _ {i} (\sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \kappa)\tag{A2}
$$

Adding the two inequalities above, we get,

$$
h _ {i} (\sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \kappa) \geq h _ {i} (\sigma^ {\mathrm{C}}, \sigma^ {\mathrm{NC}}, \kappa)\tag{A3}
$$

If we can show that $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial h _ { i } } { \partial \sigma _ { i } } > 0 \forall \sigma _ { j } \in ( \sigma ^ { \mathrm { N C } } , \sigma ^ { \mathrm { C } } ) } \end{array}$ with $\sigma _ { i } =$ $\sigma ^ { \mathrm { { C } } } ;$ <sup>8 ð Þ</sup>, for the above inequality to be true, we will need $\sigma ^ { \mathrm { C } } { > } \sigma ^ { \mathrm { N C } }$ , which will constitute our proof. Now,

$$
\begin{array}{c} \frac {\partial h _ {i}}{\partial \sigma_ {j}} = \alpha_ {i} I _ {j} + \sigma_ {j} [ \alpha_ {i \sigma j} I _ {j} + \alpha_ {i} I _ {j \sigma j} ] - m _ {i \tau i} [ I _ {i \sigma j} + \alpha_ {i} I _ {j} \\ + \sigma_ {j} \{\alpha_ {i \sigma j} I _ {j} + \alpha_ {i} I _ {j \sigma j} \} ] \end{array}\tag{A4}
$$

Note that, as discussed initially, $\alpha _ { i \sigma j } { > } 0$ . Further, since $\alpha _ { I } { > } 0$ , we can re-write $I _ { i \sigma j }$ as

$$
\frac {\partial I _ {i}}{\partial \sigma_ {j}} = \frac {\partial I _ {i}}{\partial \alpha_ {i}} * \frac {\partial \alpha_ {i}}{\partial \sigma_ {j}} = \frac {\partial \alpha_ {i}}{\partial \sigma_ {j}} \bigg / \frac {\partial \alpha_ {i}}{\partial I _ {i}} > 0\tag{A5}
$$

However, no such determination can be made regarding the sign of $I _ { j \sigma j } .$

Going back to the expression of $\textstyle { \frac { \partial h _ { i } } { \partial \sigma _ { j } } } ,$ , and noting that $m _ { i \tau i } { > } 0$ , we find that all the terms in the expression are positive, except the term $\alpha _ { i } I _ { j \sigma j } .$ . However, as we have noted above, since $\alpha _ { \kappa } { < } 0$ , and $0 \leq \alpha \leq 1$ , when κ is sufficiently large, α will be small enough so that the term $[ \alpha _ { i \sigma j } I _ { j } + \alpha _ { i } I _ { j \sigma j } ] > 0$ , and the expression for $\frac { \partial h _ { i } } { \partial \sigma _ { j } }$ is therefore positive. This completes the proof. The result is thus stylistically similar to Claim 1 of Sakakibara [36].
