# Implementation costs of IS-enabled organizational change

Rajeev Sharma <sup>a,\*</sup>, Philip W. Yetton <sup>b</sup>, Robert W. Zmud <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, AH 307, 307 W. Brooks, Norman OK 73019, United States Australian School of Business, The University of NSW, Sydney 2052, Australia

## Abstract

Organizations incur substantial costs in managing organizational changes associated with the implementation of information systems (IS). However, the relationship between organizationa changes and IS implementation costs is not well understood. Extending current research on IS-enabled organizational change, we draw on configuration theory to develop propositions identi fying drivers of IS implementation costs. To test the propositions, we analyze the changes and imple mentation costs involved in three IS implementation initiatives. The analysis confirms that interdependencies between changes are key drivers of IS implementation costs. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Keywords: IS implementation; Implementation costs; Configuration theory; IT-enabled change; Organizational change; IS success; Case study research

## 1. Introduction

Organizations incur substantial costs in managing the changes associated with the implementation of information systems (IS). Therefore, it is important to be able to esti mate these costs prior to the implementation efort in order to assess the economic feasibility of an IS initiative and to commit adequate resources to the implementation efort (Ryan, Harrison, & Schkade, 2002). However, these costs are often underestimated or even ignored in organizational decision processes associated with IS project funding and implementation planning (Ryan et al., 2002). One explanation for this is the absence of a theoretical framework for understanding the efects of anticipated organizational changes on IS implementation costs (Ryan et al., 2002).

This paper extends prior research, drawing on configuration theory to identify drivers of IS implementation costs. Specifically, we identify interdependencies among the organizational changes associated with an IS implementation as key drivers of those costs. The paper begins by introducing a configuration-based framework for analyzing IS implementation costs and developing a set of propositions that follow from this framework. Next, the propositions are assessed via analysis of qualitative data from three case studies. Finally, implications for research and practice are discussed.

## 2. IS implementation costs

Organizations expend considerable resources to successfully implement IS applications (Table 1), in addition to the costs expended on acquiring and/or developing the application. These resources include many which can be procured externally or from an internal information services function, including trainers to develop end user skills, change management consultants, technology consultants, work process analysts and software engineers for software modifications and integration services. Estimates of the monetary costs associated with such resources are typically obtained from the information service providers (external or internal) and are readily available to organizations to consider them in planning and evaluation decisions.

In addition to the costs on information services resources, organizations also expend considerable internal resources on managing the changes associated with IS implementation.<sup>1</sup> These include, for example, the extensive engagement of top and middle managers in eforts to mutually adapt an information system to its organizational context, in orchestrating changes in organizational structures and processes, and in resolving the conflicts that invariably arise (Markus, 2004; Sharma & Yetton, 2003); the deployment of ‘‘super users’’ to train other users (Carte, Schwarzkopf, Shaft, & Zmud, 2005); and the eforts of end users to learn and adapt to the new technologies (Kang & Santhanam, 2003–04; Sharma & Yetton, 2007). In addition, organizations experience considerable time lags in achieving routinized use of IS, often experiencing performance declines during those periods (Leonard-Barton, 1988). Frequently, organizations fail to account for the costs of such internal resources expended on IS implementation in their IS investment decisions and implementation planning (Ryan et al., 2002).

We thus define IS implementation costs as consisting of the costs borne by an organization for both the external and internal resources expended to achieve routinized use of new IS applications. These costs are frequently substantial and, in some cases, they exceed the acquisition and/or development costs (Leonard-Barton, 1988; Markus & Keil, 1994; Soh & Sia, 2005). For example, Sauer (1993) describes the high costs experienced when an information technology-enabled business solution is introduced into its operating context.

The focus of this study is on understanding the implementation costs expended on internal resources incurring opportunity costs. Identifying the factors that influence those resource opportunity costs faces two important limitations. First, such costs are rarely accumulated in accounting systems as being associated with the implementation of specific information systems. Hence, unlike the monetary costs incurred on acquisition/development and on procuring external resources to support implementation eforts, it is usually not feasible to quantify costs expended on internal resources. In this study, consequently, only qualitative assessments of the relative costs incurred on information systems projects are undertaken.

Second, while prior literature has highlighted the need to expend organizational resources on managing IS-induced changes (Ryan et al., 2002; Swanson, 1994), there is no theoretical framework for extrapolating the efect of those changes on implementation costs. Ryan et al. argue that incremental innovations incur lower implementation costs than do radical innovations due to the more limited organizational change involved. They also argue that administrative innovations incur lower implementation costs as compared to technical innovations. However, the incremental/radical and tech nical/administrative distinctions are dificult to make in practice as many IS initiatives, including ERP, SCM and CRM implementations, involve both radical and incremental components, and include both administrative and technical domains. An alternative characterization of IS-induced change that is not subject to this limitation is required for understanding the efects of these changes on implementation costs. Drawing on the literatures on IS-enabled change and configuration theory, we develop such a theo retical framework.

## 3. A configuration-based perspective on IS implementation costs

The implementation of IS applications is often associated with significant organizational change (Barrett, Grant, & Wailes, 2006; Markus, 2004), frequently involving changes to multiple organizational elements, including but not limited to technology components, organizational structures, and individual roles and skills (Markus & Keil, 1994; Markus & Robey, 1983; Orlikowski, 1993; Zmud & Cox, 1979). Further, these multiple changes are often found to be interdependent (Wagner & Newell, 2003), as change in one element often leads to a change event in another element.

A number of scholars have drawn on the interdependencies of multiple changes and ofered conceptual or theoretical frameworks for characterizing these change processes. IS implementation has been framed as a process of the mutual adaptation between technology and organization (Leonard-Barton, 1988), as simultaneously achieving an IS application’s technical and organizational validity (Markus & Robey, 1983; Schultz & Slevin, 1975), and as the creation of a new social order (Wagner & Newell, 2003). These and other frameworks drawing on sociotechnical systems theory implicitly employ a configurational perspective to model the organizational changes associated with IS implementations (Ryan et al., 2002; Sarker, 2000). Here, we formally draw upon configuration theory to examine the efect of organizational changes on IS implementation costs.

Configuration theory (Doty & Glick, 1994; Doty, Glick, & Huber, 1993) conceptualizes organizations as consisting of multiple interacting elements, including task, technology, structure and people. A key insight is that organizational efectiveness is a function of the level of internal fit, or alignment, between these multiple organizational elements (Chan, 2002; Donaldson, 2001). Changing any element of a configuration in fit, such as technology, can reduce the level of internal fit and result in lower levels of performance (Johnston & Yetton, 1996; Markus & Robey, 1983; Sarker, 2000). Higher levels of performance are then contingent on regaining fit around a new configuration.

Organizational theorists ofer a number of configurational frameworks for organizational analysis. Leavitt (1965) presented a configuration-based framework defined by four elements – task, technology, people and structure. Mintzberg (1979) described five idealtype configurations – the simple structure, machine bureaucracy, professional bureaucracy, adhocracy and the missionary – distinguished by the critical control and coordination mechanisms employed. Similarly, Miles and Snow (1978) developed a configuration-based framework defined by strategy, structure and process, which has been employed to understand the strategic alignment of IS (Hirschheim & Sabherwal, 2001). The practitioner literature also ofers a number of configuration-based frameworks, of which the 7-S framework (Peters & Waterman, 1982) is perhaps the best known.

Here, we adopt the MIT90s configurational framework (Scott Morton, 1991). Extending Leavitt’s (1965) framework, the MIT90s framework describes organizations as characterized by five interdependent organizational elements – strategy, structure, technology, management processes and individual roles and skills (Fig. 1). This framework has been employed within the IS discipline to study a number of aspects of IT-enabled change. For instance, Yetton, Johnston, and Craig (1994) examine the dynamics of change in a small architectural firm employing CAD technology, identifying the sequence in which strategy, structure, technology, management processes and individual roles and skills changed. They compare that sequence with the normative strategy-driven dynamic described in the literature. Extending this analysis, Hsiao and Ormerod (1998) identify the changing dynamics at diferent stages of the change process and identify four archetypes for the implementation of IT-enabled strategic change.

In another application of the MIT90s framework, Johnston and Yetton (1996) examine the conflict arising when a bank, implementing a merger, attempted to integrate incompatible IT configurations. The MIT90s model has also been employed to describe managerial challenges in realizing the strategic options ofered by more sophisticated levels of ITinduced transformation (Venkatraman, 1991), the strategic alignment between IT strategy and business strategy (Macdonald, 1991), and the people, structure and process issues in IT-enabled organizational change (Mckersie & Walton, 1991). While that research stream demonstrates the value of employing the MIT90s framework to analyze IT-enabled change, it does not specifically analyze the efect of changes on IS implementation costs. Here, we add to the limited research stream on the costs of implementing IT-enabled change.

## 3.1. Configurations, organizational change and IS implementation costs

## 3.1.1. Extent of change and implementation costs

IS-based change involves changes not only to the technical core of organizations, but also to its social fabric (Zmud & Cox, 1979), potentially afecting all elements of the MIT90s configurational model (Fig. 1). The number of elements of the MIT90s model that do undergo change is a measure of the extent of change associated with an IS implementation.

The introduction of IS applications frequently leads to changes to organizational structures, the nature of managerial jobs, the locus of decision-making, the level of centralization, and the nature of management process for control and coordination (Groth, 1999; Leavitt & Whisler, 1958; Markus & Robey, 1988; Scott Morton, 1991). IS applications have also been associated with micro-level changes to individual roles and skills, including but not limited to task autonomy, variety, significance and identity (Gill, 1996). Typically, both macro- and micro-level changes are required, in various degrees, for efective IS implementation. For example, Orlikowski (1993, pp. 321–322, 326–327) describes the multiple changes that took place in an organization as a result of its adoption of a CASE tool: new practices for the control and coordination of project teams, increased stress on busi ness analysis and user interaction during projects, reorganization of the IS division from a decentralized to a centralized structure, and an increased importance of negotiation and conflict resolution skills for IS personnel.

The changes resulting from the introduction of IS applications are not limited to the function or division of the organization where the applications are implemented. Rather, they cascade to other parts of the organization, and even to external organizations (Benjamin & Levinson, 1993). For example, the introduction of supply chain management systems introduces strategic changes in the procurement function and other internal functions, including accounting, manufacturing and marketing, and even in operationa procedures of an organization’s suppliers (Hsiao & Teo, 2005; Lee, Farhoomand, & Ho, 2004).

The responsibility for identifying, orchestrating and managing the organizational changes associated with an IS implementation efort rests with the organization’s manag ers (Grant, 2003; Leonard-Barton, 1988; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). As the extent of organizational change increases, organizations need to deploy more managerial and enduser resources to undertake these changes successfully, thus incurring greater implementation costs (Zmud & Cox, 1979). Formally:

Proposition 1. IS implementation costs are a positive function of the number of configurational elements that undergo change.

## 3.1.2. Configurational change and implementation costs

Proposition 1 does not account for the interdependencies between changes or for the efect of the changes on the prior state of internal fit. IS-induced change impacts organizational configurations in two distinct patterns. The first of these change patterns involves isolated change within one or more configuration elements. We refer to this change pattern as elemental change. For example, consider a simple technology upgrade that occurs over a weekend and is essentially invisible to end-users. Such elemental change is unlikely to require much, if any, involvement of an organization’s management or end-users. As a consequence, IS-enabled elemental change is expected to incur low implementation costs.

The second change pattern involves changes to interdependent configuration elements and, as a consequence, disturbances of internal fit. We refer to this change pattern as configurational change. Here, a new configuration in internal fit needs to be achieved through ‘‘mutually dependent changes in technology, organization, and process’’ (Benjamin & Levinson, 1993, p. 28). This dynamic process of mutual adaptation (Leonard-Barton, 1988) invariably requires involvement by an organization’s management and results in impaired performance for some time, both of which increase implementation costs. Formally:

Proposition 2. Configurational change incurs higher implementation costs than elemental change.

## 3.1.3. Interdependence and implementation costs

Implementation costs are expected to increase as the required level of task and organizational integration increases (Benjamin & Levinson, 1993, p. 30; Markus & Robey, 1983). Successfully implementing interdependent change requires coordinated actions by a number of individuals, many of whom are separated by organizational boundaries and unable to coordinate efectively (Wagner & Newell, 2003). Top management is thus required to play an increasing role in coordination as the extent of interdependence in changes increases, contributing to higher implementation costs. Additionally, increasing interdependence among change elements increases the extent to which end users’ task environments must be altered, requiring further managerial intervention (Purvis, Sambamurthy, & Zmud, 2001; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). Formally:

Proposition 3. IS implementation costs are a positive function of the number of interdependent elements involved in configurational change.

Further, we distinguish between the efects of sequential and reciprocal interdependence (Galbraith, 1973; Thompson, 1967): sequential interdependence refers to a one-way influence path between two configuration elements, while reciprocal interdependence is involved when two configuration elements mutually influence each other. Reciprocal interdependence involves greater coordination costs than does sequential interdependence (Mintzberg, 1979) as the resultant organizational changes tend to be emergent and dificult to predict in advance (Wagner & Newell, 2003). Formally:

Proposition 4. Configurational change involving reciprocal interdependencies incurs higher implementation costs than configurational change involving sequential interdependencies.

## 4. Methodology

This study adopts a positivist case study approach to examine the propositions developed above (Dube & Pare, 2003; Yin, 1984), in contrast to critical or interpretive case study research, where the objective is social critique or understanding the social con struction of reality (Klein & Myers, 1999). Specifically, this study analyzes three cases of IS implementation, each of which involved the implementation of the same IS application at multiple sites of a large health care organization. Within-case and cross-case analyses are conducted to test the propositions developed (Dube & Pare, 2003; Eisenhardt, 1989). The framework of configuration theory is employed as a guide to search for relevant evidence to test the propositions. In addition, competing explanations drawn from a diferent theoretical perspective are tested to add to the validity of the findings (Dube & Pare, 2003; Eisenhardt, 1989). The case evidence was also interrogated for new insights that extend the theory developed here (Dube & Pare, 2003). Assessment of relative implementation costs across the cases was made based on qualitative data. Since the focus of this study is on internal resources incurring opportunity costs (Table 1, Column 2), assessment of implementation costs was based on the rela tive extent of management and end-user eforts involved in implementation across the cases.

## 4.1. Research site and selection of cases

This research was carried out within Area Health Services (AHSs) responsible for providing health care services within the Australian State of New South Wales (NSW).<sup>2</sup> The AHSs operated as autonomous entities under the overall supervision of the NSW Depart ment of Health (DOH). Funded by the DOH, each AHS was responsible for providing health care services within a geographic area, through a network of hospitals, clinics and other facilities.

The initial focus of this research was in the broad area of IS innovation, though the spe cific research questions addressed here evolved during the course of the research project. Initial exploratory interviews were conducted with the initiators and developers of a num ber of IS applications. Three applications were identified for further investigation: Datapower, a Human Resources Information System; VMoney, a specialized payment system; and, Mimate, a financial information and management reporting system. All three applications were developed in one AHS but were implemented also in a number of other AHSs.

The focus of this study is the implementation efort involved across the three cases. For the purpose of analysis, the implementation project undertaken to successfully implement an application is analyzed as a case. Thus, each case analyzes the implementation of the same application across multiple AHSs, and not the analysis of behavior within an AHS. Investigating the implementation of multiple applications across multiple sites provides a rich source of data for theory development, drawing on the logic of replication (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1984).

The three implementation cases share a number of similarities: they are all within the general area of hospital administration; they had a common initiator and a common project manager; they were implemented across multiple sites; and they employed the same set of developers and technology platforms. These similarities serve as natural controls that add to the internal validity of the conclusions (Dube & Pare, 2003). However, there were also important diferences across the cases. The organizational task(s) involved in the three cases were diferent. Further, the line managers responsible for implementation were different across the cases, as were the intended end users. Such variations are likely to have produced quite diferent dynamics and outcomes across the multiple cases, promising to generate rich qualitative data for analysis and theory building.

## 4.2. Data collection

The primary mode of data collection consisted of 25 detailed semi-structured interviews with key participants in 11 organizations (Table 2). The interviews were conducted by the first two authors and a research associate. The initial phase of interviews was conducted between April 1992 and March 1993. Informal contact was maintained with key personnel over the next three years and a second set of interviews was conducted during March–May 1996. The interviewees included the initiator of the three applications, the project manager for the three applications, the developers, users and key decision makers. Typically, the interviews lasted between 45 and 90 min. Most interviews were recorded and transcribed. In addition, the interviewers maintained their own field notes. Some internal records were also examined.

## 4.3. Data analysis

The data were analyzed in multiple stages. Initial analysis was conducted by the first two authors working independently to identify tentative themes emerging from the data. This was followed by discussions between the first two authors to critique the initial impressions and to identify themes to be pursued in subsequent data collection. Later, the cases and analysis were presented to a group of fellow researchers for critique. A detailed case study was written up to become intimately familiar with the data (Southon & Yetton, 1995).

This was followed by rigorous within- and cross-case analyses. Initial analysis focused on explaining participation in the cooperative development of information systems (Sharma & Yetton, 1996). The data were revisited and analyzed over the 2005–2006 period with the third author, who had not been involved previously in data collection or analysis. This analysis developed the research questions addressed here: identifying the drivers of IS implementation costs.

Within-case analyses of the three cases were conducted to identify the unique themes and patterns relating to implementation costs emerging from the data (Eisenhardt, 1989). This was followed by a cross-case analysis, which revealed substantial variation in implementation costs across the three cases. Subsequent analysis was directed toward testing the propositions developed above.

Case transcripts were searched manually to identify all references to the key constructs suggested by current implementation theories and configuration theory. This was augmented by an automated search to identify references that might have been overlooked in the manual search. The key words searched were suggested by the theories examined, including change, performance, management support, strategy, structure, technology, management processes, individual roles and skills, radical, incremental, technical and administrative. The identified text segments were tabulated separately for each case under headings such as roles and skills, strategy, structure and technology. The text was analyzed jointly by the first two authors in assessing the propositions. Subsequently, the entire case data were examined to validate the conclusions drawn and to obtain novel theoretical insights.

Implementation costs for each case were assessed based on two factors – the extent to which internal resources (specifically, management and end-user eforts) were expended on implementation and the extent to which factors contributing to implementation costs were present (specifically, the extent of conflict, time taken to routinized use and performance decline). The full set of interview transcripts was searched manually to make qualitative assessments of the relative implementation costs incurred across the three cases. While, as stated earlier, obtaining quantitative measures of such costs is usually not feasible, the ordinal scale obtained by assessing relative implementation costs across the three cases serves to test the propositions developed here. To increase the reliability of the assessments of implementation costs, the case transcripts were also searched for disconfirming evidence.

## 5. Case descriptions: the Wentworth projects

The AHSs were funded by the DOH, which also exercised direct financial control over each AHS’s budget and capital expenditure, including expenditure on IT and IS. The DOH developed and controlled the major IS applications used by all AHSs, including HOSPAY for payroll services, HOSFIN for financial management and HOSBILL for billing services.

The DOH had a substantial backlog of IS applications and did not give high priority to requests by individual AHSs. This resulted in marked dissatisfaction across the administrative units of the AHSs with regard to administrative and financial applications, which were approved, funded, developed and run centrally by the DOH. However, individual AHSs could not command suficient resources to develop applications to meet their individual administrative needs.

Graham Jenkins, the Director of Finance and Budget at Wentworth Area Health Service (WAHS), believed that, if the AHSs pooled their resources, they could fund the development of the required administrative systems. The idea was accepted in principle by other AHSs and several applications were initiated, three of which are the subject of this research.

## 5.1. Case 1: The Datapower implementation project

The first project proposed by Jenkins was the development of Datapower, a Human Resources application to download data from HOSPAY, generate a range of reports then provided in hard-copy by HOSPAY, and allow AHSs to query the data in real time for data analysis. Although there was a long-standing request from the AHSs to DOH to allow real-time access to HOSPAY data, the DOH had not approved the project.

Jenkins invited all ten metropolitan AHSs to form a consortium to develop a package to address their shared needs. Although there was no prior history of AHSs collaborating to develop IS applications, Jenkins was confident of achieving participation. Four AHSs joined WAHS as foundation members of the Datapower consortium and, within three months, the first version of Datapower was released. Another four AHSs joined the Datapower consortium within the next year.

## 5.1.1. Implementation costs for Datapower

Datapower was implemented successfully across all participating AHSs, incurring low implementation costs. No AHS or user reported performance decline as a result of introducing the application. Upgrades of Datapower were released every few months, which suggests that, for every upgrade, routininzed use was achieved in a very short period. Over the following two years, further upgrades of Datapower were released, with the program growing from a simple package to become the backbone of the HR information system, with high usage, user satisfaction and performance gains:

‘‘[Datapower] has borne reasonable fruit and very cost efective fruit. . . It is a way of getting things done you wouldn’t otherwise get done, in a very cost efective way.’’ (IT Manager, AHS implementing Datapower)

Overall, Datapower incurred low implementation costs, considering the extent of management and end-user efort, conflict, performance decline and time to routinized use. A search of the interview transcripts found no references to implementation dificulties. Given that Datapower was implemented in all nine participating AHSs, this suggests a high degree of reliability for the assessment of implementation costs.

## 5.2. Case 2: The VMoney implementation project

Shortly after the initiation of Datapower, Jenkins initiated the development of VMoney, a package designed to handle payments to Visiting Medical Oficers (VMOs). VMOs (specialists, such as surgeons and anesthetists) provided services to AHSs under contracts negotiated between the DOH and the professional associations of the VMOs.

These centrally negotiated contracts governing VMO payments were complex and each AHS had a staf member dedicated to managing the payments. Many AHSs supported the manual payment process with custom-developed stand-alone database or spreadsheet-based systems running on personal computers. There was a great deal of dissatisfaction with the VMO payment process across the AHSs and many had considered developing a better system but could not commit adequate funds for the development.

Jenkins initiated the development of VMoney at Wentworth, with the same analysts who were developing Datapower. The first version of VMoney was released three months after the initiation of the project. Jenkins then promoted VMoney to other AHSs, ofering it on the same terms as for Datapower. Despite the high level of dissatisfaction with the VMO payment process and the success of the Datapower consortium, only three AHSs joined the VMoney consortium.

Three years after the introduction of VMoney, the DOH entered into a new agreement with VMOs, involving significant changes to the payment process and forcing all AHSs to change their existing payment systems. Jenkins had monitored the negotiations leading to the new agreement and anticipated the changes required. VMoney was designed to comply with many of the requirements of the new agreement. For the few thousand dollars of consortium entry fees, AHSs could obtain immediate access to a product that ‘‘. . .to write from nothing would have easily cost a hundred thousand dollars . . .It was an easy decision’ (Finance Director, participating AHS). Within a year, all AHSs had joined the VMoney consortium.

## 5.2.1. Implementation costs for VMoney

Though the cost of procuring VMoney was relatively low, AHSs experienced signifi cantly higher implementation costs, compared with the costs for Datapower. AHSs reported performance decline and the time taken to achieve routinized use was long – up to 2 years in some cases. Implementing VMoney also required considerable eforts by top and middle management and end-users to resolve conflicts that arose during implementation.

As the Finance Director of one AHS remarked, nearly 18 months into implementation:

‘‘[VMoney] was a very dificult application to introduce. . . and we are still going through quite a bit of pain in that regard.’’ (Finance Director of AHS, a late adopter of VMoney)

Similar experiences of high implementation costs were experienced in other AHSs:

‘‘You will find at [AHS], they were able to successfully put it in to their peripheral hospitals . . . [but] . . . they couldn’t get it to work at [the main hospital] . . . Well, surprisingly, the last two sites . . . that put it in . . . have been hopeless . . . [One] was an absolute debacle . . . [that] went on for nearly two years.’’ (Area Finance Oficer, WAHS)

VMoney was finally implemented successfully in all the AHSs though some AHSs experienced greater implementation costs than others. Taking into account the extent of performance decline, the time taken to achieve routinized use and the eforts of top and middle management and end users to achieve successful implementation, VMoney incurred substantially higher costs than those for Datapower. A search of the interview transcripts found many references to dificulties with implementing VMoney and few references to easy implementation. Given that VMoney was implemented in all AHSs, this also suggests a high degree of reliability for the assessment of implementation costs experienced with VMoney.

## 5.3. Case 3: The Mimate implementation project

The Mimate (acronym for Management Information mate) project was also initiated by Jenkins shortly after the successful implementation of Datapower. Just as Datapower was designed to download data from DOH’s HOSPAY package to support analysis and reporting of HR-related data, Mimate was designed to download data from DOH’s HOS-FIN package to support the analysis and reporting of financial data. Jenkins developed the initial prototype, working with the same analysts who worked on the two earlier applications, and ofered it to other AHSs on the same terms as for Datapower and VMoney.

## 5.3.1. Implementation costs for Mimate

Three AHSs decided to join WAHS in the Mimate consortium. Of these, one dropped out before starting implementation. The other two experienced significant dificulties in implementation: Both experienced extended periods of performance decline and were unable to achieve routinized use even 2–3 years after implementation. Significant conflict needed to be resolved by the intervention of top management.

‘‘[AHS] had Mimate for about two or three years and didn’t do one thing with it . . . There was so much in-fighting going on that it just meant that they couldn’t get the thing of the ground . . . [AHS] has got Mimate - they are running it, but they are not running it to its capabilities.’’ (Area Finance Oficer, WAHS)

Mimate was successfully implemented only by WAHS. The other two AHSs that implemented it did not manage successful implementation even after 3 years. A search of the interview transcripts found no reference to any successful outcome with implementing Mimate in those two AHSs, while there were numerous references to implementation difficulties. Overall, considering the extended period to routinized use, the extent of conflict and the eforts of top and middle management, the implementation costs incurred for Mimate were substantially higher than for VMoney. However, given that Mimate was implemented in only two AHSs other than WAHS, the reliability of this assessment cannot be considered as high as those for the other two cases. We return to this issue in the cross-case analysis in Sections 7.1 and 7.2, where we provide additional assessments of implementation costs for Mimate. ## 6. Organizational changes and variation in implementation costs across cases

The above description of the cases reveals considerable variation in implementation costs across the three cases (Table 3). In the analysis below, we identify the changes involved in the three cases in each of the organizational elements identified in the MIT90s framework (Fig. 1), and the interdependencies between the changes. We then conduct a cross-case analysis to test the propositions developed above.

## 6.1. Changes involved in Datapower implementation case

## 6.1.1. Changes to technology

Datapower provided on-line access to data already available to AHSs in hard copy. The introduction of the new application involved only incremental changes to the existing technology, specifically, the new application with the capability to download data from an existing data source. Datapower did not involve any additional investments in technology and did not change the technological infrastructure in any other way.

## 6.1.2. Changes to individual roles and skills

The introduction of Datapower did not require users to acquire new skills. They were already using on-line systems for other applications. Nor were the users required to perform new or diferent tasks, as they were already using the hard copy of the data provided by Datapower.

## 6.1.3. Changes to strategy, structure and management processes

Datapower did not involve any changes to organizational policies, practices or structures.

‘‘The majority of the [Datapower] system is a standard system that is being used in a standard way throughout all the hospitals . . . There haven’t been any major areas of disharmony or conflict amongst the users . . . I can’t think of anything where we have had to modify our practices.’’ (HR Manager, WAHS)

In addition, there was little variation across AHSs in the way they used Datapower.

‘‘Basically [with Datapower], we all had the same problem. We might have had differences about little things like how it might operate. With Datapower, we are talking about a set bit of data, that is, what was available . . . in terms of payroll. You couldn’t create data . . . [so] there wasn’t much to haggle over, not too much to have diferences of opinion about.’’ (Computer Manager, participating AHS)

Changes resulting from the introduction of Datapower are summarized in the shaded box in Fig. 2. Changes were limited to one element of the configuration, with no interdependencies with any other elements. Therefore, the implementation of Datapower involved only elemental change.

## 6.2. Changes involved in implementing VMoney

While Datapower involved only incremental change to the technology element of the organizational configuration, implementing VMoney involved changes to multiple elements of the organizational configuration within which the VMO payment process was embedded.

## 6.2.1. Changes to technology

VMoney required technical changes to make existing accounting systems interoperable with VMoney. While VMO payments had previously been a stand-alone process, VMoney integrated it with the Accounts Payable component of each AHS’s accounting system:

‘‘[VMoney] passes that [payment] information into the accounting package, so that, instead of having to punch the information into Accounts Payable and your financial package, you just electronically interface it up to the accounting package.’’ (Area Finance Oficer, WAHS)

The changes to technology are considered incremental as the introduction of VMoney was not accompanied by additional investments in technology platforms or other application systems.

## 6.2.2. Changes to individual roles and skills

The implementation of VMoney also introduced incremental changes to the roles and skills of VMO payment staf. The new agreement required numerous changes to the VMO payment process, including the collection of additional data not only for calculating payments but also for enabling a higher level of reporting, auditing and control over VMO payments. This was achieved by mandating a standard timesheet and payment calculation procedures for VMoney:

![](/api/attachments/NJD3JMZC/fulltext/images/2b3fc87eab2f158d18fd298cd711b83ff023853a7d5690e2a0c99da8d1218968.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Changes involved in implementing Datapower.

‘‘[Before VMoney], they were making judgments on what a payment should be, and often they were quite wrong. Now, we have the VMoney system audited to make sure it is an accurate reflection of the [oficial policy], and also the payment methodologies.’’ (Area Finance Oficer, WAHS)

Prior to adopting VMoney, each VMO payment clerk performed the payment calculations. Many of the skills possessed by the payment clerks were still relevant in mastering VMoney. However, it did require them to acquire new skills in running a new type of payment process that interfaced directly with the AHS’s accounting system and which was subject to external audit. Finally, many AHSs that had stand-alone VMO payment processes running in all their facilities, took the opportunity to centralize VMO payments, expanding the role of VMO payment clerks.

Implementing VMoney also required incremental changes to the roles that Medical Administration, Accounting Departments and VMOs played in the payment process. Medical Administration authorized VMO services and received and approved timesheets submitted by VMOs, while the Accounting Department was responsible for approving and making the payments. The new roles reflected the payment process embedded in VMoney.

## 6.2.3. Changes to management processes

Finally, the implementation of VMoney introduced incremental changes to management processes employed to control VMO payments. With the DOH demanding improved financial accountability, this had become an important objective for the AHSs as VMO payments constituted a substantial proportion of their budgets. VMoney provided AHSs with reports that were used to analyze, track and control VMO expenditures. Previously, many AHSs had interpreted the VMO contracts ‘generously’ in order to build special relationships with their VMOs and to ensure greater reliability in their availability. It was not uncommon for VMOs to be paid diferently by individual AHSs for the same services, although all AHSs were governed by the same contracts. With VMoney subject to external audit, and the need to control expenditures, this flexibility was no longer available.

## 6.2.4. Changes in strategy and structure

The implementation of VMoney did not involve any changes to strategy or to the organizational structure.

## 6.2.5. Interdependencies between changes

The successful implementation of VMoney required coordination across the three shaded elements in Fig. 3. The design of VMoney was initially shaped, among other considerations, by the desire of hospital administrators to improve control over VMO expenditures. A number of features embedded in VMoney supported that objective. They included a detailed breakdown of VMO activities as inputs, and standard management reports analyzing VMO payments as outcomes. The changes in management processes that AHSs experienced were shaped by the features embedded in VMoney:

![](/api/attachments/NJD3JMZC/fulltext/images/117193eb5f2ed71e0a46c086599413dc0e72bab17b37de1c1766a1ee780db6a4.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Changes involved in implementing VMoney.

‘‘We had to sort of change our practices to fit the functionality of [VMoney].’’ (Finance Director of AHS that was a late adopter of VMoney)

The changes in individual roles and skills were shaped both by the design of the payment process embedded in VMoney and by the management processes that emerged. VMO payment oficers had to learn the specific features embedded in the technology to process payments, as well as the specific controls over VMO payments required by the new management processes. Successful implementation of VMoney required that management processes and individual roles and skills be adapted to the changes in technology in order to create an adequate level of internal fit.

The implementation of VMoney involved configurational change, with sequential interdependence between the changes within the three elements of technology, management processes, and roles and skills. Overall, the changes associated with the introduction of VMoney and their interdependencies are presented in Fig. 3.

## 6.3. Changes involved in implementing Mimate

The implementation of Mimate required changes to multiple elements of the organizational configurations. Whereas VMoney involved changes to only three elements of the configuration, Mimate involved incremental changes to all elements of the configuration, including implications for AHS strategies.

## 6.3.1. Changes to technology

As with Datapower and VMoney, Mimate involved incremental changes to technology to enable existing accounting systems in each AHS to transfer data to Mimate and to make the two systems interoperable:

‘‘Areas put their budgets in [Mimate] and the actual [expenditures] get interfaced over [into Mimate], so that’s where they do the comparison . . . You put all the purchase orders in, you pay all the invoices, you pay the cheques and you get the information into the general ledger and just extract the general ledger information into Mimate.’’ (Manager, Business Systems, participating AHS)

As with Datapower and VMoney, the introduction of Mimate involved only incremental changes to technology as no additional investments in technology were required to implement Mimate.

## 6.3.2. Changes to individual roles and skills

Implementing Mimate required managers to develop new skills in budgeting. This included managing the performance of their units against approved budgets. Such formal management processes for performance control were not standard operating practice at the AHSs:

‘‘It was leading towards them being made responsible for individual budgets . . . It was pushing the responsibility downwards.’’ (Budget Accountant, WAHS)

‘‘The managers went from a situation where they had virtually no information . . . to every responsibility centre . . . being able to view their financial data.’’ (Area Finance Oficer, WAHS).

## 6.3.3. Changes to management processes and organizational structure

Mimate was designed as part of an AHS’s internal control and performance management process, allocating costs and budgets to specific departments and supporting department-based monitoring of financial performance. Implementing Mimate required AHSs to clarify the allocation of costs and responsibilities between cost centers. To do that required negotiating mutually acceptable relationships between a number of cost centers, including materials management, purchasing and clinical units. The new arrangements for allocating costs involved structural changes in the manner by which tasks, responsibilities and budgets were assigned across organiza tional units:

‘‘Goods and services . . . [is] one area that is not really well established as to what expenses need to spread out to responsibility centers . . . I would say . . . probably 80% . . . is all going to a central responsibility centre. The question is: Do you divide it down into the various [responsibility centers]? That really hasn’t been established and the question is whether it is worthwhile.’’ (Budget Accountant, WAHS)

‘‘Mimate . . . gives us information on our budgets, how much we have used and what has been given to us as budget. It gives us more insight to what is actually in the budget, so we are putting forth more questions about what the budget is all about and how they enter the journals and those sort of things.’’ (Director of Nursing, Dept of Surgery, DH I)

## 6.3.4. Changes to strategy

The introduction of Mimate was a response to the DOH demand for greater financial accountability. This move was prompted, in part, by increasing health care costs that had to be borne by the State Government. There was political pressure to rein in health care costs and AHSs were undergoing a gradual strategic shift, with greater emphasis on financial eficiency and a new emphasis on managing patient care to meet financial and medical objectives simultaneously. The introduction of Mimate enabled the AHSs to track costs by departmental units and by specific types of patient conditions.

## 6.3.5. Interdependencies in changes

As with VMoney, the successful implementation of Mimate involved interdependencies in the changes to the elements in the MIT90s model. The new management processes embedded in Mimate shaped the changes in management processes and individual roles and skills required for successful implementation, resulting in sequential interdependence (shown in Fig. 4 by single-headed arrows):

‘‘We haven’t started using [Mimate] yet because we had to restructure our Chart of Accounts’’ (Finance Oficer of AHS that adopted Mimate)

In addition, the implementation of Mimate involved reciprocal interdependencies among strategy, structure and management processes (shown by double-headed arrows in Fig. 4). All three elements had to be realigned through complex negotiations involving unit managers and senior management before Mimate could be used:

‘‘At the moment, 60% of drugs is charged to Pharmacy. Do we want to go that way or do we want to charge that 60% out to the Wards?’’ (Budget Accountant, WAHS)

‘‘Nowadays, you can look at the budget and say ‘What’s Special Services?’. Because they don’t know where to slot a particular purchase they have put it under Special Services.’’ (Director of Nursing, Dept of Surgery, DH I)

Decisions involving changes to existing cost center structures influenced the way in which the budgets and goals were set up within Mimate. Changes to cost center structures, which impacted on the budgeting processes, were, in turn, dependent on the strategic objectives of top management: ‘‘It comes down to what you want the Clinical/Divisional heads to be responsible for.’’ (Budget Accountant, WAHS). In turn, the management processes and unit structures that emerged from this process influenced the strategic objectives of the AHS. The complex changes involved in the implementation of Mimate are summarized in Fig. 4 (single-headed arrows representing sequential interdependencies and double-headed arrows representing reciprocal interdependencies).

## 7.1. Interdependencies and implementation costs

A key premise underpinning the propositions developed here is that interdependencies among the changes in elements of the MIT90s model drive implementation costs. The cross-case analysis above supports the study’s propositions regarding the efect of interdependencies on implementation costs. Further analysis of case data provides additional support for the propositions.

Implementation costs incurred by both VMoney and Mimate were related to the interdependencies between the elemental changes. For instance, as quoted above, one AHS initially adopted Mimate but could not use it till related changes had been put into place: ‘‘We haven’t started using [Mimate] yet because we had to restructure our Chart of Accounts’’ (Finance Oficer of AHS that adopted Mimate). This AHS incurred substantial implementation costs as top management tried to negotiate a new Chart of Accounts before implementing Mimate, confirming the efect of sequential interdependence on implementation costs. In addition, negotiating the complex reciprocal interdependencies in changes between structure and management processes led to significant conflict and high implementation costs. This AHS finally abandoned Mimate and decided to develop a similar package more suited to its specific configuration:

‘‘[They] couldn’t agree on what their structure was . . . They were unable to work out how they would roll the information up to suit their needs – management needs as opposed to clinicians needs.’’ (Area Finance Oficer, WAHS)

Similarly, sequential interdependencies between changes in technology and management processes contributed to the high implementation costs incurred in the case of VMoney:

‘‘We had to sort of change our practices to fit the functionality of the product, which didn’t match the way we wanted to administer our VMOs . . . [It] required a lot of pain and [aggravation] for us in the Accounts Payable area and our Medical Administration area.’’ (Finance Director of AHS that was a late adopter of VMoney)

AHSs that decided not to implement Mimate and VMoney explained their decision in terms of the misfit of the technology with their existing configurations and the resulting high costs of managing conflict and implementing interdependent changes. While this does not provide direct evidence supporting Propositions 1–4, it does show that the decisionmaking models employed by the managers who rejected Mimate and VMoney are consistent with the theory developed here:

‘‘You had to be designed the way [WAHS] are in terms of their structures, e.g. your Finances and Patient Services have to be set up in a particular way and ours are not. There would have been . . . change required to do that, which would have [been detracting] . . . The structures were diferent and the hence the data requirements were slightly diferent . . . You end up with this conflict . . . (This) isn’t a Mimate area.’’ (Computer Manager, AHS that did not adopt Mimate)

‘‘[Other Areas wouldn’t be involved in VMoney because] a lot of it depends on how the organization is structured and who is looking after the IT side of things and what they see as a problem area . . . They really don’t see why they should change.’’ (Finance Oficer of AHS that was early adopter of VMoney)

The above analysis supports the theory developed here that high implementation costs observed in configurational change (Proposition 2) are due to interdependencies in the changes involved. Further, it suggests that managers implicitly, if not explicitly, accounted for interdependencies in changes and resultant efects on implementation costs in their adoption decisions. Generally, managers did not anticipate significant implementation costs for Datapower and, therefore, were willing to adopt it. However, most recognized in advance the higher implementation costs for Mimate and VMoney, which accordingly reduced adoption propensities. Even the moderate implementation costs expected for VMoney appear to have presented significant barriers to initial adoption. However, when an external contingency intervened, in the form of a new VMO agreement, implementation costs were overwhelmed by the cost of non-compliance with the new agreement and AHSs became willing to implement VMoney.

## 7.2. Implementation costs and misfit

The case data also enable us to extend prior theory relating the costs of change to the extent of misfit. Prior theory suggests that the costs of change can be expected to be high in the case of high levels of misfit, as between diferent ideal type configurations such as a machine bureaucracy and an adhocracy (Johnston & Yetton, 1996; Soh, Kien, & Tay-Yap, 2000; Soh & Sia, 2005). However, prior theory is silent on the costs of change in the face of small misfits, as between configurations that are variants of the same ideal type.

All AHSs belonged to the same ideal type configuration, the machine bureaucracy. This was a result of the uniformity in administrative systems imposed across the AHSs by the DOH. Mimate was developed to download data from HOSFIN, a package shared by all AHSs. Further, it mirrored the prescribed Chart of Accounts. The DOH exercised strong central control over financial administration and IT infrastructure, including specifying a common Chart of Accounts. Nevertheless, each AHS had developed variations around the practices imposed by the DOH.

‘‘Their procedures are the same . . . Even then . . . each area still has its own idiosyn crasies. The Department of Health says the Chart of Accounts is this . . . We have ten Area Health Services and really there are ten diferent ways to adjust it . . . [Our] purchasing oficer’s . . . procedure for doing things is . . . totally diferent to [AHS].’’ (Manager, Business Systems, participating AHS)

‘‘There is no uniformity necessarily throughout the health system in the way they do things in a lot of Areas. I mean, a lot of the other Areas have diferent accounting systems to us and don’t work on the responsibility centre [structure].’’ (HR Manager, WAHS)

The variations meant that Mimate, having been designed initially to fit the specific configuration of Wentworth AHS, could have resulted in low fit with the configurations pre vailing in other AHSs. However, since all AHSs exhibited strong similarities due to the central control of DOH and belonged to the same ideal type configuration, the extent of misfit can be considered small in comparison to misfit between diferent ideal types (Doty et al., 1993; Johnston & Yetton, 1996). Nevertheless, the AHSs both anticipated and experienced high implementation costs. Extending prior theory, this study suggests that implementation costs for overcoming even small misfits can be quite high, as in the Mimate case.

We now return to the issue, raised in Section 5.1, of the reliability of our assessment that Mimate incurred high implementation costs. The evidence presented in this section and in Section 7.1 suggests that high implementation costs were a consideration in the rejection of Mimate by non-adopting AHSs. The expectations of non-adopters are consistent with the experiences of the AHSs that implemented Mimate. While not providing direct evidence, it does increase confidence in our assessment that AHSs implementing Mimate incurred high implementation costs.

## 7.3. Alternative explanations for variations in implementation costs

Below, we analyze the three cases from an alternative theoretical perspective, cooperation theory (Ring & Van de Ven, 1992, 1994), that could ofer plausible explanations for the pattern of implementation costs observed across the three cases. This is a relevant perspective to consider since all three cases involved cooperative development and, more importantly, Datapower was the first attempt by AHSs to develop an IS application under this governance structure. It is possible that diferences in trust, governance and cooperation across the cases could explain the diferences in outcomes (Contractor & Lorange, 1988; Gulati, 1995; McAllister, 1995; Ring & Van de Ven, 1992, 1994; Smith, Carroll, & Ashford, 1995). Considering alternative plausible explanations from an alternative theoretical perspective adds to the rigor of the analysis and, therefore, improves the validity of the findings (Eisenhardt, 1989).

Cooperation theory predicts that organizations enter into cooperative relationships when they do not possess adequate resources to achieve a desired outcome (Contractor & Lorange, 1988). A consortium approach provides members with advantages, including ownership, control, shared learning and support, that make it advantageous for members to enter into cooperative relationships (Ring & Van de Ven, 1994). Cooperative relationships, such as consortia and strategic alliances, may be the only feasible form of governance when neither market-based nor hierarchy-based forms of governance are feasible (Ring & Van de Ven, 1992). Consistent with this prediction, we find that the DOH’s failure to meet a long-standing need, combined with the inability of individual AHSs to develop their own solutions, led AHSs to explore a cooperative relationship for developing Datapower:

‘‘[The Area Health Services] wanted some sort of MIS development out of the payroll system and we just couldn’t wait any longer for the DOH to come up with the ultimate solution.’’ (Finance Oficer, AHS participating in Datapower)

However, individual AHSs were unable to develop their own packages as ‘‘there wasn’t a push in our organization to devote the resources to it.’’ (Computer Manager, AHS participating in Datapower).

Cooperation theory also predicts that trust is a critical antecedent enabling organizations to undertake cooperative relationships (Ring & Van de Ven, 1992). Trust, as a social mechanism governing risk, is available only to organizations in a socially and personally embedded environment (McAllister, 1995; Ring & Van de Ven, 1992). Consistent with this prediction, trust played an important role in the high level of participation in the Datapower consortium:

‘‘Graham Jenkins and myself have known each other for quite a long time. I have known him since he was up at . . . Hospital. I have been on quite a number of working parties with him.’’ (Finance Oficer, AHS participating in Datapower)

‘‘[Jenkins reputation is that he is] entrepreneurial, sees an opportunity and wants to take it . . . He wanted what the hospitals wanted.’’ (Manager Business Systems, Area Health Service participating in Datapower)

Cooperation theory also predicts that trust is not only an essential antecedent to cooperation but is also reflected in the conduct of the relationship (Smith et al., 1995). Consistent with this prediction, contracts were negotiated and agreed on the basis of trust. Contract negotiations were highly informal and issues arising from opportunistic behavior and bounded rationality were not discussed:

‘‘When a person like Graham (Jenkins) says: ‘‘I think I can work a deal whereby we can get functionality, I agreed.’’ (Computer Manager, AHS participating in Datapower)

Cooperation theory also predicts that initial cooperative relationships are likely to be governed by contracts that include elaborate safeguards (Gulati, 1995). However, contrary to this prediction, the Datapower consortium did not impose any monitoring mechanisms during development. Jenkins was given a free hand to develop the products, with the other participants virtually relinquishing their contractual rights to participate in product development.

‘‘I didn’t have to put virtually any time in other than attend a monthly meeting and put my piece in about direction, how it was going, where it was going.’’ (Computer Manager, AHS participating in Datapower)

In general, analysis of the Datapower case finds support for a number of propositions following from cooperation theory. It is possible that the high levels of trust and cooperation, a simple governance structure and the absence of opportunistic behavior could explain the success and low implementation costs of Datapower. As the three consortia were running concurrently, the key players involved were the same and similar conditions prevailed across the three consortia, cooperation theory would predict similar outcomes across the three cases. Contrary to this prediction, implementation costs varied signifi cantly across the three cases. Cooperation theory does not ofer a plausible explanation for the diferences in implementation costs across the cases.

Further, cooperation theory predicts a dynamic pattern of cooperation over time that is inconsistent with the observed pattern. Cooperation theory predicts that initial attempts at cooperation attract few participants, but initial success and repeated positive interactions generate increasing levels of trust and cooperation (Gulati, 1995). The pattern of cooperation predicted by the theory is: low level of participation in the first consortium, Datapower, and higher levels of participation in VMoney and Mimate following the success of Datapower. Contrary to this prediction, the observed pattern was: high level of partic ipation in the first consortium, Datapower, followed by low levels of participation for VMoney initially and for Mimate.

We conclude that alternative plausible explanations that could be drawn from cooperation theory are inconsistent with the case evidence. This increases confidence in the validity of the conclusions drawn.

Organizational innovation theory could also provide an alternative explanation for the variation in implementation costs across the cases. It predicts that technical innovations incur higher implementation costs than administrative innovations and that radical innovations incur higher implementation costs than incremental innovations (Ryan et al., 2002). However, given the limitations of the available data, we are unable to investigate propositions following from this theory.

## 8. Discussion

This research has developed a theoretical framework to explain IS implementation costs. The use of configuration theory integrates and extends earlier frameworks identify ing the roles of mutual adaptation, organizational validity and fit in the implementation of information systems (Leonard-Barton, 1988; Markus, 2004; Markus & Robey, 1983).

Propositions developed from the theoretical framework are supported by the analysis of case data. In particular, we find that implementation costs increase when (a) the extent of change is high, (b) configurational change is involved, as compared to elemental change, (c) the number of interdependent elements involved in configurational change increases and (d) reciprocal change is involved as compared to sequential change.

## 8.1. Limitations

Support for the theory developed here can be considered only tentative, given the nature of evidence ofered. More rigorous testing is required to validate the theory. In particular, while Propositions 1, 3 and 4 are supported, the efects are confounded in the case data. Further research is needed to validate that the efects are independent.

It is also possible that the relationships observed are due to the idiosyncratic characteristics of the research site. For instance, all three cases investigated involved consortium development, an arrangement that is not common in practice. Further, the cases occurred in organizations that are part of the Australian health care system, which has an organizational structure that is not common in other sectors. In addition, all three cases examined are administrative innovations involving incremental changes carried out at a gradual pace. It is possible that the conclusions drawn may not hold in other contexts.

Finally, though analyst bias in interpreting the case data cannot be entirely ruled out we attempted to mitigate it in a number of ways. First, the case data were evaluated independently by the first two authors. Second, the analysis was carried out in consultation with the third author who had not been involved in data collection. Finally, we reviewed our analysis with a number of respected scholars from both the qualitative and quantitative research traditions and revised it based on the feedback.

The assessment of implementation costs is not uniformly reliable across the three cases. VMoney was implemented in all AHSs and Datapower was implemented in all but one AHS. This yields a suficient number of data points to make a reliable assessment of implementation costs for these two cases. In contrast, Mimate was implemented in only two AHSs beside WAHS. While there is evidence that implementation costs in both those cases were very high, the reliability of this assessment compared to the other two cases is lower. While evidence from the one AHS that abandoned Mimate after initial adoption and others that did not adopt Mimate is consistent with our assessment of high implementation costs, this assessment needs to be treated cautiously. Since support for Proposition 4 (reciprocal interdependencies incur higher implementation costs than sequential interdependencies) is based on a comparison of the relative implementation costs experienced in Mimate and VMoney, support for this proposition also needs to be considered cautiously.

In developing and evaluating the propositions, this paper has focused on the efect of organizational changes on one component of implementation costs – opportunity costs of internal resources. We believe that efect on external resources incurring monetary costs is similar. However, this assumption needs to be tested in future research.

Another limitation of this work is that, by focusing on organizational elements and their interdependencies, the obviously critical role of organizational actors and their efects on implementation costs have been ‘abstracted’ away. Complementing the configurationbased analysis presented here with alternative theoretical frameworks that focus on the roles of individual actors is likely to yield even richer analyses of implementation contexts and associated costs. For instance, Sarker, Sarker, and Sidorova (2006) employ actor-network theory to understand the complexities of IT-enabled business process change. Their analysis describes the roles of top management, other actors and their coalitions in business process change, and suggests an alternative theoretical framework to understand implementation costs.

## 8.2. Implications for research

This research extends current theory in two ways. First, employing a configuration framework, it disaggregates IS-enabled organizational change into change in organizational elements identified by the MIT90s model. This enables a more robust understanding of how implementation costs are likely to vary depending on the extent of change expected in configuration elements and the interdependencies amongst these changes. This is an advance over prior theory which employs a higher-level construct, organizational change, in understanding the behavior of implementation costs.

Second, while the role of fit in explaining implementation costs has been identified in prior research, the discussion has been incomplete. It does not identify the elements between which fit needs to be established. The use of configuration theory addresses this limitation, with the MIT90s model identifying elements within which fit needs to be created. Overall, the theory developed here extends current frameworks for the explanation, prediction and control of implementation costs.

## 8.3. Implications for practice

The framework developed here can help managers to implement IS innovations successfully by improving their capability to predict and to control IS implementation costs. When implementation costs are underestimated, organizations are likely to settle for sub-optimal outcomes, such as project abandonment or partial implementation. It is not uncommon for even technically strong products to sufer implementation failures due to unanticipated implementation costs. Such outcomes are potentially avoidable if decision makers are able to develop a more robust understanding of the implementation context.

The framework developed here can also help managers estimate the extent of imple mentation costs at the initiation stage of projects. This contributes to a higher likelihood of successful implementation in two ways. First, organizations can create the appropriate conditions for success under conditions of configuration-based change. This includes the allocation of senior management and other key stakeholders to support the implementation. Second, organizations can evaluate the implementation costs and business benefits of alternative project designs, making cost-benefit trade-ofs at the initiation and design stages. Undertaking this exercise would also reveal to managers the deep structure of their organizations and generate valuable knowledge for the implementation stage and beyond

The framework developed here can also be used to devise tactics for controlling imple mentation costs. Current theory suggests that reducing the pace of change reduces implementation costs (Benjamin & Levinson, 1993; Gallivan, Hofman, & Orlikowski, 1994). This is particularly relevant for innovations characterized by a high degree of trialability or divisibility, where controlling the pace of change enables both end users and managers to engage in efective learning and adaptation, contributing to successful implementation (Ettlie, Bridges, & O’Keefe, 1984; Gallivan et al., 1994). Extending prior recommendations, this study suggests that the reduction in implementation costs could be a consequence of simplifying the interdependencies rather than simply decreasing the pace of change. An implementation tactic that reduces the pace of change without simplifying the interdependencies is unlikely to produce substantial reductions in implementation costs.

## 9. Conclusion

Analyzing a proposed IS implementation based on the nature of the induced organizational changes produces better estimates of implementation costs than would otherwise arise. In particular, such analyses must focus on understanding the nature of the interdependencies likely to exist across the induced changes. As depicted through the case studies that have been analyzed, what may appear on the surface to be an incremental IS implementation may, in fact, prove to be a very complex and dificult undertaking. The ideas developed here are useful for scholars interested in both IS implementation and the management of ISenabled change as well as for practitioners who confront these issues on a regular basis.
