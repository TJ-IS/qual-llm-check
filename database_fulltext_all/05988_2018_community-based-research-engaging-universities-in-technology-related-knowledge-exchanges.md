## A B S T R A C T

Many universities are disengaged from their local regions. While universities are hotbeds of knowledge with the potential to generate new and disruptive technologies, their development strategies and public relations engagements rarely emphasize the potential of universities to act as loca and regional economic engines. Similarly, information systems (IS) and other scholars generally aim to make international, not local impacts. Loca contributions are often viewed as distractions from more visible and highly rewarded roles in the global scholarly community, such as publishing in international journals. This Global Challenge article argues that universities' local ecosystems are appropriate targets for IS scholarly activity, particularly when this is undertaken in the form of community-based research (CBR). Following conceptual discussions of ‘community’ and key elements of CBR. we draw on the authors' personal research experiences to evaluate the potential contributions of IS scholars in terms of CBR projects focused on technology-related knowledge exchange. After outlining implications and potential future directions for IS researchers, we close by presenting a call to IS scholars to intensify their local ecosystem research collaborations in technology-related knowledge exchanges contexts, in order to increase the relevance and local impact of their research.

## 1. Introduction

Many universities are disengaged from their local regions. While universities are hotbeds of knowledge with the potential to generate new and disruptive technologies, their development strategies and public relations engagements rarely emphasize the potential of universities to act as local and regional economic engines. The University of Cambridge public website, for example, has a section dedicated to ‘Global Cambridge’ (which describes Cambridge as ‘a global university’), but no comparable section for ‘Loca Cambridge’ (although there is a thriving ‘Silicon Fen’ established around the university). Similarly, the Queen's University website prominently highlights its international engagement but only recently created a brief ‘Queen's and Kingston [the local community] section.

Information systems (IS) and other scholars within the ‘ivory towers’ generally aim to make international, not local impacts. They seek to assist the university in achieving prized international rankings by gaining visibility as global scholars, principally by publishing in a select group of international academic journals. Local contributions are often viewed as distractions from these more visible and highly rewarded roles in the global scholarly community. This Global Challenge article argues for the importance of universities' local ecosystems as appropriate targets for IS scholarly activity. While global engagement is to be applauded, loca challenges should not be overlooked. IS scholars can make significant contributions in the context of local crises (e.g., in the light of natural disasters and terror (Leong, Pan, Ractham, & Kaewkitipong, 2015; Levallet & Chan, 2018), economic opportunities (Chan, Jacobs, Dixon, and Ragetlie, 2013; Chan, Dixon, and Dukelow, 2013; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015), and moral imperatives (e.g., the expectation that universities and scholars will be good citizens (Ahuja & Chan, 2014)).

We argue that IS scholars in today's universities can increase the local relevance of their research by increasing their involvement with the communities and regions in which they are located. By working in multidisciplinary teams that incorporate industry representatives as well as academic sta<sup>f</sup>, and by focusing on community challenges that involve IS-relevant themes (e.g., relating to technology, organizations, or knowledge management), IS researchers working in university contexts can become more e<sup>f</sup>ective citizens of their cities and economies.<sup>1</sup> In this article, we focus on the potential contribution of IS scholars to technology-related knowledge exchanges in universities' local ecosystems. Technology-related knowledge exchange involves the transfer and transfor mation of technological expertise, broadly interpreted to include, for example, technical knowledge, user experience and organi zational insight, from one organizational location to another. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne university ecosystems as local systems constituted by interorganizational exchanges and collaborations that address stakeholder needs and seek to advance the collective good by mobilizing scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technical insights from within academia (Nkhata, Breen, & Freimund, 2008). Ecosystems seek to tackle communit meta-problems that transcend the boundary of any single organization, thus presenting rich opportunities for local engagement and transformative change. By engaging with these opportunities, IS scholars can make signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to pressing challenges within their university ecosystems, while also contributing critical perspectives that acknowledge the limits of transformation in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts.

Below, we <sup>fi</sup>rst consider de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of ‘community’ before outlining key elements of community-based research (CBR). We then outline the importance of community-university technology-related knowledge exchanges and consider the contributions that IS researchers can make in this context. To do so, we highlight the value of the mobilization of IS research <sup>fi</sup>ndings that a<sup>f</sup>ect communities (e.g., on broadband deployment, digital platforms, digital ecosystems, and sustainability). We illustrate these exchanges using research that authors have personally conducted or coordinated. That is, we use both the literature and our own persona experience (e.g., Chan, Jacobs, Dixon, et al., 2013; Chan, Dixon, et al., 2013; Farrington, Isabel, Holland, Barrett, & Oborn, 2014). After outlining implications for IS researchers, we close by presenting a call to action to IS scholars to intensify their local ecosystem research collaborations in order to increase the relevance and impact of their research.

## 2. De<sup>fi</sup>ning community

While the term community is commonly used by scholars, practitioners, and citizens (Chavis & Wandersman, 1990), the term has multiple potential meanings depending on context. Gus<sup>fi</sup>led (1975) proposes a useful distinction, dividing potential meanings of the term into two main categories: (a) community as geographical place, based on neighborhood, city, region, state, or international region, and (b) community as a set of human relationships, which are not necessarily bounded by geographical limitations. The two categories are not mutually exclusive: individuals' sense of community is shaped both by relationships and locality, such that, for instance, integrating a focus on individuals' relational quality in the design of public spaces can build a holistic view of the community (McMillan & Chavis, 1986; Talen, 1999).

Additional dimensions of communities can also be canvassed, including linguistic, ethnic, political, environmental, and economic communities. Christopherson and Clark, for instance, de<sup>fi</sup>ne regions as spaces of ‘coherent economic interests’ and ‘natural economic unit[s]’, thus adding an economic dimension to the geographical and relational concept of community outlined by Gus<sup>fi</sup>led (1975). Consequently, in this article we use the term ‘community’ to allude to geographical place, locally-rooted relationships, and loca economic activity, referring at once to local geographical regions in which universities are physically placed, the range of knowledge and technology exchange relationships that characterize local university ecosystems, and the economic dynamics that thereby arise.

## 3. Challenges and opportunities in community-based research (CBR)

Jason and Glenwick (2016: 2) discuss the challenges of studying community-based phenomena and undertaking community engagement, emphasizing in particular the need for multidisciplinary strategies to address complex challenges. They state, for example, that ‘impediments that communities confront, such as inadequate resources or insu<sup>fi</sup>cient technical knowledge, may require a variety of di<sup>f</sup>erent strategies’. Similarly, SSHRC (the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council, a Canadian funding agency) argues that [c]ommunities are presented with an increasingly complex mix of opportunities and challenges with multiple social, economic and cultural dimensions’ (SSHRC, 2012). They go on to suggest that local challenges are ‘best addressed at the local and regional levels by the local and regional groups that best understand the needs of, and the factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting, particular communities’, and make particular reference to the potential for stronger linkages between universities and community organizations:

[These alliances] can be enormously e<sup>f</sup>ective and yield important bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for them both … [by] foster[ing] new knowledge, tools and methods to develop the best strategies for diverse aspects of intervention, action research, program delivery and policy development that will be appropriate for our rapidly changing times. (SSHRC, 2012).

Recognizing that universities typically prioritize international over local communities, Halseth et al. (2016: 5) note that ‘universities are being prodded to be more relevant to the conditions and challenges facing [local] society,’ adding that ‘[r]esearch should matter’ (emphasis in original). In Canada, for example, a recent government report stresses the responsibility that academia shares with the government for the health of the workforce and economy (Premier's Highly Skilled Workforce Expert Panel, 2016). In response, universities increasingly acknowledge the importance of their technology transfer, innovation, and workforce development mandates at both local and national levels. For example, in Ontario, Canada where one of the authors works, university strategic mandate agreements have recently been established requiring that universities explicitly articulate their community contributions.<sup>2</sup>

In this context, Christopherson and Clark (2007: 123) build on economic dimensions of communities (as discussed above) to develop an account of ‘learning regions’, understood as an extension of <sup>fi</sup>rm-based innovation models at the level of the community and incorporating strong networks alongside ‘an elite population of knowledge producers and knowledge users’. Learning regions are high-tech areas that have responded ‘successfully to the competitive challenges presented by a knowledge-based economy’, including components such as: 1) a major research university, 2) researchers who are encouraged to engage in practical activities, 3) venture capital for startups, and 4) a local entrepreneurial tradition and expertise on the management of startups. University academics, including IS scholars, can view their university communities as potential learning regions and play their part in ensuring that this potential is realized. IS scholars' participation in community-university knowledge exchanges, for example, can provide learning and employment opportunities for students, create jobs, revitalize communities, foster innovation hubs, and enhance community problem-solving capacity. Engagement of this kind can also generate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for IS scholars through the achievement of synergy between ‘basic’ and ‘applied’ research, with these terms understood in terms of involvement with local communities (i.e., ‘basic’ research is relatively independent of, whereas ‘applied’ research is closely involved with, local communities). Halseth et al. (2016:7), for instance, state that

in practice, we have found more synergy than separation between the notions of basic and applied research. Whenever we conduct an applied research project in partnership with a community or organization, for example, we invariably <sup>fi</sup>nd that the results speak to issues being debated within the academic literature.

That is, community engagement creates opportunities for ‘double hurdle’ research, which is both relevant and rigorous (Pettigrew, 1997), although the extent to which these opportunities are realized is likely to vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly across contexts depending on project design, objectives, participants, timing, and resources, among other factors. We now consider these factors in more detail through the lens of community-based research.

## 4. What does community-based research involve?

Community-based research (CBR), which is part of a family of research approaches including participatory research, participatory action research, transdisciplinary research, and community-based participatory research, can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘research that is conducted by, for or with the participation of community members [Typically, CBR is] driven by awareness of the complexity of problems and the desire to address … issues using multiple sources of expertise’ (Halseth et al., 2016: 17). It represents the ‘engaged university’ and involves mutual learning. CBR represents a continuum in which the extent of participation of community members (e.g., local decision-makers and organizations) varies and the amount of power community members have to determine research goals and processes also varies (Farrington, 2016; Halseth et al., 2016). Partners can be engaged (or not) in determining the research topic, designing the research question, study design, data collection, data analysis, reporting, and so forth. A wide range of topics, theories, and methods has been utilized in practice (Jason & Glenwick, 2016). In the health services research context, one of the authors (Farrington) has been involved in several technology-related projects involving community stakeholder participation as a form of priority-setting. In one study of e-learning training modules for carers responsible for elderly residents of care homes (Farrington, 2014), Farrington organized a workshop with local policymakers, managers, carers, and education specialists in order to inform study design. In another study focused on knowledge exchange and electronic/paper care records in community-based intellectual disability teams (Farrington et al., 2014), Farrington adopted a more informal approach characterized by discussions with collaborators active in academic disciplines outside IS and directly relevant to the research topic. In both cases, di<sup>f</sup>erent approaches allowed community stakeholders to in<sup>fl</sup>uence and enrich the course of future research projects, creating more relevant outputs for their professional lives.

Judith Rodin, a proponent of CBR, describes the University of Pennsylvania's e<sup>f</sup>orts to achieve economic and social change in its local community during her term as President (Rodin, 2015). In this period, the university served as an agent of change: Throughout the University, in all its departments and schools, many faculty members became substantively, deeply engaged. This was no longer scholarship about the community; this was scholarship for the community, its needs, and its potential’ (Rodin, 2015: 17; emphases in original). In the research experience of one of the authors in particular (Chan), we have found it helpful to study community challenges that involve important and complex issues, a<sup>f</sup>ecting the lives of thousands to millions of individuals. In particular, it has been productive in terms of both knowledge generation and application to focus on new issues that require new knowledge gen: eration (versus novel applications of existing knowledge). These issues may permit in-depth, longitudinal study and analysis over time. They may require multidisciplinary community-university or government-industry-university. triple helix’ (Etzkowitz & Leydesdor<sup>f</sup>, 2000) investigations. For IS scholars, digital issues (e.g., the digital city and the digital economy) may be particularly

relevant.

Many IS scholars, however, strongly resist a community focus, arguing for example that community participation and researcher involvement can lead to perceived or real forms of bias (regardless of the unavoidability of subjectivity and positionality in socia science research more widely). Additionally, some scholars cite perceived disadvantages of CBR, including perceived or actual lack of rigor, lack of generalizability if there is a focus on a single community, and reduced researcher control (Halseth et al., 2016). In addition, processes and outcomes may be indeterminate, and projects may not unfold as planned (Ball, 2014). More fundamentally, some argue that CBR diverts academic e<sup>f</sup>ort from ‘purer’ purposes such as the generation of knowledge, which many see as the core purpose of universities. Thus Christopherson and Clark (2007: 129) write:

To the extent that the competitiveness-oriented goals behind regional innovation systems interfere with or shift resources away from the central mission [of the development of knowledge], they undermine the university as a knowledge-creating institution’; there is ‘intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed pressure placed on universities to serve as ‘economic engines’.

Another concern is the potentially poor <sup>fi</sup>t between the demands of CBR and criteria used for evaluation and advancement for IS scholars. The prioritization of articles in top (mostly international) journals places pressure on IS academics to write to meet the demands of these journals and to publish quickly. Successful and rigorous CBR, however, often unfolds over extended time periods. It may involve taking time to build relationships with community partners and translating research <sup>fi</sup>ndings for community partners so that they can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the studies undertaken. There can also be burdensome ongoing obligations. For example, years after <sup>fi</sup>nishing community projects, CBR researchers may receive invitations to community events, and be on a plethora of community mailing lists. This can occur long after the funding for the project has ended and the papers have been published. This has been the authors' own experience, and at the time of writing one of us (Chan) has been asked to provide input to a representative of the Government of Canada based on a community project completed over 10 years ago.

Finally, skeptics argue that CBR researchers may become involved in local politics if they study their immediate community and comment on controversial matters, yet this may be unavoidable at times, as one of us (Farrington) has experienced in research on local communities in Latin America (Farrington, 2012). Power and politics may interfere with research either in current or in future projects. This concern is particularly important in light of growing recognition that what is presented as ‘local’ knowledge may represent a narrow range of interests, potentially strengthening already powerful voices and marginalizing others (Cooke and Kothari, 2001).

Despite these potential drawbacks, CBR presents many possible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to IS scholars. CBR can result in the development of ‘shared understandings’ and ‘shared languages’ with study subjects (e.g., local citizens and business owners), greater internal and external study validity, more robust <sup>fi</sup>ndings, deeper understandings, and strengthened impacts (Halseth et al., 2016). Anthropologists, educational scholars, human geographers, and others commonly use a range of social science research methodologies (e.g., ethnography, case study, and participant observation) for the in-depth study of dynamics in particular communities, adding intellectual depth and validity to CBR projects. Undergraduate and graduate students can be involved in CBR projects, in course work, research assistantships, internships, summer projects, and dissertations. There are also teaching bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in terms of the potential to generate teaching materials such as company examples and community stories that can be used in the classroom (ethics clearance permitting). Furthermore, knowledge mobilization can be very e<sup>f</sup>ective as a result of prior community engagement. Community partners seeking research <sup>fi</sup>ndings or data to support evidence-based decision making are often willing to repeatedly visit websites, read email/text communications, participate in blogs, arrange TV and radio interviews, attend seminars and workshops, and encourage colleagues to participate in research projects (Chan, Jacobs, Dixon, et al., 2013; Chan, Dixon, et al., 2013). Consequently, while CBR can be demanding in terms of resources and ongoing time commitments, it can also produce rigorous research and a number of academic and other bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 4.1. Knowledge co-production and mobilization

Many IS researchers study knowledge management, and consequently have important knowledge assets to contribute to their local communities, especially when this focus is combined with technology-relevant research. For instance, some scholars study the phenomenon of knowledge mobilization even as they go about mobilizing IS research <sup>fi</sup>ndings (i.e., new knowledge) to a<sup>f</sup>ect community activities, connectedness, and sustainability (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding broadband deployment, digital platforms, and digital ecosystems). Other IS academics study the development, di<sup>f</sup>usion, and management of new technologies themselves, processes which inevitably extend the focus of IS research beyond the narrowly technical to embrace social issues arising from the embedding of technology in sociotechnical and sociomaterial networks (Orlikowski, 2007). For scholars working in these areas, there are many possible information technology (IT) research applications of great value to local businesses, insofar as these businesses participate in technology design, use, and experimentation. Additionally, scholars utilizing critical perspectives can usefully problematize notions of community and local knowledge in order to open up a wider range of voices in local community involvement, for example by designing participatory processes to involve previously marginalized parts of a given community (Chan & Benecki, 2013).

By engaging in technology-related knowledge exchanges in university ecosystems and learning regions, IS scholars generate the potential to improve the health and competitiveness of their communities. By sharing research <sup>fi</sup>ndings that use local data, they can immediately provide knowledge that addresses local needs. In this way, a knowledge creation-dissemination-use cycle may be strengthened and shortened, heightening innovation and productivity. When academic researchers collaborate with non-academic partners in local university communities, they are more likely to formulate new research questions, make discoveries, and impact local communities (Phipps. Cummings. Pepler. Craig, & Cardinal. 2016). Involving non-academic partners in the co-production ot research increases the likelihood of advancing knowledge and community impact (Van de Ven, 2018). This type of research inquiry requires co-production between academic scholars and non-academic partners in local communities at di<sup>f</sup>erent stages such as research formulation, dissemination, organizational uptake, and community implementation (Phipps et al., 2016). The co-production at each stage bene<sup>fi</sup>ts both academic researchers and non-academic partners. While there are a wide range of potential co-production pathways depending on timelines, resources, partners, and knowledge exchange models, Fig. 1, below, describes one e<sup>f</sup>ective coproduction pathway between academic researchers and non-academic community partners.

A well-known de<sup>fi</sup>nition of knowledge states that ‘[k]nowledge is the capacity (potential or actual) to take e<sup>f</sup>ective action (Bennet & Bennet, 2006: 24). In other words, knowledge is tied to action. This realization reinforces the importance of CBR and of knowledge mobilization, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the process of creating value or a value stream through the creation, assimilation, leveraging, sharing and application of focused knowledge to a bounded community’ (Bennet & Bennet, 2006: 17). One of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of CBR is the increased likelihood of value realization.

Knowledge mobilization (KMb) can also be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ‘reciprocal and complementary <sup>fl</sup>ow and uptake of research knowledge between researchers, knowledge brokers and knowledge users both within and beyond academia in such a way that may bene<sup>fi</sup>t users and create positive impacts’ (SSHRC, 2012). Knowledge mobilization focuses on ecosystem knowledge exchanges. Below, we illustrate technology-relevant knowledge exchanges within university ecosystems, using information systems and management research that one of the authors (Chan) has conducted, supervised, and/or coordinated. Much of this research has been tied directly to the economic revitalization of communities and much of it has been conducted in community-university research teams. The research has been conducted in Canada and India to date, although studies in the UK and the US are planned. The studies have aimed to help strengthen regional, even national, performance. They have focused on regional innovation in general and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, on digitally-enabled business innovation in university ecosystems (in particular, in university incubators). The research has been used to inform public policy, management practice, and digital practice in areas of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant importance to Canadian citizens and busi nesses. As a Canadian researcher, most of Chan's studies have been funded by SSHRC (the federal agency that supports social sciences research). A number of CBR projects that Chan has led are multi-disciplinary. Four of these are outlined below. They have a rural focus because Chan's university is located in the largely rural southeastern area of the province of Ontario

## 4.2. Illustrating knowledge co-production and mobilization through CBR projects

The <sup>fi</sup>rst project we discuss here was Broadband Strategic Research and Impact Analysis: Development of a Partnership and Evaluation Framework for Assessing the Social and Economic Impacts of Provincial Broadband Investments. This impactful study was funded by the Ontario Ministry of Government Services (MGS) and by the Ontario Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural A<sup>f</sup>airs (OMAFRA). Using publicly available economic data, the research demonstrated that where the government made costly broadband investments in the region, job employment rates and economic vitality signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased. This project had substantive policy implications which were communicated, with the assistance of project partners and sponsors, to both the federal and provincial governments.

Another multi-year project, the SSHRC-funded Research Partnerships to Revitalize Rural Economies Project, involved <sup>fi</sup>fteen researchers at <sup>fi</sup>ve universities (including Chan's). The primary research themes were: 1) Rural Knowledge Work and Entrepreneurship; and 2) Innovation and Sustainability in Creative Rural Communities. Several technology-related studies were included. For example, one project examined the use of technology by remote small business owners, while another project modelled employment and wage impacts of broadband deployment in southern Ontario. A committed (academic and community) leadership team and steering committee guided this research, which included over 30 organizational partners.

Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on their experience of involvement in this CBR project, one community partner emphasized important bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in terms of the generation of new and more granular knowledge regarding speci<sup>fi</sup>c regions:

The knowledge generated through partnerships between rural communities and [the academic team] is groundbreaking… [Preexisting] data for … southern Ontario doesn't drill down enough to demonstrate how di<sup>f</sup>erent the east is from southwestern Ontario … but [the academic team] does a good job in helping us develop that data. Before [the academic team] engaged in that activity there was virtually no form of research going on in eastern Ontario that could lead to activities that could bene<sup>fi</sup>t our communities, whether that was the utilization of technology… awareness of the creative economy… to research speci<sup>fi</sup>c on our population … I think it's the collective work that has gone on that has brought to light key issues that impact all of eastern Ontario.

Another community partner re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the reputational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts arising for non-academic partners working in collaboration with academic stakeholders in community research: ‘It gave us credibility that we might not have otherwise had because we are very much a rural region … Engaging in academic projects adds that gravitas to what we are doing here.’ This partner went on to note how involvement in CBR not only generates new forms of knowledge, but also new ways of accessing and applying academic knowledge:

the fact that you have respected institutions like Queen's University actually paying attention to things that are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to us and also looking at those things in other rural communities and also giving us access to academic research that we can look at and see how that applies - that has been bene<sup>fi</sup>cial.

A third study, also SSHRC-funded, was Revitalizing Rural Economies by Mobilizing Academic Knowledge – A Knowledge Impact in Society (KIS) Project. This multi-year knowledge mobilization initiative developed relationships and communications products to <sup>fi</sup>nd, translate and deliver academic knowledge to increase the economic vitality of rural eastern Ontario communities. This knowledge was not limited to the IS domain. However, much of the knowledge shared (e.g., regarding technology for small business growth) was IS-related. Group support system-facilitated discussions were held in 14 communities shown in Fig. 2, below. (Kingston, shown in red, is where Chan's university is based.) Chan and her research team travelled to each of these communities to identify local research/ knowledge needs. Workshop attendees asked for studies to be completed, or information provided, on topics such as workforce development in the knowledge economy, downtown revitalization in remote communities, using technology to attract immigrants, and IT-enabled green economy innovations. They then systematically conducted literature reviews and new studies to meet knowledge and research needs.

When asked about the value of the project, community stakeholders described a range of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, spanning economic, environmental, and infrastructural topics. For example, one businessperson outlined how the project had enabled the survival of his business, with knock-on bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the surrounding community:

They [the researchers] brought speci<sup>fi</sup>c information and perspectives [that were] previously unavailable … It's resulted in the survival of my business, and the survival of my business ensures the survival of the small community I live in.

A participant in a di<sup>f</sup>erent community, whose interests focused on green issues, described how the academic team ‘helped us identify current and emerging recreational water and wastewater issues and determine the best ways we can use new technologies to address those issues.’ In still another community, a respondent emphasized how a workshop had created a much-needed public space for deliberation regarding investment in IT infrastructure: The rural broadband workshop tapped into our need to discuss ideas that speak to leveraging the considerable IT investment to drive economic revitalization and global participation.

More widely, the project resulted in unprecedented access to academics and academic research in many communities across southern Ontario. In this context, one project partner highlighted how the project had disrupted organizational silos by enabling community, government, and business stakeholders to interact with academic partners:

Up until this project, it was a silo driven [approach] - the community was in a silo, the academics were in a silo, the government was in a silo, and businesses were in a silo. What the research has really done is facilitated our access to the silo that is the academy. That was something we were having problems with before. So just giving us entrance into that world was very important.

It should be noted, however, that challenges were also experienced. These surfaced in the fourth and <sup>fi</sup>nal project described in this section, funded by Canada's Rural Secretariat and focused on assessing The Impact of Knowledge Mobilization on Rural Economic Development. Chan and colleagues conducted workshops, interviews, and surveys to assess the extent to, and the ways in, which academic knowledge (including IS/IT knowledge) had contributed to the vitality of rural communities in Southern Ontario. The project examined the e<sup>f</sup>ects of past academic research (including the Chan projects<sup>3</sup>) on rural development. It documented the satisfaction levels and perspectives of key community, government, business, and academic stakeholders, and also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed new information, tools, and action still needed to revitalize rural regions (Chan, Jacobs, Dixon, et al., 2013; Chan, Dixon, et al., 2013).

As outlined above, many participants were very satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. In a survey of key partners, on a Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 being very dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the CBR experience and 5 very satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the CBR experience, the mean partner satisfaction score in the survey was 4.21, and the median score was 4.25. No rankings were below 4.0. (Eleven partners completed the survey anonymously.) Partners expressed satisfaction with both the experience and outcomes of the academic-community partnership. Nonetheless, some partners highlighted areas in which challenges were experienced, including the following: the need for academic partners to acknowledge the di<sup>fi</sup>culty of recruiting participants for time-consuming research involvement for long-term (rather than short-term) bene<sup>fi</sup>ts; the relational and dynamic challenges arising from discontinuity of key personnel in academic and community contexts; <sup>fi</sup>nancial limitations; disconnects between academic and community goals; and uncertainty regarding responsibility for future action based on new research insight. Excerpts from the study's <sup>fi</sup>nal report (Chan, Jacobs, Dixon, et al., 2013; Chan, Dixon, et al., 2013), which discuss these challenges, are included in Appendix 1.

The challenges outlined above highlight the need for academics involved in CBR projects to ensure that projects are designed with the involvement of community stakeholders, with clear communication regarding expectations, objectives, personnel, and timelines for future action. Nevertheless, despite these challenges, the various projects described above did allow IS academics to study important issues such as technology investments, deployment, adoption, management, and impacts, as well as knowledge exchanges, knowledge management, and innovation in the local region in which the university is based. ## 5. Implications for IS research

We have highlighted the importance of IS researcher involvement in knowledge exchanges in local university ecosystems. We believe that IS scholars should not shy away from large, real-life, messy, complex, multi-faceted problems that are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to solve. While they may not be tackled adequately by individual IS researchers, they can be addressed as part of a community-university alliance. Conducting research in the university community can be academically and socially rewarding. Knowledge mobilization and transfer can reach high levels of e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Research relevance and impact increase when the scholar in<sup>fl</sup>uences regional policies, programs, and planning. This said, we recommend that IS scholars focus on IS phenomena when engaging the local university community. In order to e<sup>fi</sup>ciently utilize research knowledge and expertise, IS academics should retain their core disciplinary focus despite the many interconnections between IS challenges and other social, business and economic issues. As IS researchers, it may be prudent to study technology-related, community-relevant phenomena like digital innovation, digital cities, and digital economies that are IT-infused from beginning to end, and that also have the potential to transform life, business, ecosystems, and communities.

One important area of potential focus for future IS-related CBR projects relates to technology exchanges and, in particular, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of university-sponsored incubators (TBIs). Technology exchanges can be used to fuel industry clusters, or ‘agglomerations of competing and collaborating industries in a region networked into horizontal and vertical relationships involving strong common buyer-supplier linkages’ (Stimson, Stough, & Roberts, 2006: 242). Although there are many technologies and community settings that can be studied within this general context, we believe that particularly strong IS research opportunities exist in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of universitysponsored technology-based incubators (TBIs). Technologies generated and developed within university environments can be used to generate startups that are then fostered within TBIs (Ahuja & Chan, 2016; Chan & Ahuja, 2015; Oppong-Tawiah & Chan, 2016). TBIs help to generate shared end-markets, strong buyer-supplier linkages, and shared technology and know-how, and can provide loca economies with strategic leadership, digital infrastructure, market intelligence, networking, capacity building, learning processes, and digital resources (Stimson et al., 2006). TBIs can be used to create critical masses of technology-led <sup>fi</sup>rms, facilitating intensive technology-related knowledge sharing, and creating value by providing entrepreneurs and startup <sup>fi</sup>rms with resources they cannot readily a<sup>f</sup>ord on their own, from basic physical space to access to experts and technology (Amezcua, 2010, 2013; Dolińska, 2015). Incubators help <sup>fi</sup>rms survive in the challenging early stages of business operations, providing ‘space, services, advice and support designed to assist new and growing [startup] businesses to become established and pro<sup>fi</sup>table (Joseph, Bordt, & Hamdani, 2005: 7; Hamdani, 2006).

IS researchers studying university-based TBIs can foster the development and use of emerging technologies, thus helping to address critical technology challenges. They can study and foster digital technology capabilities in TBIs, and by so doing indirectly enhance the performance of startups, incubators, and wider university innovation ecosystems and local economies. In recent years, Chan has used CBR approaches to examine technology's role in facilitating commercialization or ‘mind to market’ transformation in TBIs (Ahuja & Chan, 2016; Chan & Ahuja, 2015; Oppong-Tawiah & Chan, 2016; Ardito, Petruzzelli, & Albino, 2015. Chan has also examined the use of emerging digital technologies within startups in university-a<sup>fi</sup>liated TBIs, focusing on digital innovation, or ‘the use of digital technology during the process of innovating’ (Nambisan et al., 2017: 223). Chan's goal is to understand how startups innovatively leverage IT and how TBIs use IT to jump start and catalyze ‘innovation ecosystems’ (Thorp & Goldstein, 2013). Practica study results have guided TBI directors and helped startup founders to improve their companies' digital capabilities and digital innovation.

## 6. A call to action

Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the role of the university in the community, Rodin (2015: 182) writes:

A university has the power to be a great agent of change. It has the intellectual. financial. and human resources to take on the challenge of community transformation. It espouses values that embrace shared community, diversity, and engaged discourse.

To this we would add that universities also have important technology resources. In the 21st century, information systems and technology are seamlessly woven into the fabric of everyday work and life. To be relevant and to have high impact, IS researchers should address messy, large, technology-related challenges (Niederman et al., 2017) such as those in local university ecosystems. To do this, IS researchers must increasingly embrace partnerships with researchers in other disciplines, and with non-academics (e.g., policy-makers and decision-makers), in the communities in which they are located. They need to be e<sup>f</sup>ective citizens and scholars of their communities, cities, and economies (Rodin, 2015). In this context, Rodin (2015, p. 19) writes that ‘it is not enough for us to produce brilliant, imaginative doctors, lawyers, scholars, and scientists who will push the envelopes of their disciplines or professions; we must also engage them in the large issues of our day, in the ferment of our times and our society, and in the issues in our own backyard.’ More recently, Hazelkorn (2017) re<sup>fl</sup>ects on the need for universities to become more civically engaged with their local communities:

Disturbingly, many universities have become civically disengaged. They have transformed themselves into self-serving private entities less engaged or committed to their nation/region as they eagerly pursue their world-class position … We sit at a historic junction one in which higher education has the opportunity and responsibility to play a critical role in (re)building a shared sense of societal purpose and identity … There is an onus on universities and colleges, of all missions, to rethink and reshape relations with their publics and the state, and to re-orient themselves, alongside their students, sta<sup>f</sup> and graduates, and the wider community, to become an intellectual force to bridge the gap between local, national and global.

We suggest that one highly productive avenue for university-community engagement arises in the context of CBR projects, and that IS scholars have distinctive (and much-needed) contributions to make in terms of projects focusing on technology-related knowledge exchange. While challenges of various kinds frequently present themselves in such projects (see Table 1 and Appendix 1), CBR projects can nevertheless lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant transformations at regional and local levels. We acknowledge that some periods of the scholar's academic career (e.g., post-tenure) lend themselves more easily to an ecosystem-focused research program, than other periods (e.g., pre-tenure). However, IS scholars may wish to consider playing small roles in local university initiatives even when they cannot lead CBR projects. By engaging in CBR work, IS scholars can engage in important local research that also has potential for global impact.

To further the agenda of academic (and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally IS scholarship) involvement in technology-related community transformation, we outline in this <sup>fi</sup>nal section a set of four guidelines for IS researchers wishing to engage in high-impact technology-related CBR projects. These guidelines are derived from our experiences of CBR and other relevant research processes, and are designed to focus attention on the most pressing issues arising in project design, governance, and implementation. These guidelines are not intended as de<sup>fi</sup>nitive, but rather as a useful waypoint in a continuing journey towards greater local and regional impact on the part of IS scholars and their research.

1. Balance community and academic priorities. In our experience, high impact community engagement involves research co-de-<sup>fi</sup>nition and co-production. The community itself, rather than IS researchers, can best identify critical community challenges (Conrad & Sinner, 2015); consequently, dialogue and negotiation are essential in order to identify gaps in relevant knowledge bases and opportunities for technology-related knowledge exchange to generate local bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Engaging the community (e.g., a city or region) to establish key research questions is an essential middle step between conducting research in isolation in the researcher's o<sup>fi</sup>ce, on the one hand, and tackling global challenges, on the other.

2. Invest in multi-helix collaboration. Ideally, CBR projects should aim to disrupt organizational silos by bringing together previously separate institutional actors (e.g., from community, government, business, and academic backgrounds) and enabling them to work together towards shared goals on the basis of intensive, on-going knowledge exchange. In collaborations of this kind, challenges frequently arise in terms of divergent organizational contexts and knowledge exchange dynamics, so considerable investment is needed, on the part of IS researchers, in the attempt to understand these varied dynamics and their possible impact on technology-related knowledge exchanges.

3. Listen to the widest possible range of voices. CBR projects, as discussed above, can create public spaces in which stakeholders can deliberate over shared priorities for community-focused research involving multidisciplinary partners. However, it is essential to recognize the prevalence of power disparities at all social levels, including local and community scales; as such, it cannot be assumed that the mere creation of a public space will inevitably allow equitable participation from all stakeholders. IS researchers should therefore attempt to ensure that participatory mechanisms are designed to enable involvement from a wide range of stakeholders, for example by attempting to minimize the <sup>fi</sup>nancial cost of participation and by carefully facilitating public discussion to avoid dominance by more powerful stakeholders

4. Clarify rules of engagement. When planning CBR projects (and indeed any kind of participatory co-design process; Farrington, 2016), it is vital to attain clarity in terms of what is expected from all stakeholders in terms of resources (e.g., time commitment, <sup>fi</sup>nancial cost, or other burdens), and what is expected in terms of outputs (e.g., interim and <sup>fi</sup>nal project deliverables). While it is important to listen to community voices in terms of priority-setting (see above), expectation management is also a crucial part of CBR work, in order to avoid disappointing stakeholders regarding the outcomes of current projects as well as limiting the attractiveness of future CBR projects by ‘poisoning the water.

As stated above, these guidelines are intended to serve as a starting-point for IS researchers, rather than as a de<sup>fi</sup>nitive account of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and challenges of technology-related CBR projects. We hope, however, that they may prove useful to IS researchers seeking to respond to our call for action. In a world economy increasingly characterized by interconnectedness and mobility, local communities face a growing range of economic, technological, infrastructural, and other challenges – challenges that IS scholars are often uniquely well-placed to address. Successful community-based research requires signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time- and resource-commitment on the part of all stakeholders, including universities and scholars themselves; as Hazelkorn states, ‘[t]here is no time for complacency’. Nevertheless, by taking account of the challenges as well as the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of CBR work, we believe that IS scholars are wel placed to enact transformative, technology-related changes in their local communities.

## Appendix 1. Challenges experienced in The Impact of Knowledge Mobilization on Rural Economic Development CBR project

A limitation raised by one partner regarding academic partnership focuses on the issue of time. When ‘outside folks’ hold forums, workshops, or focus groups, small businesses and community agencies ‘want to know… how does this have immediate practical application to us’ because they have limited time to lend to academic research which tends towards longer-term impacts on policy Although the partner recognized ‘the incredible importance of the research,’ they found that ‘it's just really hard to convince people that this is going to be a good use of your time without specific short-term benefits to the participants. [The partner] conceptualized this issue as the challenge of possessing the communication infrastructure to ‘translate the research into action.’ The challenge of knowledge translation ‘takes a fair amount of facilitation and some real speed work’ within individual rural economies …

One partner cited a subsequent turnover in local elected oficials as a reason why their community may not have used academic resources to their full potential. When individuals or positions shift, potential momentum may be lost, and ‘it does afect the level of commitment… and resources available … and you almost have to start all over again in terms of relationship development.’ Despite this transition, positive relationships between this particular community and [the academic team] were created and maintained. Another partner cited a community context in which an elected council that understood and supported innovation was replaced by a new counci that ‘didn't see any shovels in the ground and therefore did not want to be attending needless meetings even though we have brought new [opportunities] to their door.’ The need for continuity is important on both the academic and community sides of partnerships.

Academic-community research projects can also be limited by financial constraints and by partner expectations of each party's financial circumstances. [A key partner] explained that communities need to understand that universities have financial realities as well. Communities that reauest research to solve specific problems can only expect so much pro bono work' and must not come hat in hanò without resources’ because the university does not have the capacity to work in this way.

Many partners addressed the need for mutual understanding between academics and communities in order to make academic research useful. Several partners favored a locally driven, geographically-delineated, model of research over one that involves academics finding communities that fit into their individual research interests. As one partner reflected, ‘I guess the most useful thing in a community is really asking the community what questions it needs answered.’ [A partner] critiqued the academic culture where researchers ‘are asking questions; they are not trying to solve problems.’ Likewise, another partner noted that ‘professors have a particular area of research’ and create connections with communities based on how they might connect to existing lines of research rather than asking communities what research they require

[Yet another] partner candidly stated, ‘the research is more for you guys than the community and I don't think that is appropriate.’ This partner felt that the community and [the academic team] did not agree on the purpose of [the joint research]. The partner asserted, ‘I love the fact that [the university] is out here and doing stuf in the community but nothing sticks, so the agenda should be the community's agenda, not [the university's] agenda.’ This partner would have preferred that one local issue be chosen as the focus of the [academic team's] research. They had thought that their community's central issue would be the focus; however, the scope was wider. The partner remarked, ‘part of the problem is we have people here that don't quite work out together and you get stuck in the middle of all that.’ A teamwork approach, subscribed to by all community and academic participants, is needed in order to make the relationship more productive.

Another partner elaborated that [research] should be tied to clear action items because ‘very little happened’ after[wards]. Although the partner acknowledged that ‘it's up to the local players, actually, to take the report and run with it,’ it was evident that ‘some pieces … need to be who is going to do what and who owns this; the research is great but who owns the next step?’
