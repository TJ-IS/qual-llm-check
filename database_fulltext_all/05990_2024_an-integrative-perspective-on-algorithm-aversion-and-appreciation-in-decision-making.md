# AN INTEGRATIVE PERSPECTIVE ON ALGORITHM AVERSION AND APPRECIATION IN DECISION-MAKING<sup>1</sup>

Ekaterina Jussupow Department of Business, Law and Economics, Technical University of Darmstadt, Darmstadt, GERMANY {ekaterina.jussupow@tu-darmstadt.de}

Izak Benbasat Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC CANADA {izak.benbasat@sauder.ubc.ca}

Armin Heinzl Business School, University of Mannheim, Mannheim, GERMANY {heinzl@uni-mannheim.de}

People have conflicting responses for support from algorithms or humans in decision-making. On the one hand, they fail to benefit from algorithms due to algorithm aversion, as they reject decisions provided by algorithms more frequently than those made by humans. On the other hand, many prefer algorithmic over human advice, an effect of algorithm appreciation. However, currently, we lack a shared understanding of these constructs’ meaning and measurements, resulting in a lack of theoretical integration of empirical findings. Thus, in this research note, we conceptualize algorithm aversion as the preference for humans over algorithms in decision-making and analyze approaches in current research to measure this preference. First, we outline the implications of focusing on a specific understanding of algorithms as computational procedures or as embedded in material or nonmaterial objects. Then, we classify four decision configurations that distinguish individuals’ evaluations of algorithms, human advisors, their own judgments, or combinations of these. Consequently, we develop a classification scheme that provides guidance for future research to develop more specific hypotheses on the direction of preferences (aversion vs. appreciation) and the effect of moderators.

Keywords: Algorithm aversion, algorithm appreciation, decision-making, trust, artificial intelligence, advice-taking

## Introduction

With the technological advances in artificial intelligence (AI), many human decisions are now supported by algorithms (Fügener et al., 2022; Jussupow et al., 2021). Algorithms can, for example, help human decision makers enhance medical diagnostic accuracy or predict future sales. However, despite the widespread adoption of various algorithms in practice, academic research repeatedly finds that decision makers fail to fully benefit from algorithms in decision-making.

This phenomenon has fueled interdisciplinary research on algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015), offering different explanations for why individuals react adversely to algorithmic versus human decisions. For example, research has indicated that algorithmic errors result in a more vigorous rejection of algorithmic advice than human errors (Dietvorst et al., 2015). However, we are still not certain whether individuals develop algorithm aversion or, instead, favor algorithms over humans and thus appreciate algorithms for decision-making (Logg et al., 2019). To add complexity, individuals can respond positively and negatively depending on the measure applied (Dennis et al., 2023; Starke et al., 2022), or they can alternate between algorithm aversion and appreciation (Turel & Kalhan, 2023). Consequently, the empirical landscape on algorithm aversion is fragmented with conflicting results, which makes it challenging to develop cumulative knowledge.

To resolve such empirical conflict, we need to consider contingency factors in algorithm aversion, which could enable us to specify the circumstances under which algorithm aversion or appreciation is more prevalent. However, two areas of incommensurability limit the degree to which findings of different empirical studies can be compared.

First, the type of algorithm applied in a specific study on algorithm aversion is rarely theorized. Many studies consider a wide range of information technologies (IT), mostly systems based on artificial intelligence (AI), as their unit of analysis, while others refer to algorithms as non-IT artifacts, such as mathematical formulas. However, the theoretical implications of various understandings of algorithms are often not considered, and many scholarly papers treat the technology as a black box. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the preference for a human or algorithmic advisor, e.g., for an AI chatbot that provides medical advice, results from general assumptions about how the agent approaches the decisionmaking or whether it is specific to a given context or technological system.

Second, studies on algorithm aversion differ in their understanding of aversion. For example, Dietvorst et al. (2015) compare how individuals adjust their forecasting behavior toward algorithmic or human forecasts before and after having experienced errors, while others, such as Rebitschek et al. (2021) and Dennis et al. (2023), compare how individuals perceive humans versus algorithms prior to interacting with them. Other studies that follow similar decision configurations, i.e., that compare individuals’ responses to humans versus algorithms, have not labeled their study as algorithm aversion research (Goodyear et al., 2017), although in literature reviews on algorithm aversion, they have been considered as such (Burton et al., 2020; Jussupow et al., 2021; Mahmud et al., 2022). Consequently, algorithm aversion research includes a variety of dependent variables such as trust (Dennis et al., 2023), confidence in algorithms (Dietvorst et al., 2015), fairness (Starke et al., 2022), and emotional responses (Prahl & Van Swol, 2021). However, what constitutes the unique characteristics of algorithm aversion is currently unclear.

This research note specifies algorithm aversion in decisionmaking as the preference for humans over algorithms in decision-making. Decisions can materialize in different forms, such as forecasts, estimates, or dichotomous yes or no decisions. They are provided to individuals as advice, as output to be evaluated, or as a decision that individuals can delegate to algorithms and humans. With this definition, we can address the above-mentioned shortcomings by classifying different understandings of algorithm aversion in decision contexts. First, we use the theory of digital objects (Faulkner & Runde, 2019) to differentiate between two perspectives on algorithms, i.e., algorithms (1) as computational procedures and (2) as embedded in nonmaterial (e.g., a specific software program) or material objects (e.g., robots or autonomous cars). Second, we investigate how choosing between distinct decision configurations allows researchers to measure different aspects of the construct. Therefore, we classify empirical approaches based on four decision configurations of human and algorithmic agents involved in decision-making processes and the decisions that are made. Finally, we develop a classification scheme that helps us disentangle measurement types of algorithm aversion in decision-making. This facilitates future research to develop a more nuanced perspective on the construct, taking us further than the empirical dualism of algorithm aversion and appreciation.

## Algorithm Aversion and Algorithm Appreciation

Algorithms can generally be referred to as “a procedure for solving a … problem in a finite number of steps that frequently involves repetition of an operation” (Merriam-Webster, 2023), which mostly, but not necessarily, involves using a computer. Research on algorithm aversion dates back to Meehl’s (1954) book Clinical versus Statistical Prediction, which differentiates between two methods of combining data and reaching a decision: informal (clinical or subjective) judgments and mechanical (actual, statistical) methods. Meehl (1954, p. 15) analyzed the hesitance toward using statistical or actuarial predictions to help patient treatment, defining the statistical approach as arriving at a prediction by equations. Later, Grove and Meehl (1996) argued that despite repeatedly presented evidence that actuarial prediction methods were on par or even more accurate than clinical predictions, human professionals were generally reluctant to apply these methods, instead favoring clinical judgments in making predictions. In their argument, Grove and Meehl (1996, p. 293) differentiated between the clinical method, which relies on human judgment, and the mechanical method, involving “a formal, algorithmic, objective procedure (e.g., equation) to reach a decision.” Building on this understanding of different procedures, Grove et al. (2000, p. 19) explicitly refer to mechanical prediction as being based on computer programs.

However, the first to explicitly introduce the term algorithm aversion was Dietvorst et al. (2015, p. 114), who used it to describe that “evidence-based algorithms more accurately predict the future than do human forecasters. Yet when forecasters are deciding whether to use a human forecaster or a statistical algorithm, they often choose the human forecaster.” Dietvorst et al. applied a general understanding of algorithms, referring to algorithms as entities that “encompass any evidence-based forecasting formula or rule” (p. 114). They thereby aligned with prior research that compared individual responses to either human judgment or statistical formulas (Eastwood et al., 2012) or that investigated how to combine expert knowledge with standardized procedures (e.g., Kleinmuntz, 1990). Thus, in the initial research on algorithm aversion, it did not matter whether the result was derived from a statistical formula or a computer program, as both were treated as examples of the same method for deriving a prediction.

Since 2015, heterogeneous conceptualizations and assumptions regarding the construct of algorithm aversion have emerged, leading to two main areas of disparity. First, algorithms are referred to not only as standardized procedures but also as different IT artifacts, such as recommender systems (Castelo et al., 2019) or AI systems, which, while capable of performing complex tasks more autonomously, are opaque and can learn over time (Bauer & Gill, 2023; Dennis et al., 2023; Kim & Duhachek, 2020). The implications of these different understandings of algorithms have yet to be theorized.

Second, there are different understandings of “aversion” due to the heterogeneity of measured dependent variables and different research streams in which the variables feature. Definitions of algorithm aversion include a negative response to algorithmic errors (Dietvorst et al., 2018), suggesting that individuals’ perceptions of algorithms change as they interact with algorithms and experience their errors. Some definitions assume that individuals do not arrive at optimal decisions while using algorithms, referring to aversion as the “reluctance of human (decision-makers) to use superior but imperfect algorithms” (Burton et al., 2020, p. 220) or “human preferences for and against making use of algorithms” (Rahwan et al., 2019, p. 483). Other definitions are more general, referring, e.g., to negative attitudes such as mistrust (Logg et al., 2019, p. 91) or to “a behavior of neglecting algorithmic decisions in favor of one’s own decisions or other’s decisions, either consciously or unconsciously” (Mahmud et al., 2022, p. 1). These assume that algorithm aversion can occur before interacting with algorithms and can be understood as a general negative predisposition toward algorithms. In addition, there is a current controversy about whether the preference for human judgment results from the actual decision quality (Hannon et al., 2024) or from a “bias,” i.e., individuals’ implicit negative attitudes toward algorithms (Jussupow et al., 2020; Turel & Kalhan, 2023). Further, with more research on algorithm aversion, conflicting findings have emerged, indicating that humans often do follow algorithmic suggestions, displaying algorithm appreciation rather than aversion (Logg et al., 2019, p. 90).

To account for this broad range of perspectives, we use a very general definition of algorithm aversion and define it as the preference for humans over algorithms in decision-making. We argue that the distinctive element of this construct is comparing individuals’ responses to algorithms and their responses to humans. Observing algorithmic or human errors can be conceptualized as one moderator that can change the direction of preferences. Further, as decision makers preferences can manifest in two directions, either as aversion or as appreciation, we refer to the preference for or against algorithms in the following.

## Conceptualizing Preferences for or against Algorithms in Decision-Making

In the following, we elaborate on how this perspective can help us classify and integrate the results of the experiments on algorithm aversion and appreciation. We first describe two perspectives on algorithms and then distinguish four decision configurations to measure preferences for or against algorithms.

## Specifying Algorithms

Faulkner and Runde’s (2019) reasoning in the theory of digital objects provides a foundation for developing a layered perspective of algorithmic materiality. First, algorithms can be considered merely a computational procedure without an IT artifact. This includes Meehl’s (1954) and Dietvorst et al.’s (2015) understandings, which focus on how individuals respond to statistical formulas. Second, algorithms can be considered IT artifacts embedded in nonmaterial and material objects. For example, the output (e.g., text) generated by a machine-learning algorithm can be a fundamental part of a nonmaterial software program for a chatbot that interacts with an individual through natural language and provides different types of recommendations through a digital interface. Further, algorithmic output can be displayed through material objects such as robots or computer screens. Hence, algorithms can have different material and nonmaterial properties, such as interfaces and contextual embedding, which influence the interaction with human actors. Layer 1: Algorithms as Computational Procedures

Studies that consider algorithms as computational procedures have theorized individuals’ processes of building their beliefs and assumptions about how algorithms work, comparing them to human decision-making procedures. Individuals can evaluate either the computational procedure (i.e., a statistical model or a machine learning method), the input data (e.g., whether training data is valid), or the output/result (e.g., forecasts, decision recommendations, texts, and images) generated by the algorithm (Christin, 2017).

Individuals differ in their beliefs about how humans and algorithms make decisions. They associate the human problemsolving approach with intuition, which is based on learned experience, and they perceive this approach as one enabling accurate, personalized, and innovative decisions (Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020; Dietvorst et al., 2015; Longoni et al., 2019). In contrast, the algorithmic decision-making process is perceived to be predefined, standardized, and based on statistical patterns (Longoni et al., 2019). Therefore, individuals anticipate that algorithms are neither able to account for unexpected events and unique situations nor for qualitative, unstandardized information (Grove & Meehl, 1996; Longoni et al., 2019). However, at the same time, before becoming familiar with algorithms, many individuals expect algorithms to perform faultlessly, i.e., to follow a perfect automation schema (Dzindolet et al., 2002) or to operate objectively and neutrally (Sundar, 2008). Thus, individuals hold conflicting beliefs about algorithms. Often, these beliefs are not based on knowledge (Kim & Duhachek, 2020) but on an “algorithmic imaginary” (Bucher, 2017, p. 30), which are “ways of thinking about what algorithms are, what they should be and how they function” since algorithms are not directly visible to individuals (see also Anthony et al., 2023).

## Layers 2a and 2b: Algorithms Embedded in Nonmaterial or Material Objects

The second perspective on algorithms considers algorithms in their materiality, which cannot be distinguished from the task, the actors, and the specific context in which the algorithms operate. Following the theory of digital objects (Faulkner & Runde, 2019), this perspective can be described in terms of different layers. Algorithms as computational procedures are embedded in different software programs (nonmaterial objects) or have different material characteristics (as material objects) in a specific context: they can, for example, be sales agents (Adam et al., 2023), chatbots (Castelo et al., 2019), medical decision aids (Bankuoru Egala & Liang, 2023; Gaube et al., 2021), or robots (Kim & Duhachek, 2020), each with a different user interface, and operate in different contexts, such as healthcare (Longoni et al., 2019), estimation tasks (Logg et al., 2019), or team decisions (Dennis et al., 2023). Therefore, different material and nonmaterial entities can carry the same output of an algorithmic procedure but cause different individual responses.

This perspective yields additional theoretical implications for algorithm aversion research, going beyond the perspective of algorithms as computational procedures. First, the specific algorithm cannot be isolated from its context (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Faulkner & Runde, 2019). For example, an algorithm can be embedded in a digital agent with different levels of agency regarding humans, from reacting only to a specific input (reflexive agents) to performing systematic tasks autonomously (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). Algorithms can be implemented to execute decisions autonomously or be limited to only offering support to humans (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006).

A second theoretical implication refers to individuals’ responses to algorithms being influenced by how the algorithms interact with them. The computer-as-a-socialactor (CASA) paradigm (Nass et al., 1994) suggests that individuals interact with computers similarly to how they interact with humans, modeling their interaction with computers on salient and representative human behaviors. We transfer this to how individuals respond to algorithms. Hence, the more social cues an algorithm includes, the more that individuals change their preferences, as the algorithm prompts responses similar to those typically used for humans. Additionally, this research area considers possible negative effects of computers’ human likeness, as in the “uncanny valley” effect, according to which a nonhuman entity that appears almost human elicits feelings of eeriness (Mori, 1970). Theories on anthropomorphism specify the circumstances under which individuals recognize human characteristics, such as a human mind in IT artifacts (Seeger et al., 2021), and therefore explain additional favorable or unfavorable responses to algorithms depending on their embedding in nonmaterial and material entities. We discuss these layers’ two facets (nonmaterial versus material) more elaborately in the implications for future research section.

## Conceptualizing the Preference for or against Algorithms

We distinguish four decision configurations of how preferences for or against algorithms are empirically measured. A prototypical study on algorithm aversion often uses advice-taking as a decision task and assesses how individuals respond to human advice (illustrated as a medical doctor’s decision in Figure 1) versus algorithmic advice (illustrated as a computer’s decision in Figure 1). These studies are primarily experiments that claim causality between exposure to an algorithm and individuals’ negative responses to it while controlling for confounds. In these experiments, humans and algorithms often provide identical decision content. However, these decisions vary in, for example, type (forecasts, recommendations, advice, predictions), quality, accuracy, and timing. Thus, algorithm aversion is measured through the difference in responses to the two agents, considering emotional, cognitive, intentional, or behavioral measures (aversion measure).

Figure 1 displays a prototypical aversion finding showing that participants in Condition 1 react favorably to a human medical doctor, while participants in Condition 2 respond negatively to a computer that, in decision terms, provides the same diagnostic medical advice. By keeping the advice identical, this prototypical study can demonstrate that it is not the content of the advice that drives individuals responses; instead, it is their perception of algorithms, seen either as computational procedures or as embedded in material or nonmaterial objects. Appreciation would mean the opposite, i.e., a positive response to the computer and a negative response to the human agent. To classify empirical approaches, we need to differentiate two kinds of human actors: (1) Individuals (I) who display algorithm aversion or appreciation by evaluating the decisions generated by different agents; also, individuals who can develop decisions on their own and arrive at different judgments. (2) Human agents (H) who can make decisions or provide advice that individuals evaluate and who may also have different properties, such as varying levels of expertise. Figures 2a-d illustrate four resulting decision configurations, which we discuss in detail later.

## Approach 0: Algorithms without Comparison to Human Assessment (Figure 2a)

An empirical study can measure general beliefs and behaviors toward algorithms without necessarily including a human agent as a basis of comparison. It can identify negative general attitudes toward algorithms (“I do not like algorithms”). For example, Bucher’s (2017) study investigated individuals’ general beliefs about algorithms and indicated that individuals make broad general assumptions about how algorithms work. Similarly, Egala and Liang (2023) studied clinicians’ algorithm aversion by asking participating clinicians to evaluate different attributes of mobile clinical decision support systems. Findings delivered by this approach reveal the role of general mechanisms in influencing individuals’ responses to algorithms, i.e., their propensity for aversion. However, as this approach lacks a human comparison, its findings generally do not offer much insight into what drives the preference for a human alternative.

## Approach 1: Comparison of Decisions (Figure 2b)

The first approach can measure algorithm aversion as a reluctance to follow suggestions based on algorithms. It considers the individual decision makers’ own judgments as the baseline for measuring the preference for humans over algorithms. Such studies will conclude that algorithm aversion (or appreciation) prevails if individuals do not (or do) take the algorithmic advice (i.e., D<sub>A</sub>) and follow their own (or the algorithm’s) initial decision (i.e., D<sub>I</sub>) instead. These studies focus on whether or not people use algorithms under different circumstances. For example, Dietvorst et al. (2015) measured how experiencing errors differently affected users’ confidence in algorithms and their own judgments. In another example, Kawaguchi (2021) examined whether sales representatives follow their own judgment versus advice produced by an algorithm (Condition 1) in contrast to an algorithm based on human workers’ input (Condition 2), which, therefore, includes human input in developing the algorithm. Overall, studies following this approach can be linked to prior work on trust and adoption of recommender systems (Wang & Benbasat, 2005) since they conceptualize individuals’ adoption as following algorithmic suggestions.

## Approach 2: Comparison of Agents (Figure 2c)

Studies following the second approach consider how individuals’ perceptions differ with respect to algorithms versus human agents. In these studies, the algorithm or human agent arrives at a specific decision (i.e., D<sub>A</sub> and D<sub>H</sub>), such as whether to hire a specific person (for a review, see Starke et al., 2022). These studies frequently present varying decisions regarding positive/negative outcomes and varying descriptions of the human agent, e.g., as an expert or a nonexpert. Thus, studies that use this approach regularly measure individuals’ beliefs regarding algorithms, contrasting them to beliefs about human agents, often where individuals are limitedly familiar with algorithmic processes. For example, Rebitschek et al. (2021) investigated laypeople’s error estimations and willingness to accept algorithmic errors. The authors offered participants two different descriptions in a between-subject design by describing the algorithm:

Employers in Germany obtain information about applicants from various sources to make a preliminary selection. There are computer programs that use certain criteria such as grades or work experience to assess whether an applicant is suitable and will be considered (Rebitschek et al., 2021, supplementary materials, p. 4, our emphasis).

In contrast, in the human expert condition, they provided the following description:

Employers in Germany obtain information about applicants from various sources to make a preliminary selection. An expert, e.g., a personnel manager, uses certain criteria such as grades or work experience to assess whether an applicant is suitable and will be considered (Rebitschek et al., 2021, supplementary materials, p. 4, our emphasis).

In this scenario, individuals perceived algorithms as making more false positive errors but fewer false negative errors than human experts.

Further, this approach allows the assessment of individuals’ responses in comparing various configurations and roles of algorithms and humans. For example, Adam et al. (2023) demonstrated that customers’ beliefs and final decisions differed depending on whether they had interacted with a human sales agent, an automated sales agent, or a combination of the two. Luo et al. (2020) compared aversion to sales coaches (human versus AI) across levels of individuals’ expertise. In another example, Dennis et al. (2023) compared individuals evaluation of AI systems as team members across different performance configurations. Overall, studies that follow this approach compare various configurations of human agents and algorithms with heterogeneous dependent variables. Yet the main focus is on comparing individuals’ responses to different agents, with secondary attention paid to their own decisions.

## Approach 3: Comparison of Agents and Decisions (Figure 2d)

Studies using Approach 3 investigate augmented decisionmaking, often following the judge-advisor framework (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006) in which individuals make an initial estimate (i.e., D<sub>I</sub>), then receive advice from an algorithm or a human agent (i.e., D<sub>A</sub> or D<sub>H</sub>), after which they can revise their estimate (i.e., D<sub>I</sub>). Hence, these studies account for two human comparisons, i.e., individuals’ own judgments (i.e., D<sub>I</sub>) and their perceptions of a human advisor in contrast to an algorithmic advisor.

To illustrate this approach, Logg et al. (2019) asked participants to estimate the weight of people shown in photographs. Next, they provided a suggestion developed by an algorithm calculating other participants’ prior responses or a human advisor’s responses. Participants then made their final judgment, based on which the researchers assessed the relative adjustment participants made regarding each advisor (using the weight on advice measure). They found that individuals follow the algorithm more than other people’s advice. In a more recent example, You et al. (2022) applied this decision configuration to measure how the weight on advice changes depending on information about the algorithmic performance. These studies measure how beliefs about human agents and algorithms are calibrated after repeated interactions. Again, some studies have measured individuals’ responses to different configurations of human agents and algorithms. For example, Prahl and van Swol (2021) assessed how much advice was taken from forecasting provided by either a human or an algorithm. After 10 trials of interacting with a particular advisor, the advisor was replaced by either a human or an algorithmic agent, allowing researchers to assess the unique effect of humans being replaced by algorithms across two task characteristics. Changing advisors had no significant effect on advice utilization. However, if algorithms replaced humans, individuals experienced more negative emotions and faulted the advisor for mistakes more frequently than they did in the other configurations.

Overall, studies in this category allow measuring individuals responses to two (or multiple) different human and algorithmic agents in direct comparison to how much the various agents account for their judgments. This enables us to study interactions between an individual’s own judgment, the type of output provided, and the characteristics of the human and algorithmic agents.

## Development of a Classification Scheme

Understanding algorithm aversion in decision-making as the preference for humans over algorithms enables us to classify research on algorithm aversion along two dimensions (see Table 2), namely the applied understanding of algorithms and the decision configuration (Figures 2b-d) as a comparison with human agents. We demonstrate the utility of this classification scheme by giving an example in the form of a hypothetical algorithm aversion study intended to measure patients’ reliance on a machine learning-based chatbot that recommends a specific treatment. The study compares individuals’ reliance on the chatbot to their reliance on a human doctor recommending the same treatment.

First, we can categorize this study according to the layer of algorithmic materiality. Suppose the study is interested in the algorithm as a computational procedure (Layer 1). In that case, it should theorize why and how individuals respond to machine learning algorithms and which properties of this specific computational procedure influence their responses. Focusing on this layer requires researchers to consider specific moderators, such as the type of computational model, its accuracy, or individuals’ literacy, as we elaborate below. However, this study can also consider the nonmaterial embedding of the algorithm (Layer 2) and investigate why and how individuals respond to a specific chatbot compared to a human doctor. To focus on Layer 2, the study needs to consider the chatbot’s specificities, such as its degree of human likeness and way of interacting (Seeger et al., 2021) or its similarity to the user (Al-Natour et al., 2011). Further, this study could consider the implications of the medical context as a moderator of the effect. Also, focusing on both layers, this study could try to understand whether individuals’ negative responses result from a specific manifestation of the algorithm (medical chatbot) or a general negative response to computational procedures.

Second, we can categorize our study based on the measurement approach. As this study compares how individuals respond to a human medical doctor versus an algorithm, it can be classified as Approach 2 (Figure 2c, Comparison of Agents). As we demonstrate below, this type of study is sensitive to the human agent’s properties, i.e., to human expertise.

## Conceptualizing the Effect of Moderators and Underlying Assumptions

Focusing on a layer of algorithmic materiality and choosing a specific decision configuration has three implications for the underlying understanding of the construct algorithm aversion/appreciation (see Table 3). First, it affects whether algorithm aversion/appreciation is understood as directed towards a specific algorithm in a specific social context or as a response to algorithmic problem-solving per se. Second, it influences which decision factors a study needs to consider as the complexity of decision configurations increases. Lastly, studies use different operationalizations of algorithm aversion across approaches and layers. ## Increasing Algorithmic Specificity through Layers of Algorithmic Materiality

As researchers focus on different layers of algorithms, they make different assumptions on algorithm aversion/appreciation and need to consider different moderators. Specifically, by considering higher layers of algorithms (Layer 2a and Layer 2b), algorithm aversion becomes a better-defined construct because the study examines aversion to / appreciation of an algorithm in a specific embedding (e.g., a medical chatbot). This has several implications for moderators of construct algorithm aversion.

On the one hand, studies considering algorithms as a computational procedure focus on individuals’ assumptions about algorithmic problem-solving approaches and compare them to individuals’ general assumptions about human problem-solving approaches. Further, these studies may consider the implications of general characteristics, such as whether the reasoning underpinning an algorithm is opaque due to machine learning models (Kawaguchi, 2021) or the implications of the underlying training data’s characteristics. For example, a study that focuses on this layer can consider whether individuals’ responses differ based on the type of computational procedure an algorithm applies, e.g., a regression versus neural network, the capabilities of each system (Baird & Maruping, 2021), and how the results are influenced by human knowledge of algorithmic problem solving (Kim & Duhachek, 2020).

On the other hand, studies focusing on the second layer of algorithms need to account for additional moderators. Such moderators include the algorithm’s framing, the algorithm's embedding in a specific decision context, or the user interface, all of which refer to a specific material and nonmaterial embedding of the algorithm.

First, the task and the social context shape the algorithmic approach’s appearance regarding, for example, its capability of addressing the problem (Castelo et al., 2019) and its degree of agency in decision-making (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). Second, individuals respond differently if the algorithm is embedded in a system with a more humanlike interface that is more interactive (Castelo et al., 2019). Specific cues on the user interface trigger even more social behaviors toward algorithms. One such cue could be the similarity to the user (Al-Natour et al., 2011), resulting in the tendency to perceive software agents as more humanlike and to evaluate them more favorably because of that (Qiu & Benbasat, 2009; Seeger et al., 2021). Lastly, individuals beliefs can be influenced by how the algorithm is introduced in an experimental study (framing). For example, Hou and Jung (2021) demonstrate that individuals respond differently depending on whether the algorithm is introduced as “AI,” as an “algorithm,” or as a “computer.” The description alone can invert the findings from aversion to appreciation because individuals are more inclined to follow advice from AI systems than from algorithms due to their expectations about AI system capabilities. Additionally, without changing the underlying computational procedure, aversion can be reduced if algorithms are described as learning (Kim & Duhachek, 2020) or considering individuals’ uniqueness (Longoni et al., 2019).

Further, the material manifestation of algorithms can influence individuals’ responses to them. For example, algorithms can be embedded in material objects, such as cars or robots, with which individuals interact in the material world. Recent reviews on trust in AI (Glikson & Woolley,

2020) and user interaction with conversational agents (Diederich et al., 2022) suggest that the embodiment of an algorithm in the form of a physical robot compared to a virtual agent affects individuals’ responses, and thus also their preference for or against algorithms. Also, the agent’s physical presence increases positive attitudes, especially if individuals can interact with robots in face-to-face settings (Li, 2015) because these settings allow additional nonverbal interaction. Also, the physical form of the robot influences whether individuals are more or less willing to accept robots as coworkers (You & Robert, 2024). According to the CASA paradigm, all these features can reduce the differences between humans and algorithms. Thus, elements of the material presence of algorithms can be central moderators of algorithm aversion effects.

There are different relationships between Layer 1 and Layer 2 that researchers on algorithm aversion need to consider. First, since algorithms as computational procedures are included in considering algorithmic material embedding, individuals’ responses to specific software programs can be influenced by their general beliefs about algorithms as computational procedures. In particular, human beliefs about algorithms’ general capabilities can affect whether they perceive a specific software program as applicable to a specific task. Second, the relationship may be additive, in that theoretical mechanisms of algorithmic material embedding increase the effects of mechanisms in algorithms as computational procedures. For example, individuals can have distinct negative beliefs about algorithms as computational procedures, which result in them experiencing aversion. These negative beliefs might increase if the algorithm is embedded in a social context that values human intelligence highly, such as in healthcare. Thus, the effect of negative beliefs, i.e., aversion, is amplified. Third, considering the cognitive preferences of individuals toward algorithms as computational procedures versus their material embedding may be conflicting. For example, suppose individuals believe that algorithms perform tasks in a standardized manner (following a computational procedure). In that case, such beliefs can result in a favorable attitude regarding algorithm use in tasks requiring these skills, such as analyzing a large amount of data. However, if this algorithm interacts in a highly humanlike way (nonmaterial embedding), it could negatively influence individuals’ beliefs about the algorithms’ competence for a specific task.

## Considering the Effects of Increasing Complexity of the Decision Configurations

By choosing a specific decision configuration, the complexity of underlying considerations increases. First, studies that focus on comparing responses to an individual’s own decisions (Approach 1, Figure 2b, and Approach 3, Figure 2d) need to consider the cognitive processes of individuals evaluating their own decisions against algorithmic suggestions. Specifically, these studies need to account for the relationship between individuals’ decisions and the alternative provided by the algorithm, i.e., whether the decisions are confirming or conflicting (Jussupow et al., 2021). They should additionally account for the development of confidence individuals have in their judgments (Fügener et al., 2022) or for individuals’ general preference for their own judgment. Individuals who prefer their own judgment tend to discard any advice regardless of whether the source is human or algorithmic—known as egocentric advice discounting (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006). Hence, if only an individual’s own judgment is used as the baseline in measuring algorithm aversion (Approach 1, Figure 2b), it is necessary to distinguish between the algorithm’s unique impact and the egocentric advice discounting effect (Logg et al., 2019) and to determine individuals’ assessment of their confidence (Fügener et al., 2022). These cognitive processes might blur the effect of individuals’ unique responses to algorithms.

Second, in approaches that include a human agent as a basis of comparison, (Approach 2, Figure 2c and Approach 3, Figure 2d), the study needs to theorize why humans responses to algorithms and human agents differ. Such studies are sensitive to the type of human comparison agent selected for a specific empirical investigation and to the relationship between the human agent and the individual. For example, aversion is more profound if an algorithm is compared to a human expert, such as a medical doctor, versus a human nonexpert, such as other participants in an experiment (Hou & Jung, 2021). However, these findings might also change depending on an individual’s relationship with the human agents. For example, an individual who has had a bad experience with a doctor might prefer algorithms to humans. In addition, individuals’ appraisal processes in evaluating human agents’ expertise are complex (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006). All these processes can affect the results if a study chooses to compare responses to algorithms with responses to human agents.

Third, studies across decision configurations differ in their temporal perspective on algorithm aversion. Similarly to trust trajectories (Glikson & Woolley, 2020), individuals preferences for humans over algorithms (algorithm aversion) or algorithms over humans (algorithm appreciation) can change over time. Figure 3 illustrates an archetypical temporal pathway from initial preferences to calibrated preferences and depicts how approaches differ in their temporal focus. As preferences can develop in a temporal dimension, we exemplify two prominent but conflicting avenues.

On the one hand, multiple studies suggest an inverted pathway from an initial state of algorithm appreciation through taking advice from algorithms, experiencing algorithmic errors, and then turning the appreciation into algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015). However, it remains unclear whether algorithm aversion stabilizes (calibrated preferences) or whether it can be reduced or even reverted to algorithmic appreciation after sufficient experience with algorithmic errors. For example, despite perceiving errors, we might still prefer Google Maps’ advice over a colleague’s due to our extensive experience of using it. On the other hand, multiple studies point toward initial aversion, i.e., perceiving algorithms as less credible than humans, which then manifests in taking less advice from the algorithm (Castelo et al., 2019; Hou & Jung, 2021), which signals a direct temporal relationship between initial aversion and calibrated aversion. Often, studies differ in their temporal focus based on their choice of a specific decision configuration. Specifically, studies following Approach 1 (Figure 2b) focus primarily on algorithm aversion in the first few decisions, studies in Approach 2 (Figure 2c) focus primarily on algorithm aversion before interaction with algorithms, and studies in Approach 3 (Figure 2d) focus primarily on calibrated preferences.

Further, studies can differ in the role of individuals they assume. Studies comparing decisions often focus on the role of individuals as algorithm users. In contrast, many studies that compare how individuals respond to human agents do not have individuals formulating their own decisions; instead, they are affected by the decisions humans or algorithms make, thus exhibiting second-order preferences (e.g., Bauer & Gill, 2023; Langer & Landers, 2021). For example, individuals who have experienced humans’ discriminating behaviors expect algorithms to be more neutral and fairer—or at least less biased—than other humans (Pethig & Kroenung, 2023).

In sum, as research considers more complex configurations of individuals’ evaluations of their own judgments as well as human and algorithmic agents, the underlying mechanisms become more complex. For example, as studies consider human-algorithm hybrids and configurations of multiple humans and multiple algorithmic agents, they have to account for the theoretical implications of multiple sources, such as the consistency between sources (Xu et al., 2020). Also, they have to consider additional characteristics of decision-making interactions, such as timing, when individuals can decide between their own judgments and advice from human agents or algorithms.

## Difficulty in Comparing Measurements

Lastly, researchers must be careful when comparing results across layers of algorithmic materiality and decision configurations because studies differ in the measured dependent variables for two reasons.

First, studies adjust their measurement to suit what they want to assess. As outlined in the example about the medical AI presented in the Development of a Classification Scheme section, the research focus differs depending on whether researchers are interested in how individuals generally respond to algorithms versus a specific algorithm. For a specific algorithm, the measurement is often adjusted to the given context, which makes it difficult to compare measurements across layers of algorithmic materiality.

Second, decision configurations strongly affect the role of individuals and what the human alternative is. Studies differ in their measurement of preferences depending on the decision configuration users choose. For example, in comparing users’ own judgment with responses to human or algorithmic agents, studies mostly use the weight of advice measure that allows a (behavioral) quantification of how strongly individuals adjust toward specific advice that a specific agent provided or how much they stick with their own judgment (e.g., Logg et al. 2019). Studies that compare responses to different agents (Approach 2), however, mostly use different attitudinal and intentional measures, such as perceived competence or intention to rely on advice, as dependent variables. While attitudes and behaviors correlate strongly regarding preferences for or against algorithms, the decision to take specific advice can be driven by additional factors like the perceived quality of the advice.

In sum, research comparing results across the classification scheme should be done carefully because the studies differ not only in their underlying understanding of the construct but may also differ in their measurement of algorithm aversion.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Classifying studies on algorithm aversion in decision-making based on how they measure the preference for algorithms over humans in decision-making enables researchers (1) to address conflicting empirical findings if individuals are either averse to or appreciative of algorithms and (2) to theorize more specifically about why individuals are averse to algorithms.

Regarding the first point, we disentangle different understandings of the preference for or against algorithms. This will allow future research to formulate more specific hypotheses on the prevalence of algorithm aversion or appreciation by considering the isolated and combined effects of measurement dimensions and decision configurations. For example, considering the construct dimension, future research could determine whether individuals’ algorithm aversion only manifests in their perceptions and attitudes but not in their behavior. Considering the implications of specificity, i.e., the layer of algorithmic materiality allows researchers to theorize how the specific material embedding of the algorithm influences the directions of preferences and reveals conflicting mechanisms. For example, Layer 2 (the nonmaterial embedding) can easily confound effects from Layer 1 (the algorithm as a computational procedure). Individuals might be generally averse to rather than appreciative of algorithms. However, this aversion might not be visible in a specific research design due to the compensatory effect of the nonmaterial embedding, e.g., because the algorithm has an avatar (Layer 2). Hence, questions arise about whether individuals are generally averse to algorithms (or algorithmic errors) or whether their aversion targets a particular software program that operates in a given social context. Thus, aversion could be a generalized preference against algorithms per se or directed toward a specific experience with a specific technological artifact. Considering the effect of multiple measurement dimensions can help us develop further research questions across different decision configurations of human-AI collaboration. For example, connecting algorithmic specificity to an individual’s role can result in research questions asking whether individuals are averse to specific manifestations of algorithms depending on the power dynamics between algorithms and individuals. Since algorithms are increasingly being used to manage individuals (Kellogg et al., 2020), as with Uber’s algorithm managing individual drivers, the degree of algorithm aversion individuals develop could be influenced by how much power an algorithm has over individuals.

Second, we connect the accelerating research on algorithm aversion to other research streams. On the one hand, we relate algorithm aversion to related constructs. Studies that follow Approach 1 (Figure 2b) should consider prior research on the use of and trust in advice-giving systems (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Wang & Benbasat, 2005, 2008) because they understand algorithm aversion as the reluctance to use algorithms. These research streams have investigated various human, technological, interactional, and contextual factors in determining how humans accept suggestions developed by different systems in their decision-making. Therefore, a common criticism is that by applying Approach 1, the construct of algorithm aversion is anything but novel. However, we argue that by empirically comparing human agents to algorithmic agents in decision-making in the other decision configurations, we develop our understanding of unique theoretical mechanisms beyond these literature streams. These studies can develop new theoretical explanations of how individuals’ cognitive evaluations differ if confronted with multiple advisors versus only one advisor (Xu et al., 2020). From this perspective, trust can be considered as an operationalization of preferences measuring a specific dimension (e.g., cognitive preferences). However, preferences for or against algorithms are more complex than trust alone since the underlying decision configuration, the materiality of algorithms, and the resulting measurement dimensions need to be considered. Therefore, we propose that algorithm aversion/appreciation is related to but not fully identical to trust. On the other hand, our conceptualization of algorithm aversion helps to integrate other literature streams that apply decision configurations in ways similar to Approach 2 (Figure 2c) but do not label their research as algorithm aversion. Among these are studies in behavioral economics (for reviews, see March, 2019; Teubner et al., 2015) that offer additional examples of how individuals responses to algorithms versus humans differ. This could be a promising avenue for future research to gain better insight into how algorithms influence individuals’ behaviors.

Further, our study enables future research to theorize on contingency factors for algorithm aversion. The choice of a decision configuration, a layer of algorithmic materiality, and different measurement dimensions influence which moderators need to be considered (see Table 4). For example, if a study focuses on comparing decisions (Approach 1, Figure 2b; Approach 3, Figure 2d), it needs to consider a different set of moderators than studies that mainly focus on how individuals respond to different agents (Approach 2, Figure 2c; Approach 3, Figure 2d). Among other things, these studies need to consider the human agent’s characteristics, such as its expertise (Jussupow et al., 2020), in more detail. Further, considering algorithms in their materiality results in additional moderators of the nonmaterial and material embedding.

Lastly, our classification scheme can help to advance future research on human collaboration with generative AI (GenAI), such as large language models. With recent developments in GenAI, algorithms are not only being used to provide suggestions but also to generate new content. Multiple publications have applied the reasoning and empirical designs developed in algorithm aversion research to study how humans cognitively evaluate content developed by generative models (e.g., Köbis & Mossink, 2021). These studies often find that AI-generated content is evaluated less favorably than human-generated content (Longoni et al., 2022). Again, we observe a preference for humans over algorithms. Our reasoning on algorithm aversion in decision-making can thus be transferred to human interaction with generative AI. This results in various research questions, such as: Is the preference for human content a general tendency or specific to content developed by a specific GenAI? How do human-AI configurations influence these preferences? How do these preferences change from a temporal perspective? Do errors of GenAI have the same negative effect as errors of algorithms in decision-making? Considering such questions, we are confident that our work can stimulate future research on algorithm aversion/appreciation in different contexts.

## About the Authors

Ekaterina Jussupow is an assistant professor in information systems at the Department of Law and Economics at the Technical University of Darmstadt. She was awarded the Best Paper Runner-Up Award for research on AI-augmented medical decision-making in Information Systems Research in 2021. With her background in psychology, she focuses on the influence of AI systems on decision-making processes. Her ORCiD is: 0000-0002-3009-076X

Izak Benbasat (Ph.D. University of Minnesota, 1974; Doctorat Honoris Causa, Université de Montréal, 2009) is Professor Emeritus at the Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. He was formerly CANFOR Professor of Information Systems (1990-2000), and Canada Research Chair in Information Technology Management (2001-2014). He is a member of the Order of Canada, and a Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada. He received the LEO Award for lifetime exceptional achievements in IS from the Association for Information Systems and is a Distinguished Fellow of the INFORMS Information Systems Society. He served as the editor-in-chief of Information Systems Research, editor of the IS&DSS Department of Management Science, and senior editor of MIS Quarterly. He was a Marvin Bower Fellow at the Harvard Business School in 1985-86, Hooker Distinguished Visiting Professor at McMaster University in 1996 and 2003, Shaw Visiting Professor at the National University of Singapore in 2000, and the Otto Monsted Guest Professor at the Copenhagen Business School in 2015. His ORCiD is: 0000-0002- 9731-3403

Armin Heinzl is a full professor and chair at the University of Mannheim, Germany. His research and teaching interests include digital innovation, healthcare IT, and platform ecosystems. His articles have been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, IEEE Transactions on Artificial Intelligence, and other outlets. He was awarded the Information Systems Research Best Paper Award in 2018. Further information can be found here: https://www.bwl.unimannheim.de/en/heinzl/. His ORCiD is: 0000-0001-9886-9596
