## Abstract

The development and use of knowledge-based (expert) systems has grown dramatically across a broad range of industries. Yet despite its growing importance, the study of expert systems lacks a cohesive framework for differentiating and comparing expert systems initiatives across different applications and in different industrial settings. The problem for IS managers is that a system that works in one situation may not be appropriate for another. This article presents a classification methodology for the systematic evaluation of a broad range of expert systems. Of primary concern in this study is the measurement of the complexity of such systems. Complexity in the area of expert systems consists of two basic dimensions. The first dimension is the complexity of the underlying knowledge residing with the key experts. The second dimension of the framework focuses on the complexity of the technology incorporated into a given system. This framework is then applied to a sample of 50 successfully developed knowledge-based systems. The results can be used as a foundation for generating research hypotheses for development time, budget, staffing, organizational control, and organizational participation.

Keywords: Expert systems, MIS management, technical staffing, project planning, corporate strategy

## Introduction

Knowledge-based (expert) systems are generally understood to be software applications that incorporate substantial amounts of human reasoning for problem solving and decision-making assistance. The development and successful implementation of such systems across a broad range of industries has been described in a number of journals and books (Feigenbaum, et al., 1988; Leonard-Barton and Sviokla, 1988; Sviokla, 1990; Turban and Watkins, 1986). Thus, it is clear that managing the development and use of expert systems technology is of growing importance as an increasing number of organizations seek to apply the technology to their own operations.

Despite the growing use of expert systems, the literature, as noted by Sviokla (1990), lacks a set of unifying research themes that can be applied across the increasing database of “system biographies” to guide managers in pioneering development efforts or in assessing the impact of expert systems on organizations. This article presents a classification methodology for evaluating the complexity of expert systems; it serves as a basis for examining and comparing the approaches used to manage the development of different types of applications. In addition, this article describes the results of applying this framework to a sample of 50 expert systems. $^{1}$ The results of the data analysis are then used to generate hypotheses regarding expert systems development and policy issues for management.

# A Methodology for Classifying Expert System Projects

A conceptual framework for classifying expert systems based on knowledge and technology complexity was developed by Meyer and Curley (1991) and is used as the foundation for this article. The objective of the classification framework is to create an effective metric that captures the key contingencies that affect the development process. The process of grouping or “classifying” expert systems assesses the two most basic and fundamentally important dimensions of an expert system: its embodied knowledge complexity and its technological complexity. Knowledge complexity is defined as the degree of depth and specialization of the internalized knowledge of human experts, the scope of the decision-making process (Clancy, 1985), and the level of expertise required required (Dreyful, 1986), including discipline-based knowledge, that is incorporated into the expert system application. Technological complexity, on the other hand, is defined as the depth and scope of the programming effort, the user environment (Prerau, 1985), and related technical efforts involved in building such systems and in implementing them in production environments.

Both aspects of complexity are critically important for understanding how to manage the development of knowledge-based systems. Support in the literature for focusing on these two types of complexity may be found in Davis (1984), Gorry and Scott Morton (1971), and Newell and Simon (1972). The notion of actually classifying expert systems by looking at characteristics of embodied decision making was implemented first by Clancy (1985), who used “heuristic” or decision-making style as a classification approach on a sample of early expert systems. Knowledge complexity impacts the choice of “experts,” the duration and intensity of their participation, and the systems designers selected to work with them. One will find that expert systems range from those covering a single, narrow domain, with comparatively little specialization in embodied expertise, to those that incorporate multiple domains, some of which are highly specialized and therefore represent “deep” knowledge and involve substantial levels of information uncertainty for many information inputs.

The specific use of technological complexity has precedent in prior technology management research (Meyer and Roberts, 1989; Roberts and Berry, 1985). Greater technological complexity, which in many instances entails greater technological uncertainty and the need for experimentation, has been shown to be associated with higher levels of both project risk and different R&D approaches for a variety of different technology-based businesses and products (Meyer and Roberts, 1989). As will be described, our concept of a technology platform goes beyond examining specific hardware and software to include the overall technology environment encompassing non-inferencing technologies and systems integration. In summary, the two critical dimensions of this framework that directly affect project scale and development strategies are knowledge and technology requirements.

## The classification framework

Conceptually, the combination of the knowledge and technological dimensions produces four generic types of expert system applications. The groupings are based on the four conceptual combinations of “high” and “low” pairings of embodied complexity in the two dimensions, as illustrated in Figure 1 (Meyer and Curley, 1991). Examples of the different types of systems, drawn from our field sample (to be more fully described later) are described below to more clearly illustrate the characteristics of each type of system.

## Quadrant I: Low Knowledge/Low Technology

Containing the lowest levels of domain and technical complexity, these information systems can often be developed by end users with basic software tools training. Such systems encompass single domains of knowledge and contain what may be viewed as relatively common or available knowledge. They are also relatively “simple” in value-added computer technology, i.e., what the expert system developer does by way of programming. Within the classification framework, this means that Low Knowledge/Low Technology systems tend to be stand-alone PC applications without significant database or communications requirements.

An example of this type of expert system is a PC-based application called TAX, developed by a major European airline. This system is used to monitor the travel of employees who have been stationed abroad for more than a year. Within the time span covered, the system identifies the periods for which no country income tax is incurred for employees returning home. As a matter of company policy, the airline had previously paid the tax expense for such employees and had used a manual monitoring system to identify when a tax “exempt” period would occur. The expert system now keeps track of employee trips and displays this information to assist in planning elective business and pleasure travel so as to reduce the tax liability. TAX incorporates only a tiny fraction of one knowledge domain, tax law, and runs on a non-networked personal computer. It was developed using a popular PC-based shell in less than three months and at a development cost of about \$15,000.

Quadrant II: High Knowledge/Low Technology

These systems incorporate “deep” knowledge, i.e., elaborate reasoning in specialized fields. In addition, these systems often span multiple complex domains and resolve uncertainty in the information inputs used by the expert system in its reasoning process to make decisions. While containing a high degree of knowledge intensity, these systems are nonetheless technologically uncomplex and tend to operate on single hardware platforms utilizing small databases.

An example of this type of system is the COAL ADVISOR, developed by a large American engineering service company. COAL assists buyers in making coal purchasing decisions for power plants. Over 100 characteristics of coal (such as burning, pulverization rate, environmental impact, emissions, etc.) are incorporated into this system. Three separate domains—coal analysis, boiler design and operation, and plant maintenance and cost—are embodied in this system. In addition, the multiple domains are covered in substantial depth by the expert system itself. Technically, COAL is a non-networked PC application that has a strong graphical user interface. The total development effort required to build this domain-intensive system was three years and cost over \$2 million.

## Quadrant III: Low Knowledge/High Technology

This group of expert systems shares the basic characteristics of having relatively simple knowledge bases but complex value-added technology. They require substantial development efforts in terms of programming, systems integration, and systems administration. These expert systems applications have characteristics similar to other large software development efforts. That is, they require formalized project management techniques and the careful inclusion of existing DP/MIS development and testing procedures.

An example of this type of system is EXTEL, a system developed jointly by a major European computer firm and a national telephone service. EXTEL assists customers in choosing the best phone service to meet their calling needs. It assesses the local and long distance calling patterns of customers, calculates rates based on various plan options and recommends an optimal service plan. The knowledge domain is fairly common and straightforward, requiring little expert interpretation. The technology complexity, however, is high because EXTEL is dispersed throughout hundreds of branch offices in Germany. This requires regular networking to access updated databases. Systems integration was a major effort in the development of EXTEL. The system has also been made operational on multiple hardware platforms. Total development time for EXTEL was three years and the cost was between \$1 and \$2 million.

## Quadrant IV: High Knowledge/High Technology

Complex in both knowledge and technology, this last group of systems is comprised of large-scale knowledge engineering and technology implementation projects. The embodied knowledge is both “broad” and “deep,” and the value-added technology is highly complex in areas such as knowledge base programming, database management, and systems integration. These systems typically address a core business process, gathering and synthesizing key information from across the organization.

An example of such a system is the Life Underwriting System (LUS). This is an underwriting system for life insurance applications. It was created by a leading American reinsurance company. The project has been a classic case of intrapraneurship within an established organization. Operational on both PC and mainframe environments, this system combines medical, underwriting, and actuarial knowledge in a highly complex system to screen and rate life insurance applications. New applications are submitted to the expert system, which screens out immediate “accepts” and “declines” based on medical, financial, and avocational risk factors. Then, the remaining cases are resolved through interactive consultation that draws on extensive medical knowledge bases. The system is also being integrated with mainframe administrative systems for the sharing of data and for the pricing of underwritten cases based on specific insurance products. Total development time required more than three years and cost more than \$5 million.

Another example of a Quadrant IV system is XCON, a system that has been described elsewhere (Bachant and McDermott, 1984; Leonard-Barton and Sviokla, 1988; Sviokla, 1990) and embodies high levels of both knowledge and technology complexity. More specifically, XCON addresses the product configuration process of a large computer manufacturer, incorporating information inputs from manufacturing, engineering, product marketing, and sales. The size of the company's product portfolio and the number of components used in the assembly of its products have made XCON a large-scale development effort that is considered by management to be a cornerstone in the firm's ability to deliver customized computer systems to every customer.

## Operationalizing the framework

To operationalize the conceptual framework of knowledge and technology complexity, we developed and tested a series of variables to measure each dimension of complexity. A pilot study using six completed expert systems in industry was first performed. This study was used to tighten the methodology. Then a new study of 50 fielded expert systems was conducted during an 18-month period between 1988 and 1990, the results of which are reported here. It is important to note that all of the expert systems used in this study have passed the prototype stage and are “production” systems. The sample was gathered by convenience; no comprehensive list of expert systems applications was readily available from which a sample could be drawn. In the following pages we define the specific variables chosen and the measurement techniques applied to operationalize the classification methodology.

## Defining knowledge complexity

The knowledge complexity dimension of the classification framework assesses the complexity of the underlying knowledge domains and the decision-making process, as well as the degree to which knowledge complexity is embodied in a specific expert system. Drawing on and synthesizing from previous work (noted under each variable) we structured our examination of knowledge by assessing the following variables:

## Breadth of Domain(s)

The breadth of the embodied domains is a measure of the number of specific distinct fields of expertise employed by the “experts” whose reasoning is incorporated into an expert system (Harmon, et al., 1988; Hart, 1986). By examining the domain content for a specific decision-making process with knowledge engineers and experts, the number of specific domains or distinct fields of expertise modelled into the system was identified. This approach is consistent with a basic architectural principal of expert systems design, articulated by Davis and others, that “the knowledge of how to use the rules must be separated from the rules themselves, dividing the expert system into an inference engine and a knowledge base" (Davis, 1984 p. 20). A critical dimension, then, in classifying expert systems is to identify how many separate knowledge bases or domains exist within a given system. For example, in TAX only one domain, that of tax law, is required for the application. In contrast, LUS, the system to underwrite life insurance applications, requires the separate fields of medical science, underwriting, actuarial science, and financial analysis. Scored as 1, 2, or 3, respectively, the following measures were employed:

## Depth of Domain(s)

The depth of embodied domains required by the decision-making process is assessed by combining the level of advanced educational training and duration of applied work experience required of the key experts. To assess the level or depth of specialization of the domain(s) embodied in the decision-making process, we examined both the education and work experience of the experts who contributed knowledge to the system. Much has been written about the characteristics of experts, their methods of acquiring skill, and their application of that skill to problem solving (Newell and Simon, 1972; Schank and Childers, 1984). Dreyfus and Dreyfus (1986) describe five stages of skill acquisition from novice to expert. Their model is based on field experiments with a variety of professionals, including doctors, nurses, and chess masters. Their research indicates that expertise requires both mastery of a body of context-free rules and theories as well as the know-how and intuitive ability gained by experience. To capture this, the authors relied on surrogate measures to indicate the depth of the domains incorporated into the decision-making process. A given process was assessed using these two factors of education and work experience by looking at the collective backgrounds of the domain experts and scoring domain depth as follows:

<table><tr><td>Depth</td><td>Description</td><td>Score</td></tr><tr><td>Common</td><td>To be an expert, the individual(s) did not need to have the knowledge gained through an advanced degree in the given domain(s) (i.e., PhD, M.D.), nor substantial applied work experience (10 years or more in the specific domain(s)).</td><td>1</td></tr><tr><td>Deep</td><td>To be an expert, the individual(s) required either (1) an advanced degree and less than 10 years work experience in the specific domain(s), or (2) more than 10 years&#x27; work experience but no advanced degree.</td><td>2</td></tr><tr><td>Deepest</td><td>To be an expert, the individual(s) required both an advanced degree and more than 10 years&#x27; work experience.</td><td>3</td></tr></table>

These three gradations were then scored as 1, 2, and 3, respectively. If more than one domain was identified for a given system, then domain depth was assessed for each of the domains, and the final score derived by taking the highest of the individual domain depth scores. In other words, the most intensive domain in terms of the experts' respective backgrounds was employed for the valuation of this factor.

## Rate of Change of Domain(s)

This measures the extent to which experts become obsolete and thus ineffective in their problem-solving capabilities without ongoing domain-based education and/or research. This variable is an assessment of the degree to which the underlying disciplines or fields of expertise were changing through advancements in theory or application. Some domains are relatively fixed, others changing only occasionally, while other domains are in a continuous state of advancement. High rates of domain change increase knowledge complexity for a given expert system. Partridge (1987) suggests that knowledge updating requirements can be seen as either static or dynamic. We extended this scale to three levels, scored as 1, 2, and 3 respectively:

<table><tr><td>Rate</td><td>Description</td><td>Score</td></tr><tr><td>Low</td><td>Someone who was an expert 5 years ago who added no new knowledge could still be an expert today.</td><td>1</td></tr><tr><td>Moderate</td><td>An expert of 2 years ago, and who added no new knowledge, could still be an expert today.</td><td>2</td></tr><tr><td>High</td><td>Remaining an expert requires updating of knowledge on a continuous (i.e., yearly) basis.</td><td>3</td></tr></table>

In a decision-making process where more than one domain was identified by the domain experts, the highest score for the collective set of domains was used in the calculations.

## Domain Penetration

The degree of domain coverage by the expert system is a measure of the completeness of computerization for each of the specific domains embodied in a given expert system. In other words, a domain expert may be a world authority in his or her given field, but a system in which he or she participated may only capture a small portion of that domain expertise. For example, in a comparison of two life underwriting expert systems created by two different companies, Meyer, et al. (forthcoming) showed clear differences between the two systems in terms of domain penetration. One of the underwriting applications performed only simple screening of new life insurance applications, while the second was a comprehensive system that evaluated a wide range of medical, financial, and vocational problems. This measure therefore assesses the degree to which the expert system captures the full scope of its targeted domains. Higher levels of domain penetration increase the knowledge complexity of an expert system. The domain experts participating in a given system were asked to assess the degree of domain penetration for each of the embodied domains according to the following five point scale of domain penetration. Once again, if multiple domains were embodied in a given application, each domain was evaluated for this measure, and then the highest score was employed in subsequent calculations:

## Comprehensiveness of System Outputs

This measure examines the system's outputs to evaluate the extent to which an expert system solves its targeted problems or decision areas. Reflecting prior work in the field of decision theory (Newell and Simon, 1972, Shannon, 1948), we categorized specific outputs (i.e., problem resolution information displayed, printed, or in some other way communicated as in an electronic message) as being either:

\- Problem Diagnosis

\- Recommended Actions

\- Actual Solutions

\- Hypothesis Testing

For example, the output of a medical diagnosis system may suggest possible causes for a patient's symptoms but not suggest remedies. Another, more complete system might suggest prognoses as well as diagnoses. In examining a given expert system, we looked for the presence of each of these generic types of solution outputs. A system was evaluated for comprehensiveness depending on the presence of these generic categories of outputs in the visual and/or printed information outputs of the system, and scored as follows:

## Breadth of Information Inputs

Decision theorists (Shannon, 1948, for example) differentiate between the decision process itself in problem solving and the information used by experts in their decision processes. We therefore examined what may be called the "raw" information inputs, or entities, for a given expert system. An example of an information input might be a new life insurance application in the case of the LUS. Or, it might be information received from a hospital in the form of a medical examination. Such inputs can either be entered by the user or "downloaded" into the system by queries on external databases. In terms of evaluating this dimension, we asked respondents to identify specific information inputs as logical entities. If only one or two inputs were present, this factor was scored as a 1. TAX, for example, uses only two information inputs, employee data on past travel and planned future travel dates. Next, if three or four information inputs were identified, this variable was scored a 2. For example, the COAL ADVISOR reads in coal characteristics data and plant configuration information as well as operations cost and performance data in order to produce a purchase recommendation. Finally, if there were five or more information inputs, a score of 3 was given. In the LUS, for example, there are numerous information inputs, including information from new life insurance application forms, existing policy information, medical and financial investigations, and product pricing information. ## Ambiguity of Information Inputs

In some instances, source information is absolutely clear. In other instances, however, it is ambiguous in its raw form and requires that the problem-solver interpret and in other ways provide additional meaning to these data for them to be useful in his or her decision making. Information ambiguity must also be resolved in an expert system that seeks to effectively capture decision processes; the presence of this ambiguity can significantly increase knowledge complexity. For example, in EXTEL the calling history data of customers requires no additional interpretation. In contrast, in LUS the underwriter must frequently interpret information inputs, such as EKGs in medical examinations or net worth statements in financial histories. For purposes of measuring information ambiguity, we defined three categories, scored 1, 2, and 3, respectively:

## Defining technology complexity

While we were able to build on the substantial body of previous research in the area of classifying knowledge by various dimensions of complexity, a less extensive range of sources exists for classifying the embodied technology of information systems according to complexity. The variables needed to assess technology complexity were based on the limited available research, supplemented by our experiences in the pilot study. In designing these variables, no attempt was made to ascertain whether a company's choice of technology tools was, in fact, optimal. Rather, our objective was to document and classify the technologies actually used in this sample of successfully developed systems.

In terms of technological complexity, the expert systems that we examined generally ranged from stand-alone PC-based systems (with simple user interfaces and little database integration) to those that are networked applications (with large systems integration and database management requirements). ## Diversity of Platforms

The diversity of platforms is a measure of the degree to which the expert system has been ported across different computer architectures and operating systems. Creating an expert system for multiple platforms, or for a very special-purpose computer, increases the difficulty of the development effort. For the purposes of measurement, we differentiated between development and delivery platforms and gathered information only for the latter (i.e., the types of hardware on which the operational or production system was made to function for users). Additionally, we distinguished between computer architecture (i.e., Intel versus VAX versus Sparc versus IBM 4331, for example) and operating system type (DOS versus Unix versus VMS versus CICS). ## Diversity of Technologies

As expert systems have migrated from special-purpose machines, a number of researchers (Bobrow, et. al., 1986; Kerschbert, 1987; Pedersen, 1988) have noted the difficulty of integrating expert system technology with existing computer technologies. In this measure, we sought to evaluate the diversity of other basic software technologies, the presence of which requires additional specialization in the programming team and is therefore important in understanding technological complexity. For example, two frequently encountered non-inferencing technologies in expert systems are database management systems (found in LUS and COAL ADVISOR) and graphics subsystems (found in EXTEL). We factored out the presence of networking technology, which was set aside to be addressed separately. In terms of measurement, the following choices were used to identify non-inferencing technologies:

• No other technologies

\- Graphics (as in graphics programming libraries)

\- Database management systems

\- Spreadsheets

\- Imaging

\- Voice

For the scoring of this variable, the number of non-inferencing technologies embodied in the delivered systems was counted and incorporated into the technology complexity calculation. An additional point was assigned for each additional technology present. Thus, the scores for this variable ranged from 1 to 6.

## Database Intensity

To capture the addition to complexity that the database component of an expert system creates for the development team, we measured the cumulative size of the underlying reference databases accessed by the expert system in the course of processing (i.e., the size of the database accessed by the inference engine or logic in the course of programming). To qualify, such databases must be considered an internal component of the ES; this measure did not include external databases of other information systems accessed for certain information inputs. An initial dividing point of one megabyte of data for size seemed reasonable because beyond this point developers must begin to worry about optimizing access methods, transaction logging, access synchronization, and rigorous backup and recovery mechanisms. ## Scope of the Knowledge Base Programming Effort

The degree of difficulty in the encoding of the knowledge base acquired from experts is clearly a key factor in assessing the technical complexity of a project (Harmon, et al., 1988). Adequately capturing this degree of difficulty, however, proved challenging. One might consider measuring man-hours spent in development, but variations in programmer productivity make that measurement unreliable in comparing different systems. We decided to examine the number of “rules” (because most systems except for the simplest example-based systems employ some form of rule specification as a basic element of logic), and the total size of the “knowledge base,” which in more complex systems will include “object” specifications as well as “rules.” In doing this, we differentiated between the knowledge base itself and associated databases used by or within the expert system (the database portion is considered in a prior variable). Therefore, our method for deriving an assessment of the difficulty of the knowledge base programming effort meant counting the number of rules and the total size of the knowledge base. ## Calculation of total measures for knowledge and technology complexity

To summarize, the classification methodology described above employed seven variables to assess knowledge complexity and eight variables to assess technology complexity. To develop summary scores, the results were tabulated on these variables using an additive model whereby all variables were summed with equal weights. Since the variables were measured using different scales, we created the equal weights by multiplying to create a common denominator and then normalizing on a scale to a maximum of 100 in the following manner:

Knowledge = ((Domain Breadth \* 20) + (Domain Depth \* 20) + (Rate of Domain Change \* 20) + (Domain Penetration \* 12) + (System Outputs \* 15) + (Breadth of Information Inputs \* 20) + (Ambiguity of Information Inputs \* 20)) / 4.2.

Technology = ((Diversity of Platforms \* 10) + (Diversity of Technology \* 5) + (Database Intensity \* 10) + (Network Intensity \* 10) + (Knowledge Base Programming Effort \* 10) + (Diversity of Information Sources \* 10) + (Diffusion \* 6) + (Systems Integration \* 6)) / 2.4.

In Figure 2, the four representative quadrants were created by employing the mean score for each dimension of complexity. These quadrants were developed as the foundation for generating management hypotheses to be presented in the next section of this article. It may be argued that the use of an equal weight additive model is somewhat arbitrary and that some variables (such as the depth of the domain or the degree of networking required) increase the complexity of a system more dramatically than their equal weighting would suggest. We tested this by performing a sensitivity analysis and increased the weight by a factor of 2 for two variables “domain depth” and “systems integration effort.” This tabulation resulted in a new mean for knowledge complexity of 65 and a new mean for technology complexity of 55. The results of this analysis showed no appreciable difference in the number of cases classified in each quadrant (see Table 3). Therefore, the equal weight additive model chosen was an approach that best combined operational simplicity and measurement accuracy.

## Development time

One would expect that the time required to develop a knowledge-based system would be related to knowledge and technology complexity. Indeed, our data confirmed this. One of the points to note from the data in Table 4 is that regardless of final systems classification, prototype development times are remarkably similar for systems in all quadrants with the exception of Quadrant IV. The Quadrant IV prototypes took longer to create because the survey respondents indicated that rather elaborate, extensive system designs were developed in addition to knowledge-base prototyping, as is appropriate for larger, strategic applications.

The development times required to transform prototypes into operational systems was observed to be strongly different for the quadrants. Quadrant I prototypes could be rapidly turned into fielded systems with moderate amounts of additional work. Quadrant II systems, on the other hand, required substantially more effort to achieve this result—largely in the area of knowledge engineering and testing—and yielded multi-year development cycles. Quadrant III systems also had similarly long development times. However, we observed that the major elements of these efforts tended to be in the areas of systems integration where linkages to external applications and/or databases had to be developed. Quadrant IV systems entailed both aspects of work and, as we observed, required substantially more time to create production systems.

These findings and the nature of our sample lead to the following specific hypotheses to be validated in future research.

H1: For successfully developed knowledge-based systems, a significant relationship exists between development time and the combined knowledge and technology complexity embodied in such systems.

Table 4 contains average development time information. We also studied the individual case data for differences between knowledge and technological complexity in their respective relationship to development time. Differentiating between the development of prototypes versus the development of completed systems, we believe that greater amounts of knowledge complexity would extend development time for prototypes more than technology complexity. There are two reasons for this supposition. First, most prototypes are created as stand-alone subsystems, thereby sparing for later aspects of applications and systems integration key ingredients of technology complexity measured in our classification methodology. Second, our field interviews showed that the knowledge engineering, modelling, and testing tasks are far from a certain process. Thus, the higher the amount of knowledge complexity in a system, the greater the degree of uncertainty for developing the knowledge bases required for proving the feasibility of the application. Upon closer study, the data for the 50 systems in the sample appeared to confirm this premise. This finding yields a second research hypothesis:

H2: For successfully developed knowledge-based systems, embodied knowledge complexity will show a stronger correlation with prototype development time than will technological complexity.

When we turned to overall development time for completed production systems, the data then suggest that as the technology complexity of the systems increases, overall development time for completed production systems becomes greater than for systems with high levels of knowledge complexity. In other words, Quadrant III systems take longer to achieve full production status than Quadrant II systems. The explanation for this is that having completed a working prototype for a technology-intensive application, the development teams then had to address specific technical tasks of systems integration, accessing data through networks, and so forth. More people were involved, and more computer equipment was purchased. In Quadrant II, knowledge-intensive systems, these aspects of technology complexity were not encountered, and, thus, overall development times were shorter. A third research hypothesis was therefore suggested from these data:

H3: For successfully developed knowledge-based systems, technology complexity will show a stronger correlation with overall development time than will knowledge complexity.

## Development budget

The information provided in Table 4 also shows that the classification methodology produced generic groups of systems that were notably different in terms of the amount of money spent to create them. The simplest knowledge-based systems in Quadrant I averaged approximately \$100k to create completed systems. The companies spent on average about \$250k to build both knowledge-intensive Quadrant II and technology-intensive Quadrant III applications. Those systems that were both highly complex in knowledge and technology had budgets that averaged about \$1 million. In fact, six of the systems in the sample exceeded the \$1 million level for total budget, and several of these had approached \$5 million to field a production system.

Additionally, upon closer examination of the two dimensions of complexity, we observed that high levels of technology complexity were accompanied by higher budgets than for those systems that were complex only in knowledge. As discussed above, the creation of distributed applications that have high levels of integration between the expert system and other systems in a company takes greater resources on the part of the development team. For example, the life underwriting system (LUS) used to illustrate Quadrant IV systems earlier in this article required the development of database linkages to both client administration systems and external industry databases for medical and financial investigations. The different data models between these application entities complicated the integration beyond simple network data retrieval. In a related manner, the telephone equipment system (EXTEL) described earlier was made costly by sheer scope of the diffusion of the application; knowledge and databases had to be updated across hundreds of usage sites.

In terms of future research, these observations yield two additional hypotheses:

H4: For successfully developed knowledge-based systems, a significant relationship exists between development budget and the combined knowledge and technology complexity embodied in such systems.

H5: For successfully developed knowledge-based systems, embodied technology complexity will show a stronger correlation with development budget than will knowledge complexity.

## Organizational control and the role of DP/MIS

In addition to time and budget information, we gathered data concerning organizational control over development and the participation of other departments in the development effort. In terms of organizational control, we were particularly interested in contrasting business unit control versus that of corporate data processing departments. Corporate DP has traditionally played a strong role in designing, staffing, and managing the creation of information systems in companies. For knowledge-based systems, we suspected that the opposite would be true. There were several reasons for this proposition. First, given that the goal of these systems was to model and hence computerize some aspect of complex decision making, the source of innovation for knowledge-based systems would tend to be marketing, sales, manufacturing, or other types of existing business units. The extension of idea generation to project control seemed logical. Second, once the projects were initiated, systems that targeted uncommon, specialized knowledge and decision making would require close access to the “domain experts.” Business unit control, as opposed to corporate DP department control, would facilitate this access.

These ideas found substantiation in the data. Of the 50 systems in our sample, 68 percent were conceived originally by domain experts in existing business departments within the respective companies. Only 14 percent of the sample were originated by a programmer in a DP department or, as in several companies, by an “AI specialist” working within a special AI group reporting to DP management. The remaining 18 percent of the systems were originated in sources external to the respective companies: 8 percent by a customer, 6 percent by a computer company selling products or services to the respective companies, and 4 percent by consultants.

We then examined the organizational unit that had effective control over the development effort. Table 5 shows the frequencies observed in the sample. Existing business departments controlled 62 percent of the projects, while DP/MIS departments controlled 34 percent. In several instances where large-scale strategic systems were involved, a new business unit was formed to control development, staffed with individuals from both existing business departments and DP/MIS. No where in our sample was a development effort managed by an external concern.

The instances of DP/MIS control led us to probe into those instances where different forms of control were more suited to successful knowledge-based systems development. Once again, the classification methodology proved useful for differentiating management approaches in this area. The lower portion of Table 5 shows a breakdown of business unit versus DP/MIS control by quadrant of the classification methodology. The contrast of these two forms of control for Quadrant II knowledge-intensive systems versus Quadrant III technology intensive systems can be seen in these data. As we studied the cases further, it became clear that DP/MIS was more likely to manage the development effort under the conditions of high technology complexity and low knowledge complexity. Such projects typically targeted confined, readily-learned domain knowledge and at the same time had high technical requirements. This obviously played to the strength of DP/MIS. On the other hand, when a system targeted specialized deep areas of domain knowledge, business unit control was the more suited form. These observations suggest the following research hypothesis:

H6: For successfully developed knowledge-based systems, there is a systematic relationship between the organizational locus of control over the development effort and the relative complexity of a system's embodied knowledge and technological complexity.

The data also show, however, that even in instances where DP/MIS in the sample companies did not control the development efforts, it still played a strong participatory role that was important for successful project outcomes. In the survey, we tracked the composition of development teams, as well as the departmental affiliation of individuals who performed critical functions. Those critical functions tracked were domain expertise, knowledge engineering, knowledge-based programming, database programming, and systems integration. For the 17 applications managed by DP/MIS, members from these respective computing departments tended to perform all of the critical functions with the exception of providing domain expertise, which invariably came from existing business departments or customers. For the remaining 33 cases controlled by business managers, DP/MIS was nonetheless an important player in 10 additional applications. In these 10 cases, the role of DP/MIS tended to be in the areas of knowledge engineering and knowledge-based programming and, in some instances, in database programming and systems integration.

## Conclusion

As the discussion above has shown, the classification methodology based on assessing knowledge and technology complexity allows us to consider a range of development management issues using our sample of successfully developed advanced information systems. From a research perspective, this process of studying issues with an approach that is contingent upon complexity has yielded a set of hypotheses concerning time, budget, control, and participation that we will seek to test in the future. We hope that this research will produce findings that assist practitioners in the budgeting, scheduling, staffing, and management of knowledge-based system development efforts.

The practitioner might also find the classification methodology useful for the planning of new projects. At the very least, the classification scheme presented in this article provides a useful checklist of issues that management should take into account when developing plans for ES implementation. Managers may be able to pre-classify their intended projects with the classification methodology and then plan resource allocations in consideration of the concepts and data presented in this article. For example, the development effort for systems that are technologically complex will benefit from an early involvement with the corporate MIS group. Or a knowledge-intensive system will require very close linkages with, if not control by, the business departments wherein the required domain expertise exists. Additionally, new project proposals can be screened against a classified pool of existing knowledge-based systems' successes within a company so that management can focus new efforts toward levels of knowledge and technology complexity managed effectively in the past. Finally, for ongoing expert systems projects that evolve over time, the consequences of such evolution toward either greater knowledge or technology complexity can be assessed in terms of expected project requirements.

At a higher level, the management of complexity in the development of new information systems has always been central to effective project management. For the new generation of advanced informations systems discussed in this article, we have sought to provide a field-tested framework for assessing the key components of such complexity and thereby provide a foundation for future research and management practice.

# Executive Overview

This article reports on the third of a series of studies that gauge the relative importance of issues facing IS management. The survey population consists of institutional and board members of the Society for Information Management. The survey is thus clearly biased in favor of the viewpoint held by senior IS executives from large organizations. The results cannot be claimed as representative of IS managers in general, but the selected survey population is quite likely to be more perceptive of current trends than samples drawn from most other groups.

The ranking of the top 10 issues was as follows:

1. Information architecure

2. Data resource

3. Strategic planning

4. Human resources

5. Organizational learning

6. Technology infrastructure

7. IS organization

8. Competitive advantage

9. Software development

10. Telecommunications systems planning

Some interesting observations can be drawn from these results. Only six of the top 10 issues from the 1986 survey remained in this latest study (completed in early 1990). It appears that greater attention is being paid to infrastructure issues: the communications network, data sharing mechanisms, and application development processes. This is a very healthy new emphasis, because the continuing success of the IS staff is likely to depend primarily on whether they can deliver to users an effective technical envelope within which applications can be developed productively.

One could argue, in fact, that the availability of an effective infrastructure addresses every one of the top 10 issues. Clearly it includes issues 1, 2, 6, 9, and 10. The link may not be so obvious with respect to strategic planning (#3), organizational learning (#5), and competitive advantage (#8). However, if one accepts the proposition that the only way to develop an effective information system—be it operational or “strategic”—is through an evolutionary process, then it becomes patently obvious that organizations must put in place an infrastructure capable of adapting flexibly to new application requirements. The availability of such a platform would likely reduce a compulsive focus on up-front strategies and requirements, which no organization does particularly well. It would instead provide a means for the organization to adapt effectively to a largely unknown—and unknowable—future.

The increased interest in the issue of human resources (issue #4) is similarly encouraging. Without the right people—smart, educated, properly trained, empowered—almost nothing we can do is likely to bring great success; with the right people, it is difficult to fail. The survey indicates that this issue is rightly getting greater attention than it has in the past.

The results of this study are helpful in pointing out where we should be focusing our attention. Although the results change somewhat from one survey to the next—suggesting that nothing is immutable in our business—IS professionals can benefit from the assessment gained from a significant sample of senior IS executives. Both practitioners and academics alike sometimes seem reluctant to emphasize the technical roots of our profession. The results of this study put a stamp of legitimacy o., concerns about the technical base of information systems.
