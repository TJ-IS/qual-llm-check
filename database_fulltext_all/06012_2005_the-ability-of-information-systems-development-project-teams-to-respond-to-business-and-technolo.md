# The ability of information systems development project teams to respond to business and technology changes: a study of flexibility measures

Gwanhoo Lee<sup>1</sup> and Weidong Xia<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Technology, Kogod School of Business, American University, Washington, DC, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Information and Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A.

## Abstract

The socio-technical perspective suggests that information systems development projects (ISDPs) involve both organizational and technical dimensions. As both the organizational and technical aspects of ISDPs frequently change, the ISDP team’s flexibility in responding to these changes has become a critical success factor for system development. While the importance of ISDP team flexibility has increased, the research literature lacks a consistent definition and validated measures of the construct. Drawing upon the socio-technical and the capability-based perspectives and using a systematic multi-stage approach, we identified major business and technology changes and developed measurement scales of ISDP team flexibility along two dimensions: Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency. The results of a confirmatory factor analysis using survey data from 505 ISDP managers suggested that the final measurement scales exhibited adequate levels of measurement properties including unidimensionality, reliability, discriminant validity, factorial invariance and nomological validity. In addition, our results revealed an interesting negative relationship between Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency, indicating a trade-off between the two dimensions of flexibility. Our result indicated that while the ISDP teams experienced and responded more extensively to business changes than technology changes, they were much less efficient in dealing with business changes than technology changes. ## Introduction

Information systems (IS) development is a complex process. It is a sociotechnical process rather than a purely technical one (Mumford, 1983; Newman & Robey, 1992; McChesney & Glass, 1993; Lyytinen et al., 1998; Al-Mudimigh et al., 2001; Davidson, 2002). As such, information systems development project (ISDP) teams must deal with both organizational and technical issues. Many of the ISDP success/failure factors are organizational rather than technical (Lyytinen et al., 1998; Schmidt et al., 2001). In addition, both organizational and technical components change frequently during an ISDP, making IS development even more complex and difficult to manage.

Today’s business environments are changing at an unprecedented rate. The turbulent economy, globalization, innovation, and business restructuring result in a hypercompetitive, high-velocity business landscape (Eisenhardt, 1989; D’Aveni, 1994). Organizations often produce systems that are irrelevant and ineffective because business conditions and contexts change rapidly. ‘By the time you design, program, and implement business application software, the world has changed anyway,’ said an IS executive of a large U.S. airline company in our interview. This statement speaks to the volatility of business needs and user requirements that ISDP teams must deal with.

Information technology (IT), at the same time, is changing at an ever-increasing rate (Benamati & Lederer, 2001). Innovative technology is invented continuously. Current technologies easily become obsolete. Average product life cycles of IT have declined drastically and are projected to continue to decline (Hamel & Prahalad, 1996). IT vendors upgrade their software and hardware products more frequently than they did in the past. As a result, organizations must continuously adopt and integrate new technologies into their existing portfolios of business applications and IT infrastructure. ‘You may have a technology that seemed like the right choice at the time, but is obsolete when you put it in,’ said an ISDP manager that we interviewed.

The success rate of IS projects has historically been low. The Standish Group reported that only 16–28% of IS projects could be considered successful (Standish Group, 1994, 2001). From the socio-technical perspective (Leavitt, 1964; Cummings, 1978; Mumford, 1983), one important reason for these low success rates is a lack of the ISDP team’s ability to manage not only technical components but also organizational components (Lyytinen et al., 1998; Schmidt et al., 2001). Failure to address these socio-technical changes often results in an incongruence between the system and its socio-technical context, which in turn may lead to project failure (Curtis et al., 1988; Nidumolu, 1995). Project performance tends to increase as the ISDP team becomes more flexible and effective in responding to business and technology changes (Gefen & Keil, 1998; Gefen & Ridings, 2002). Academics and practitioners alike suggest that flexibility is a necessary condition for coping with the uncertainty and ambiguity in the new dynamic environments (Chakravarthy, 1997; Evans, 1999; Alter, 2000; Overby, 2001). However, the contemporary IS organizations seem to be far from being flexible. For example, Benamati & Lederer (2001) found that IS organizations often failed to use effective coping mechanisms to respond to technology changes.

Although the importance of ISDP team flexibility has been widely recognized and has recently attracted IS researchers’ attentions (Duncan, 1995; Gefen & Keil, 1998; Byrd & Turner, 2000; Gefen & Ridings, 2002), the research literature on this topic is still in early development stages. Little is known about the key types of business and technology changes that occur during ISDPs. There is a lack of valid, reliable scales for assessing ISDP team flexibility. Without validated measures, it is difficult to develop theories of ISDP team flexibility, particularly about the antecedents to and the effects of ISDP team flexibility.

There were four major objectives of our research. First, we identified the major types of business and technology changes that occur during ISDPs. Second, we identified the key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility. Third, based on the major business and technology changes and the key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility, we developed and validated measures of ISDP team flexibility. Finally, using the ISDP team flexibility measures and the survey data, we explored the relationships between the different dimensions of flexibility and compared the measurement results of ISDP team flexibility across different segments of project size and duration.

This research is intended to provide a basis for advancing theories regarding ISDP team flexibility by conceptualizing major business/technology changes, identifying key flexibility dimensions, and developing and validating empirical measures of ISDP team flexibility. This research also makes a practical contribution by developing a conceptual framework and measurement tool that managers can use to communicate and assess ISDP team flexibility and identify any gaps that exist between an ISDP team’s current flexibility level and its desired flexibility level in order to address effectively the dynamics of its business and technology environments.

The paper is organized as follows. We review relevant prior literature in the next section. We discuss our conceptualization of changes and ISDP team flexibility in the following section. We then present research methods and results. We conclude the paper with discussions of the theoretical and practical implications of the study results, as well as limitations of our study and directions for future research.

## Literature background

## Socio-technical perspective on IS development

The socio-technical perspective has been widely used to study organizational changes. It has also been used extensively in the IS literature (Mumford, 1983; Paddock, 1986; Davis et al., 1992; McChesney & Glass, 1993; Lyytinen et al., 1998; Al-Mudimigh et al., 2001). Grounded in the general systems theory (Von Bertalanffy, 1950), the socio-technical perspective views organizations as consisting of two independent, but linked systems: a social system and a technical system. The social system typically includes two components: actor and structure (Leavitt, 1964; Cummings, 1978). In the context of IS development, actor includes various stakeholders such as users, managers, and IS staff. Structure denotes organizational context and institutional arrangements under which an IS is developed. The technical system generally consists of the equipment and methods of operations used to transform materials into products (Cummings, 1978). In the context of IS development, technology includes methods, tools, hardware/software platforms, and infrastructure that are used to develop and implement a business software system (Cooprider & Henderson, 1991; Lyytinen et al., 1998).

According to the socio-technical perspective, since any socio-technical component can create disturbance to the system under development, the success of IS development is determined not only by the technical components but also by the social components (Dill, 1958; Mumford, 1983). Some researchers suggested that system development failures could be attributed more to social and organizational factors than technical factors (Doherty & King, 1998a, b). Therefore, organizations must take both the social and technical dimensions into account in an IS development process. It is critical to address changes in any socio-technical component because these changes cause incongruence between the socio-technical components, which in turn can lead to a failure or underperformance of the system development (Lyytinen et al., 1998). Since the socio-technical components of ISDPs are in continuous changes, a key challenge for a team is to effectively respond to those changes and to incorporate them into the design and development of information systems.

## Business and technology changes during ISDPs

An ISDP’s business context frequently changes during the development process. These business changes subsequently result in changes in user requirements of the system under development. Prior literature has discussed various types of business and user requirement changes (Jalote, 2000; Whitten et al., 2001). For example, the CMM literature makes distinctions between technical user requirements and non-technical user requirements (SEI, 1994). The function point analysis literature views business requirements as consisting of inputs, outputs, logical internal files, interface files, and external inquiries (Low & Jeffery, 1990).

In addition to business context changes, technologies also change during an ISDP. Typically, an ISDP team deals with two types of information technologies: software development tools and infrastructure technologies (Cooprider & Henderson, 1991). Software development tools include programming languages/tools, computeraided software engineering (CASE) tools, and packaged software. These tools are selected for specific ISDPs based on business needs and technological capabilities. In contrast, infrastructure technologies are shared by multiple ISDPs. According to Duncan (1995), infrastructure technologies include platform technology, network/ telecommunication technologies, key enterprise data, and core enterprise applications.

Researchers have viewed business and technology changes as critical software development risks. For example, Boehm (1991) ranked business requirement changes as a top software project risk. According to

Schmidt et al. (2001), software project risks include various business and technology changes such as unstable corporate environments, changing scope/objectives, introduction of new technology, and instability of technical architecture.

## Capability-based perspective on organizational flexibility

The Merriam-Webster’s Dictionary defines flexibility as ‘a ready capability to adapt to new, different, or changing requirements.’ Consistently, the capability-based perspective has defined organizational flexibility as an organizational capability to respond to environmental changes (Barney, 1991; Grant, 1991). For instance, strategic flexibility is viewed as the organizational capability to develop a firm’s strategy to respond to environmental changes in a timely and appropriate manner (Das & Elango, 1995). Manufacturing flexibility is defined as a production system’s capability of exhibiting a wide range of states or behaviors to meet changing customer demands (Slack, 1983). Similarly, IS researchers have defined IT infrastructure flexibility as the organizational capability to support a variety of information technologies and information services (Byrd & Turner, 2000; Duncan, 1995).

The dynamic capability perspective has recently gained increasing attentions from management researchers. Dynamic capabilities are defined as a firm’s abilities to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external resources, competences and capabilities to address rapidly changing environments (Teece et al., 1997; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). Organizational flexibility is enhanced by developing various dynamic capabilities (Volberda, 1998).

Flexibility is a complex, multidimensional construct (Sethi & Sethi, 1990; Bahrami, 1992; de Leeuw & Volberda, 1996). As summarized in Table 1, researchers have suggested various dimensions of flexibility. For example, flexibility can be measured in terms of range, variety, or extent of organizational responses to environmental changes (Slack, 1983; Evans, 1991; Bahrami, 1992; Das & Elango, 1995; Sanchez, 1995; Volberda, 1996). These dimensions capture the ‘scope’ aspects of flexibility. Flexibility can also be measured in terms of time, cost, or effort that is required for the organization to respond to environmental changes (Slack, 1983; Evans, 1991; Pindyck, 1991; Bahrami, 1992; Athey & Schmutzler, 1995; Das & Elango, 1995; Sanchez, 1995; Volberda, 1996; Nelson & Ghods, 1998). These dimensions capture the ‘efficiency’ aspects of flexibility.

## ISDP team flexibility

Drawing upon the socio-technical and the capability based perspectives, we define ISDP team flexibility as the ISDP team’s ability to effectively and efficiently respond to business and technology changes. We view ISDP team flexibility as a project-level organizational capability that utilizes project resources to respond to the changing business and technology contexts during the project.

As shown in Figure 1, IS development is executed within the socio-technical contexts. Facing frequent changes in business and technology contexts, ISDP teams must continuously sense and respond to these changes. ‘We plan but always new issues come up,’ said an ISDP manager in our interview. Indeed, flexibility is necessitated by changes in the socio-technical system. If business and technology are contextually stable, there is no need for the introduction of flexibility. In order to measure ISDP team flexibility, therefore, we must first understand the major types of business and technology changes during ISDPs.

Managing business changes requires different types of resources, knowledge, expertise, and capabilities than managing technology changes. That is, an ISDP team may be effective in managing technology changes, but the team may be concurrently ineffective in managing business changes.

In order to meet rapidly changing market demands and customer needs, organizations frequently change their business strategies, business processes, and organizational structure. These changes in the business context often lead to changes in user requirements of the system under development, including system objectives, system scope, input/output data, data structure, and user interface.

ISDP teams need to incorporate technological changes. Technology changes include changes in both application development tools/methods and infrastructure components. Changes in one technology component may affect other technology components. The system under development must accommodate changes in the existing technology systems of the organization. Otherwise, the final system may not be compatible with the other technology components of the organization.

Consistent with prior literature, we view ISDP team flexibility as a multi-dimensional construct. Although researchers have proposed several dimensions of organizational flexibility, there has been no agreement on which flexibility dimensions are most important to the context of ISDPs. Our research identifies key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility. These dimensions are empirically derived from ISDP managers’ inputs. We then develop measures along the dimensions to assess ISDP team flexibility.

## Methods

We used a systematic approach to develop the measurement scales of ISDP team flexibility. As shown in Figure 2, this approach consists of six stages: (1) identifying major types of business and technology changes, (2) identifying key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility, (3) constructing measures, (4) collecting large-scale survey data from ISDP managers, (5) refining and validating the measures using confirmatory analysis techniques with the survey data, and (6) using the measures and the survey data to examine relationships between dimensions of ISDP team flexibility and to compare ISDP flexibility measurement results across different project sizes and durations.

## Stage 1 – identifying major types of business and technology changes

We first generated an initial pool of change types based on literature review as well as field interviews and focus group discussions with ISDP managers. We then used a pretest and a sorting procedure to refine the list of business and technology changes. In the pretest, 15 ISDP managers assessed the importance and frequency of the initial list of business and technology changes. The items that had high mean scores in both importance and frequency were retained.

A sorting procedure adapted from Moore & Benbasat (1991) was used to assess qualitatively the construct validity of measurement items. The four judges were IS researchers with an average of 8 years of work experience. Each item was printed on a 3 - 5-inch index card. The judges were asked to carefully read each card and place it in an appropriate category. An additional category, ‘too ambiguous/unclear’ was included so that the judges could place a card into that category if the card did not seem to fit into any of the predefined categories. After completing the sorting procedure, the judges explained reasons for placing cards into the ‘too ambiguous/ unclear’ category, if any. The item placement hit ratio was used to indicate how well the judges were able to sort the items into the target constructs. The hit ratio was calculated by dividing the number of items correctly placed by the total number of items.

## Stage 2 – identifying key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility

In this stage, the key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility were identified. An initial pool of ISDP team flexibility dimensions was generated through a review of the prior literature, field interviews, and focus group discussions with ISDP managers. To ensure adequate coverage of the initial dimensions, we reviewed a wide range of literature including strategic management, organization theory, operations management, and IS.

We performed a pretest with 15 ISDP managers to select the most important dimensions of ISDP team flexibility. The respondents assessed the relative importance of the various flexibility dimensions. Two most important dimensions were selected based on importance scores and further discussions with ISDP managers.

## Stage 3 – constructing measures

Combining the major types of business and technology changes with the key dimensions of flexibility, we constructed an initial survey instrument for assessing ISDP team flexibility. To further modify and refine measurement items to improve the overall format and wording of the questionnaire, we pilot-tested the initial survey instrument with seven ISDP managers.

## Stage 4 – collecting large-scale survey data

A web-based online survey was used to collect data from a large-scale sample. The source of the survey respondents was the Information Systems Specific Interest Group of the Project Management Institute (PMI-ISSIG). The PMI-ISSIG sponsored this research by providing access to their membership information and promoting our survey study to their members. Two criteria were used to select the 1740 target respondents: (1) North American PMI-ISSIG members and (2) project managers (not specialists such as programmers or systems analysts) who had managed a recently completed ISDP.

A PMI-ISSIG-sponsored email letter with a hyperlink to our online survey was sent to the target group. To encourage participation, survey participants were entered into a draw to receive 10 awards of a PMI-ISSIG official shirt and 40 awards of a \$25 gift certificate from a wellknown online consumer retail store. A reminder was sent

2 weeks after the initial PMI-ISSIG-sponsored email was sent out, followed by a second email reminder 2 weeks later. In total, 565 responses were obtained, resulting in a response rate of 32.5%. After incomplete and invalid responses were excluded, 505 valid responses were obtained for the final analysis, representing an effective response rate of 29.0%.

Table 2 illustrates the sample characteristics. The sample represented various industry sectors, ranging from manufacturing, financial services, software, consulting, retailing, transportation, healthcare, to utility. The sample also represented a wide range of organizations and projects in terms of annual sales, number of employees, project team size, project budget, and project duration.

The goodness of fit of the hypothesized measurement model was assessed using such indicators as $\chi ^ { 2 } ,$ , the ratio of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to degrees of freedom, the goodness of fit index (GFI), the adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI), the standardized root mean square residual (RMR), and the comparative fit index (CFI). The measurement model is considered to have satisfactory fit if it meets the following criteria: insignificant $\chi ^ { 2 }$ (the smaller $\chi ^ { 2 } ,$ the better the model fit), the ratio of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to degrees of freedom $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } )$ smaller than 5, GFI above 0.90, AGFI above 0.80, standardized RMR less than 0.10, and CFI above 0.90 (Wheaton et al., 1977; Jo¨reskog & So¨rbom, 1989; Chin & Todd, 1995; Hu & Bentler, 1995; Byrne, 1998).

We assessed unidimensionality and convergent validity by testing if a single factor model demonstrates a sufficient level of goodness of fit. Reliability was assessed by computing the composite reliability index. The composite reliability $( \rho _ { c } )$ represents the proportion of the variances among the measures that can be attributable to the underlying latent variable (Werts et al., 1974). One of the advantages of using this reliability index is that it is free from the restricted assumption of equal importance of all indicators on which the Cronbach’s a is based. Following Werts et al. (1974) and Venkatraman (1989), we calculated the composite reliability from the factor loadings of each indicator and error variances (Composite reliability $( \rho _ { c } ) = ( \sum \lambda _ { i } ) ^ { 2 }$ Variance ${ \bf \nabla } \cdot ( A ) / ( ( \sum \lambda _ { i } ) ^ { 2 }$ Variance $\left( 4 \right) + \sum \theta _ { \delta } ) )$ . A measurement scale is considered as reliable if its $\rho _ { c }$ is above 0.50 (Bagozzi, 1981).

We assessed the discriminant validity of the measurement by testing if the correlation between a pair of latent variables was significantly different from unity (Venkatraman, 1989; Sethi & King, 1994). A model with the correlation constrained to one was compared with an unconstrained model. A significantly lower $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value for the unconstrained model, when compared with that of the constrained model, indicates discriminant validity between the pair of latent variables.

Tests of factorial invariance examine if the same factorial structure and properties of the measurement model can be replicated across different segments of the population (Marsh & Hocevar, 1985; Byrne, 1998). To the extent that factorial invariance across certain segments (e.g., project size) is established, the measurement model is considered to be applicable and the results comparable across those segments. Factorial invariance can be tested through a hierarchical approach. First the overall goodness of fit of the measurement model across the segments is tested. If the measurement model varies across the segments, there is no need to proceed with the subsequent tests. On the other hand, if the overall model fits well across the segments, the invariance of the factor loadings of the measurement items is then tested. If this test is passed, the invariance of the path coefficients between the first- and the second-order factors is tested. In this research, we tested the factorial invariance of the flexibility measures across project size (small, medium, large), project budget (small, medium, large), and project duration (short, medium, long).

Nomological (or predictive) validity assesses if a construct measured by the new measures is associated with other constructs whose measures are known to be valid, as the theory would predict. If the relationships are consistent with the theoretical prediction, the new measures are considered to have satisfactory nomological validity. In this research, we examined the relationships between ISDP team flexibility measurement results and user satisfaction with the information system delivered by the project.

## Stage 6 – using the measures and the survey data to analyze ISDP team flexibility

We used the measures that were developed and validated in the above stages and the survey data to examine the relationships between the dimensions of ISDP team flexibility. In addition, we segmented the sample to compare the ISDP team flexibility measurement results across project size, budget, and duration. These comparisons across segments are important to reveal critical results that may be masked or misrepresented by using the overall sample means.

## Results

## Major types of business and technology changes

Our initial list of changes included 24 types of business changes and 10 types of technology changes. Business changes consisted of three subgroups: (1) changes in the setup of an ISDP, such as system delivery date and project budget; (2) changes in the functional requirements of the system being developed, such as system objectives, system scope, input data, output data, business rules/ processes, data structure and user interface; and (3) changes in non-functional or operational requirements, such as system throughput, system response time, system reliability, and documentation.

Technology changes were composed of three subgroups: (1) changes in software programming languages/tools, systems analysis/design methods, and CASE tools that the ISDP team uses to develop the business system; (2) changes in technology architecture, hardware platform, operating systems, network/telecommunication, and infrastructure; and (3) changes in the other systems that the application under development will interface with, and in the enterprise master data that the application will use.

As a result of the pretest with 15 ISDP managers, several types of changes were dropped because of relatively lowranked importance and frequency. The overall item placement hit ratio of the sorting procedure with four judges was 92%, indicating that the items were largely being placed as they were intended to be. A few items were identified as being too ambiguous or unclear by the judges. One business change type was dropped from the list.

## Dimensions of ISDP team flexibility

Drawing upon prior literature and field interviews with ISDP managers, we generated two groups of flexibility dimensions. The first group included dimensions that tapped into the scope aspects of the ISDP team’s responses to changes. These dimensions measured ISDP team flexibility in terms of range, variety, or extent, of the team’s responses. The second group included dimensions that tapped into the efficiency aspects of the ISDP team’s responses to changes. These dimensions measured flexibility in terms of time, cost, or effort that is required for an ISDP team to respond to changes.

In the pretest with ISDP managers, two dimensions emerged as the most important dimensions of ISDP team flexibility: (1) extent of ISDP team response and (2) effort required by the ISDP team to respond to change. The first dimension represents the extensiveness of ISDP team response and is related to such scope dimensions as range and variety. The second dimension represents the efficiency of ISDP team response and encompasses such efficiency dimensions as time, cost, and difficulty. The efficiency dimension captures ISDP team flexibility in terms of the amount of additional effort required by the ISDP team to manage the business and technology changes.

## Measurement scales for ISDP team flexibility

After identifying the major business and technology changes during ISDPs and the two key dimensions of ISDP team flexibility, we combined them to construct measurement items for assessing ISDP team flexibility. These items measured the ISDP team’s ability to respond to eleven business and seven technology changes along the Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency dimensions.

## Validation of response extensiveness scale

We validated the unidimensionality, internal consistency reliability, and discriminant validity of the measures. Unidimensionality requires that one single factor underlies a set of measures (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). To test unidimensionality, we examined the goodness of fit of the two first-order factor models; one for EXT\_B and the other for EXT\_T.

As shown in Table 5(a), both EXT\_B (GFI ¼ 0.97, AGFI ¼ 0.94, RMR ¼ 0.026, CFI ¼ 0.99) and EXT\_T (GFI ¼ 0.97, AGFI ¼ 0.92, RMR ¼ 0.022, CFI ¼ 0.98) demonstrated satisfactory levels of unidimensionality. Both EXT\_B and EXT\_T had a composite reliability $( \rho _ { c } )$ of 0.91, indicating satisfactory levels of reliability.

Discriminant validity was assessed by examining the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference between the constrained model and the unconstrained model. The results shown in Table 5(b) suggested that the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference between the two models was significant $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ difference ¼ 9.07, d.f. ¼ 1; Po0.01), indicating satisfactory discriminant validity. In conclusion, the empirical results supported the validity and reliability of the measurement scale of the Response Extensiveness dimension of ISDP team flexibility.

## Validation of response efficiency scale

Unidimensionality was tested by examining the goodness of fit of the two first-order factor models; one for EFF\_B and the other for EFF\_T. As shown in Table 6(a), EFF\_B (GFI ¼ 0.97, AGFI ¼ 0.93, RMR ¼ 0.034, CFI ¼ 0.97) and EFF\_T (GFI ¼ 0.98, AGFI ¼ 0.95, RMR ¼ 0.024, CFI ¼ 0.99) demonstrated satisfactory levels of unidimensionality. The composite reliability indices of EFF\_B and EFF\_T were 0.88 and 0.86, respectively, indicating satisfactory levels of reliability.

As shown in Table 6(b), the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference between the constrained model and the unconstrained model was significant $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ difference ¼ 7.02, d.f. ¼ 1; Po0.01), indicating satisfactory discriminant validity. In conclusion, the empirical data supported the validity and reliability of the measurement scale of the Response Efficiency dimension of ISDP team flexibility.

## Nomological Validity

Nomological validity of ISDP team flexibility was tested by examining whether or not the relationships between ISDP team flexibility and other constructs with known valid measurements were consistent with what the theory would predict. In this research, we tested the effect of ISDP team flexibility on user satisfaction with the information system delivered by the project.

Prior literature suggests that ISDP team flexibility positively affects user satisfaction. For example, ISDP team responsiveness was found to positively affect user perception and evaluation of the final system (Gefen & Keil, 1998; Gefen & Ridings, 2002). The more flexible the ISDP team is, the more likely that the final system accommodates the most recent business and technology environments of the organization, resulting in more satisfied users. Therefore we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. Response extensiveness positively affects user satisfaction with the system.

Hypothesis 2. Response efficiency positively affects user satisfaction with the system.

We tested a LISREL model that specified the effects of Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency on user satisfaction. The results suggested that the hypothesized structural equation model fitted the data well $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 1 2 2 . 5 2 , \quad \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } \quad \quad \mathrm { r a t i o } = 3 . 2 7 , \quad \quad \mathrm { R M R } = 0 . 0 5 3 ,$ CFI ¼ 0.97). ## Discussion

The focus of this paper is on the conceptualization and operationalization of the ISDP team flexibility construct. Drawing upon the socio-technical and the organizational capability literatures, we conceptualized ISDP team flexibility as the ability to respond to changes in its business and technology environments. Using a systematic multistage approach, we identified major types of business and technology changes, and developed measure scales for assessing ISDP team flexibility along two dimensions: Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency. Test results suggested that the measurement scales demonstrated satisfactory levels of measurement properties, including unidimensionality, reliability, discriminant validity, and nomological validity. In addition, the factorial invariance test results suggested that measurement scales can be applied across different segments of project sizes and durations. Analyses of the flexibility measurement results of the 505 ISDPs in our sample demonstrated an interesting negative relationship between the two dimensions of ISDP team flexibility. Further data analyses revealed reversed patterns between the measurements results of response efficiency and those of the changes/response extensiveness. The results have important implications for both research and practice.

## Implications for research

This paper contributes to the research literature by making a first attempt to conceptualize and operationalize the ISDP team flexibility construct, and providing initial measurement results of the various changes and the abilities of the ISDP teams to respond to those changes. Projects are the basic units through which organizations manage their IS development activities. Given the fast changing business and technology environments, the success of an ISDP increasingly depends on its team’s ability to respond to changes. Despite the importance of understanding ISDP team flexibility, the research literature on this topic is in an early development stage while being primarily anecdotal in nature. There is a critical need for rigorous theories that managers can use as guidelines to understand and manage both changes and their responses to changes. A significant gap in the research literature is the lack of clear definitions and valid measures of ISDP team flexibility. Without commonly accepted definitions and measurements, it is difficult to develop and test theories, compare results across empirical studies, and accumulate theories that managers can use. This paper serves as a starting point to address these deficiencies.

The major types of changes identified in this study highlight the socio-technical nature of the ISDP project environments. Although various changes have been discussed in prior literature, no research has systematically identified the major types of business and technology changes during an ISDP. The results suggested that ISDP teams experienced significantly more business changes than technology changes, prompting the importance of attending to the social and organizational aspects of the ISDP context.

The measurement model tested in the study provides an initial operational definition and instruments that researchers can use to advance theories about ISDP team flexibility. The second-order factor structure of the measurement model suggests that ISDP team flexibility is a multi-dimensional construct, and thus should be treated as such in future research. In addition to confirming the reliability and construct validity of the measurement scales, the factorial invariance test results suggest that the measurement scales can be generalized or applied across different segments of project scales in terms of project team size, budget, and duration. Validation of measurement applicability across different segments of the population has significant research implications. Without establishing measurement applicability, if the measurement results of ISDP team flexibility are significantly different across the various segments, it is difficult to conclude whether ISDP teams in different segments have different levels of flexibility, or the differences are simply measurement biases because different measurements are needed for different segments.

The negative relationship between the two dimensions of flexibility further highlights the trade-off between the scope of the ISDP team’s responses (Response Extensiveness) and its ability to efficiently address changes (Response Efficiency). The measurement results of the ISDP team flexibility revealed a significant flexibility gap: ISDP teams experienced and responded more extensively to business changes than to technology changes, but the teams were significantly less efficient in dealing with the former than with the latter. This finding suggests that researchers need to help managers attack the flexibility gap by developing theories about how ISDP teams can increase their capabilities of managing business changes so that the relationship between Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency becomes positive instead of negative.

## Implications for practice

In our last 5 years of field research involving close interactions with IS executives and ISDP managers in 14 large companies, we have observed that managers have difficulties in articulating and agreeing on what constitutes ISDP team flexibility, which in turn leads to the difficulties of appropriately assessing and improving flexibility. Many ISDP teams operate in a reactive mode when dealing with changes, relying on ad hoc measures and procedures. This research provides an initial framework to help managers to articulate and assess the dynamics of their project environments and to improve their ability to respond to major changes.

The measurement model and scales can be applied in several ways. Managers must recognize that ISDP team flexibility is a multi-dimensional concept consisting of two distinct flexibility dimensions (Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency) and two distinct categories of changes (business and technology). The specific types of changes identified in this study can be used as an initial checklist from which managers can develop their own list of changes that they must attend to and prioritize based on their project contexts. The measurement scales of ISDP team flexibility can be applied to assess first their current ability and then their desired ability to respond to changes. Based on the current and the desired levels of flexibility, they can identify any existing gaps. Once the gaps are identified and prioritized, managers can take organizational measures to control and reduce those gaps, thus improve ISDP team flexibility.

The measurement results of ISDP teams in our sample have important practical implications. The research finding that business changes were more prevalent than technology changes suggest that managers must prioritize their attention and efforts accordingly. Furthermore, managers should set up projects in ways that would enable them to sense and respond to business changes in a timely manner. As we discussed earlier, while ISDP teams experienced and responded more extensively to business changes than technology changes, they were less efficient in dealing with business changes than with technology changes. Since we define ISDP team flexibility as a project capability, this flexibility gap can be translated as a capability gap. This capability gap is consistent with the observations that we have obtained from our field research. One explanation for the capability gap is that the ISDP teams typically do not have direct control over the business changes identified in this study. In addition, in the planning stage, most ISDP teams do not pay adequate attention or allocate sufficient resources to dealing with business changes. Another explanation of the capability gap is that as most IS organizations have historically developed strong technical foci and capabilities, ISDP teams typically have more technical skills than business skills. To reduce the flexibility gap, managers must first recognize the need to identify and address such gaps, and then attend to major factors that may cause the gap. Our field research indicates that managers can improve their project team’s ability to sense and respond to business changes by such approaches as having a business unit manager leading the project team, allocating slack resources in project planning, encouraging IT personnel to form partnerships with business users, and enhancing organizational and interpersonal skills of IT personnel.

The finding that smaller (shorter) projects experienced fewer changes than larger (longer) projects, but were more efficient in dealing with changes provides an empirical support for the recent IS development practice that stresses short-life-cycle, fast delivery approaches. Compared to large projects, small projects are easier to define requirements and require less communications and coordination when changes occur. In many organizations, IS projects are required to deliver measurable results in 3–6 months. When a project is too large to be completed in 3–6 months, as in the case of enterprise systems implementation, the project can be divided into phases, or time-boxes, with well-defined deliverables for each stage or time-box. The results of our study suggest that managers can more effectively sense and respond to changes if they keep their projects small or short.

## Limitations and directions for future research

Limitations should be recognized when interpreting and applying the results of our study. First, as an initial attempt to conceptualize and operationalize the ISDP team flexibility construct, we focused on identification of the most important types of changes in the project’s business and technology environments and selection of the most critical flexibility dimensions. Thus, there is a possibility of an inadvertent exclusion of other types of changes and flexibility dimensions. For example, since we conceptualized ISDP team flexibility as the ability to respond to the changes in the project environments, the change types we identified did not include changes in the composition of the project team itself, particularly personnel changes. Further research is needed to validate and extend our results by examining other types of changes. The two flexibility dimensions, Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency, were selected because they were judged by the ISDP managers to represent and cover the specific aspects of flexibility suggested by the literature, such as range, effort, time, cost, and difficulties. Future research should examine the relationships between the two dimensions we identified and those specific flexibility aspects.

In testing the nomological validity of the measurement, the same respondent provided information about both the independent and the dependent variables. This data collection method might cause potential common source biases. Future studies should test the nomological validity using different informants for the independent and the dependent variables.

Sound measurements can only be established through a series of replications and validations. Future research should validate and refine our measurement scales with comparative data. In addition, in order to ensure the appropriateness of applying the measurement scales to different types and contexts of ISDPs, future research is needed to examine the robustness or generalizability of the ISDP team flexibility measurement scales across different types and contexts of projects. In this study, we used the factorial invariance test to validate the generalizability of the measurement scales across project team size, budget size, and project duration. Similar factorial invariance methods can be used to test the robustness or generalizability of the measurement scales across other project segmentations, such as types of projects (in-house development, off-the-shelf software implementation, or systems maintenance) and different systems development methodologies (structured design or objected-oriented method).

This paper developed and established the validity and reliability of the measurement scales of the ISDP team flexibility construct. The next logical step for research is to use the measurement scales to develop and test theories related to ISDP team flexibility. ISDP managers need to understand not only how to identify their projects’ flexibility gaps but also how to close the gaps. The measurement scales can be used to assess the current and the desired levels of flexibility, thus identify flexibility gaps. To close the flexibility gaps, however, theories are needed to provide managers with insights about the organizational antecedents and mechanisms that improve or hinder ISDP team flexibility. To the extent that project managers can control and manipulate those antecedent factors, they can achieve the desired level of ISDP team flexibility. Since our results suggested a flexibility gap in the contemporary ISDP teams’ efficiencies in dealing with business changes, future research studying the determinants of ISDP team flexibility should pay particular attention to the organizational and project factors/mechanisms that improve the team’s response efficiency to business changes.

Theoretical developments should also examine the impacts of ISDP team flexibility on organizational and project outcomes. ISDP team flexibility is meaningful only when it is linked to certain desirable organizational and project outcomes. Flexibility will not have uniform effects on all organizational and project outcomes across all contexts.
