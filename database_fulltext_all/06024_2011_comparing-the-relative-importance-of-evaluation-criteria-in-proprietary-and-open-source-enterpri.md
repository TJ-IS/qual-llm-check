# Comparing the relative importance of evaluation criteria in proprietary and open-source enterprise application software selection – a conjoint study of ERP and Office systems

Alexander Benlian\* & Thomas Hess\*

\*Ludwig-Maximilians-University Munich, Institute for Information Systems and New Media, Ludwigstr. 28, 80539 Munich, Germany, email: benlian@bwl.lmu.de, and thess@bwl.lmu.de

Abstract. Until recently, organizations willing to acquire application systems have had no choice but to adopt proprietary software. With the advent of open-source software (OSS), a new model for developing and distributing software has entered the stage. OSS has evolved from a generally horizontal infrastructure towards more highly visible applications in vertical domains, giving information systems (IS) managers more degrees of freedom in their selection of enterprise application software (EAS). Although a large body of research exists on the relative impor tance of evaluation criteria for proprietary EAS, the role of OSS in the EAS evaluation process has received little attention so far. To address this research gap, this study represents the first empirical investigation to compare the relative importance of evaluation criteria in proprietary and open-source EAS selection. Through an online survey, we evaluated the responses of IS managers of 358 organizations to a conjoint study spawning 8592 trade-off pair comparisons and 3580 purchase evaluations on proprietary and open-source enterprise resource planning (ERP) and Office software packages. The results show that the relative importance of evaluation criteria significantly varies between proprietary and opensource ERP systems. Implementation factors such as ease of implementation and support are much more crucial in the evaluation of open-source than of proprietary ERP systems, which is generally due to IS managers’ risk mitigation behaviour. Interestingly, there are no major differences in the ranking of evaluation criteria between proprietary and open-source Office systems. We conclude our paper with a detailed discussion of our findings and their implications for researchers, com panies, EAS vendors and open-source communities.

Keywords: open-source, proprietary software, enterprise application software, evaluation, selection, ERP, Office, conjoint analysis

## INTRODUCTION

Initially disparaged by software-adopting firms as suffering from teething problems like bad software quality and insufficient customer support (Lee et al., 2009), open-source software (OSS) has matured and surpassed the stage in which it seemed to be business relevant only in horizontal application domains (Fitzgerald, 2006). OSS is increasingly moving from deployment in back office, invisible infrastructures (e.g. operating or database management systems) to front office, tangible deployment of IS applications in vertical domains. In short, OSS has begun to be incorporated into the mainstream and is considered to be a sustainable phenomenon in the software industry (Bonaccorsi et al., 2006). According to industry reports, the worldwide market for OSS was worth US\$1.8 billion in 2006 and is expected to reach a total market value of US\$5.8 billion in 2011, of which around 25% is predicted to be allotted to application and 75% to infrastructure software (Wurster et al., 2008). Along the same lines, a number of commercial OS application software providers such as SugarCRM or Compiere for enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems have proven how to build a viable business model around the provision of OSS in vertical domains jeopardizing the territories of proprietary enterprise software incumbents.

Consequently, organizations have begun to treat the sprawling, quick-changing OSS market as a serious business, and have realized that open-source also opens up more choices in the selection of enterprise application software (EAS). As major proprietary software vendors, such as SAP, raise their annual maintenance fees inconsistently over time, the open-source alternative not only enables firms to avoid vendor lock-in and use as a bargaining chip. It also promises to outrival proprietary software models in the fulfilment of organizational requirements along key evaluation criteria such as package attributes (i.e. reliability, functionality, ease of use and cost) as well as implementation attributes (i.e. ease of customization, ease of implementation and support). Realizing the benefits of opensource EAS and the additional degrees of freedom in EAS selection, information systems (IS) managers in organizations are increasingly faced with a trade-off between open-source and proprietary EAS. However, without knowing which evaluation criteria are more or less important during open-source and proprietary EAS selection, the danger is high that IS managers are not able to distinguish mandatory from desirable features required in and around a system and will rush to hasty and uninformed investment decisions. Although significant research effort has been devoted to study the relative importance of selection criteria for proprietary EAS (e.g. Jadhav & Sonar, 2009), previous research has so far neglected to assess what relative weights IS managers ascribe to key evaluation criteria in open-source EAS selection.

In this study, we attempt to fill this research gap by addressing the following questions:

1 What are companies’ preferences with regard to the relative importance of evaluation criteria in the selection of open-source EAS compared to proprietary EAS?

2 Why are companies’ preferences with regard to the relative importance of evaluation criteria in the selection of open-source and proprietary EAS similar or different?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we integrate a substantial body of literature concerning proprietary EAS evaluation with literature dealing with characteristics of OS communities that is relevant for EAS evaluation. In section 3, we describe our research methodology used in this study to collect and analyse empirical data from an online-based conjoint study. In section 4, we present the results of our empirical analysis. Section 5 concludes with a discussion of theoretical and practical implications of our findings, as well as limitations and future research opportunities.

## BACKGROUND AND PRIOR WORK

## Prior research in proprietary EAS evaluation

A considerable body of knowledge has accumulated over the last decades on analysing the relative importance of evaluation criteria in EAS selection (e.g. Bacon, 1992; Jadhav & Sonar, 2009). In this respect, a multitude of evaluation factors have been used to assess different kinds of EAS ranging from management support EAS such as Office, Accounting, Expert or Decision Support systems to (cross-)functional support EAS such as ERP, customer relationship management (CRM) or e-commerce systems. While the evaluation criteria assessed in these studies most often covered functional, quality-, vendor- and cost-related characteristics, the level of detail and thus the granularity highly varied across these studies. Based on a comprehensive literature analysis on EAS evaluation factors, Keil & Tiwana (2006) came to the conclusion that seven evaluation criteria – namely cost, functionality, reliability and ease of use (so-called package attributes) as well as ease of customization, ease of implementation and support (so-called implementation attributes) – are the most used and most salient factors that have been assessed so far. These seven factors not only provide a practical and manageable list of evaluation criteria, but also allow researchers to analyse a parsimonious model on EAS evaluation which is an important ingredient for good theory development (Whetten, 1989).

In this study, our major aim is to take this established set of evaluation factors and add further insights into their relative importance in proprietary and open-source ERP and Office system selection. We chose these two application types because they can be considered as standing at the opposing ends of a continuum of implementation (and customization) complexity and thus bring along very different sets of business requirements, which is relevant to paint a more differentiated picture for proprietary vs. open-source EAS evaluation. While ERP systems are enterprise-wide software packages that usually require high levels of customization during their implementation (Olsen & Saetre, 2007), Office suites usually do not have to be customized extensively to the individual needs of companies because of their high level of standardization (Haigh, 2006).

Regarding the relative importance of evaluation criteria in ERP selection, extant literature (see Table A1 for an overview in Appendix A) found that package attributes predominantly outranked implementation attributes during ERP evaluation (e.g. Baki & Cakar, 2005). In this respect, the functionality of the ERP system was most often cited as the most critical evaluation factor as without proper functionality, the implementation and use of the ERP system would be worthless (e.g. Wei et al., 2005). Interestingly, due to a high perceived dissatisfaction with the overall quality of ERP systems, reliability was also consistently mentioned to be one of the top factors that should be considered in ERP evaluation (e.g. Keil & Tiwana, 2006). Empirica studies on the relative importance of the cost to acquire an ERP system yielded diverse results. While some studies found that cost is the most tangible factor that is needed as input to business case evaluations and directly affects the information technology (IT) budget, others found that costs are a subordinate evaluation criterion because it was only an indirect measure of the value that an ERP system can create for a company’s business (e.g. Teltumbde, 2000). Similarly, ease of customization was predominantly assigned a medium relative importance. Although companies valued the flexibility they gained with greater ease of customization, many IS managers indicated that they did not consider it as an imminent factor in ERP evaluation (e.g. Keil & Tiwana, 2006). The ease of use of ERP systems as evaluation factor has been seen at the lower end of most empirical ranking studies. Although it would contribute to increase working productivity to some extent, it is most frequently considered to be only a means to capture the benefits from the underlying software functionality (e.g. Calisir & Calisir, 2004). As the implementation of ERP systems entail large implementation efforts and organizational transformations, IS managers rated ease of implementation’s relative importance in ERP evaluation as rather low. Main reasons are that – as the ERP vendor market is already relatively mature – they would not perceive a relative difference between vendors’ implementation capabilities (e.g. Lien & Chan. 2006). The same reason is most often put forth for the support of ERP systems provided by vendors and/or system integrators (e.g. Keil & Tiwana, 2006). This would overall lead IS managers to focus more on the ERP system’s package attributes.

Research on the relative importance of critical evaluation factors in Office system selection is embedded into the stream of office automation literature that goes back to the late 1970s and early 1980s (e.g. Hirschheim, 1985). In their seminal study, Beck & Lin (1981) proposed a hierarchical system selection methodology for automated office systems and found in a case study that the functionality and ease of use would be the two most important factors in Office system evaluation. These results were also largely confirmed by follow-up studies (e.g. Seidmann & Arbel, 1983; Sassone, 1987). Office suites are used in day-to-day business throughout the company (Haigh, 2006). If these systems did not provide proper functionality to support processes and constrained users to do their work in an efficient way, the acquisition of Office systems would completely miss its target (Ngai & Chan, 2005). Anderson & Chen (1997) have found that ease of use have even gained in importance over time due to new software capabilities and user interface improvements, while functionality have remained the dominant factor in Office suite selection. Although Office systems were often mentioned to be relatively inexpensive compared to bigger EAS acquisitions such as ERP or CRM systems, prices for Office system licenses would considerably vary in the software market making costs a relatively more important factor to consider in Office suite evaluation (Ngai & Chan, 2005). According to empirical studies, the ease of customization was reported to have a low to medium relative importance in Office suite selection (e.g. Deacon et al., 2004). Because Office systems are mature and highly standardized software products, most companies take it for granted that proprietary Office suites offer standardized modules (e.g. add-ins or plug-ins) and interfaces that facilitate the extension of the program’s functionality. In the same vein, companies do not consider support services as being a crucial evaluation criterion because there is not much of a difference between Office system vendors that all provide a dedicated support team (e.g. Boehm & Abts, 2007). In the majority of empirical studies investigating the relative importance of reliability and ease of implementation in Office system evaluation, these two evaluation criteria ranked lowest. According to these results, IS managers reasoned that as Office systems did not depend on network connections and high volumes of data transmissions, they would not be as vulnerable to data breaches and system outages as ERP or CRM systems would be (Noble, 1995). Finally, empirical studies found that the ease of implementation was a less important evaluation factor especially in Office suite selection because the installation of Office software usually does not entail large deployment complexity, interoperability issues and training efforts, which is rather known from ERP implementation projects (e.g. McNurlin & Sprague, 2005).

Overall, it can be noted that a solid body of knowledge has accumulated in ERP and Office evaluation research over the last couple of years. However, existing findings all refer to the selection of proprietary EAS. With the advent of open-source EAS though, IS decision makers are increasingly faced with the challenge to evaluate not only proprietary but also open-source EAS.

## The evaluation of open-source EAS

## Open-source software and the emergence of open-source EAS

OSS refers to software that is distributed under an Open Source Initiative license that guarantees the buyer the right to read, redistribute, modify and use the software freely (Ljungberg, 2000). OSS is not necessarily free of charge, however. An open-source distributor may charge for services related to distributing, implementing or otherwise supporting the software, or there may be charges for proprietary extensions (Dahlander, 2005). By contrast, proprietary software is distributed under a license that restricts, to a much greater extent, its buyers’ usage (Nelson et al., 2006). Proprietary software usually belongs to a specific individual or company and is often protected by a patent or copyright law, while its source code is kept closed (Hemphill, 2006). Open-source can be seen as bazaar-like movements where generally Internet-based communities of predominantly voluntary software developers, bug testers, patch submitters and end-users collectively develop software based on communications that are openly accessible and publicly available over the web (Raymond, 2001). These movements are distributed, informal and loosely coupled communities kept together by strong common values based on intrinsic motivation (e.g. intellectual gratification, learning opportunities, enjoyment) and altruism (Krishnamurthy, 2006). Open-source development efforts are referred to as ‘projects’, and developers generally support continued development.

The open-source movement is predicted to challenge the status quo in the software marketplace, since OSS has moved beyond the desktops of code hackers and is now in production in a growing number of corporate IS departments (Watson et al., 2008). A number of commercial OS application software providers such as SugarCRM or Compiere for ERP systems have also shown that the provision of open-source EAS in vertical domains is not only a fad, but a serious and sustainable business model. Furthermore, due to its decentralized software development practices and governance structures that are primarily based on non-pecuniary rewards, OSS is not only considered to provide a different user experience but also suggests requiring an emphasis of evaluation criteria that is different to that in traditional EAS evaluations (Bonaccorsi & Rossi, 2003).

## Open-source EAS in fulfilling key evaluation criteria

Given the paucity of empirical research in open-source EAS evaluation, we reviewed academic papers and practitioner articles on how OSS fulfils the most salient evaluation criteria considered in open-source EAS evaluation.

First of all, cost is frequently cited as key criteria that makes open-source EAS appealing to companies (Ven et al., 2008). The typical way of describing this appeal is that OSS is free o cost. However, while OSS licenses for open-source EAS can be obtained for zero or low cost, the total cost of ownership is not zero. Compared to proprietary EAS, costs for open-source EAS are rather shifted from acquisition to implementation, customization and bug fixing (Waring & Maddocks, 2005). In terms of functionality, open-source EAS is said to provide functionality that proprietary packaged EAS often does not have (Bessen, 2005). Firms with specialized requirements can benefit from access to the software’s source code, and thus may modify and augment the functionality of the application system according to their specific needs. As a downside, OSS developers are often blamed for their distance to real businessuser needs, because OSS has traditionally built on infrastructural software where the functional requirements are typically derived from the needs of system administrators (Fitzgerald, 2006). The reliability of OSS is often discussed in light of software code quality. OS advocates emphasize the superior quality of open-source compared to proprietary software due to the possibility of massive code-level peer reviews, and developers’ more intrinsic motivation and higher sense of ownership in the OS community (Capra et al., 2008). Empirical studies have found, however, that testability, simplicity, readability and self-descriptiveness as indicators of OSS structural code quality provided results lower than the quality implied by proprietary standards (e.g. Stamelos et al., 2002). Opinions are also divided over the criterion ‘ease of use’. Since OSS developers are known to emphasize function over form, most OSS projects are driven by the functional requirements of developers who tend to regard the design of graphical user interfaces as ‘non-sexy’ work (Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003). In contrast, researchers arguing in favour of OSS in general, and of open-source EAS in particular, claim that it has considerably progressed from these early, amateurish stages (Fitzgerald, 2006).

Using a multi-case studies design, Waring & Maddocks (2005) found that organizations benefited from open-source EAS’ high ease of customization. Through the open access to the source code, using OSS enabled organizations to provide tailored applications instead of the limited choices that proprietary software packages may offer. On the other hand, literature reviews on best practices frameworks in SW development and use (e.g. ITIL, SPICE) revealed that the process of OSS customization is not sufficiently covered by these frameworks and thus still entails a high level of process uncertainty (Keßler & Alpar, 2009). Proponents of open source EAS argue that OSS, in contrast to proprietary software, is based on open standard interfaces that allow software to be more easily implemented and integrated into an existing application landscape (Panetto & Molina, 2008). On the other hand, substantial training costs for users accustomed to using proprietary software, along with costs that dovetail the opensource system with customized in-house applications, are cited as examples for considerable implementation obstacles (Joch, 2004). Finally, another potential rub for organizations looking to deploy open-source applications lies in the support that is provided around the EAS. Many organizations view open-source applications as risky, with little or no assurance that the software will be supported in the present and future. However, it is claimed that similar to proprietary EAS, there is a spectrum of support models available for open-source EAS: do it yourself, join a consortium, get consultancy or vendor support (Rahtz & Metcalfe, 2004).

## EMPIRICAL METHODS

## Data collection procedures

In order to analyse the relative importance of evaluation criteria across open-source and proprietary ERP and Office systems, we conducted an online survey during June and July 2009. All German companies of different industries were chosen as population of this study. Company size restrictions were not necessary, because both proprietary and open-source ERP and Office systems are suitable to be implemented and used in companies of all sizes (Kumar & Hillegersberg, 2000). For our online survey, a random sample of IS managers in 1500 German companies was drawn from Hoppenstedt’s firm database and invited by mai and email to participate. In our cover letters, we explicitly addressed those IS managers tha were, among other things, responsible for the procurement of EAS, as they were the most adequate respondents for our study. The survey underwent both a pre-test and pilot phase. Content and face validity of our survey instrument was ensured by asking 10 practitioners and three academic experts to fill out the survey and then provide feedback on usability and comprehension. To reduce self-reporting bias, each participant was given the opportunity to receive a report regarding how his/her firm position compares to firms of similar size and industry along the key selection criteria in this study. Our final survey instrument comprised three parts (see Figure 2).

In the first part, participants were provided introductory instructions including the study context and definitions of the evaluation criteria. In the second part, participants were randomly assigned to two different groups. One group had to answer questions regarding proprietary EAS, the other regarding open-source EAS. In either of these two groups, participants had to conduct two successive adaptive conjoint analyses (ACA) on ERP and Office systems (i.e. the order of ERP and Office systems presented to the participant was also randomized) and rate their overall satisfaction with the fulfilment of these evaluation criteria based on their own experience or from hearsay. In part 3 of the online survey, participants were asked to answer questions on socio-demographic data such as industry affiliation, firm size, prior experience with open-source EAS and software purchasing decisions.

## Survey sample and respondent demographics

We received 358 completed sets of responses, resulting in a response rate of 24%. Our online-survey system had randomly assigned 182 companies to the two proprietary ACAs, 176 to the two open-source ACAs. This provided us 8592 trade-off pair comparisons between software packages (12 pair comparisons ¥ 358 respondents ¥ 2 ACAs per respondent) and 3580 purchase evaluations of software packages (5 purchase evaluations ¥ 358 respondent ¥ 2 ACAs per respondent) for our statistical analyses. Non-response bias was assessed by verifying that (1) respondents’ demographics (see Table 1) were similar to those of previous studies and (2) by ensuring that early and late respondents were not significantly different (Armstrong & Overton, 1977).

All t-tests between the means of the early and late respondents showed no significant differences, and the demographics of our sample were similar to the demographics reported by other software evaluation studies (Keil & Tiwana, 2006). The random sample included firms with the following industry split: manufacturing (27%), wholesale and retail trade (21%), financial intermediation (17%), telecommunication, information, media and entertainment industries (TIME) industries (14%), construction and real estate (10%), logistics (6%), public and health care sector (4%) and electricity/gas/water supply (1%).

Almost three fourth of the respondents were IS managers, one fourth were either senior IS executives (e.g. Chief Information Officer (CIO), Chief Technology Officer (CTO)) or business operations managers. On average, they had 9.8 years of experience in the management of information systems, and had previously been involved in making EAS selection decisions for around 33 software packages. Furthermore, they had accumulated significant experience about OS applications in the past. More than 60% answered that they have been using OS EAS in their companies for more than 2 years. Altogether, the respondents were highly qualified to answer the survey questions.

## Adaptive conjoint analysis

Conjoint analysis (CA) is a decompositional method to determine respondents’ relative preferences for different product attributes by statistically calculating part-worth utilities (Green & Srinivasan, 1990). In comparison with compositional methods, the advantage of decompositional methods is that, during the process of data collection, respondents can evaluate complete product configurations rather than just individual attributes. Adaptive conjoint analysis (ACA) is an advancement on the classical full-profile CA, as it incorporates the advantages of letting respondents evaluate complete product configurations but does not require every possible combination to be presented (Johnson, 1987). In IS research, ACA has been primarily used in the context of feature analysis of specific application systems (e.g. Ballou & Pazer, 1995; Mookerjee & Mannino, 2000). However, it has been widely neglected in the EAS evaluation literature that has been dominated by methods such as the analytic hierarchy process (e.g. Lai et al., 2002) or the nominal group technique (e.g. Teltumbde, 2000). Our choice of the adaptive conjoint design approach was thus motivated by three considerations. First, while conjoint analyses are frequently used by marketing researchers to understand the trade-offs between product attributes in purchase decisions, they are still an under-utilized method in the IS literature and thus can make a relevant methodological contribution. Second, this approach combines the generalizability of a survey with the precision of a laboratory experiment. Third, a conjoint design allowed us to examine how managers concurrently consider several criteria in assessing each EAS by deriving relative weights for the decision criteria that are based on a utility function.

In the context of our study, an IS manager’s total utility from a software package configuration is equal to the sum of the utilities he or she receives from each one of the specifications comprised therein: $u _ { i c } = u _ { i } ( c o _ { c } ) + u _ { i } ( f _ { c } ) + u _ { i } ( r _ { c } ) + u _ { i } ( e o u _ { c } ) + u _ { i } ( e o c _ { c } ) + u _ { i } ( e o i _ { c } ) + u _ { i } ( s _ { c } )$

Let $U _ { I C }$ denote an IS manager i’s total utility from a software package configuration c. We assume attributes to be compensatory in nature, thus justifying a simple addition approach. The total utility is a function of $u _ { i } ( k _ { c } )$ , representing consumer i’s part-worth utility (or part-worths) from the specification of the criterion k ∈ {cost co | functionality f | reliability r | ease of use eou | ease of customization eoc | ease of implementation eoi | support s} in product configuration c (Johnson, 1987). In this model, the total utility of a software package configuration is the sum of its part-worths. They are determined by the ratio between a factor’s utility range and the utility ranges of all factors and thus are percentage weights. In terms of our key research questions, the relative weights answer the question what evaluation criteria matter more or less relative to others in EAS evaluation decisions. The typical ACA procedure consists of three steps, which are described in more detail for our study’s context in Appendix B (Johnson, 1987).

## RESULTS

## Relative importance of evaluation criteria

Figure 3 shows the relative weights (or part-worth utilities u (k )) managers implicitly ascribed to each factor in the evaluation of proprietary and open-source ERP and Office systems while going through the ACA steps. It also shows the results of paired sample t-tests to test for mean equality of the relative weights.

For the selection of proprietary ERP systems, managers ranked reliability and functionality highest, ease of implementation and support lowest. Interestingly, the factor cost ranked only fifth among all seven factors. Most notably, the ranking of evaluation criteria for opensource ERP systems was almost turned upside down. While cost. support and ease of implementation were the top three selection criteria, reliability and functionality were among the least important factors besides ease of customization. Thus, while EAS package attributes were the dominant factors in proprietary ERP environments, implementation attributes ranked much higher in open-source environments. Comparing the relative importance of single evaluation factors across proprietary and open-source ERP systems using paired sample t-tests revealed significant (mean) differences for all factors except for ease of use. The results thus show that there is a stark and significant difference in IS managers perception regarding the criticality of selection criteria in proprietary and open-source ERP system environments.

For the selection of proprietary Office systems, the top three selection criteria are ease of use, functionality and cost, whereas ease of customization and ease of implementation were at the bottom of the ranking. A slightly different picture could be found for open-source Office systems where support joined the top two factors ease of use and functionality as third most important selection criteria. Cost ranked fourth, while reliability and ease of customization ranked lowest. Once again, we compared the relative weights of selection criteria across proprietary and open-source Office systems with paired sample t-tests. We found that the differences between the relative weights of proprietary and open-source Office systems are much smaller and most often not significant. We could identify significant differences just for the factors cost, ease of implementation and support.

Overall, when moving from a proprietary to an open-source software environment, we could identify for both application types that ease of implementation and especially suppor become more important as evaluation criteria, whereas reliability and functionality decrease in importance.

The results for proprietary ERP systems show that IS managers are relatively satisfied with the achievement of implementation attributes (i.e. in particular ease of implementation and support), while they are rather dissatisfied with the fulfilment of functionality, ease of use and reliability. Furthermore, IS managers were also moderately dissatisfied with the cost associ ated with proprietary ERP systems. The results for open-source ERP systems show quite a different picture. While IS managers are rather satisfied with functionality, reliability and ease of customization, they are rather dissatisfied with ease of use, cost, ease of implementation and support. Similar to our results in Figure 3, paired sample t-tests revealed significant differences between proprietary and open-source environments regarding the overall satisfac tion with the fulfilment of evaluation criteria.

In contrast to ERP systems, the differences between satisfaction values for proprietary and open-source Office systems were not significant in most cases (see Figure 5). Except for the factors cost $( t = 2 . 1 7 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and support $( t = 3 . 6 4 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ), where significant differences could be found, the mean values of satisfaction ratings for proprietary and open-source Office systems were approximately on par. Accordingly, satisfaction with the fulfilment of functionality and ease of use was relatively low for both proprietary and open-source Office systems, while it was moderately high for reliability and ease of customization. Finally, overall satisfaction was highest for the fulfilment of ease of implementation.

## DISCUSSION

## Research implications and contributions

By integrating previous literature on proprietary EAS evaluation with OSS literature and by empirically comparing evaluation behaviour in proprietary and OS EAS regimes, our study could derive several crucial implications that both complement and significantly extend existing research.

The main finding in our study is that preferences regarding the prioritization of criteria in open-source and proprietary EAS evaluation increasingly deviate from one another with higher levels of implementation complexity and customization requirements surrounding the EAS evaluation. In this respect, the ranking results for open-source and proprietary ERP evaluation differed considerably. While IS managers attached more importance to package criteria (i.e. reliability and functionality) in proprietary ERP evaluation largely validating previous empirical results (e.g. Bernroider & Koch, 2001; Keil & Tiwana, 2006), they put relatively more weight on implementation criteria (i.e. support and ease of implementation) in open-source $E R P$ evaluation. From the results of our study, we can infer that decision makers may still not feel comfortable with open-source in the post-acquisition phase when it comes to implementing and supporting an EAS that brings along high levels of organizational change and implementation complexity. This perception was particularly reflected in high dissatisfaction levels regarding the fulfilment of implementation attributes (i.e. for ease of implementation and support) in open-source ERP evaluation. This result is also in line with previous studies in OS research that found that IS managers sense there is still more leeway for opportunistic behaviour (such as shirking or free riding), less professionalism and less ability to enforce commitment and legislation (to appropriate private returns) in an opensource than in a proprietary software environment (e.g. Dahlander, 2005).

Letting open-source touch the inner core of a company, which is in particular required for systems with high implementation and customization efforts, makes IS managers not only anxious but also cautious. By putting emphasis on implementation attributes during opensource ERP evaluation, IS managers thus try to assess these indicators more rigorously in order to anticipate future implementation risks and reduce their (current) uncertainty and dissatisfaction. This risk mitigation or uncertainty avoidance behaviour is usually common in situations where decision consequences are difficult to predict and may entail highly negative repercussions (e.g. Cyert & March, 1963; Brunsson, 2007). By contrast and in line with previous empirical results (e.g. Akkermans & van Helden, 2002), future risks during proprietary ERP implementation can be mitigated by a company by integrating approved and exhaustive sanction mechanisms into the provider contract (e.g. penalties or bonus-malus system) that decrease IS managers’ uncertainty during system evaluation resulting in lower weights for implementation criteria. In the OSS world, these sanction mechanisms do most often not apply, as the absence of hierarchical governance mechanisms (such as employee contracts or directives) not only nurtures an environment of creativity but also of opportunistic behaviour (Bonaccorsi et al., 2006).

The small deviations in the ranking results for the evaluation of proprietary and open-source Office systems can also be explained by the pattern of risk mitigation. Since implementation efforts and organizational change in the wake of an Office system acquisition are much lower than for ERP systems, negative consequences and thus future risks from opportunistic behaviour in open-source environments are also considered much lower in magnitude (Brunsson, 2007). Implementation attributes that help IS managers assess future developments thus are given a relatively lower importance in Office than in ERP system evaluation. The other way round and in line with previous results in proprietary Office system evaluation (e.g. Anderson & Chen, 1997), package attributes such as functionality and ease of use are rather given more weight in the evaluation phase – for both proprietary and open-source Office systems.

From a methodological point-of-view, this study is – to the best of our knowledge – the first to introduce ACA into the software evaluation literature. So far, techniques such as the analytic hierarchy process, fuzzy-based approaches and the nominal group technique have been the dominant methods to help assess the relative importance of evaluation criteria (Jadhav & Sonar, 2009). By providing respondents the possibility to evaluate complete product configu rations rather than just individual attributes in an efficient way (i.e. ACA does not require every possible combination to be presented), ACA offsets much of the weaknesses of previous methods, while entailing additional strengths (e.g. high ease of use and high internal validity of results).

## Managerial implications

The empirical results in our study have created transparency on IS managers’ general pref erences on the relative importance of evaluation criteria in EAS selection. This higher level of transparency is not only relevant for managers who are charged with making EAS package assessments, but also for software providers and open-source communities.

IS managers responsible for selecting EAS can learn from this study what a representative sample of decision makers considers to be the most important evaluation criteria in proprietary and open-source ERP and Office selection. As has been reported in previous EAS evaluation studies (e.g. Keil & Tiwana, 2006), managers often spend considerable time constructing an exhaustive list of evaluation criteria, but put relatively little time considering what weights to assign to the various criteria. When this occurs, important considerations on factors such as functionality or ease of use of a system may be assigned less weight than they deserve. In this regard, the empirically derived weights of the evaluation criteria of this study can be used as a guideline or even benchmark to which IS managers can compare their own thinking when selecting a proprietary or open-source EAS. More specifically, they can be used in scoring methods or choice models (such as linear weighted attribute or linear assignment models) that are oftentimes an integral part of requirement specifications (Anderson, 1990).

Our empirical results can also be useful for organizations and open-source communities developing EAS packages, since our findings reveal IS managers’ preferences as to what factors should be emphasized in software development processes, public relations (PR) and marketing activities, as well as support practices. For example, it is particularly noteworthy that reliability, functionality, and ease of use – for which the satisfaction fulfilment scores of user companies are lowest – are perceived to be most important in ERP system evaluation. Based on this divergence, ERP vendors should think about improving their PR and support activities to emphasize that they really do care in providing the level of reliability, functionality and ease of use that is needed to satisfy their customers. It is also interesting to note that prospective buyers of ERP systems place much more emphasis on implementation attributes such as support and ease of implementation when evaluating open-source as compared to proprietary ERP systems. Proprietary EAS vendors may learn from these results that they should demonstrate their strengths in pitches against open-source ERP offerings that they indeed can provide professional support as well as an environment with plenty of technical interfaces and IT system integrators.

Finally, project coordinators in open-source communities may learn from our results that there are still significant weak points in the development of open-source EAS, especially for those application systems that have to be significantly adapted to the individual and idiosyncratic needs of companies. For both open-source ERP and Office systems, support stood out as crucial factor that was among the top three selection criteria in the ACA ranking and with which companies were highly dissatisfied. In order to address these support issues, opensource project administrators should think over different options to provide support that is more professional than in the past. Companies were also rather disappointed with the ease of implementation of open-source ERP systems. OS communities thus seem to have a lot of catching up to do to help user organizations integrate open-source EAS into their application landscape by providing additional application programming interfaces that easily connect to proprietary standards of legacy systems.

## Limitations, future research and conclusion

Our research study has a number of limitations that create interesting opportunities for future research. First, since our research model was tested using cross-sectional data, our findings provide only a snapshot in time. Since both proprietary and open-source EAS are subject to an ongoing software development cycle, replication studies must show the validity of our results over time. Second, we limited our empirical analysis to two types of EAS, namely ERP and Office systems. Although we believe that these two application types cover a high level o variance in terms of implementation complexity, further research should also include other application types (e.g. CRM systems). Finally, we used a key informant method for data collection. This method, while having its advantages, also suffers from the limitations that the data reflects the opinions of one person. We therefore recommend that future research studies consider designs that allow data collection from multiple respondents within an organization.

In closing, this study presented an empirical analysis of the relative weightings of evaluation criteria for open-source and proprietary EAS. The research has been novel in that it was the first study to compare empirically the relative importance that managers ascribe to various factors in the evaluation of open-source and proprietary EAS. The results provide insights into what factors IS managers value most in acquiring open-source EAS in comparison to proprietary EAS and thus should help them separate the wheat from the chaff. We hope that ou empirical study will encourage further research to investigate how open-source software will transform enterprises’ IS usage behaviour not only on the infrastructure, but also on the enterprise application level.
