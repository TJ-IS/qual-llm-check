# Have We Crossed the Uncanny Valley? Understanding Affinity, Trustworthiness, and Preference for Realistic Digital Humans in Immersive Environments

Mike Seymour<sup>1</sup>, Lingyao (Ivy) Yuan<sup>2</sup>, Alan R. Dennis<sup>3</sup>, Kai Riemer<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Sydney, Australia, mike.seymour@sydney.edu.au <sup>2</sup>Iowa State University, USA, lyuan@iastate.edu <sup>3</sup>Indiana University, USA, ardennis@indiana.edu <sup>4</sup>University of Sydney, Australia, kai.riemer@sydney.edu.au

## Abstract

Developers have long strived to create virtual avatars that are more realistic because they are believed to be preferred over less realistic avatars. However, an “uncanny valley” exists in which avatars trigger aversion when they are almost but not quite realistic. We used a field study to investigate whether users had different affinity, trustworthiness, and preferences for avatars with two levels of realism, one that was close to human-realistic and one a cartoon caricature. We observed behavior, conducted one-on-one interviews, and collected survey data from SIGGRAPH conference attendees who either participated in a live discussion session between two avatars in a VR environment, or observed it via 3D VR headsets or on a large screen 2D video display. Eighteen sessions were conducted over four days, with the same person animating the human-realistic avatar and different guests animating the caricature avatars. The guests who interacted with the human-realistic avatar had a positive experience in the VR environment. The observers had positive evaluations of both avatars while acknowledging their different levels of realism. They rated the human-realistic avatar as more trustworthy, had more affinity for it, and preferred it as a virtual agent. Participants who observed the interview through VR headsets had an even stronger affinity for the human-realistic avatar and stronger preferences for it than those who observed via the 2D screen. Effect sizes ranged from medium to large. Our results suggest that it is now possible to cross the uncanny valley with human-realistic avatars rendered in real time.

Keywords: Virtual Reality, Intelligent Virtual Agent, Uncanny Valley, Affinity, Trustworthiness

## 1 Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) seeks to immerse the user in a computer-mediated environment that simulates the real world yet is different from it (Burdea & Coiffet, 2003; Steuer, 1992; Zyda, 2005). Virtual reality is achieved by placing the viewer in a three-dimensional (3D) projected encapsulated space (typically via a headset) by using a display that overlays the physical environment surrounding the user (e.g., a semitransparent set of glasses) or by using a stereoscopic two-dimensional (2D) display screen. A VR world can also be viewed on a regular 2D monitor, but this reduces the threedimensional effect. Headset-viewed VR is more immersive than a 2D screen because the user is immersed in the projected reality and is free to move and explore the space from different viewing angles. The interaction between the viewer and the projected reality differentiates the immersive VR experience from viewing on a computer screen where the view of the scene does not change as the viewer’s position changes (Burdea & Coiffet, 2003; Zyda, 2005). VR can be free of digital characters, but much attention has been paid to improve the ability of the viewer to interact with virtual characters (Burdea & Coiffet, 2003). Such interactions range from the simplest form of observing the animated characters as a part of a predetermined scene to the most complex form in which virtual characters, who are believably human, interact with the viewer.

There has been a steady move toward creating more realistic characters and avatars (Seymour et al., 2018) and highly human-realistic avatars (Kelly, 2020; Wang et al., 2015). One of the most popular theories is the “uncanny valley” phenomenon, which refers to the effect that user affinity increases as realism increases but drops sharply (hence a valley) as digital characters become almost but not quite human-realistic (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Mori, 1970; Mori et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015). Much research has studied the uncanny valley theory; however, prior research has primarily examined user reactions to viewing static images or scripted prerecorded videos of avatars (Mathur & Reichling, 2016; Wang et al., 2015). An unanswered question is how realtime unscripted interactive communication affects user perceptions (Wang et al., 2015).

The development of human-realistic avatars is an important area of research, as is understanding how users react to them (Seymour et al., 2018). Does a highly realistic human avatar trigger greater affinity or trust than a cartoon caricature avatar, and do individuals prefer one over the other? Since developers spend millions of dollars to push such technologies forward, companies are making deployment decisions, and users are encountering such avatars more frequently, these issues have both theoretical and practical implications. For example, affinity and trust in avatars and virtual agents are important factors influencing whether consumers visit and purchase from online retailers (Etemad-Sajadi, 2016). To better understand the human reaction to human-realistic avatars, this study used a mixed-method design combining observation, qualitative interviews and a quantitative survey because such a design is particularly useful for studying new technologies (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016). We have three research questions:

RQ1: Is it possible with current technology to create human-realistic avatars that cross the uncanny valley?

RQ2: How do user perceptions of affinity, trustworthiness, and preferences differ between avatars with different levels of human realism?

RQ3: How does the virtual environment (i.e., immersive 3D or traditional 2D) impact user perceptions of, and preferences for, avatars with different levels of human realism?

## 2 Theoretical Background

VR has moved from being a research curiosity to providing a common platform for the video gaming and movie industries. Its application has gained “legitimacy in business and educational settings for their application in globally distributed, project management, online learning and real-time simulation” (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010, p. 810). Despite its increasing popularity, it has been adopted by very few companies and little information systems research has examined it (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010; Xi & Hamari, 2019).

With the introduction of inexpensive consumer-grade VR headsets, the situation is beginning to change. Organizations are experimenting with VR and more information systems research is beginning to study VR. Individual virtual competency is an important individual characteristic that affects job performance, job satisfaction, and trust in virtual teams (Wang & Haggerty, 2011). Based on individuals’ levels of virtual competency, managers can establish more effective groups working in virtual reality (Wang & Haggerty, 2011). One stream of research focuses on understanding the design features of VR and their impact on users in specific domains. Zahedi, Walia, and Jain (2016) proposed a framework for new design features in VR that increases patients’ understanding, trust, effectiveness, and intention to use VR for virtual medical visits. Researchers have also looked into the impact of VR and its potential for e-learning. It has been found that VR provides as good a learning environment as a classroom. Specifically, VR provides good support for individuals to develop a sense of presence (Franceschi et al., 2009).

Research shows that users may see an avatar either as a direct extension of the self or as an entity separate and distinct from the self (Schultze, 2011). At the heart of the VR experience is the issue of agency and whose identity observers believe they are experiencing. While avatars are a mix of realism of their driving participants, they also exist as fantastical representations and look and act differently than the person controlling them.

One of the key issues on the technical side of VR research is the creation of avatars that are more realistic (Kelly, 2020; Seymour et al., 2018). From a technical perspective, it remains a challenge to create a realistic human-looking digital avatar. Producing a realistic human face, even in a noninteractive environment, is only achievable with high-end visual effects drawn from the movie industry (Klehm et al., 2015). Excellent work has been done in producing highquality facial rendering and animation and we are on the cusp of being able to conduct realistic facial rendering in real time (Seymour et al., 2018).

## 2.1 The Uncanny Valley and Affinity

The 40-year-old uncanny valley theory (Mori et al., 2012) plays a key role in research on users’ reactions to avatars. The theory argues that users have greater affinity for avatars that are more realistic and more humanlike. User affinity increases as the avatar becomes more human-realistic until the avatar is almost realistic, at which point affinity drops dramatically, creating a “valley,” because an almost realistic avatar triggers unease in users (see Figure 1). As realism increases further, the valley is crossed and the avatar’s affinity increases to its highest level.

Uncanny valley theory uses the concept of “affinity,” which comes from an original Japanese word and is thus open to interpretation in terms of its translation into English. The preferred translation is “affinity,” meaning a natural liking (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Mori et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015), although some researchers have translated it as “familiarity,” “pleasantness,” “acceptability,” or “empathy” (Wang et al., 2015). Affinity indicates whether an avatar is in or across the uncanny valley. The theory also predicts a stronger effect when viewing an object that moves versus a still image.

Uncanny valley theory, along with its nonlinear response to realism, was developed from the study of robots, but it has been widely used in theorizing and evaluating digital characters, such as avatars in AR and VR. Thus, the notion of “crossing the uncanny valley”

has attracted much research and commercial attention (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015). As might be expected for a theory that began with robots and has been extended to digital avatars, research has produced mixed results (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015). The inconsistent findings may be attributed to the ways in which different studies have implemented the nonhuman characters, whether robots, dolls, or digital avatars (Wang et al., 2015), and whether the nonhuman characters have been represented using their entire bodies, just their faces, or other body parts such as hands alone (Kätsyri, et al., 2015). Wang et al. (2015, p. 394) offer a good summary of this research, concluding: “In summary, although the notion of the uncanny valley is plausible and is supported by plentiful anecdotal evidence, rigorous controlled studies have yielded mixed support for its existence.”

In the movie industry, the uncanny valley has also been used to induce aversion by creating characters “in the valley.” Horror movies and TV shows often leverage our uneasiness with clowns, dolls, and almost-human characters to increase impact (Wang et al., 2015; Wolfe, 1997). However, in some cases, the effects are unintentional. Almost-human characters in animated movies have unintentionally triggered uneasiness, sometimes leading to bad reviews (e.g., the digital conductor character in the Polar Express movie of 2004) (Kaba, 2013).

We note that the term “uncanny” has been used in different ways by different researchers. Some VR researchers have appropriated the word “uncanny” to mean not perfectly human-realistic (i.e., not indistinguishable from a real human). In this paper, we follow the original theory and use “uncanny” to refer to the state where viewers experience emotional repulsiveness and aversion toward 3D avatars. This in turn clarifies that crossing the valley does not require the avatar to be so realistic that it is indistinguishable from an actual human, just realistic enough so that it has more affinity than caricatures or less realistic than avatars located within the valley.

The cause(s) of the uncanny valley phenomenon are not clear, but there are many different theories (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015). Three theories are particularly relevant for our research. The first theory argues that the drop in affinity is caused by perceptual surprise (Mitchell et al., 2011; Saygin et al., 2011). In the first 100-300ms after seeing what could be a human face, our subconscious initially concludes that the almost-human avatar is a human and creates an expectation of its humanity. It then directs our conscious attention to focus on it. Our conscious attention is surprised when it determines that the avatar is not actually human and this surprise triggers a negative emotion.

A second theory argues that we perceive the almosthuman avatar to be human but its less-than-perfect features cause us to dehumanize it (Wang et al., 2015). Dehumanization involves a process whereby we perceive a human to lack the attributes that comprise what it means to be a human. It occurs when we perceive people as members of an out-group that is different from the in-group of people like ourselves and view them as animals (less intelligent) or machines (lacking emotions) (Haslam & Loughnan, 2014; Viki et al., 2006), which thus triggers negative emotion.

A third theory is based on evolution and argues that our responses to almost-human avatars are subconscious reactions related to self-preservation (Mori et al., 2012). We subconsciously perceive almost-human avatars to be humans exhibiting a psychopathic personality disorder (Tinwell et al., 2011). These almost-human avatars are perceived to be callous and dishonest because they fail to accurately display emotions and/or behave in ways that are different from normal healthy humans, thus triggering negative emotions.

All these theories argue that changes in affinity are not deliberate; affinity is driven by subconscious processes that are beyond conscious control. The first two theories are based on visual perceptions triggering subconscious processes—a static image is sufficient to trigger aversion. The third theory argues that visual behavior triggers aversion, meaning that the observer must interact with the avatar to trigger the effect; viewing a static image is not sufficient.

The central argument of uncanny valley theory is that as humanlike realism increases, affinity increases: users have greater affinity for human-realistic avatars than less realistic avatars such as caricature avatars (with the exception of almost-human avatars located in the uncanny valley) (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Mori et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015). Thus, differences in affinity represent a key indicator of whether an avatar is in or across the uncanny valley.

Empirical studies that have examined the uncanny valley primarily use static images or scripted video clips; few have explored interactivity (Seymour et al., 2018). Therefore, we have little understanding of how users perceive avatars during interactions. The human face plays an important role in communication (Seymour et al., 2018) because a great deal of information is communicated nonverbally through our facial expressions (Sproull et al. 1996; Williams, 1977). Cartoons and caricatures lack the effects produced by intricate human facial muscles and thus present a much narrower array of nonverbal communicative signals. We theorize that more humanrealistic avatars have the potential to improve communication. According to uncanny valley theory, nearly human-realistic avatars that have successfully crossed the uncanny valley should engender more affinity than caricature avatars (Mori et al., 2012). Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: Users have greater affinity for a human-realistic avatar than a caricature avatar.

## 2.2 Trustworthiness

Trust is an individual’s willingness to be vulnerable to the actions of another for a particular action, irrespective of the trustor’s ability to monitor or control the trustee (Mayer et al., 1995). Trustworthiness is an assessment of whether another person or thing is worthy of trust (Mayer et al., 1995). Trust occurs between people (Mayer et al., 1995) but also applies to information systems (Benbasat & Wang, 2005; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Lowry et al., 2008; Vance et al., 2008).

Mayer et al. (1995) argue that trust is a function of the trustor’s disposition to trust and the trustor’s assessment of the trustee’s ability, integrity, and benevolence. If trust surpasses the perceived risk, the trustor will engage in trusting behavior. Trust is refined through personal interaction (Lewicki & Bunker, 1995; Mayer et al., 1995); it is granted or not granted, actions are taken, and the resulting outcomes are used to revise the perceptions of the trustee’s trustworthiness (Mayer et al., 1995). This sequence is repeated over and over as the trustor and trustee gradually acquire knowledge of each other (Lewicki & Bunker, 1995).

The trustor’s disposition to trust is independent of the trustee; it is a “generalized attitude” learned from experiences of fulfilled and unfulfilled promises (Mori et al., 2012; Rotter, 1967, 1980) and varies from person to person. Disposition to trust is “akin to a personality trait” and in some cases has been treated as one, it is a general tendency that forms a “trusting stand” from past experience (McKnight et al. 1998). Individuals with a high disposition to trust are more likely to presume trust and are less likely to perceive situational stimuli as negative (Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et al., 1998).

The other three elements of trust are based on the trustor’s assessment of the trustee (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1998; Mayer et al., 1995; Robert et al., 2009) and are revised based on interactions with the trustee (Lewicki & Bunker, 1995; Mayer et al., 1995). Ability refers to skills that enable the trustee to be competent within some specific domain. Ability is key because the trustor needs to know that the trustee can perform the task the trustee is being trusted to perform. Integrity is adherence to a set of principles that the trustor finds acceptable and indicates the extent to which the trustee’s actions are likely to follow the trustee’s espoused intentions. Benevolence is the extent to which the trustee is believed to exhibit interpersonal care and the willingness to do good, even absent a profit motive. Benevolence is important over the long term because it suggests that the trustee has some attachment to the trustor beyond the transaction in which trust is being conferred.

Ability and integrity may be more important than benevolence when a task is transaction oriented because the trustor merely needs to have confidence that the trustee has the ability and integrity to complete the transaction as agreed upon (Gefen & Straub, 2004). For advice giving or recommendations, benevolence may be more important because to provide good advice the trustee must take the trustor’s best interests into account, regardless of any profit motive.

Benevolence and integrity are human characteristics (Gefen & Straub, 2004). While we can think of machines as having the ability to perform a task, they lack the fundamental capability to adhere to principles (integrity) or feel interpersonal care (benevolence). Therefore, human-realistic avatars are more likely to be perceived as having integrity and benevolence than caricature avatars that are clearly nonhuman. Because integrity and benevolence affect trustworthiness, human-realistic avatars will likely be perceived as more trustworthy than caricature avatars.

We argue that this same relationship holds between human-realistic avatars and less realistic human avatars that lie in the uncanny valley. Regardless of the cause, the end result is the same: we consider almostrealistic human avatars to be unlike normal humans and ascribe lower levels of trustworthiness to them. Therefore, we theorize that human-realistic avatars are more likely than almost-realistic human avatars to be perceived as trustworthy. Thus, differences in trustworthiness are one indicator of whether an avatar is in or across the uncanny valley. This leads to our second hypothesis:

H2: Users ascribe greater trustworthiness to a humanrealistic avatar than to a caricature avatar.

## 2.3 User Preferences

Affinity and trustworthiness are two important characteristics of virtual agents (Etemad-Sajadi, 2016). Affinity has often been linked to increased preferences for interaction with avatars and websites in general (Cafaro et al., 2016; Loiacono et al., 2007). Likewise, trustworthiness is an important factor influencing both interpersonal preferences and preferences for websites as well as increased interactions with both (Gefen & Straub, 2004; McKnight et al., 1998). We argue above that human-realistic avatars should induce greater affinity (H1) and greater trustworthiness (H2) than caricature avatars. Taken together, we theorize that human-realistic avatars are likely to be preferred as virtual agents over caricature avatars and thus hypothesize:

H3: Users prefer a human-realistic avatar to a caricature avatar as a virtual agent.

## 2.4 Display Format

There are two different ways in which VR can be used. One is an immersive 3D environment, (typically a VR headset). The other is by projecting the virtual world onto a flat 2D display screen. The 3D headset differs in two theoretical ways from the 2D screen. First, the 3D headset enables the user’s view to change as the user moves his or her head or moves around physically. Users are able to peer around objects to see them from a different angle in the same way they can in the physical world. Second, the 3D headset ensures no disruptions to the virtual world. Unlike the 2D screen, which enables users to see other objects in their physical world (e.g., their desk), the 3D headset masks the user’s physical world so that the user can only see the virtual one. We believe that these two mechanisms strengthen the effects of the virtual experience in that they heighten the differences between the humanrealistic avatar and the caricature avatar.

Research comparing 2D VR on screens with 3D VR headsets has revealed some important differences. Ashraf et al. (2015) briefly summarized prior research and conducted a randomized experiment comparing laparoscopic surgery using 2D screens and 3D headsets. This study, along with prior research on the use of VR in surgery and surgical training, found that participants performed better (e.g., achieved faster times and fewer errors) while using 3D headsets. It is important to note that these tasks require direct physical interaction, which our study does not. Nonetheless, we hypothesize:

H4: Individuals who view avatars using immersive 3D VR headsets, as opposed to 2D screens, (a) feel more affinity toward the human-realistic avatar, (b) ascribe greater trustworthiness to a humanrealistic avatar, and (c) are more likely to prefer a human-realistic avatar.

## 3 Research Methodology

VR technology has rapidly advanced in recent years but is still a relatively new topic to IS researchers. In this study, our overall goal is to understand how people respond to human-realistic avatars. We seek to explore the various responses people had to their experiences with avatars of different levels of realism and test our hypotheses by comparing two types of avatars. Thus, we determined that a mixed-method approach using both quantitative and qualitative methods is more appropriate than single approaches using either qualitative or quantitative methods alone (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh, et al., 2016).

Our goal corresponds to what Venkatesh et al. (2013) call complementarity—i.e, the use of different methods to gain a more comprehensive understanding of a phenomenon. We used a concurrent monostrand mixed-methods approach by simultaneously employing qualitative observations, qualitative interviews, and a quantitative survey to answer the research questions (Venkatesh et al., 2016). We used an equivalent design with both qualitative methods and quantitative methods contributing equally (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Table 1 presents the research questions and the data used to answer each.

We conducted the mixed-methods field study at the 2017 SIGGRAPH conference. SIGGRAPH offered an ideal testing ground because it is a major conference routinely attended by graphics design professionals and enthusiasts. One of the challenges of testing VR is the novelty effect. People tend to rate a new technology more positively on the first encounter, with perceptions gradually dropping over time as users gain more experience with it (Lie et al., 2017). Most SIGGRAPH attendees have had ample exposure to VR and digital avatars and are therefore less likely to suffer from novelty effects.

## 3.1 MEETMIKE Event Description

Data collection was conducted at the MEETMIKE event series over four days of the conference. The series featured Mike Seymour interviewing 18 leading guest experts in the field of digital human technology in real time using a human-realistic avatar (“Digital MIKE”) in a virtual studio setup in “Sydney.” The sessions were part of the conference’s VR Village. Each session lasted about 20 minutes. The event environment was a constructed space at the conference that allowed two participants—the “host” (Mike) and a “guest”—to sit on either side of a black curtain, seeing and hearing each other exclusively via VR. The host and the guest engaged in a conversation on the topic of the history, progress, and future of digital human technology. Figure 2 shows the physical layout of the events.

## There were four roles:

1. The host, Mike Seymour, conducted the sessions on stage. Digital MIKE, a highly human-realistic virtual avatar, was developed to look like Mike Seymour.

2. The guest was a well-known industry expert working in visual design and/or the movie industry. There were 18 different guests, one for each session. Each guest was represented by a unique caricature avatar that was custom designed to be a caricature of the guest, meaning that there were 18 different caricature avatars.

3. The VR audience members were four SIGGRAPH participants who observed the interview in VR using VIVE headsets. Since each person participated once, there were 72 VR observers.

The non-VR audience members were SIGGRAPH participants who observed the interview via traditional 2D monitors. Audience size varied with a mean of 30 people per session, comprising 550 people in total. The motion of the avatar was driven by a stereo pair of computer vision cameras worn by the host, augmented by a VIVE capture volume for head movement (using a VIVE “puck” mounted on the head-mounted camera rig (HMC)). Complex custom code using deep-learning face-tracking techniques was used to produce highfidelity facial input data, provided by industry partner Cubic Motion. This input was then interpreted into the rendered expressions of Digital MIKE. Digital MIKE’s face had an extensive range of emotion and expression created by a custom facial rig developed by another partner, 3lateral (Figure 3). Because of the complexity of the model and quality of the textures and rendering, only a chest-up character could be rendered at the required 90 stereo frames per second.

The guest was presented as a caricature avatar. The guest’s avatar was produced using artificial intelligence (AI) technology from industry partner Loom.ai. The guest wore a VR headset, which had been specially modified to provide stereo eye and mouth tracking, via the addition of two sets of small stereo computer vision cameras. This headset enabled the guest to experience the experiment in VR but at the cost of a lower complexity and fidelity avatar. The guest avatar provided tracked head and arm movements that enabled the guest to speak, move, and produce hand gestures. The guest’s caricature avatar used estimated facial expressions, created from each guest using deep-learning extrapolation from the reduced input of mouth and eye positions alone. For all VR participants and the host, these inputs were presented in VIVE capture volumes that enabled the characters to be rendered in the virtual space with the correct head movement. The small audio delay due to computer processing was adjusted to maintain synchronicity between the audio and the avatars.

## 3.2 Pilot Testing

A major challenge was producing expressive faces without creating the awkward realism of a character located in the uncanny valley. ## 3.3 Data Collection and Analysis

The data corpus consisted of three data sets: (1) transcripts of the interviews with the expert guest interviewees during each session; (2) exit interviews with participants who witnessed the session (VR and non-VR audience members) and with some of the guests themselves; and (3) a concurrent survey of participants. We excluded the comments of four guests because they were directly involved in the technical aspects of creating the avatars (e.g., one guest contributed to the computer vision subsystem). All guests formally agreed to the use of their comments for research. The qualitative interviewees were selected randomly at the end of each session and all granted explicit permission to be interviewed and recorded.

## 3.4 Session Observation

We analyzed the three types of data concurrently but separately. The first type is observation data from the session recordings of the interactions between the host and the experts who served as guests on stage. These guests were chosen because they had deep VR and/or avatar expertise and understood the current state of the art. All guests spoke directly to the host, making eye contact and experiencing natural facial mirroring behavior during the interaction.

The guests were 93% male, and 7% female. Each guest spoke about their experiences with Digital humans or VR for about 20 minutes, which allowed time to become acclimatized to the experience and equipment. Most of the discussions were unique to each guest, except for the end of the session when each guest was asked what they thought about the Digital MIKE avatar. The question was put casually to elicit a direct personal response, not an in-depth professional assessment. This initial question then led to follow-up discussions about the Digital MIKE avatar. The audio was transcribed and then thematically analyzed in NVivio.

## 3.5 Guest and Observer Interviews

The second set of data comprises separate qualitative interviews of the participants who observed the session, as well as interviews of two guests. Thirtythree one-on-one semistructured interviews were conducted in total. Twenty interviewees (59%) viewed the event on the 2D screens, eleven (32%) used the VR headsets, and the remaining two (6%) were guests. Two thirds were male (66%) and all were adults of working age (over 20 and less than 60); the average age was 36. The interviews were later thematically analyzed using NVivo qualitative data analysis (QDA) computer software (see Table 3).

## 3.6 Quantitative Surveys

Surveys were distributed to observers at the end of each session, including those wearing VR headsets and those watching on the 2D screen. None of the qualitative interviewees also completed a survey. There were 157 valid surveys collected (on average nine per session); 43% were from audience members wearing headsets. About 68% of the respondents were male and 71% were Caucasian.

## 3.7 Validity

We ensured validity within each of the three sets of data by following the normal and customary processes of qualitative research (for the observations and interviews) and quantitative research (for the surveys), and across data sets by using mixed-method principles. We begin with the qualitative research. We ensured design validity (Venkatesh, et al., 2016) by studying an event targeted at experienced VR users, providing detailed descriptions and photographs of the research setting, plus a video of the avatar itself, and recording all observations and interviews. Analytical validity (Venkatesh, et al., 2016) was ensured by linking the interview questions and observation analysis to the theoretical constructs of interest, pre-testing the interviews and observations, having one interviewer do all the interviews, and having two qualitative researchers do the analyses. We ensured inferential validity (Venkatesh, et al., 2016) by conducting the interviews immediately after the sessions and by making sure that critical terms used by the guests and observers meant what the researchers believed them to mean (e.g., “uncanny”).

For the quantitative study (Venkatesh, et al., 2016), we ensured construct validity through pilot tests and by administering the surveys immediately after the sessions; we ensured convergent validity through Cronbach’s alpha, and statistical conclusion validity by checking assumptions and using hierarchical linear model (HLM) and general linear methods (GLM) as appropriate for the data.

To ensure the overall validity, quality, and legitimation of the meta-inferences, we triangulated across the conclusions produced by the three separate sources of data (Venkatesh, et al., 2016). The three sources of data and their analyses have somewhat complementary strengths and weaknesses. The strength of the observation data is that they are authentic samples of behavior from expert individuals directly interacting with the humanrealistic avatar; the weakness of the observation data is that we only had 18 respondents and do not know what attitudes might underlie their behavior. The strengths of the interview data are that we can ask about attitudes, probe for deeper understanding, and have access to a larger sample; the weaknesses of the observation data are that they are self-reported and that qualitative analyses are subject to interpretation. The strength of the quantitative data is that they present statistical conclusions relatively free from biases but lack the richness of interviews.

## 4 Results

We report results first separately for each of the three sets of data, beginning with the qualitative parts, then we present meta-inferences to answer the overall question: “Have we crossed the uncanny valley?”

## 4.1 Observation of Guest Interactions during the Session

The first set of qualitative data comprise the observations of the guests interacting with Digital MIKE. The guests interacted with Digital MIKE in a manner similar to how they would have interacted with Mike Seymour in person in a traditional panel-type discussion at a conference. The human-realistic avatar did not pose a barrier to communication. One common test of the uncanny valley is an aversive response when interacting with an avatar that lies in the uncanny valley (e.g., inability to hold eye gaze, obvious discomfort in body posture, ending the interaction). All guests made eye contact with the Digital MIKE, talked, interacted, and matched verbal cues. None of the 18 guests displayed any type of aversive response, and none ended the discussion prematurely (see Table 5).

The guests’ language during the interaction was characteristically relaxed, conversational, and flowing. The positive emotional and conversational nature of the language was typical. It demonstrated engagement with rather than detachment from the host. The guests referred to the digital host as the real host, speaking as if it were a real person, and did not objectify the avatar as a separate entity. Comments such as “your hair looks great” were expressed, rather than the avatar’s hair looks great. The language of the guests indicated that they did not perceive the host avatar as separate from the person controlling it. Only when asked to comment on specific technical features, did guests refer to the avatar as “it.” During other times the host was referred to as “Mike,” indicating they were talking directly to a real person. When asked if they felt MIKE was looking at them, they indicated positively. For example: Mike: “Does it feel like I’m looking at you now? Response: “Yes, yes, absolutely”.

While the topic of the conversations often discussed the theory of the uncanny valley, it was in the abstract rather than an expression of actually experiencing any negative reaction. For example, when one guest was asked if he found the interaction off-putting, he commented,

no, not at all actually. I was wondering what this would be like, but I hadn’t thought about it until we just started talking about it, but actually it seems completely normal. It’s like when you and I were in New Zealand and we were talking there a few weeks ago.

The guest added, “we have a lot of conversations on the phone with people rather than in person and this feels far more like an ‘in person’ experience than a conference call or something similar.”

Toward the end of each session, the guest on stage was asked what they thought about the human-realistic avatar (omitting the four guests involved with the technical development of Digital MIKE). All 14 guest opinions were positive, with comments such as “it works, it’s good.” All guests indicated they had become comfortable with the avatar and felt that they were directly communicating with the real host, Mike Seymour. One guest commented: “I think this is essentially a successful proof of this type of interaction which we both know will be everywhere in the future.” He also added “this is just fantastic what you’re doing here,” and (humorously said) “after this experience, chatting with you in reality is going to be a bit dull!”

One guest expressed a concern that the realistic MIKE had to be very realistic or it could be misinterpreted. Two guests made critical comments that the avatar’s teeth shading and/or eye moisture were not quite right. None of the guests indicated that the avatar felt uncanny or expressed a desire to leave the session. None said that the medium hampered effective communication. There were no themes related to being ill at ease or struggling to maintain a normal conversation. One guest did qualify their positive opinion, by indicating that visual realism unmatched with accurate fidelity of movement was a potential problem, and one not present in more cartoon representations.

We’re still having trouble with motion tracking reconstruction, the correct movement of muscles in real time. And when there is a mismatch, then that subliminally comes across, as Freud’s fear of the uncanny. We read that (mismatch) as perhaps neurological damage and that makes us uncomfortable. If you pull back the realism to something more cartoony, then I’d be perfectly okay having a conversation.

A theme that emerged was how quickly the discussion moved into a standard style of conversation without any focus on the technology; the technology receded into the background. Three guests explicitly expressed this, stating that the discussion felt like a relaxed normal conversation. For example: “this is really interesting, seeing you here [in the VR setting)]. It’s just like I’m talking to Mike because, actually, I’d kind of forgotten about the VR aspect of all of this.”

A minor theme was that the guests found the experience in the immersive 3D VR to be different than watching a prior session on the large 2D monitors. One guest enthusiastically stated his preference for the 3D immersive experience over watching the same process on a 2D screen. “It was a little ‘funky’ in 2D, if I’m going to be honest, looking outside. Looking at it in 3D, in VR, is amazing. It’s truly awesome. I mean, I haven’t seen a digital face this good, close up in VR before.” Another guest noted: “It’s stellar, no lie, no BS. It’s really cool.”

## 4.2 Observer and Guest Interviews

The observer interviews constitute the second set of qualitative data. The observers were unaware during the session that they would be asked to be interviewed. All those who participated in interviews formally agreed to be interviewed and recorded. The benefit of the field setting was the high prior VR experience (84% reported prior VR use). Virtually no one indicated that the presentation was novel and thus distractingly overwhelming, although two participants indicated that some level of novelty still remained.

Thematically there was a positive affinity for the human-realistic avatar and, within the 33 qualitative interviews analysis, data saturation was achieved. Nodes for positive sentiment were further branched into themes highlighting a particular appreciation for the accuracy and believability of the host’s eyes. Observers reported that the host avatar was distinguishable from a real person but reported it to be appealing, indicating positive affinity. The observers preferred the realistic host avatar over the caricature guest avatars, but this was not a reaction against the guest avatars; there was no dislike of the caricatures (see Table 6).

The qualitative sentiments suggested in all but one case that the observers were able to become involved in watching and listening to the discussion without being distracted by the appearance of the avatar. A typical comment was:

In the beginning, I was so amazed about what was going on, I wasn’t really homing in on what was happening. But then once past the initial shock of the technology, I did home in that they’re just having a conversation.

The majority of the observers felt that the avatars enabled meaningful discussion. One observer commented, “I see the screen and I can feel their personalities.” Another observer, when asked if they were interested in what was being discussed by the avatars, responded: “yeah, actually I was. I was engaged in the whole thing … I was blown away with MIKE … I didn’t think it would be that good. I was very, very impressed.”

Two guests were also interviewed after their session. Both reported positive affinity with the host interviewer’s avatar. One guest, who knew the actual Mike, mentioned that,

it was eye opening, literally. If it was somebody I didn’t know, then maybe the impact wouldn’t have been as great, but knowing him and knowing what he looks like and how his behavior is, his mannerisms … it was like I was talking to him. I mean really close [to the same experience].

There was variation in the interpretation of the uncanny valley concept. Some people seemed to use the term “uncanny” interchangeably with “not perfectly human-realistic,” while others used the term as defined by Mori et al., (2012) to denote a lack of affinity bordering upon rejection. For example, one participant said “The eyes kept kind of glitching … that’s the only time I saw, the uncanny valley … It wasn’t as if this was like—what was that movie with Tom Hanks,” which was clarified to “the one with the train,” referring to Polar Express (2004). Several other observers indicated that they witnessed “uncanny” aspects or “uncanny” effects. All were asked separately “Did you find it repulsive?,” and all answered directly with, “No.” Thus, we conclude that, for these observers, it was clear that the host avatar was not fully human-realistic but it did not elicit the uncanny response as defined by Mori et al. (2012).

Several participants spontaneously described the guest avatar as a cartoon, and one explicitly stated that “the uncanny valley is not a big problem for both of the characters.” No participant mentioned either avatar as being repulsive or off-putting. This validated our expectation that the guest avatar was on the left side of the uncanny valley. Only one participant explicitly stated that he felt both avatars were “deep” in the uncanny valley but answered the follow-up question by stating that neither avatar was repulsive or offputting; again, we conclude that this participant was using the term “uncanny” to mean “not completely human-realistic.”

There was a wide range of differing sensitivity to visual fidelity, with some people admitting they just did not notice some facial aspects, while others were keenly focused on those exact same facial aspects. The majority of comments about the eyes were positive and the majority of comments about MIKE’s mouth identified a lack of fidelity or poor audio timing; yet a few people did not notice these aspects or perceived the exact opposite.

## 4.3.5 Meta-Inferences across Analyses

One of the advantages of mixed-methods research is that it enables the researcher to develop meta-inferences that integrate the different data analyses (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016). Meta-inferences are typically theoretical statements about a phenomenon, including its boundary conditions, and are based on induction, deduction, and/or abduction. We developed three meta-inferences. The first one allows us to formulate a nuanced answer to the question: “Have we crossed the uncanny valley?” The additional two metainferences provide additional insights regarding identity and a boundary condition predicting an aversion to this technology if driven by AI. Both lead us to formulate propositions for future research.

## 5 Have we Crossed the Uncanny Valley?

Whether or not an avatar has crossed the uncanny valley lies in the eyes of the beholder, as different people may have different views. Even though every observer might not agree, as we outlined in Section 3, if a pattern of results shows greater affinity for the human-realistic avatar than the caricature avatar, and neither elicits repulsion, it can be concluded that the human-realistic avatar has crossed the uncanny valley, even though it may not be perfectly human-realistic.

The guests who interacted with MIKE experienced positive emotion toward the human-realistic avatar during the interaction and experienced no revulsion toward either avatar. Observers in the audience who viewed the discussion on a 2D screen or via 3D VR headsets reported positive emotion for the humanrealistic avatar and experienced no revulsion toward either avatar. Participants who completed the quantitative survey reported statistically significant higher affinity for the human-realistic avatar, trusted “him” more, and preferred “him” more. Therefore, we conclude that the human-realistic avatar Digital MIKE crossed the uncanny valley.

We summarize our conclusion in Figure 9. On the lefthand side of the uncanny valley, we located one of the caricature faces used in this study. At the bottom of the valley, we locate the uncanny image, more realistic yet less appealing, as established in our pilot testing. In the top right of the figure, we locate the photograph of an actual human face. Given our conclusion, we can now locate the face of Digital MIKE on the right side of the valley, with higher affinity than the caricature but lower affinity than the real face.

## 5.1 Avatars versus Humans

Our interview participants shifted in their perception of identity between seeing the session as interactions between avatars and interactions between the humans controlling the avatars. In some cases, the avatars were spoken of as separate from the humans, while in others, the avatars stood in place of the real humans. Both the host’s and the guests’ prior reputations enabled some participants to have some level of familiarity with one or both. When discussing appearance, participants saw the avatars as extensions of the humans (e.g., how “real” the MIKE avatar looked).

Figure 9. An Uncanny Valley Example Based on the Same Individual (photographs courtesy of the authors)

However, in speaking of the topic discussions and emotional responses to the experience, the respondents’ language shifted so that the avatars were the source of the emotion, not the humans. When asked to comment on the technology, respondents saw the avatar as a stand-in for the human, but when asked to comment about the interaction, respondents saw the avatar as the actor, with its human controller fading into the background.

This situation may be a good embodiment of Goffman’s (1959, 1974) dramaturgical framing of social interaction as theater. Although Goffman’s work is based on face-to-face communication among humans, his terminology can usefully be appropriated to describe interaction among avatars, particularly the portion that segments interaction into “front stage” and “back stage.” Front-stage behavior is characterized by the presence of an “audience,” individuals who expect one’s actions to be consistent with an official role in their relationship to the audience. Back-stage behavior is characterized by interactions among “teammates,” people who share the same role with respect to the audience.

In our study, the avatars are on the front stage and the humans are the back-stage controllers. For those viewing on 2D screens, the front and back stages were simultaneously present, with the front stage on the 2D video display and the back stage physically present in their visual field. For those viewing on the VR headsets, only the front stage was visually present.

Our participants recognized the distinction between the front-stage avatars and the back-stage humans controlling them. Yet the distinctions were the strongest when discussing the technology, which forced them to separate the front stage from the back stage. The distinctions blurred or disappeared when they discussed the interaction—respondents appeared to engage with the front stage and forget about the back stage, which motivates the following proposition:

Proposition 1: When avatars are highly humanrealistic, the existence of a human controller becomes backgrounded during the interaction and the avatar is perceived as the acting entity.

## 5.2 The Uncanny Valley from a Dramaturgical Frame

Previous theories tend to explain the uncanny valley in terms of image fidelity (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015); the avatar is an imperfect rendering of a human and our subconscious thus triggers an aversive reaction because it is surprised (Kätsyri, et al., 2015; Mitchell et al., 2011; Saygin et al., 2011), it dehumanizes the avatar (Wang et al., 2015), or perceives a psychopath (Tinwell et al., 2011).

Goffman’s (1959, 1974) dramaturgical framing helps us understand that the avatars on the front stage were separate from the back-stage humans controlling them. But what would happen if we were unsure about what was controlling an avatar? Is an avatar controlled by a human or is it a nonhuman virtual agent controlled by AI?

Our interviews suggest there may be an emotional bias against dealing with a realistic-looking avatar that is an artificial virtual agent controlled by AI. It is this awareness of the lifelike, yet artificial presence that several participants expressed concerns about and wanted to avoid. A typical comment from those who expressed reservations when invited to extrapolate on future AI uses, was that the realistic human-looking host avatar, if not driven by an actual human, would “creep you out, but at the same time it is really cool.”

Some went further. When asked to imagine the humanrealistic avatar as a virtual assistant such as Apple’s Siri, one interviewee said, “I don’t think I would want to see a super real face. I feel like I would be more comfortable with a distinction between me and her.” Another said that a realistic face would be something they would like to see on an assistant, but it would be “a little confusing” because of the lack of clarity between what was human and what was AI.

Finally, a couple of interviewees rejected the notion outright. They responded positively to the avatar driven by a real person, but speculated that if this had been driven by AI, they would “possibly find it creepy,” “probably be too much,” or a “bit spooky.” While a minority opinion, it is important to note that the sample was drawn from SIGGRAPH, attended by people who are technically literate and positively dispositioned toward new technologies in this space.

This discomfort arose only when the realism of the avatar approached a near-perfect human form. There were no concerns about the caricature avatars. We conclude that at lower levels of realism, this lack of perfect reproduction prevented a sense of deception and thus presented no issues. However, once the avatar becomes highly realistic, users may find the lack of knowledge about who or what is back stage controlling it as unsettling as prior uncanny valley visual responses. We speculate that a highly realistic human avatar controlled by AI could generate a sense of unease because our subconscious would perceive the avatar as human but, consciously, we would know it was not, thus creating cognitive dissonance.

This theoretical framing leads to very different predictions for our ability to cross the uncanny valley. This framing would lead us to conclude that affinity would increase as the realism of front-stage avatars increases until we reach the uncanny valley. The ability to cross the uncanny valley depends on the back-stage controller. If the back-stage controller is human, then increasing realism will enable us to cross the valley. If the back-stage controller is AI, then some users might not cross the valley; increasing the realism of the interactive character might only increase our cognitive dissonance, leading to lower affinity. Thus:

Proposition 2: When avatars are known to be controlled by nonhuman entities, such as artificial intelligence, they will not cross the uncanny valley.

## 6 Discussion

We used a mixed-method approach to provide a richer understanding and better insights than a single method relying on either qualitative or quantitative data collection. The field setting also provided high ecological validity. Taken together, our meta-inference across the qualitative and quantitative analysis suggests that the human-realistic avatar crossed the uncanny valley. The guests showed no aversion or perceptions of uncanniness as they interacted with the avatar. The vast majority of study participants reported in interviews and on the surveys that they had greater affinity for the more human-realistic avatar than the caricature avatar, perceived the human-realistic avatar to be more trustworthy, and preferred it as a virtual agent. Participants wearing VR headsets had stronger affinity for the more human-realistic avatar and were more likely to prefer it as a virtual agent.

At the same time, our interview results suggest some cautionary caveats to this finding. Humans are hardwired to interpret human faces. Our brains can read faces with far more fidelity than any other object. Evolution has left us with the ability to quickly identify and reject artificial faces that are only approximately close to realistic (Meng et al., 2012). Not only can we detect these inferior renditions, but we unconsciously react to them far less favorably than a simple cartoon caricature (Mori et al., 2012). As VR and augmented reality (AR) become more common, it will become important to ensure that the human faces we see in these environments do not trigger aversion. Our findings suggest that we now have the technical means to cross the uncanny valley, with some important limitations.

The more human-realistic avatar was perceived to be more trustworthy than the caricature avatar. Trustworthiness is an important factor in both interpersonal interaction (Mayer et al., 1995) and interaction with technology (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Lowry et al., 2008; Vance et al., 2008; Wang & Benbasat, 2008). Our avatars were technology artifacts controlled by humans and designed to induce a perception of humanness, so trustworthiness is important, regardless of whether they are perceived to be technology, human, or a bit of both.

Our participants could distinguish between the frontstage avatar and the back-stage controlling human, but this distinction blurred as discussion moved from the technology to the emotional effects (e.g., affinity). Survey participants reported they would prefer the more realistic avatar as a virtual agent, but those interviewed raised concerns about a realistic-looking virtual agent that was not human but controlled by AI. We conclude that we can cross the uncanny valley when avatars are controlled by humans.

Whether participants viewed the interaction using VR headsets or on a 2D screen affected affinity and preferences, but not trustworthiness. The VR headsets obscured the back stage, while the front stage and back stage were always visually present when using the 2D screens. We speculate that affinity and preference may be more superficial emotions than trustworthiness which requires more thought; thus, they may be more readily influenced by the viewing media.

In summary, we believe this research makes three contributions. First and foremost, we showed that it is possible for a highly human-realistic avatar rendered in real time to cross the uncanny valley. This opens the door for the application of this technology to video games and AI agents, which both require real-time rendering. Second, we found that such human-realistic avatars engender more trust and people prefer them. This shows some of the benefits of deploying such human-realistic avatars in games and as advice-giving AI agents. Third, we identified an important constraint/opportunity: these effects were much stronger when viewed in 3D (i.e., a headset) than on a traditional 2D monitor. While 3D headsets are common in video gaming, they are less common in organizational technology deployments, thus suggesting a limitation of their power but also revealing an opportunity (i.e., the need for more widespread deployment of 3D technology in organizations).

## 6.1 Limitations

This study suffers limitations associated with field studies. The first limitation is that we cannot statistically establish causality between the level of realism and trust, affinity, and preference because we did not randomly assign participants to treatments nor avatars to controllers. We selected our participants for post-event interviews “at random” and attempted to survey every observer. However, we did not achieve true randomization. Therefore, because there was no control group and because we could not achieve true randomization, we are not able to establish causality in the same way that is possible in controlled lab experiments.

The second limitation is that the participants for this study were attendees at the leading graphics conference. While established as an important design choice of the study, our findings may nevertheless not be generalizable to the general population. We have suggested, however, that our sample of visual effect and graphic design professionals and enthusiasts is more suited to answering our research questions than undergraduate students or technology neophytes, for example. Individuals who have limited exposure to VR technology are more likely to succumb to a novelty bias, which would make their responses more positive. Thus, we believe that our sample is likely to be more representative of user responses once users have gained experience with the technology, or technology use has become routine. Therefore, our findings are likely to be conservative; one might expect that the initial introduction of this technology to new users with little experience would trigger stronger positive responses.

We also note that using the local student population could have biased the results because the host is a university lecturer and is thus already known and institutionally framed as a trustworthy individual in the university community, whereas the expert guests would have been largely unknown to the student population. In contrast SIGGRAPH participants were unlikely to have a bias in favor of the host, as the guests were equally well-known to the audience. We nevertheless controlled for knowing the host, but the results showed no statistical significance associated with such awareness.

A third limitation is that we did not vary the level of realism of Digital MIKE because of the complexity involved; the level of realism of Digital MIKE was a collaborative effort from several major entertainment and video game companies. The technology was cutting edge and expensive, and it was technically difficult and expensive to create even the one highly realistic avatar. We attempted to mitigate this by using 18 different guests, each with their own cartoon caricature. The effects could also be caused by underlying differences in affinity, trustworthiness, and preferences for the human controllers (i.e., Mike and the 18 individual guests), not the avatars. We did control for familiarity with both Mike and the guest. Mike Seymour, the host, was not more qualified or more well-known than the expert guests.

The fourth limitation is the existence of other possible explanations of our findings. Affinity is not purely driven by the visual appearance of the avatars but influenced by a collection of different factors, such as visual and auditory elements, and the interaction between the avatar and other avatars. It is possible for the viewers to develop different expectations for the voices of the caricature avatars and therefore may be less willing to accept the true voice of the real guest, leading to lower affinity, trustworthiness, and preference. However, the audience was informed of the identity of the host and guests prior to the interviews and could see the real people on stage. We believe there is no misunderstanding about the identities of the avatars, but we acknowledge this as a possible but unlikely explanation of our findings.

## 6.2 Theoretical Implications

We conclude that the human-realistic avatar Digital MIKE successfully crossed the uncanny valley. Ours is the first study of its kind to reach this conclusion for an avatar rendered in real time. Therefore, we view our research effort as both the beginning of a longer journey and the foundation for future research in this domain, rather than an endpoint. While we used a field study to collect our data, more testing using different methodologies, including case studies, lab experiments, neuroscience experiments, etc., need to be done. We did not investigate the impact of any single design aspect of Digital MIKE, such as visual, auditory, or interaction aspects. Hence, more opportunities lie in establishing the impact of single design factors in developing avatars for crossing the uncanny valley.

Our results show that people perceived the more human-realistic avatar to be more trustworthy than the caricature avatar. Trust is influenced by perceptions of ability, integrity, and benevolence (Mayer, et al., 1995); thus, one explanation of these differences is that the more realistic avatar might have been perceived to have more ability, integrity, and/or benevolence than a caricature. More research is needed to understand how highly human-realistic avatars influence trust and the factors that influence trust (i.e., ability, integrity, and benevolence).

Our meta-inferences also suggest an alternative theory for the uncanny valley and raise some boundary conditions for our ability to cross it. Future research should explore the role of back-stage actors in influencing the uncanny valley response. Our participants knew that the front-stage avatars were being controlled by back-stage humans and not by AI.

If the participants believed that a front-stage avatar was being controlled by back-stage AI, could such an avatar cross the uncanny valley? We need more research to test the proposition that it is not only what is visible on the front stage but also the back-stage controller that influences affinity and thus our ability to cross—or not cross—the uncanny valley.

Our results also suggest that VR headsets matter but we need more research to better understand why. Is it because VR headsets make the environment more immersive or appear more real? Or, is it because, in our study, VR headsets removed the back stage from view, and thus strengthened the perceptions of the avatar as an entity separate from its controller? If so, then would a 2D screen that removes the back stage from view have similar effects?

## 6.3 Practical Implications

We make two contributions to practice. First, given that users have more affinity and trust for more humanrealistic avatars, we recommend that developers implement more lifelike avatars. Yet, we do not necessarily advocate the adoption of digital humans for all user interfaces or interactions, given that our findings might not translate to AI-driven agents. Future research is needed to investigate the role of AI in driving digital avatars. Second, the way in which users view the avatar is important; we thus recommend that organizations deploy VR headsets for VR applications.

## 7 Conclusion

We aimed to understand whether it is possible to create human-realistic avatars rendered in real time that can cross the uncanny valley. We examined this question using a mixed-methods approach in a field setting combining observation, interviews, and surveys by assessing affinity, trustworthiness, and preferences for human-realistic avatars compared to caricature avatars. Our findings demonstrate that it is now possible to cross the valley. Our research contributes indirectly to a better understanding of virtual reality and its impact on human perception, attitude, and behavior.

## About the Authors

Mike Seymour is a researcher and lecturer in the Discipline of Business Information Systems at the University of Sydney Business School. Mike leads the Motus Lab exploring advanced human-computer interaction (HCI) solutions and applications. Mike’s expertise and research interests cover the areas of HCI, digital humans, machine learning, virtual production, and the societal implications of digital humans. Mike is an engaged researcher with industry-funded research with partners worldwide, especially in media and entertainment. Mike’s research has been published in various journals, including Journal of the Association for Information Systems and Communications of the ACM, and in proceedings of several conferences including the International Conference on Information Systems, the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, and the ACM SIGGRAPH Conference.

Lingyao (Ivy) Yuan is an assistant professor of information systems in the Debbie & Jerry Ivy College of Business at Iowa State University. Her research interests include topics on the impact of noncognition behavior and decisionmaking, especially the impact of emotion, on human-computer interaction, computer-mediated communication, decision-making, and collaboration. She has conducted research in the fields of electronic commerce, anthropomorphism, and the digital human. She has been published in information systems journals, including Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Decision Sciences, and in the proceedings of several conferences, including the International Conference on Information Systems, the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, and the INFORMS Annual Meeting. She also serves on the editorial board of the International Journal of Electronic Commerce.

Alan R. Dennis is a professor of information systems and holds the John T. Chambers Chair of Internet Systems in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. He was named a Fellow of the Association for Information Systems in 2012. Professor Dennis has written more than 150 research papers, and has won numerous awards for his theoretical and applied research. His research focuses on three main themes: team collaboration, fake news on social media. and information security. He also has written four books (two on data communications and networking, and two on systems analysis and design). His research has been reported in the popular press almost 1000 times, including in the Wall Street Journal, Forbes, USA Today, The Atlantic, CBS, Fox Business Network, PBS, Canada’s CBC and CTV, UK’s Daily Mail and the Telegraph, Australia’s ABC, France’s Le Figaro, South Africa’s Sowetan Live, Chile’s El Mercurio, China Daily, India’s Hindustan Times, and Indonesia’s Tribune News. He is the past president of the Association for Information Systems.

Kai Riemer is a professor of information technology and organisation and head of the Discipline of Business Information Systems at the University of Sydney Business School. Kai is founder and leader of the Digital Disruption Research Group (DDRG), convener of the annual DISRUPT.SYDNEY<sup>TM</sup> conference, and co-host of Australia’s leading business and technology podcast “The Future, This Week.” His expertise and research interests cover the areas of disruptive innovation, technology appropriation, digital work practices, futures studies, social networking, and the philosophy of technology. Kai has extensive experience with industry-funded research and leads a Linkage project initiative on managing end-user technologies, sponsored by the Australian Research Council. Kai’s research follows practice theoretical and nonorthodox approaches and appears in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, or Information Systems Journal. Kai is a board member of Journal of Information Technology and Business and Information Systems Engineering.
