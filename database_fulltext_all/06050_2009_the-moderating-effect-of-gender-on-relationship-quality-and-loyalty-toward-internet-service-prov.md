# The moderating effect of gender on relationship quality and loyalty toward Internet service providers<sup>§</sup>

Manuel J. Sanchez-Franco \*, Angel Francisco Villarejo Ramos, Felix A. Martin Velicia

University of Seville, Seville, Spain

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 2 November 2006 Received in revised form 12 January 2009 Accepted 19 February 2009 Available online 4 March 2009

Keywords: Gender Relationship quality Satisfaction Trust Commitment Loyalty Partial least squares

## A B S T R A C T

Relationship quality – based on Commitment–Trust theory – can explain and predict the success of a relationship between an ISP and its customers, measured in terms of loyalty. We proposed a model to express the relationship and included gender as a moderator in the relationship. We then developed a questionnaire to test the model empirically and used a sample in Spain to check its validity and reliability. Analysis provided strong support for our hypotheses that gender exerted a significant moderating role on our model relationships. The influence of trust on commitment and of commitment on loyalty was significantly stronger for females than males, while the effects of satisfaction on commitment and of trust on loyalty were significantly stronger for males. Implications were drawn for practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

Individual factors, particularly gender, play a major role in the acceptance and use of IT and online services. Nevertheless, the relative importance of gender is a recent topic: little mention is traditionally made of the differences between the attitudes of males and females. We initiated our study in part to fill this gap.

Firstly, the essence of commercial practice should be the development of long-term, high-value relationships with customers. The promotion of relationship quality and customer loyalty, in a virtual, competitive, global commercial environment, is crucial to both companies and their economic sectors. For instance, every customer of e-commerce sites must choose an ISP for Internet access and the ISP must find ways to satisfy and retain the customer [2].

Secondly, research shows that feelings of males about IT have traditionally been more favourable than that of females [13,26]. Females tend to show greater risk aversion and less trust in the use of the Internet. Males score more highly for attitude and intention to use and for their instrumental motivation for acceptance of the Internet.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Relationship quality and loyalty

Given the benefits to an organisation of the promotion of genuine customer loyalty, it is useful to analyse its background. Genuine customer loyalty is a composite concept combining both relationship quality and loyalty. On the one hand, loyalty has been defined as the behavioural intentions to keep on using the brand (or company) in the future [30]. On the other hand, relationship quality depends on the interaction between the firm and its customers; and is based on the assumption that users’ loyalty is initially determined by satisfaction, trust, and commitment [5], which depend on their relationship.

## 2.1.1. Satisfaction

Our study focused on non-economic aspects of satisfaction; that is, the favourable affective response of customers [4] who find the cumulative service interactions rewarding, fulfilling and stimulating.

Here, the relationship between satisfaction and commitment is not the outcome of a single transaction. Moreover, some of the conflicts between satisfaction and trust are due to judgemental evaluation of a specific encounter.

## 2.1.2. Trust

Trust is the psychological state that makes a person willing to be vulnerable to another person and assumes favourable results of other’s behaviour. Therefore, it is ‘‘the confidence in the exchange partner’s reliability and integrity’’ [22]. High levels of perceived risk cause intangible factors, such as reliability and trust, to play a major role in building online loyalty. As Lin [16] commented, online communication needs trust, since such activities lack face-to-face contact. Specifically, the ISP sector is associated with high levels of vulnerability and uncertainty, general feelings of concern about privacy, identity theft, and system security.

## 2.1.3. Commitment

Commitment is the desire to maintain a relationship. It is evaluated by assessing its components [6]. Morgan and Hunt defined it as the belief of one of the parties that the results of the relationship were so important that it was worth making the effort to maintain it. There apparently are two commitment traits: affective (based on loyalty and a feeling of belonging) and calculated (based on rational evaluation). Here, loyalty is greater when it stems from affective commitment.

2.2. Relationships between satisfaction, trust, commitment and loyalty

## 2.2.1. Satisfaction and commitment

As Tax et al. [28] showed, satisfaction can lead to commitment. It reinforces the users’ decision to participate in the services being offered. Thus our first hypothesis:

H1. Satisfaction will positively influence commitment.

## 2.2.2. Satisfaction and trust

Satisfaction is an antecedent to trust [8]. If expectations are met, users will be satisfied and confident. Satisfaction leads to the belief that the same quality of service will be delivered in future. Thus:

H2. Satisfaction will positively influence trust.

## 2.2.3. Trust and commitment

There is a positive relationship between trust and commitment; parties who generate sufficient value from their interaction will be prepared to maintain commitment. It is reasonable to assume that the relationship between the two concepts must be similar to that in other distribution channels [7]. Indeed, trust reduces perceived risk, increases the perception that short-term sacrifice will result in long-term gain, and reduces the transaction costs in the relationship. Thus:

H3. Trust will positively influence commitment.

## 2.2.4. Commitment and loyalty

A desire to act implies a chance that a behaviour will occur; thus there should be a positive relationship between commitment and loyalty [11]. For instance, customers who are committed to their relationship with their service provider are likely to continue/ recommend using the service. Thus:

H4. Commitment will positively influence loyalty.

## 2.2.5. Trust and loyalty

Trust is essential when risks are perceived as significant: it generates loyalty. For example, Sirdeshmukh et al. [27] directly linked trust to loyalty, based partly on Zeithalm et al. Liu et al. [17] also examined the relationship between online trust and behavioural intentions, finding the relationship to be significant. Thus, we hypothesised:

H5. Trust will positively influence loyalty.

2.3. The moderating effect of gender on relationship quality and loyalty

Individual differences influence relationship quality: not every customer is prone to be loyal. A relevant question is thus whether the influence of relationship quality on behavioural intentions is the same for both genders. Males are more motivated by their need for self-fulfilment and more independent; they promote their individual identity, resisting status-based influence. According to the selectivity model [21], they streamline processing of external information by focusing on how it affects them. Females, however, are more expressive, more skilful in sending and receiving nonverbal messages, and are more aware of the feelings of others. They are more concerned with harmony, consensus, and interdependence. Relationships are more important to them than completing a task [3].

Males’ perception of value of a process is mainly based on its effects on their performance. They display a higher degree of extrinsic orientation in their motive than do females. Moreover, females traditionally display a lower perception of IT skill. Whitley [29] found that adult males and boys had higher levels of self-efficacy, which seems to be essential in affecting attitude towards system use. The illusion of control protects the user from possible negative effects that could otherwise cause stress. Users who feel confident are inclined to enjoy their work [25].

Because of lower self-efficacy, females tend to demonstrate greater concern for risk of online services and experience anxiety [12]. They are slower in becoming loyal users and spend longer deciding to try new services. Trust determines the extent to which a female will engage in a relationship.

Computer aptitude of males modifies their trust and commitment: they are willing to assume more risk. The relevance and significance of affective commitment as a mediator is thus lower among males; trust has more direct influence on loyalty, and lower on commitment. We therefore proposed:

H6. Trust has a greater influence on commitment among females than among males.

H7. Trust has a greater influence on loyalty among males than among females.

H8. Commitment has a greater influence on loyalty among females than among males.

Females attempt to assimilate available cues and engage in a more comprehensive analysis of information. Novice users, however, feel that they lack evaluative criteria, and do not process information about technical attributes; processors with higher levels of knowledge about products engage in greater levels of schematic processing, avoiding peripheral cues, such as source credibility and attractiveness or positive expectations about an ISP’s future behaviour. That is, the more a customer has experienced satisfaction with a process, the more positive the accumulated transaction experience (non-economic satisfaction). Females, however, tend to underestimate the relationship between satisfaction and commitment.

We thus proposed:

H9. Satisfaction has a greater influence on commitment among males than among females.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Survey development and pilot study

A self-administered questionnaire was designed to use in collecting opinions about satisfaction, trust, commitment and loyalty; socio-demographic data; and self-efficacy levels in the Internet domain.

Satisfaction was assessed by adapting the scale proposed by Bhattacherjee [1] for the electronic context. The affective commitment scale of Geyskens et al. [9] was used, adapted for the online service being analysed. The trust scale of McKnight et al. [20] was followed. The proposed scale includes the main dimensions considered for trust: benevolence, integrity and competence. Finally, loyalty was assessed based on the proposals of Zeithaml et al. The proposed scale comprises four main dimensions: (a) references/recommendation, (b) repurchase intention, (c) price sensitivity and (d) complaining behaviour—see Appendix A. We used the self-efficacy scale of Ghani and Deshpande [10] – see Table 1B – a five-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’, 1, to ‘‘strongly agree’’, 5.

Four marketing professors knowledgeable in e-commerce techniques approved the suitability of the wording and format. Moreover, a total of 28 pilot customers were randomly selected for interview during the first phase of the study. Their comments resulted in refinement of the instrument in terms of its clarity, completeness, and readability.

## 3.2. Data gathering and sample

Data were collected from a sample of customers, over the age of 16, of commercial ISPs in Spain. Firstly, the exclusion of invalid questionnaires due to duplications or empty fields gave a final sample size of 815 domestic users connected to the ADSL Internet service offered by Telefo´nica Espan˜a (this ADSL service occupies more than 43% of the national market share). The survey period was 4 weeks.

There were significant differences between male and females. Overall, the male sample rated themselves as more skilled in the Internet domain than females. See Table 1B.

## 3.3. Data analysis

Hypothesis testing was conducted using PLS Version 3.00 Build 1058. PLS places minimal restrictions on measurement scales, sample size, and residual distributions, employs a componentbased approach for estimation purposes, and can handle formative factors, unlike LISREL. This is particularly relevant for our research.

Firstly, according to the formative constructs, Mayer et al. [19] noted that trust beliefs are not trust per se, but help in building its foundations. They are not necessarily interchangeable. Secondly, most researchers assume that the construct of loyalty is reflective. Positive loyalty is reflected in the service provider’s ability to keep its customers, paying price premiums, communicating concerns to others, and communicating concerns to the company. We question this, however. These dimensions are related to different facets of loyalty. For instance, as Lam et al. [15] argued, repeat patronage pertains to customer retention and recommendation to customer attraction. Indeed, these authors confirmed discriminant validity between repeat patronage and recommendation. Reibstein [24] also suggested that online prices were relevant in attracting customers but not in retaining them. Therefore, if respondents focused on these differences, a reflective construct could be invalid.

To summarise, three latent dimensions formatively make up the second-order construct of trust: benevolence (3 items), integrity (4 items) and competence (4 items). Likewise, four latent dimensions: references (3 items), repurchase intention (3 items), price sensitivity (3 items) and complaining behaviour (3 items), formatively make up the second-order construct of loyalty.

## 4. Results

A PLS model is analysed and interpreted in two stages: the assessment of the reliability and validity of the measurement model, and the assessment of the structural model.

## 4.1. Measurement model

The initial measurement model was first evaluated using the full sample (815 individuals), all items and dimensions, and then the PLS results were used to eliminate problematic items.

Individual reflective-item reliability was assessed by examining the loadings of the items with their respective construct. Individual reflective-item reliabilities – in terms of standardised loadings – were over the recommended acceptable cut-off level of 0.7, except for COMM3, TR6 and LOY7 (>0.6). The results obtained were, therefore, acceptable. Despite accepting this intermediate threshold, items were left in the model only if the rest of the reliability criteria were fulfilled.

Construct reliability was assessed using the composite reliability (r ). The composite reliabilities for the multiple reflective indicators were well over the recommended acceptable 0.7 level. Also, we checked the significance of the loadings with a resampling procedure (500 sub-samples) for obtaining t-statistic values. They all are significant. See Appendix A.

Convergent and discriminant validities were assessed by stipulating that the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) by a construct from its indicators should be at least 0.7 (i.e. AVE > 0.5) and should be greater than that construct’s correlation with other constructs. All latent constructs satisfied these conditions, except trust beliefs. The integrity dimension was finally incorporated into the benevolence dimension (i.e. goodwill dimension: benevolence plus integrity) [cf. 23]. See Appendices A and B.

Secondly, the measurement model was reevaluated using each sample (females and males). Initially, based on the previous definition of trust and loyalty scales, both constructs were initially designed as second-order factors. This implied that these higher order constructs were measured by a number of first-order latent dimensions. Subsequently, the items for the dimensions of trust and loyalty were optimally weighted and combined using the PLS algorithm to create latent variables scores. The dimensions – or first-order factors – become the observed indicators of secondorder factors. The resulting scores reflect the underlying constructs more accurately than any of the individual items, by accounting for the unique factors and error measurements that may also affect each item.

## 4.1.1. Latent constructs

Most of the individual reflective-item reliabilities – in terms of standardised loadings – were over the acceptable cut-off level of 0.7. The only loading lower than 0.7 was 0.64 (COMM3—female sample; >0.6). The results obtained were acceptable. See Fig. 1.

The composite reliabilities for the multiple reflective indicators (r ) were well over the recommended acceptable 0.7 level. See Table 2. We also checked the significance of the loadings with a resampling procedure (500 sub-samples) for obtaining t-statistic values. They all were significant. See Fig. 1.

AVE measures for latent constructs exceeded 0.5 (see Table 2). We, therefore, maintained the convergent validity of the latent constructs of the model. Also, the square root of the AVE was much larger than all other cross-correlations for both samples. We thus maintained the discriminant validity of the latent constructs of the models. See Table 2.

## 4.1.2. Emergent constructs

For constructs using formative measures (emergent constructs), the weights provide information regarding the makeup and relative importance of each indicator in the creation/formation of the component. Dimensions are not necessarily correlated and, consequently, traditional reliability and validity assessments have been argued as inappropriate and illogical [18].

Firstly, the analysis showed that the repurchase and references dimensions had a significant impact on loyalty, and benevolenceintegrity and competence on trust. See Fig. 1. We adopted the proposals made by several authors regarding the non-significance of price sensitivity and the presentation of complaints. One possible explanation lies in the environmental culture of the samples analysed. Furthermore, in our research, belief in lower price sensitivity was not a singular quality of the samples. An explanation could also be found in the moderating effect of the costs of change.

Secondly, we analysed the possible multicollinearity among formative indicators. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was far below the common cut-off threshold of 5.

## 5. Discussion and limitations

Previous research showed that individual differences would influence the relationship between the company and the customer, since not every customer is prone to be loyal per se. A relevant question then, was whether the influences of relationship quality on behavioural intentions are equal for both males and females.

## 5.1. Implications for theory

The results allow us firstly to conclude that males and females become loyal in their behaviour for different reasons. Females experience a lesser influence of trust on loyalty. Females’ usage behaviour only becomes loyal once they have formed their attitudes towards use (commitment, H4 and H8), which are based on the lower levels of perceived control, the greater associated risks and their type of information processing (i.e. piecemeal analysis of information). Therefore, trust will also determine the extent to which females will engage in a relationship (H3 and H6). Conversely, males show greater self-efficacy and impatience, which directly translate into the importance of the links: (a) satisfaction ! commitment (H1 and H9), and (b) trust ! loyalty (H5/H7).

## 5.2. Practical implications

The most important implication is, therefore, that managers – in order to maintain a genuine relationship between the customer and the ISP – should take account of the gender for the allocation of their marketing efforts between satisfaction and trust initiatives. Building both customer satisfaction and trust is a superior strategy to a focus on satisfaction alone.

Firstly, this would promote: (a) the gradual reduction of risk and technological anxiety; (b) the strengthening of commitment between females (who are more expressive, more averse to risk and more social) and the ISP; and (c) the direct promotion of behavioural intentions between males (more assertive and competitive) and the ISP. The ISP should (a) understand and comprehend customer needs and, above all and (b) be a company capable of attracting mutual commitments, maintaining them, and developing customer loyalty.

Secondly, commitment is shown to be a relevant mediator between satisfaction–trust framework and loyalty, especially in the case of females, who emphasise interpersonal relationships with the ISP more than males. ISPs should, therefore, prioritise support for the control perceived by females and the availability of clear rules and management procedures as well as those necessary legal aspects that will reduce the levels of uncertainty in their relationship with their ISP.

Thirdly, satisfaction decisively influences commitment in the sample of males. On the one hand, ISPs should improve their customers’ connection networks, which prevent technical problems and information overloads, and could increase the customers’ decision to participate in the delivery of the service. On the other hand, increasing brand strength will decrease the impact of other, competitive brands, and curb opportunistic behaviours, enhancing genuine customer loyalty.

## 5.3. Limitations

We should declare that the research has not been restricted to certain motives of an intrinsic or extrinsic nature, and neither

have different online usage behaviours been delimited—or cultural patterns with a notable influence, for example, on trust. In this sense, in future research genuine customer loyalty should be explained through a reciprocal model in which behaviour, personal cognitive-expressive factors and situational issues operate jointly as determinants of each other. Secondly, it would be interesting to analyse differences that are caused by the ISP. It could be possible that the evaluated ISP better fulfils males’ preferences. In the case of female-focused providers the results could then be moderated. Thirdly, the online services examined in our study were of one type—ISPs. Our conclusions – with other online services – could not be generalised. Finally, the explanation of the process of behavioural intentions formation in online context is not enough. Research should advance towards an explanation of the real behaviour expressed by online customers.
