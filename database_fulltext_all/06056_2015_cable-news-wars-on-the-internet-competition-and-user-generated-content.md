# Cable News Wars on the Internet: Competition and User-Generated Content

Gaurav Sabnis

School of Business, Stevens Institute of Technology, Hoboken, New Jersey 07030, gsabnis@stevens.edu

Rajdeep Grewal

Kenan-Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, North Carolina 27599, grewalr@kenan-flagler.unc.edu

cademics and practitioners alike recognize that user-generated content (UGC), such as blog posts, help not only predict but also boost performance (e.g., sales). However, the role of competition in the UGC domain is not well understood. Building on extant research pertaining to the UGC-performance relationship, the authors document empirical evidence for a relationship between competitor UGC and focal firm performance. Data from a 30-week period describe the viewership of competing cable news shows on Fox News, CNN, and MSNBC during the 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>–9:00 <sup>p.m</sup>. time slots. They find evidence of a statistically significant relationship between competitor UGC and viewership and of heterogeneity in the direction of these competitive relationships, positive in some time slots and negative in others. The predictive power of UGC for viewership is enhanced by 3% to 5% simply by incorporating competitors’ UGC, in addition to a show’s own UGC. Thus, the study, as well as formulation of UGC-related marketing strategies, should incorporate competitive relationships.

Keywords: user-generated content; social media; competition; cable news

## 1. Introduction

In November 2008, Kmart, worried about its competitors dominating customer conversations in the social media space, launched a gift certificate campaign for bloggers to improve its “share of social voice” (Lukovitz 2008). Apple’s “I’m a Mac and I’m a PC” campaign, clearly targeting its chief rival Microsoft, went viral and was amplified by the hundreds of parodies posted on YouTube and several blogs. Microsoft responded with an “I’m a PC and Windows 7 was my idea” campaign that involved users posting their own videos, with the aim of making them go viral too (Wilcox 2008). Organizations as diverse as Target, IBM, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation rely on user-generated content (UGC) to predict outcomes and trends (Daily Mail 2012, Hill 2012, Speedy 2011). In the world of cable news, disagreements between conservative (e.g., Fox News) and liberal (e.g., MSNBC) hosts similarly tend to spill over onto the Internet, such that viewers make blog posts and YouTube videos in support of or denigrating the hosts (Boehlert 2009). For example, according to Folkenflik (2013, p. 67), Fox News public relations staffers keep regular tabs on negative as well as neutral blog posts about their own shows as well as competitors, and allegedly counter them using 20–100 aliases to post “pro-Fox rants”. In turn, both academics and practitioners recognize the importance of social media or online UGC as an influential form of marketing communication, which they can use to interact with customers (e.g., blogs, Facebook, Twitter feeds), learn about customers and the marketplace, and make predictions. Research confirms that UGC is a leading indicator of product performance, with strong predictive capabilities (Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Liu 2006).

Because firms use UGC to compete, studying competitors’ UGC should inform a focal firm’s own performance. Yet the relationship between firm performance and competitors’ UGC has not received systematic scrutiny. Anecdotal evidence suggests such a relationship exists, but to the best of our knowledge, no study directly tests for this relationship or explains the direction and magnitude of its effects. We build on prior studies focused on the UGC-performance relationship (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2009), as well as the relationship between competition and performance (e.g., Sabnis and Grewal 2012), to add competition to the UGC context and thereby (1) explicate a relationship between competitors’ UGC and firm performance and (2) demonstrate that incorporating competitive effects significantly enhances the predictive power of UGC for performance. We include a caveat though: competitors’ UGC may have a causal impact on performance, but explaining this causality is beyond the scope of our model and data. Our study instead is primarily descriptive and correlational in nature.

Our study context involves the highly competitive cable news industry, which features three main competitors: Fox News, CNN, and MSNBC. These networks draw their maximum numbers of viewers during three consecutive weeknight prime time slots (7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>–9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> EST), during which they all air hour-long opinion/analysis shows focused on politics and current affairs. The hosts of the shows maintain relatively high profiles, making them celebrities in their own right, and many blog posts get published about the shows daily (see Appendix A for examples). The cable news industry context also offers daily viewership data that provide our performance measure (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Reddy et al. 1998). By collecting UGC and performance data on a daily basis, we obtain a rich, powerful longitudinal data set for modeling the relationship between competitive UGC and performance. Specifically, we gathered data about daily viewership and daily volumes of blog posts for cable news shows that air on the “big three” cable networks in three consecutive weeknight prime time slots (7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>–9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> EST), over a period of 30 consecutive weeks (150 weeknights) during May–November 2009. Our model specification allows for own UGC effects, reflecting the relationship between a show’s own UGC and its viewership; competitive effects, or the relationship between the competition’s UGC and the focal show’s viewership; state dependence to control for the carryover effects of viewership from the previous day and previous time slot; and unobserved heterogeneity in the shows’ inherent propensities to drive viewership. Because viewership for the three cable networks constitutes count data, we adopt a multivariate Poisson specification. We also recognize interdependencies among the viewership of the three networks and allow for unobserved heterogeneity among the networks in any given time slot to correlate, which leads to a multivariate Poisson-log-normal mixture specification (e.g., Chib and Winkelmann 2001).

In this model specification, we also recognize identification concerns with the effect of firms’ own and their competitors’ UGC variables on viewership, because UGC variables are endogenous because of omitted variables (e.g., specific news events) that could influence both viewership and UGC (Manski 1993) and are susceptible to measurement error (Sonnier et al. 2011). Beyond modeling unobserved heterogeneity and a diverse set of show and day specific effects, we take two steps to correct for the endogeneity of UGC variables: First, we include control variables to account for daily newsworthiness (using Google Trends data) and the show-specific noteworthiness of a particular episode (using data from Media Matters, a media watchdog website). Second, we use the latent instrumental variable (LIV) approach developed by Ebbes et al. (2005) to correct for the endogeneity of own and competitor UGC. Even with these steps, we cannot claim that we fully and completely control for all sources of spurious covariance. Furthermore, the assumptions associated with the LIV approach, such as the linearity of explanatory variables and separability and normality of errors and nonnormal distribution of endogenous UGC variables, apply. We test and find support for our assumptions; however, we refrain from claiming causality.

Our findings indicate a relationship between competitors’ UGC and a show’s performance that can be negative or positive, depending on the time slot. At 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, the programming is mainly news and analysis based, and the relationship between competitors’ UGC and show viewership is negative. However, in the 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> and 9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> time slots, when the shows’ emphasis is on ideological commentary, the relationship is positive. A possible explanation of this heterogeneity in the direction of the relationship is the hostile media phenomenon (e.g., Hansen and Kim 2011), which suggests that consumers of partisan media content (such as at 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>) are more actively involved with seeking out opposing viewpoints and rebutting them. However, our data do not include the variables to conclusively test such explanations. Our results, though specific to the cable news context, underline the broader imperative to account for competition when assessing the relationship between UGC and performance. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that including competitors’ UGC enhances the predictive power of models that use the firm’s own UGC by 3% to 5%. Specific to the television industry, it seems plausible that incorporating competitors’ UGC as one of the metrics for competitive effects should enhance models and strategies related to optimizing programming schedules (e.g., Liu et al. 2004).

In the next section, we begin by providing a conceptual basis for our argument and develop hypotheses. After we elaborate on our data and their sources, we discuss pertinent modeling issues to develop the model specification. Then we detail the model estimation and selection procedures and present our results before concluding with a discussion of the implications of our findings.

## 2. Conceptual Development

Extant research indicates a significant impact of UGC on the performance of media products such as movies (Liu 2006), books (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), music (Salganik et al. 2006), video games (Zhu and Zhang 2010), and television shows (Godes and Mayzlin 2004). In cable news contexts, we also expect that UGC about a show has a positive relationship with the show’s viewership.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> There is a positive relationship between the UGC of a cable show and its viewership.

We explore the nature of the relationship between the UGC of a competing cable show and a focal cable show’s viewership. Extant research provides evidence of competitive effects at play regarding the performance impact of traditional marketing mix variables such as advertising (e.g., Erickson 1985) and price (Blattberg and Wisniewski 1989). The underlying principle is intuitive: firms operate in a market populated by competitors, so a firm’s performance depends not only on its own marketing strategy but also its competitors’. Thus it is reasonable to expect that competition affects UGC, which constitutes a crucial marketing mix variable in the modern, connected world.

A possible manifestation of competitive effects in the UGC-performance relationship reflects a traditional view of competition. The structure-conductperformance paradigm (e.g., Scherer and Ross 1990) holds that if strategic moves, such as price reductions or increased advertising, improve the focal firm’s performance, they also result in a negative effect on competitors’ performance. If two products are substitutes, increased demand for one suggests decreased demand for the other. If cable news shows airing at the same time are substitutes, UGC about competitors should have a negative relationship with the focal show’s viewership, such that the substitute effect takes away viewers from competing shows in the same time slot. Thus, we suggest the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> There is a negative relationship between UGC about a competitor and a focal cable show’s viewership.

Competition among brands also could lead to an increase in overall category demand and growth of the market (Shocker et al. 2004). In a UGC context, Libai et al. (2009) find positive cross-brand influences of online word of mouth in the growth of the cellular industry; similarly, Mayzlin and Yoganarasimhan (2012) show that competition between blog rivals can increase readership for all blogs. Thus, more competitors’ UGC could lead to market expansion and increase overall cable news viewership.<sup>1</sup>

Furthermore, viewers attached to their own political in-group (e.g., conservatives, liberals) are motivated to “protect their own groups” (Coe et al. 2008, p. 205). Cable news viewers watch their preferred shows to validate their perceptions; they may occasionally watch the “other side,” in an attempt to disagree with it (Boehlert 2009). The conventional wisdom that only conservatives watch Fox News and only liberals watch MSNBC is only partly true, as evidenced by a Pew Research study (Pew Research 2009) that indicates 39% of Fox News viewers were Democrats and 25% of MSNBC viewers were Republicans. Thus an increase in Keith Olbermann’s viewership may relate to a controversial opinion he has expressed about a political issue that prompts bloggers to post their opinions about him, rather than about Bill O’Reilly or Campbell Brown. However, if the bloggers disagree with Olbermann and write about contrasting opinions expressed by O’Reilly and Brown, the increase may appear in blogs about the latter hosts. Furthermore, at least Fox News regularly tracks and counters negative as well as neutral opinions about their shows on blogs (Folkenflik 2013) and it is likely that other cable news networks do so too, further feeding the hostile ideological debates online. Thus, partisan loyalty kindled by the hostile media phenomenon can contribute to a positive relationship between competitors’ UGC and a focal show’s viewership. Combining the possibility of a market expansion and cross-show influence from ideological disagreements, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> There is a positive relationship between the UGC of a competitor and a cable show’s viewership.

## 3. Data

## 3.1. Research Context

We collected data about the three major 24-hour cable news networks in the United States—Fox News, CNN, and MSNBC—which account for more than 80% of cable news viewership. For every weeknight (Monday–Friday) for a 30-week period beginning in May 2009, we collected data about the three consecutive time slots (7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>–9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> EST) that attract the highest viewership ratings for the three networks. The networks aired one-hour shows in each slot, hosted by solitary anchors who directly compete for viewers.<sup>2</sup> During our study period, at 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, Fox News aired the Fox Report with Shepard Smith, CNN showed Lou Dobbs Tonight, and MSNBC carried Hardball with Chris Matthews. At 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, these networks aired, respectively, The O’Reilly Factor, Campbell Brown, and Countdown with Keith Olbermann. At 9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, Sean Hannity (Fox News), Larry King Live (CNN), and The Rachel Maddow Show (MSNBC) aired.

The three time slots that make up our data context are different in terms of the content and slant of programming, as well as the nature of competition. At 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> (Smith, Dobbs, Matthews), the shows’ focus is on analysis of the news, whereas at 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> (O’Reilly, Brown, Olbermann), the emphasis is on opinion-based commentary, including frequent criticism of the competitor hosts and networks. At 9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, two of the shows (Hannity on Fox and Maddow on MSNBC) are opinion and commentary based, whereas the third (Larry King) is devoid of opinion or commentary from the host and is composed of interviews. ## 3.2. Data Description

3.2.1. Viewership Data. We obtained daily viewership ratings from Nielsen Media Research.<sup>3</sup> Nielsen provides a variety of television viewership measures, such as viewership data on a minute-by-minute basis (frequently used to conduct research on television advertising; Wilbur 2008), the number of viewers who watched a show in the time slot in which it was telecast, viewership numbers in different age groups (e.g., adults 18–49 years), and regional market shares, among others. For the purpose of our investigation of the relationship between a show’s viewership and UGC, we chose a time unit that allowed for robust longitudinal analysis, contained enough instances of UGC to make the longitudinal analysis possible, and allowed for a meaningful substantive interpretation of the results. Thus, we chose a day as the time unit, allowing us to use daily viewership measures as well as daily blog post volumes. We used what Nielsen classifies as Live + Same Day (Live + SD) ratings, which estimate the viewership for a show when it airs live, as well as viewership on the same day of recordings of the show (e.g., DVR, TiVo), based on a sample of more than 9,000 households. Our measure for viewership of a cable news show for a given day then reflects the number of viewers who watched the show when it was aired live, plus those who recorded the show and watched it the same day. In Figure 1, we depict the daily viewership for the three time slots during our 30-week study period.

As is evident from Figure 1, Fox News earns the highest viewership among the three networks in all three time slots. A visual inspection reveals no discernible upward or downward trend in the viewership of any of the shows during our data collection window. We also conducted unit root tests to confirm that the data series were stationary. We found occasional spikes in viewership, related to important news stories that were in focus on those days. For example, near the end of the data period, spikes in the viewership of all nine news shows paralleled the timing of the Fort Hood shooting, in which a U.S. army officer gunned down 13 people inside a military base on November 5, 2009. Other spikes were associated with the death of Senator Edward Kennedy and major developments in the healthcare reform debate.

3.2.2. UGC Data. We used the daily number of blog posts mentioning a specific television show or its host as our measure for blog post volume, collected by running keyword-based search database queries on the Application Program interface of three blog-tracking services: Google BlogSearch (http:// blogsearch.google.com), Technorati (http://www .technorati.com), and Nielsen Blogpulse service (http://www.blogpulse.com). Prior research has shown that the volume of UGC has significant predictive power for performance (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Liu 2006); we use the volume of blog posts as the measure of UGC.<sup>4</sup>

Because the shows air on weeknights, viewership numbers are available for only five days per week, whereas blog posts appear on all seven days. To ensure that the UGC and viewership time series are of the same length, we add Saturday blog posts to Friday numbers and Sunday blog posts to Monday numbers.<sup>5</sup> Fox News leads in viewership for the three time slots but not in terms of blog posts. Furthermore, the UGC volume is higher for the later slots than for the 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> shows. The histograms of blog posts for the 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> shows (see Figure 2) display a Poisson distribution, as we would expect for count variables.

The three networks focus primarily on news reporting throughout the day, but in these evening slots, the shows take a distinctly opinionated slant. The nine shows therefore are promoted and packaged to highlight the identity and personality of the hosts, and the names of all of the shows include the hosts’ names. The hosts offer commentary and analysis, rather than simple news reporting, focused on politics, as well as interviews with guests. Almost all of the shows (cf. Larry King Live) frequently serve as soapboxes for the hosts’ ideological viewpoints (e.g., Bill O’Reilly starts his show with a “talking points memo” section, Keith Olbermann often ends with a “special comment”). The ratings of these shows are significant enough (Pew Research 2009) that advertisers and politicians take note; for example, President Obama chose to give Bill O’Reilly an interview to headline the 2011 Super Bowl broadcast (CBSNews 2011). Viewers who regularly watch cable news shows in prime time opt for news-based analysis and commentary, which is a product vastly different from entertainment shows aired on the regular networks. A study commissioned by the Public Broadcasting Service (Lehrer 2002) also concluded that “cable news networks appeal to two distinct audiences: highly ideological so-called news junkies whose daily entertainment derives from the overheated debates of the political class; and a lesscommitted group who rely on experienced newsgathering when a global crisis hits the headlines.” Thus, for a typical viewer of cable news shows in prime time, the choice of which show to watch is likely not between a news show on a cable news network and an entertainment show on a nonnews network but rather among the three cable news networks.

The nine shows represent three sets of individual competitors who directly compete in a given time slot. Because they air at the same time and offer products of a similar nature to similar consumers of cable news shows, they engage in oligopolistic competition. For any consumption occasion (i.e., time slot), we assume that a consumer who watches cable news chooses one of these three shows.<sup>6</sup> Furthermore, the three networks have positioned themselves at different spots on the political spectrum, as reflected in surveys of cable news viewers. A study by the Pew Research Center (Pew Research 2007) revealed that the average Fox News viewer self-identified as a conservative, the average CNN viewer self-identified as a moderate or independent, and the average MSNBC viewer selfidentified as a Democrat. In the three different sets of three direct competitors, we thus consider two possible competitive hierarchies: (1) at the cable news network level, driven by ideological positions, and (2) at the show level, driven by the host and the show’s content.

3.2.3. Control Variables. In addition to state dependence and unobserved heterogeneity (which we elaborate on in §4), we need to control for timevarying, show-specific, and day-specific events that could drive both UGC and viewership. To control for time-varying, day-specific events, we collected Google Trends data for the term “breaking news.” This variable should act as a proxy for the newsworthiness of a given day. To establish face validity, we checked the Google Trends score for particularly newsworthy days (e.g., Fort Hood shooting, Senator Kennedy’s death) and found that the score was higher than usual on those days. For example, compared with a median score of 0.47, the Google Trends score for the Fort Hood shooting was 0.58, and that for Senator Kennedy’s death was 0.55. Google Trends data thus control for events specific to any given day that could influence both UGC and viewership.

Next, to control for time-varying, show-specific events, such as a high-profile interview or noteworthy segment, we collected data from the media watchdog site MediaMatters.org. This site documents unusual, noteworthy incidents on cable news shows (usually with an accompanying YouTube video). Mentions of a particular show by Media Matters thus provide a measure of time-varying, show-specific events that influence UGC and viewership. We conducted keywordbased searches for all nine shows on MediaMatters.org and collected the data, which are binary: whether Media Matters documented an incident for the shows on a given day or not. For example, on July 23, 2009, Media Matters noted that Lou Dobbs was the first mainstream cable news host to raise questions about President Obama’s birth certificate; on the same day, Media Matters did not document any incidents for shows competing in the same time slot, hosted by Chris Matthews and Shepard Smith.

## 4. Model Specification and Estimation

Viewership is the number of viewers for a show, i.e., count data, which we measure on a daily basis, so the underlying data generation process should be Poisson (e.g., Wedel et al. 1993). The three viewership variables in each time slot are for competing shows and thus cannot be independent of one another; thus, with three covarying count variables, the model should have a multivariate specification. Furthermore, viewership of a show depends on its unique appeal, including but not limited to the host’s personality, the variety and popularity of guests, the political ideology espoused, and the quality of the content in terms of information and entertainment. Our model accounts for the influence of the unique appeal of each show by modeling show-specific unobserved heterogeneity. Taking into account the multivariate Poisson nature of our data and the need to account for unobserved heterogeneity, we develop a multivariate (trivariate) Poisson log-normal mixture model, for which we rely on prior literature regarding the multivariate Poisson log-normal distribution (e.g., Aitchison and Ho 1989, Chib and Winkelmann 2001, Karlis and Meligkotsidou 2007) and the bivariate Poisson log-normal regression model (e.g., Cameron and Trivedi 1998, Srinivasan et al. 2007).

$$

$$
\log (\lambda_ {n w d} ^ {s}) = M _ {n w d} ^ {s} + \Omega_ {n d} ^ {s},\tag{2}

where $\lambda _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ is the parameter of the Poisson distribution, $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s }$ is the multivariate normal intercept for viewership on weekday d for a show that airs at time slot s on network n (this intercept accounts for unobserved heterogeneity and allows for dependence across the k-equations), and $M _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ is the sum of the products of all parameters and the corresponding covariates, i.e., ’X.

To examine the relationship between viewership, own UGC, and competitive UGC, we model the Poisson mean $\lambda _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ as a function of these UGCs and other variables. In addition, there are two issues that we need to consider: (1) viewership is likely to be influenced by the inherent quality of a show and its host, thus, to account for time invariant show quality, we use an unobserved heterogeneity specification; (2) we need to recognize the endogeneity of UGC variables that is likely driven by feedback loops, missing variables (although we do include key variables as controls and unobserved heterogeneity specification), and/or measurement error (Wooldridge 2002); thus, we need to correct for endogeneity to obtain statistically consistent parameter estimates. Thus, our model specification needs to account for the following features: (1) own UGC effects $( W _ { n w d } ^ { s } ) .$ , which refers to the relationship between a show’s UGC and viewership; (2) competitors UGC effects $( C _ { n w d } ^ { s } ) ,$ which refers to the relationship between competing shows’ UGC and own viewership; (3) state dependence in the form of the daily continuity $( D _ { n w d } ^ { s } )$ of the effect of the viewership from recent days; (4) state dependence in the form of the lead-in effect $( L _ { n w d } ^ { s } ) .$ , or the carryover of viewership of shows in the previous time slot to the focal show; (5) observed $( N _ { n w d } ^ { s } )$ and unobserved heterogeneity $( \Omega _ { n d } ^ { s } )$ where observed heterogeneity is modeled through the control variables; we assess unobserved heterogeneity through show-specific multivariate intercepts that account for viewer preferences for the show’s content and host; and (6) endogeneity of the UGC variables. Thus, we rewrite Equation (2) as (where $Z _ { n w d } ^ { s } =$ $N _ { n w d } ^ { s } + \Omega _ { n d } ^ { s } )$

$$
\log (\lambda_ {n w d} ^ {s}) = W _ {n w d} ^ {s} + C _ {n w d} ^ {s} + D _ {n w d} ^ {s} + L _ {n w d} ^ {s} + Z _ {n w d} ^ {s}.
$$

## 4.2. Competitive Effects

Research on marketing communication strategies shows that product performance depends not only on its own marketing communication strategies but also those of its competitors (Dube and Manchanda 2005, Naik et al. 2005). We expect competitive effects to be similarly manifest in the UGC-viewership relationship. The relationship between competitors’ UGC and a show’s viewership might be positive or negative. For example, an increase in UGC about Rachel Maddow’s show is likely an indicator of the buzz about her show among viewers and might increase viewership of her show, which may come at the expense of Larry King’s or Sean Hannity’s viewership. An increase in Keith Olbermann’s viewership may relate to a controversial opinion he expressed about a political issue that prompts bloggers to post their opinions about him rather than about Bill O’Reilly or Campbell Brown. In contrast, if the bloggers disagree with Olbermann and write about contrasting opinions expressed by O’Reilly and Brown, the increase may appear in blogs about the latter hosts. Thus, consistent with extant research on comparative advertising (e.g., Barry 1993, Pechmann and Stewart 1990), we expect competitive effects in the UGC-viewership relationship, whether positive or negative.

To capture these possible effects, our explanatory variables are the UGC of competing shows in the time slot. In addition to the values for the same day (only those blog posts whose time stamps indicate they were posted before the show aired), we include values for the preceding two days to allow for lagged effects. Thus, the specification we use is

$$
C _ {n = N, w d} ^ {s} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {2} \beta 2 _ {n \neq N, (d - k)} ^ {s} X _ {n \neq N, w (d - k)} ^ {s},\tag{5}
$$

where $k = 0 , 1 , 2$ are the lags; $X _ { n \neq N d } ^ { s }$ are UGC volumes for shows on the other two networks that compete with the show at time slot s on network $n = N$ in week w on day $d ;$ and 2<sub>00</sub> are the corresponding show- and weekday-specific parameters for competitive effects.

## 4.3. State Dependence: Daily Continuity

The longitudinal nature of our data requires that we account for the likelihood that the value of a variable on a given day depends on the past values of the variable (as in Godes and Mayzlin 2004). Cable news shows often follow up on stories or segments on subsequent days. Thus, viewers who watched a show on day d − 1 may watch the show again on day d to follow the story. We recognize daily continuity as a state dependence factor that determines viewership on any given day. Allowing for this state dependence enables us to identify the effect of prior viewership and prevent past viewership from confounding the results of other variables. To account for this state dependence, we empirically determine the appropriate lag to be $^ { 2 , }$ and assume the value of viewership on day d is influenced by the previous two days, d − 1 and d − 2.

Many cable news shows contain specific segments that they air every week (e.g., O’Reilly runs a culture quiz every Monday and a conversation with comedian Dennis Miller every Wednesday). Viewers may tune in weekly to follow up on those segments from the previous week. To account for this effect, we also specify the value of a variable on a given day as dependent on its value on the same weekday in the previous week, so for example, for wd, the weekly lag effect would come from 4w − 15d

$$
\begin{array}{c} D _ {n w d} ^ {s} = \beta 3 _ {n d} ^ {s} Y _ {n (w - 1) d} ^ {s} + \beta 3 _ {n (d - 1)} ^ {s} Y _ {n w (d - 1)} ^ {s} \\ + \beta 3 _ {n (d - 2)} ^ {s} Y _ {n w (d - 2)} ^ {s}, \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

where $Y _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ is the viewership for the show at time slot s on network n in week w on day $d ;$ and $\beta 3 .$ are the corresponding show- and weekday-specific parameters for daily continuity.

## 4.4. State Dependence: Lead-in Effects

Researchers studying television viewing habits reveal that viewership is influenced by a lead-in effect, such that viewers watching a given show on a network tend to keep watching that network (Rust and Eechambadi 1989, Shachar and Emerson 2000). The state dependence associated with the lead-in effect should play a role in the context of cable news too. For example, viewers who watch the self-described liberal Ed Schultz on MSNBC at 6:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> are likely to persist with the network and watch the liberal Chris Matthews at 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, whereas viewers who watch the self-described conservative Bill O’Reilly at 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> are likely to remain with Fox News and watch the conservative Sean Hannity at 9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> Cable news networks are aware of this lead-in effect and try to benefit from it with “hand-offs,” or introductions in which hosts make a short appearance on the preceding show to give the audience a preview.

Although lead-in effects suggest that greater viewership of preceding shows on competing networks should have a negative effect on a focal show’s viewership, switching behaviors are possible, especially among viewers who are not strongly connected to any ideology. Viewers who watch cable news on a given night and want to hear all sides of a debate about a certain issue, for example, may watch MSNBC at 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, CNN at 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, and Fox News at 9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> Thus, the show’s aggregate viewership could benefit from lead-in effects or suffer from switching effects in relation to preceding shows on its own network and competing networks. To capture this relationship, our model accounts for the effects of viewership of preceding shows on all three networks<sup>8</sup>

$$
L _ {n w d} ^ {s} = \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {3} \beta 4 _ {n d} ^ {s - 1} Y _ {n w d} ^ {s - 1},\tag{7}
$$

where $Y _ { n w d } ^ { s - 1 }$ is the viewership for the show at previous time slot $s - 1$ on network n in week w on day $d ;$ and $\beta 4 _ { . }$ are the corresponding show- and weekdayspecific parameters for lead-in state dependence.

## 4.5. Observed and Unobserved Heterogeneity

To account for observed sources of heterogeneity, we need to control for alternative developments that could drive viewership as well as UGC. For example, a particularly newsworthy day (such as the death of Senator Edward Kennedy) could lead people to blog and watch the news more than usual. Similarly, there may be a notable interview (e.g., with the president) or a noteworthy segment on a particular show (e.g., Bill O’Reilly’s weekly segment with Dennis Miller) could drive the viewership and UGC. Thus, we use the best available objective web-based control variables that capture these effects. Specifically, we use Google Trends (for time-varying dayspecific newsworthiness) and Media Matters mentions (for time-varying show-specific events), to account for alternative effects that could drive UGC as well as viewership. We allow these control variables to also be lagged for two days in addition to the present day to maintain consistency across variables in our model specification. The exact specification we use is

$$
N _ {n w d} ^ {s} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {2} \beta 5 _ {n (d - k)} ^ {s} Z g _ {w (d - k)} + \beta 6 _ {n (d - k)} ^ {s} Z m _ {n w (d - k)} ^ {s},\tag{8}
$$

where $k = 0 , 1 , 2$ are the lags; $Z g _ { w d }$ is the Google Trends measure for day d of week w; $Z m _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ is the Media Matters mentions measure for the show at time slot s on network n in week w on weekday d; and $\beta 5 _ { n d } ^ { s }$ and $\beta 6 _ { n d } ^ { s }$ are the corresponding show- and weekdayspecific parameters, respectively.

To account for unobserved heterogeneity across shows, we use $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s }$ to represent the intercept levels of viewership that result from the unique appeal of a show, namely, its content and the personality of its host. We also expect the levels to vary with the day of the week. Because the intercept levels of viewership of shows in the same time slot are for competitors, we expect $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s }$ to covary. Thus, we define the vector $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s }$ as multivariate normal, such that $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s } \sim$ $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { M } \mathbf { V } \mathbf { N } _ { 3 } ( \Omega , \Sigma ) , \forall s = 1 , 2 , 3 . } \end{array}$

## 4.6. Identification and Endogeneity

We begin our discussion on identification of the effects specified in Equations (3)–(8) with our parameters of interest, i.e., 1 the effect of own UGC in Equation (4) and 2 the effect of competitive UGC in Equation (5). Consistent with extant literature (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Sonnier et al. 2011), UGC variables suffer from endogeneity and thus inferences concerning their effects on outcomes (viewership in our case) is jeopardized (Hausman and Taylor 1983). We believe that endogeneity of UGC variables stems from two sources: first, some unobserved third variable, such as a presidential election or a mass shooting incident can drive both viewership and UGC (i.e., omitted variable); second, as UGC measures are estimates, error in measuring UGC can also induce endogeneity (i.e., measurement error).<sup>9</sup>

An instrumental variable (IV) approach is used to correct for endogeneity, when appropriate instruments are available.<sup>10</sup> As endogeneity manifests because of nonzero covariation between an explanatory variable and the regression error term, one needs to identify instrumental variables that are correlated with the endogenous explanatory variable but not the error term (Wooldridge 2002). Google Trends data, which proxies newsworthiness of a given day, and Media Matters data, which proxies noteworthy showand day-specific events, can be seen as instruments for UGC variables. On one hand, these two variables are clearly exogenous to both viewership and UGC; on the other hand, it is difficult to argue that these two variables only influence blogs and not the error term (note the unobserved variable(s) resulting in the endogeneity issue is captured by the error term). Thus, the two variables cannot be seen as instruments (Stock et al. 2002); therefore, we treat the two variables as control variables.

As is often the case with UGC data, good instruments are not available (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2009, Sonnier et al. 2011); under such circumstances, frugal IV methods, such as those relying on high-moments (Erickson and Whited 2002) and using latent instrumental variables (Ebbes et al. 2005), are recommended (Ebbes et al. 2009). Recognizing the increasing popularity of the use of LIV in substantive research $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Grewal et al. 2010, Rutz and Trusov 2011, Zhang et al. 2009), we adopt the LIV approach to correct for endogeneity in the UGC variables. The LIV approach cannot be used if the endogenous variable is “perfectly normal” because a mixture of normal with more than one component cannot be fitted (Ebbes et al. 2005, p. 369). ## 4.7. Estimation

To estimate our model we use Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods (e.g., Gilks et al. 1998, Rossi and Allenby 2003). The estimation gives us parameters for the effects of own UGC effects, competitive effects, state dependence effects, and unobserved heterogeneity. In our MCMC setup we assume that $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s }$ for all shows to be drawn from a common noninformative multivariate normal distribution with a diagonal Wishart distribution for the precision matrix (see Appendix B for details). Thus combining Equation (2), which specifies the multivariate log-normal-Poisson mixture model and Equation (11), which incorporates the LIV into the equation, we get

$$
\begin{array}{c} \log (\lambda_ {n w d} ^ {s}) = \Omega_ {n d} ^ {s} + \beta_ {n d} ^ {s} X _ {n w d} ^ {s} + \rho_ {o w n} \eta_ {s n w d} ^ {o w n U G C} \\ + \rho_ {c o m p} \eta_ {s n w d} ^ {c o m p U G C}, \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

where $X _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ is the vector of all independent variables including the control variables. We assume normal priors for $\rho _ { o w n }$ and $\rho _ { c o m p } .$

We use conjugate and noninformative priors for all parameters. The parameter vectors $\beta _ { n d } ^ { s }$ are indexed by time slot, network, and weekday, such that they are specified for each show on each of the five weekdays. We specify the parameter vectors as distributed multivariate normal with a diagonal Wishart distribution for the precision matrix. The underlying effects we estimate could be driven by different phenomena, such as popularity of the network, time slot, or weekday. Within the cable news industry, there are three dominant networks—Fox News, CNN, and MSNBC. Each network has shows on weekdays at the time slots of 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>–9:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, with different total viewership. These shows have a base viewership that is largely stationary across our data window, but there should be systematic fluctuations in the viewership depending on which day of the week it is. For example, NFL Monday Night Football could influence the shows’ viewership on Mondays, whereas Thursday night sitcoms could influence viewership on Thursdays. Thus, the dynamics of the cable news industry suggest a hierarchy of effects, that is, cable news industry → network → time slot → weekday.

Thus, we specified the parameter distribution with three levels of hierarchy: The parameter vectors $\beta _ { n d } ^ { s }$ for a show in time slot s on network n on weekday d can be drawn from show-specific multivariate normal distributions with means $\beta _ { n } ^ { s } ,$ which result from time slot-specific multivariate normal distributions with means $\beta ^ { s } ;$ which in turn are drawn from a common multivariate normal distribution. In other words, $\boldsymbol { \beta } _ { n d } ^ { s } \sim \mathrm { M V N } _ { 3 } ( \boldsymbol { \beta } _ { n } ^ { s } , V _ { n } ^ { s } ) , \boldsymbol { \beta } _ { n } ^ { s } \sim \mathrm { M V N } _ { k } ( \boldsymbol { \beta } ^ { s } , V ^ { s } )$ , and $\beta ^ { s } \sim \mathrm { M V N } _ { k } ( \beta ^ { 0 } , V ^ { 0 } )$ , where $\beta ^ { 0 }$ is a vector of zeros, and $V ^ { 0 }$ is a diagonal matrix with large values (1,000) for variances that reflects a lack of knowledge about the means of the parameters (see Appendix B for details).

The latent instruments $\theta _ { s n w d } ^ { o w n U G C }$ and $\theta _ { s n w d } ^ { c o m p U G C }$ follow a discrete distribution with $K = 3$ support points<sup>11</sup> where each support point has a normal distribution and each respective probability has a vague Dirichlet prior. The error correlation parameters are assumed to be vague conjugate priors. We used standard MCMC procedures with three concurrent chains (Bolstad 2007). The first 5,000 iterations per chain are burn-in values; the next 75,000 iterations provide the samples for parameter estimation. To assess model convergence, we used Gelman-Rubin statistics, which showed that the model specification converged. Thus, we present the results from the time slot specification of our model. To test the significance of the parameter values, we checked whether the 95% central posterior intervals contained 0 (as is the norm in Bayesian estimation; e.g., Rossi and Allenby 2003) to verify if the estimated parameter is different from 0.

## 5.4. Predictive Validity and Robustness Checks

We assessed the accuracy of our model by testing how well the model predicted viewership in a time frame outside of our sample period. To gauge if our full model with competitive effects and LIV provided significantly greater predictive power than other specifications, we also carried out predictive tests for the following models: (1) base model (no LIV, unobserved heterogeneity, state dependence, and viewership lags); (2) own UGC model (no LIV, base model with own UGC); (3) own UGC LIV (own UGC model with LIV); (4) competitive model (no LIV, own UGC model with competitive cross effects); (5) competitive LIV model (competitive model with LIV correction); and (6) full LIV model (competitive LIV models with Google Trends and Media Matters data as controls). We chose three weeks in December 2009 and collected viewership and blog post data about the nine shows. Following the Bayesian approach used by Neelamegham and Chintagunta (1999), we included only explanatory variables in the model and treated the dependent variable of viewership as a missing variable. We then calculated the root mean square error (RMSE) between the predicted viewership numbers and the actual viewership numbers, which are reported in Table 5 as thousands of viewers, along with the deviance information criterion (DIC).

The RMSE values indicate that our full model predicts viewership reasonably well, with a difference of 52,624 between the actual and predicted value, which is significantly better than the other four specifications. The full model outperforms the base model (without UGC) by 67,341 viewers. The average daily viewership of cable news shows in our sample is about 1.4 million, so these results indicate that incorporating competitive UGC improves existing models of viewership prediction by 3% to 5%.

## 6. Discussion

Building on emerging literature related to the link between UGC (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Liu 2006) and performance, we have argued for the inclusion of competition. Our analyses of UGC and viewership data from the cable news industry confirm the relationship between UGC and performance, generating intriguing findings about competition and UGC. In our analysis of three sets of competing cable news shows, we find that the competitive effects of UGC, though statistically significant, are heterogeneous in direction: positive in some dyads of competing shows and negative in others. We note three main contributions of our study.

First, our analysis is the first direct examination of competition in UGC, which shows effects at the dyadic level and thus enables us to study the relationship between the UGC of show A and the viewership of show B, and vice versa. Recent research on UGC-related phenomena has noted the need to account for competition as a means to extend the understanding of these phenomena (e.g., Dou et al. 2013, Netzer et al. 2012). Our analysis documents the relationship between competitors’ UGC and focal firm performance, and also sheds light on heterogeneity in the relationship. Examining the effects across time slots, we find a variation in the sign of the results that indicates that though the effect of competitors’ UGC is statistically significant, the precise nature of the effect depends on heterogeneity among time slots and cable news shows.

Second, regarding heterogeneity in competitive effects, the presence of only three time slots precluded us from incorporating a moderating effect of competition to say conclusively when the competitive effect is negative and when it is positive; however, the result patterns offer face validity. The competitive effect is negative in the 7:00 <sup>p.m.</sup> time slot, when the hosts rarely engage in mutual criticism, but it is positive and strongest at 8:00 <sup>p.m.</sup>, when the hosts frequently attack one another, ideologically and personally. The hostile media phenomenon appears to be at play as an underlying mechanism that leads to heterogeneity in the competitive effects (e.g., Hansen and Kim 2011).

Third, we contribute to establishing the predictive validity of UGC models. A significant body of work focuses on UGC as a leading indicator of performance. Our predictive validity analysis, using the results from our model, shows that incorporating competitive effects significantly boosts predictive power. Comparing the predictive validity scores of the LIV model that corrected for endogeneity with models that did not underlines the importance of accounting for endogeneity (Sonnier et al. 2011).

Furthermore, our findings suggest opportunities for extending research into the role of UGC in the television industry. For example, UGC measures of competing networks and shows can provide additional metrics for optimizing television programming schedules (Liu et al. 2004, Reddy et al. 1998). Our analysis takes place at the aggregate level of daily viewership and UGC. Further research could examine the competitive influence of UGC in time-shifted viewership (e.g., Live + 7 Day ratings) or minute-to-minute competitive switching behavior, available from household panel data. Measures of individual viewers’ posting and consumption of online UGC, in conjunction with panel data measures of television viewing (collected by Nielsen Media Research), also could extend behavioral research into television viewing habits (e.g., Rust and Alpert 1984, Woltman et al. 2003).

The implications of our findings for managers in the cable news industry in particular and the television industry in general are clear: monitor competitors’ UGC. Predictions about viewership ratings likely improve when they incorporate competitors’ UGC. Furthermore, television managers can use the analysis of UGC, including competitors’ UGC, to make decisions about scheduling. Advertisers might analyze UGC as a determinant of the competing shows on which they prefer to advertise. Our findings have lessons for managers outside television too. Cable news is not the only industry in which partisan supporters carry their rivalries into online conversations. A cursory examination of Facebook and Twitter shows spirited debates between fans of competing brands, such as the iPhone and Samsung Galaxy, Pepsi and Coke, or Audi and Mercedes. Managers in all industries thus should focus on analyzing UGC about not only their own brands but also their competitors, to better understand consumer perceptions and predict performance.

Research on UGC remains in an incipient stage; with this article, we hope to have shed some light on the nature of competition in the UGC-performance relationship. The avenues for impactful further research are manifold and promising.

## Appendix B. Priors and Full Conditional Distributions

Our dependent variable is daily viewership count for every weekday for 30 weeks for TV shows that air on three networks at three time slots. Thus, we observe $Y _ { n w d } ^ { s } ,$ where s $( 1 = 7 { \mathrm { ~ P . M . } } , 2 = 8 { \mathrm { ~ P . M . } } , 3 = 9 { \mathrm { ~ P . M . } } )$ denotes the time slot, n $( 1 =$ Fox News, 2 = CNN, 3 = MSNBC) denotes the network, w = $1 , 2 , \ldots , 3 0$ denotes the week, and $d = 1 , 2 , \ldots , 5 ,$ denotes the weekday. Thus, $Y _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ denotes the viewership on day d of week w for a show that airs on network n at time slot s.

We expect viewerships of shows in the same time slot to be correlated with each other. To account for this correlation among the count variables, we model the counts as Poisson distributed, with the mean parameters of the Poisson distributions $\lambda _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ having a multivariate normal component; this makes the model a Poisson-log-normal mixture model. The full model from Equations $( 1 ) ‐ ( 2 )$ can be written as $( Y _ { n w d } ^ { s } , | X _ { n w d } ^ { s } , \Omega _ { n d } ^ { s } ) \sim \mathrm { P o i s } \bar { \mathrm { s o n } } ( \lambda _ { n w d } ^ { s } ) ,$ , where

$$
\log (\lambda_ {n w d} ^ {s}) = \beta_ {n d} ^ {s} ^ {\prime} X _ {n w d} ^ {s} + \Omega_ {n d} ^ {s};\tag{B1}
$$

$X _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ represents the vector of explanatory variables and $\beta _ { n d } ^ { s }$ represents the vector of parameters specific to the weekday d for the show on network n at time slot s.

The dependence among viewerships of shows across time slots is modeled through correlated unobserved heterogeneity components $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s }$ . Thus, say $\bar { \Omega } _ { n d } = ( \Omega _ { n d } ^ { 1 } , \ \Omega _ { n d } ^ { 2 } , \ \Omega _ { n d } ^ { 3 } )$ is distributed as a multivariate (or trivariate) normal random variable as

$$
\bar {\Omega} _ {n d} \sim \mathrm{MVN} _ {3} (\bar {\Omega}, \Sigma),\tag{B2}
$$

where $\bar { \boldsymbol \Omega } = ( \Omega ^ { 1 } , \Omega ^ { 2 } , \Omega ^ { 3 } ) ; \Sigma$ is a general variance-covariance structure to accommodate the correlation among $\Omega ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s } ,$ and thus the correlation among the $\Omega _ { n d } ^ { s } { ' } s ,$ which introduces dependence between viewerships too. So from (B1) and $( \hat { 1 } \hat { 2 } ) , \lambda _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ has a trivariate log-normal distribution, whereas $Y _ { n w d } ^ { s }$ has a Poisson-log-normal mixture distribution.

As denoted in Equations (9)–(12), we use an LIV specification to account for endogeneity that can arise from UGC regressors being correlated with the error terms in the Poisson-log-normal mixture model. To estimate this LIV specification of the model using MCMC, we explain how the conditional distributions would work for a single endogenous regressor.<sup>12</sup> Equation (B1) for a single endogenous regressor $\bar { ( 1 . \mathrm { e . } , X _ { n w d } ^ { s } ) }$ , after incorporating the LIV specification, can be written in a manner similar to Equation (12) as

$$
\log (\lambda_ {n w d} ^ {s}) = \Omega_ {n d} ^ {s} + \beta_ {n d} ^ {s} \hat {X} _ {n w d} ^ {s} + \rho \eta_ {s n w d}.\tag{B3}
$$

Substituting for $\eta _ { s n w d }$ from Equation (9), this equation can then be written as

$$
