## a b s t r a c t

Simultaneous supplies of an item by multiple online auction vendors greatly reduce the burden of arduous search that a consumer has to conduct in a brick-and-mortar market. The enriched information set endows all auction participants with added decision <sup>fl</sup>exibility and complexity. One of such <sup>fl</sup>exibilities or complications is the potential of information cross-referencing. In this study, we empirically tested if and how bidders interacted with each other through cross-referencing. Our results strongly supported the existence of cross-referencing. We also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed new strategies and tactical moves as best responses for sellers and bidders under the online simultaneous condition. This research provided another support for the path-dependent nature of online auctions as bidders were found to actively adjust their behavior in avoidance of winner's curse.

## 1. Introduction

Auction, as a form of trade, was <sup>fi</sup>rst dated to 500 B.C. in Babylon [30]. In 2006, estimated sales of online auctions amounted to \$48.5 (Forrester Research). Born to be a consumer to consumer (C2C) platform [25,31], online auctions have steadily expanded its reach into business to consumer (B2C) and business to business (B2B) markets [52,53]. According to powersellersunite.com, an online auction tracking company, there were more than 30 million auctions on the top three auction sites that the company tracks (July 13, 2009, 3:06 pm ET): ebay.com — 28,269,495, ebid.com — 2,44,8311, Bonanzle.com — 2,183,965. In Ernest Hemingway's “Old Man and the Sea”, the old man “Santiago” was depicted as “…regardless of his bad luck, has never been anything less than precise…” with throwing <sup>fi</sup>shing lines into the sea. The size of today's auction market is almost as colossal as the enormous sea where ‘Santiago’ threw in his <sup>fi</sup>shing lines. Understandably, making decisions, operational or marketing, has become equally, if not more, challenging as being “precise” on <sup>fi</sup>shing lines.

The auction market offers unprecedented bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to justify the costs and efforts of participation. Thanks to the reduced search costs and pooled market space [3], one can easily identify plenty of auction offers with a few clicks of mouse. The need to get goods supplied or have a service performed can be quickly broadcasted and/or ful<sup>fi</sup>lled via online auctions [38,42]. On the <sup>fl</sup>ip side, reduced search costs greatly complete buyer information [16,35], which alters buyer behavior and intensi<sup>fi</sup>es competition on the supply side.

Being “precise” on critical decisions has never been more overwhelmed by information. The added complexity in decision making calls for research that not only simpli<sup>fi</sup>es decision making, but also incorporates the dynamic nature of online auctions in decision horizons [17,50].

In this article, we studied a conventional practice in the new online auction context, namely, reference prices [44,49]. Many research noticed that online auctions offered added complexity where traditional assumptions no longer holds [5,39,43]. One of these assumptions was the isolated auction assumption. In the online context, auctions of the same item were abundant and the same auctions repeated over time. An individual auction was no longer isolated. The simultaneous structure offered everyone an enormous amount of reference information. How buyer behavior changed accordingly and what implications this might bring to the selling side have not been well understood. Using real auction data, we attempted to quantify how reference information in<sup>fl</sup>uenced buyer bidding behavior. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, three aspects of referencing were studied.

1. How does reference information impact a bidder's decision?

2. Is all reference information equal in terms of impacts?

3. How does feedback reputation affect potential interactions among online auctions?

The questions are worth studying in that they bear strategic values to online sellers. By understanding the cross-referencing behavior and corresponding decision implications, a seller could make more educated operational and marketing decisions. For instance, understanding how cross-referencing functions can be instrumental to decisions such as auction location and auction duration. A seller could better estimate the potential number of bidders as reference information could directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence auction entry. The reference bid information could be used by bidders to test the water of an auction and strategize his/her bids. Such strategizing shall concern a seller as it could be directly relevant to bidding process and ending prices. The existence of other factors, such as reputation, could very much weaken or strengthen the crossreferencing effect, which shed light on the decision of auction timing and better corroborated the strategic value of established reputation. These decisions are challenging yet critical to the success of any business in online auction markets. Theoretically, this study contributes to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of online auction research by employing statistical methods to “mine the rich consumer data generated by auctions” and better “characterize participant behavior in online auctions” [43]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also attested to the assumption of private value or common value of online auctions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study that empirically modeled and parameterized bid referencing in online auctions.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. In the next section, we reviewed relevant literature on online auctions. Certainly, this section was by no means exhaustive. It was designed to establish a snapshot of extant understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld. In the third section, empirical tests of bid referencing and other factors were conducted. Finally, we presented the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclude with managerial implications.

## 2. Literature review

E-business has been hyped as the catalyst for enormous changes in modern business operations [13,15,38], and [51]. Online auctions stand out due to its low search cost, aggregated platform, and intrinsic allocation ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [26]. [21] found that users on average achieved <sup>fi</sup>fteen percent savings and cycle time reductions by using online auctions [15]. [54] reported that on top of lowered unit prices, participants could also expect to increase market share or win key sales opportunities through online auctions. These bene<sup>fi</sup>ts led to fast proliferation of online auctions [24,50]. [7] reported that other than electronic data interchange (EDI) and proprietary procurement networks, business procurement transactions could also be completed through Internet auctions. [55] advocated using online environments, such as online auctions, to better manage revenue. [49] argued that instead of being a channel for lower value commodity goods, online auctions, if used appropriately, could be a viable choice even for trades of high value products. On the retail side, the success of ebay.com and amazon.com auctions best demonstrated the potential for online auctions. Online auctions created opportunities for e-retailers to dynamically manage revenues [45]. Many research [3,9,10,23], and [27] also showed that online auctions had been a testing ground for new mechanisms. Therefore, online auctions have been relevant and invaluable for both front-end revenue considerations and back-end procurement decisions [6].

Auction theory has been well developed in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of economics and operations research [36,37], and [48]. Traditional theories focused more on trading ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, auction design, bidding issues etc. However, online auctions differ greatly from traditional auctions in that an enormous amount of auctions are simultaneously offered. [28] and [29] noticed that the Internet has changed the auctions in the following ways:

• Reduced transaction costs for both buyers and sellers.

• Accessibility to more participants.

• Easier to describe and disseminate product information.

• The capability to conduct more complex auctions.

[39] further added another two differences:

• Easier to collect auction data.

• The possibility for auctions to join at any time.

In summary, it is easier and less costly to conduct auctions online where buyers enjoy access to an unprecedented amount of information [56]. This creates <sup>fi</sup>erce competition for online auction sellers. Given the added dynamics, [43] pointed out that many assumptions developed for traditional auction theory might not hold online, such as bidder symmetry, common knowledge of private valuation distributions, and the isolation of auctions [5] also found that many of the assumptions to support the traditional auction theory might not hold in the online environment. Recent studies have dedicated their efforts to better revealing the relationship between online auction design parameters and auction performance, such as auction mechanisms [33], minimum bids [1,23], secret reserve price [1,26], bid increase [3], and online reputation [9,17,60].

On empirical estimation of online bids, most of the current empirical studies focused on how individual factors, such as reputation, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced <sup>fi</sup>nal bids. Empirical studies of online auction bids normally regressed a set of hedonic factors on ending prices [3,9,10,23], and [26]. Cross-referencing effect has not been the focus. These empirical results lacked understanding of the dynamic nature of online auctions, which missed the opportunity for auction participants to achieve greater cost savings [17]. To the best of our knowledge, no direct estimation of bid referencing in online auctions has been seen. The online platform aggregated tens of thousands of auctions into one place and lowered search cost. It is only natural to believe that bidders in online auctions cross-checked multiple auctions and bids were more likely the results of cross-referencing than some isolated events. As [43] stated: “On the Internet, multiple auctions for the same product commonly occur simultaneously, and auctions for the same product or types of product repeat over time. These recurrences refute the assumption of isolated auctions.”

Marketing researchers have also studied the effect of reference prices in traditional retailing or auction settings. Consumers used reference prices to make purchase decisions or to estimate the value of an item. [44] incorporated reference prices into a consumer choice model and found that reference prices exerted a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumer behavior. [20] studied the effect of advertised reference prices on a buyer's internal reference prices. They found that advertised reference prices greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uence a buyer's internal reference prices. A recent work by [11] controlled for implicit and explicit reference prices in online auctions. Implicit reference prices were price information used spontaneously by consumers, where explicit reference prices referred to seller- or advertiser-suggested prices. They found that explicit reference prices diminished the in-<sup>fl</sup>uence of adjacent auction prices on the current auction's price. [57] speci<sup>fi</sup>cally studied reference price effect in auctions. They studied internal reference prices, external reference prices and advertised reference prices. They found that internal reference prices were always positively related to bidder valuation. External reference prices would be impactful only if it was adequately low. Advertised reference prices, on the other hand, would be negatively related to bidder valuation. Furthermore, [44] showed that the cross-referencing effect was asymmetric in nature, where brands that were perceived to be closer competitors had higher crossreferencing effects during promotions than brands that were less competing with each other, for example, a Ferrari and a Lamborghini versus a Ferrari and a Honda. Marketing studies suggested that reference price was an in<sup>fl</sup>uential factor in determining buyer behavior. As buyers scrutinized through the online auction market, they might very likely collect reference bid information as implicit reference prices. Such reference prices could also be asymmetric in nature where brands (seller reputation) altered the cross-referencing effect.

This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study that centered on cross-referencing in online auctions. Different from [57], we collected real data, instead of using experimental data from student objects. Our auction information was much more real. The auctions in [57] were in the form of “name your own price”, which did not possess the dynamics of sequential auctions. Most importantly, we studied the reference bidding effect, but not <sup>fi</sup>xed external information, such as retail prices. Advertised reference prices and external reference prices as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by [57] were relevant to our dataset. We collected bid data for a product, where its external price during the data collection window stayed pretty stable. The impact of external retail price was well controlled in our dataset. Our results would not only reveal how bids interacted, but also recommend how sellers should adjust their strategies to increase returns. These results contributed new knowledge to online revenue management [55] and online operation strategies.

## 3. Model development and empirical results

## 3.1. Hypotheses and data collection

Existing conditions could well in<sup>fl</sup>uence buyer valuations of an item. [36] argued that a bidder could obtain more information signals from previous bidders by scrutinizing existing bids. In an isolated auction, such scrutiny was only pertinent to existing bids of the same auction. In online auctions, a large amount of auctions of the same item were pooled together, which conveniently provided any bidder the opportunity to see how much others valued an item. A potential buyer, after observing a current bid $b _ { \mathrm { j t } } .$ in auction j, was likely to revise its own reservation price and place his/her bid $b _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ in auction i. This is the bid referencing effect we proposed to test in this study. Consumers constantly searched for reference information to formulate their reservation prices [11,20]. However, it was still not clear exactly how the reference information could in<sup>fl</sup>uence buyer decisions. It was only natural to believe that higher reference prices would increase a buver's reservation price and hence his/her bids To the opposite, [11] found that advertised reference prices could be negatively related to buyer valuation on an item. Similar results were found by [57] in the context of auctions. [57] further showed that if the external reference price was adequately low, the reference effects would be positive. In conclusion, price referencing effect could vary depending upon the exact measurements and conditions of studies. In our case, the reference bid poised as the most convenient and accessible reference information. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis is:

## Hypothesis 1. Buyer bids are positively influenced by reference bids.

Certainly, existence of strong competitions could be daunting. On one hand, existence of competition could compete away sales. On the other hand, it would be only normal to observe herding of competitors in the brick-and-mortar world incarnated by shopping malls. Herding could also bring more traf<sup>fi</sup>c to everyone. All sellers would see more customers thanks to the existence of established sellers. Although it was only natural to expect more customers for more established sellers, less established sellers could still enjoy “leftovers” from the larger ones. Analogous to Hypothesis 1, the auction traf<sup>fi</sup>c could be inter-related. Similar to reference prices in<sup>fl</sup>uencing current prices, reference traf<sup>fi</sup>c could also determine the current auction traf<sup>fi</sup>c. This led to our Hypothesis 2:

Hypothesis 2. Not only buyer bids, auction traffic is also enhanced by reference auction traffic.

[44] found that prices posted by other sellers could serve as the reference price and hence become competing factors among sellers. In online auctions, homogeneous item auctions were often abundant, such as in the market of electronics. Sellers made every effort to stand out. On top of price competition, reputation distinguished one seller from another. In online auctions, feedback mechanisms built online reputation. It was designed to attenuate information asymmetry issues in online auctions [9,10,17] and [61]. For instance, the Internet Crime and Convention Center, a partnership between the National White Collar Crime Center and the FBI, found that in 2004, 71.2% of all Internet-related complaints – 207,000 in total – pertained to online auctions [58]. The existence of online reputation to a great extent determined buyer trust towards a seller. Most of the extant studies explored the impact of reputation on beginning entry, but few of them looked into the impact of reputation on overall traf-<sup>fi</sup>c. [32] suggested that a seller with better reputation could attract more bids. Current research [33] mostly focused on the impact of seller reputation on the probability to receive the <sup>fi</sup>rst bid, instead of the overall traf<sup>fi</sup>c. This inspired us to propose Hypothesis 3:

Hypothesis 3. Auction traffic is influenced by seller reputation and reference auction traffic.

An interesting behavior, named “snipping”, existed in online auctions [47,50]. [43] showed that such last minute bidding was consistent with bidder rationality. Bidders tended to reveal their ultimate bids and engage in <sup>fi</sup>erce bidding war as the auction approached its end. [12] found that early bids of an auction were often less serious. If the timing of a bid could determine its perceived seriousness, it would be worth asking if timing would also affect the bid referencing effect. In another words, did the timing of a reference bid in its own auction make any difference in the eyes of later information seeking bidders? We proposed to test two aspects of bid timing. First, would the timing discount the reference value of a bid? Second, would the bid timing weaken reputation competition?

Hypothesis 4. Reference bid effect is mitigated (reduced) by reference bid timing.

Hypothesis 5. Reputation effect is mitigated (reduced) by reference bid timing.

To test the reference effect, real auction data were collected from ebay.com. Data were collected directly from auctions of digital cameras during the <sup>fi</sup>rst two months of 2004. At the time, Sony Cyber Shot DSC-F717 was just introduced and became one of the best selling models. During the two-month period, around 500 auctions of Sony F717 with at least one bid were collected. In total, there were approximately 7400 bids. For each bid, we saved the bidding time. By treating the earliest bid in our dataset as starting point zero, a time distance of each bid from the earliest bid could be calculated (we measured the time distance in seconds). The time distance enabled us to calculate the closeness between any two bids. Among all existing bids, the bid that was the closest in time distance to a bid k from another auction was treated as the reference bid for bid k. Only the closest bid, instead of the closest n bids, was used. This was justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the fact that information signals encompassed all previous signals in an auto-regressive manner. The closest bid already took into consideration of the second closest bid and so on. Having said all these, it would certainly be reasonable to believe that the second closest bid or the third closest bid could be the best reference price. The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of exactly which bid would be the best reference could be quite complicated a data mining procedure. We admit that the closest one bid constituted a proxy for reference information and it should be by no means the best or the only choice.

## 3.2. Model development

One may simply propose to estimate a model where a bid is a function of the reference bid and other factors. However, the seemingly viable idea is statistically challenging due to the basic structure of our data and the format of many online auctions. As a commonality structure for many online auction sites, ebay.com also uses the English auction format. Basically, bidders bid up. Bids are monotonically increasing. Hence, the whole series of bids constantly increases in value and wanders about with no tendency to converge to a <sup>fi</sup>xed mean [19]. Assume a series of bid, $b _ { \mathrm { i t } } , b _ { \mathrm { i t - 1 } } . . .$ under the English auction format, the previous bid relates to the current bid [37], in a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed form $b _ { \mathrm { { i t } } } = \rho b _ { \mathrm { { i t - 1 } } } + \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { { i } } } .$ . Then the variance of any $b _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is:

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left(b _ {\mathrm{it}}\right) = \frac {\sigma_ {\varepsilon} ^ {2}}{1 - \rho^ {2}}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov} \left(b _ {\mathrm{it}}, b _ {\mathrm{it-s}}\right) = \frac {\rho^ {| \mathrm{t} - \mathrm{s} |} \sigma_ {\varepsilon} ^ {2}}{1 - \rho^ {2}}
$$

where $E ( \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i } } ) = 0$ and $\mathsf { V a r } \Big ( \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i } } ^ { 2 } \Big ) = \sigma _ { \varepsilon } ^ { 2 } . \mathsf { I f } | \rho | = 1$ , the equations above are unde<sup>fi</sup>ned. The whole series is called non-stationary with unit root. To test if our data was actually stationary, we ran ordinary least square (OLS) regressions of $b _ { \mathrm { i t } } - b _ { \mathrm { i t - 1 } } = \alpha + ( \rho - 1 ) b _ { \mathrm { i t - 1 } } + \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i } } ,$ , where α was just a constant. If $\rho = 1$ , then $\rho - 1 = 0 .$ . Using Dickey−Fuller table [15], the t-statistics of the above regressions for three of the auctions with more than thirty bids were −0.47 (number of $\mathbf { b i d } s =$ 52, critical value $- 3 . 2 2 )$ , and $- 1 . 7 5$ (number of bids = 51, critical value −3.22), −1.69 (number of ${ \mathrm { b i d } } s = 4 8 ,$ , critical value 3.22) at 0.05 signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level. Unit root was very much an issue for the auctions. Due to page limit, we would not report all statistics for all <sup>fi</sup>ve hundred auctions. In conclusion, our series of data could very much assume the form of $b _ { \mathrm { { i t } } } = \alpha + b _ { \mathrm { { i t } } - 1 } + \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { { i } } } ,$ where the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for $b _ { \mathrm { i t - 1 } }$ could be one, the unit root problem.

A normal practice to remove the non-stationary issue is to take the <sup>fi</sup>rst order difference, $b _ { \mathrm { i t } } - b _ { \mathrm { i t } - 1 } [ 1 9 ]$ . If the series is integrated of order one, I(1), such difference taking should remove the nonstationary property. For instance, for the auction that earned <sup>fi</sup>fty-one bids, taking the <sup>fi</sup>rst order difference gave us a series that generated an OLS t-statistics $\mathrm { 0 f } - 4 . 7 4$ , a strong rejection of the null hypothesis $\rho - 1 = 0 .$ . Hence, we speci<sup>fi</sup>ed our model as a difference model in order to remove the I(1) property, where the dependent variable was $b _ { \mathrm { i t } } - b _ { \mathrm { i t } - 1 } ,$ , the bid increment.

The concept of bid increment, in auction literature, possesses further theoretical and practical implications. Under traditional assumptions of English auctions, a reasonable strategy would be to bid the minimum bid increment [46]. What this means is that no variation of bid increments should be observed. All bid increments are simply the minimum bid increase. However, there is another bidding strategy, bidding more than the sum of the old bid plus the minimum bid increment, which is called jump bidding [1]. [34] found that bidders placed jump bids due to the existence of bidding costs. [1] modeled jump bidding as a way to intimidate opponent bidders. [14] analytically showed that on top of bidding costs, another reason for jump bidding was the bidder uncertainty of future entry. As a result, jump bidding tended to reduce the number of bids placed. In the current literature, the economic meanings of bid increments to auction sellers are basically three fold: 1. [4] showed that a <sup>fi</sup>xed minimum bid increment could reduce the allocation ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency if the potential highest bid was between the second highest bid and the minimum bid increase. [14] found that jump bidding reduced the number of bids placed, which was certainly related to the probability of <sup>fi</sup>nal sale. Sellers would not prefer an auction that ended as a failure. 2. [46] found empirically that jump bidding on average increased winning bid, which raised the <sup>fi</sup>nal winning price. This certainly favored sellers. 3. [22] focused purely on minimum bid increment. His discussion revealed interesting ideas of bid increment relevant to this research. Bid increment could be used as a tool to test the maximum reserve of an opponent bidder. Hence, the strategic action space of an auction could be changed by the size of bid increment.

Overall, the bid increment could greatly concern a seller for his sale probability, ending price and market dynamics.

There were also econometric issues in simply including the reference bid. We labeled all bids with a timing index that re<sup>fl</sup>ected the order upon which bids came into an auction. For instance, the last bid, the highest in an English auction, was labeled bid 1 and so on. We found that there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive correlation (0.477) between the timing of a bid and the timing of its reference bid: the early bids (smaller bids) tended to have also early (smaller) reference bids. As later bid increments in an auction tended to be smaller [23,39,43], a natural inverse relationship would exist between reference bids and bid increments. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant regression result using the reference bid could be spurious as it would be hard to tell which force was at work, the natural reverse relationship by structural setup or a suggestive relationship due to bidder behavior [19]. As we chose the dependent variable to be in difference form, the reference bid was also adjusted into difference form, $b _ { \mathrm { j t } } - b _ { \mathrm { i t } - 1 }$ , in order to eliminate the potential structural in<sup>fl</sup>uence. This was basically the difference between the reference bid and the latest existing bid. We call this reference bid jump.

To test for our hypotheses and control for other factors, we added other variables into our empirical models.

➢ Seller reputation was calculated as the reputation difference between sellers of the current auction and the reference auction. Ebay.com quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed feedback summaries for each seller. We used the difference of the most recent six month reputation as our measurements, positive6<sub>i</sub>−positive<sub>j</sub> and negative6<sub>i</sub>−negative<sub>j</sub>.

➢ Hotness of an auction was introduced in order to control for the effect of heated bidding. We counted the number of bids that were placed two hours before a bid was placed. This count was used as a hotness proxy at the time the bid was placed. This measurement was also used to measure the traf<sup>fi</sup>c of bids in auctions. The heatedness for both the current auction and the reference auction was calculated.

➢ We also controlled our estimation for accessory bundle values as not all auctions were identical in accessories bundled. The difference between the reference auction's accessory value and the current auction's accessory value was calculated.

➢ Bid timing was measured by the bid timing label. Each bid, as it enters an auction, will be labeled. The winning bid was labeled one and the <sup>fi</sup>rst bid received the highest label value of an auction. This timing is a measure of a bid's position in its own auction. For instance, the very <sup>fi</sup>rst bid of an auction with 25 bids was labeled the 25th bid.

M2 was designed to test if the reference bid effect would change given the reference bid became later or earlier in the reference auction.

Similarly, to test the bid timing effect on reputation, model 3 was constructed.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{BI} _ {\mathrm{i}, (\mathrm{t}, \mathrm{t} - 1)} & = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{AJ} _ {\mathrm{i}, (\mathrm{jt}, \mathrm{it} - 1)} + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{PS} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{NG} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} \mathrm{ACC} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} + \beta_ {5} \mathrm{CAT} + \beta_ {6} \mathrm{RAT} + \beta_ {7} \mathrm{PS} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} ^ {*} \mathrm{BL} _ {\mathrm{ij}, \mathrm{t}} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {8} \mathrm{NG} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} * \mathrm{BL} _ {\mathrm{ij}, \mathrm{t}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}}. \end{array}\tag{M3}
$$

M3 was constructed to see how the bid timing interacted with reputation effect.

Auction traf<sup>fi</sup>c interactions were estimated in M4. Different from previous models, the dependent variable, the number of bids within the two hour window, was by counts. Counts of arrivals traditionally followed Poisson distribution [59] than the asymptotic normal distribution that has been often assumed in regression models. In this case, we estimated a count model. The model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation followed existing studies on auction entry decisions [32,60]. In these studies, basically three categories of variables were included, reputation (PS and NG in our model), auction operational, such as duration of an auction (AL) and accessory value differences (ACC), and market dynamics, such as the number of auctions of one day before the start of an auction (MD). Our model 4 speci<sup>fi</sup>cation was as below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{CAT} _ {\mathrm{it}} & = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{AL} + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{ACC} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{PS} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} + \beta_ {4} \mathrm{NG} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} \\ & + \beta_ {5} \mathrm{MD} _ {\mathrm{i}} + \beta_ {6} \mathrm{RAT} _ {\mathrm{j}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}}. \end{array}\tag{M4}
$$

## 3.3. Econometric estimation and model results

## 3.3.1. Econometric estimation

In the previous section, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned a difference by difference model in order to remove the unit root issue. This allowed us to better focus on complications within the variance–covariance matrix of a regression model. The error terms could be auto-correlated. Godfrey's serial correlation test [18] was conducted. Our statistics showed that the AR(1) test (6.4826 with Pr>LM=0.0109) gave the strongest rejection of the null hypothesis that the error terms were white noise. This led us to estimate a regression under a <sup>fi</sup>rst order autoregressive structure. Furthermore, we had about 500 auctions in the dataset and one could not help ponder upon the possibility of cross-sectional heterogeneity. Cross-sectional heterogeneity has been normally controlled by either the <sup>fi</sup>xed effect model or the random effect model, where the latter assumed that the heterogeneity was not related to independent variables [19]. Hausman's speci<sup>fi</sup>cation test [19] was conducted to choose between the two methods. The test returned a value of 6.18 (Pr>m=0.4033), which could not reject the null hypothesis and favored the random effect model. As the data challenged us with both cross-sectional heterogeneity and autocorrelation, we used the Parks method [40] to obtain an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimation of the variance–covariance matrix for M1, M2, and M3.

Endogeneity could be an issue for auction models due to various reasons [32]. Once present, endogeneity could seriously bias parameter estimations that ultimately weakened our results and theoretical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. To test the robustness of our results, we made further attempts to con<sup>fi</sup>rm our results with endogeneity treatments. We tested M1 as it was the building block for all following models. Our logic was that if endogeneity treatments did not improve or alter the model results, such as changes of signs of parameter estimations and/or changes of the scale of parameter values, we would be able to safely dissolve the concerns of endogeneity and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the robustness of our results. As M1 served as the building block for all following models, a con<sup>fi</sup>rmation, if obtained, should extend to M2 and M3 too. The classic method of dealing with model endogeneity is the instrumental variable method [19]. It involves the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of instrumental variables that correlate with only the endogenous variable, but not the error terms. We used a classic selection of instrumental variables in time-series data, the lagged variable [19]. As the focus of this research was on the effect of reference bid jump on bid increment, we used estimation with lagged reference bid jump as the instrumental variable. The two stage least square (2SLS) model also explicitly considered cross-sectional heterogeneity and error term autocorrelation issues. [33] raised the issue of non-normality that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence online auction data. [8] used a Monte Carlos simulation to test the robustness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of 2SLS under non-normal data distribution. The simulation results concluded that 2SLS was still a robust and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient method under non-normal data distributions. The exact equation for the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of 2SLS was:

$$
\mathrm{AJ} _ {\mathrm{i}, (\mathrm{jt}, \mathrm{it} - 1), \mathrm{T}} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{AJ} _ {\mathrm{i}, (\mathrm{jt}, \mathrm{it} - 1), \mathrm{T} - 1} + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{PS} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{NG} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}}
$$

where $\mathsf { A l } _ { \mathrm { T } } .$ was the lagged reference bid jump. Despite our efforts to treat for endogeneity issues, we will not claim that there would be no further bene<sup>fi</sup>t by adding more variables to our models. Due to the availability of data, we had to take the inadequacy as a limitation for the paper. Future research may want to expand our models to further con<sup>fi</sup>rm or test our results.

M4 happened to involve a count of arrivals, the number of bids within a two hour period. As we discussed previously, the data structure did not lend itself to normal distribution. We estimated a Poisson distribution regression. As cross-sectional heterogeneity was still present, we further estimated a count model under the negative binomial distribution to con<sup>fi</sup>rm our estimation with the Poisson distribution [41]. Negative binomial distribution is a way to accommodate cross-sectional heterogeneity in count models. The distribution is believed to be appropriate when each individual section follows Poisson distributions but the joint distribution would no longer be Poisson.

## 3.3.2. Results and discussions

M1 results were presented in Table 3. Between the OLS and Parks method results, it was obvious that Parks method successfully improved the estimation ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The adjacent jump was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive. After controlling for potential heatedness in each auctions and other possible in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factors, such as accessory value difference, our results suggested if the reference bid surpassed an existing bid by one dollar, a bidder would in general bid up by \$0.025 in his/her own bid. On average, the adjacent bid went up by \$64.28, which brought to the current auction a jump of \$1.607. At the average level, there were 6.69 bids per auction, which translated to \$10.75 in overall value. The reference auction did play a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in determining bidder valuation. In our models, the valuation was measured by bid changes, not the absolute bid itself. The economic value of such referencing turned out to be fairly small, which suggested two possible explanations. First, we controlled for many other factors that could also in<sup>fl</sup>uence bid change. Almost all of them turned out signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. This suggested that bidders did not focus on monetary reference value as the only bid-in<sup>fl</sup>uencing information. Referencing decision was multi-dimensional in nature. Second, there could be better speci<sup>fi</sup>cations to calculate the reference bid, such as a weighted combination of the closest, the second closest, or the third closest with a certain speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. We admit that it is a limitation for this study and we propose to explore the issue in future research. In conclusion, Hypothesis 1 was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that reference bid is an in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factor.

The 2SLS results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the robustness of our results against potential endogeneity issues. None of the parameter estimations underwent serious changes of sign, scale, or signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. All variables, except the reference auction traf<sup>fi</sup>c, were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the 2SLS result. The adjacent jump variable still turned out to be positive and very signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. All parameter values were very similar in scale from the Parks method results to the 2SLS results. As M1 was the building block of M2 and M3, we concluded that our model results were robust and endogeneity was not present or its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on our results was minimal.

M1 also speci<sup>fi</sup>cally tested the in<sup>fl</sup>uence from feedback reputation on buyer valuation. The feedback in<sup>fl</sup>uence has been well studied in existing literature [23,60]. It was not surprising that the feedback effects were con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in our empirical results. However, none of the existing studies studied reputation difference across auctions (note that ebay.com feedback score is a within feedback difference measurement). Existing studies only measured a seller's own reputation and its effect on the <sup>fi</sup>nal bid. [9] concluded that feedback reputation, despite the mixed results in current studies, seemed to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor for online auction bids. Essentially, reputation works in relativity. Previous studies only tested the reputation effect, to abuse the term a little bit, at a more macro/market level. Our test here speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focused on the micro/transaction level effects. Our results suggested that not only auction bids were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the feedback reputation, the <sup>fi</sup>rst order marginal change of bids was also a function of the <sup>fi</sup>rst order difference feedback reputation.

Reputation difference would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure without the digital feedback counts. In our model, positive feedback and negative feedback counts were both contrasted between the current and the reference sellers. Our results indicated that the reputation difference was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing buyer bids. Consider a case where a new comer that just came into the market with zero reputation history. In comparison to an average reputation seller with 2606.46 positive comments, the competition due to reputation would cost the new comer \$1.824 (−0.0007×2606.46) on each bid. There were on average 6.69 bids for each auction, this would translate to \$12.20 (1.824× 6.69). This had to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loss for any online sellers.

M2 and M3 results were presented in Table 4. As OLS was not ef <sup>fi</sup>cient, we did not report the OLS results for model M2 and M3. As all other variables stayed stable without drastic changes from M1, we would focus on our interaction effects in this discussion. In our dataset, the larger the timing label was, the earlier the bid was placed in an auction. A positive timing difference meant the reference bid was earlier than the current bid. Our results $( \beta _ { 6 } = - 1 . 3 5 )$ suggested that the reference bid effect was drastically weakened. On the other hand, when the difference was negative, a buyer received much more reliable and valuable signals that led to a strong boost of the reference bid effect. Hence, the mitigating effect of bid timing on bid referencing (Hypothesis 5) was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed. More interestingly, our results showed that bid referencing was also non-symmetric in nature. Different from [57] where the external reference price was measured by retail quotes, our reference was the reference bid. The reference bid re<sup>fl</sup>ected other buyers' valuations, instead of a price quote. [57] found that the lower was the external reference price, the more informative was the reference. In the case of auction bids, we found that the later was the reference bid, the more informative was the reference. Our results revealed another perspective in referencing practices. As retail prices are normally calculated results from supply functions, our reference bid estimation was closer in nature to buyer valuation on the demand side. Hypothesis 4 was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed.

Our results of current auction traf<sup>fi</sup>c showed that bidders were well aware of this. As number of bids 2 h before a bid came in increased, the bid increment actually plummeted. ## 4. Conclusions and implications

Our results showed that local bids were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by reference bids. The closest reference bid signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected bid increments. We also found that reputation difference was another factor that could determine bids for an item. The effects of both factors, bid referencing and reputation, were mitigated by bid timing. For bid referencing, earlier bids were considered less serious in terms of information value. For reputation effects, earlier bids weakened the reputation competition. Interestingly, bidders were well aware of over-bidding where the heatedness of an auction was negatively related to bid increments. Bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c, a proxy for auction heatedness, was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the traf<sup>fi</sup>c in the reference auction. However, bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c was not quite in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by reputation difference.

Price referencing was well known in marketing and consumer decision theories [11,44,49]. However, if similar arguments could be carried over to online auctions was yet to be tested. Our results provided empirical supports. If retail prices were calculated results of the supply function, our results took the perspective of demand function and tested the referencing practice directly on bids, a metric that was better related to buyer valuations. Through this study, the dynamic nature of online auctions unfolded itself in two ways. First, a seller should actively monitor neighboring auctions to better estimate his/her own bids. Empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study justi<sup>fi</sup>ed the practice. Second, online reputation was much easier to quantify, which triggered acute reputation competition. We calculated reputation difference and found that buyer actively used reputation difference to adjust and <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune their valuations. If the seller of a reference auction possessed a better/worse reputation, a bidder would accordingly decrease/increase its own bid increment. Different from existing research that focused purely on within-auction reputation and bids, our results shed light on the <sup>fi</sup>rst order marginal relationship between reputation and auction bids using across auction factors. If existing literature [32,33] built a positive relationship between bids and reputation in their original values, our study further connected the two variables on their <sup>fi</sup>rst order marginal changes. A stronger reputation not only rewards a seller higher bid, but also leads to heftier bid increments. None of the current studies revealed this relationship. The <sup>fi</sup>rst order correlation could provide interesting insights for computational studies that aim at bid forecasting.

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of accessory value difference and within-auction heatedness, in addition to reputation, revealed the multi-dimensional nature of online bidding decision. The decision was, but not solely, a result of reference bids. This certainly challenged bidders in formulating their valuations and making <sup>fi</sup>nal decisions. Accessory values played an important role in such valuation process and to a certain extent proved effectiveness of bundling strategies. Within-auction heatedness turned out to slow down bid increments. Theoretically, by bidder rationality for winner's curse, this result could offer support for the common value nature of online auctions, as least in settings such as ebay.com. For sellers, this <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that inducing bids would not be the solely bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and the overall impact could be a U shaped curve. There could be an optimal number of bids that a seller should carefully calculate [62].

Bid timing was found to be a critical mitigating variable in this study. Its mitigating effects were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in both M2 and M3. An earlier reference bid was not equally valuable in its information value as a later reference bid. This further guided online sellers in estimating their <sup>fi</sup>nal bids, where reference bids that were later in their own auctions should be given heavier weights. Earlier bids were not so serious, which is consistent with existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings in traditional auctions [12] and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the notion in the context of online auctions. The mitigating effect of bid timing on reputation further suggested that not only were earlier bids not so serious, they could even be opportunistic. As reputation constituted a critical decision factor for online bidding, weakened impacts of reputation and a positive correlation between bid index and reference timing implied that earlier bidders behaved quite differently from later bidders. Earlier bidders could be purely deal seekers that acted more upon odds than certainties or determination. Future research may want to better con<sup>fi</sup>rm our proposition here.

Mitigating effects of bid timing offered interesting strategic values for online competition. First, later reference bids were considered more serious. For sellers with similar reputation pro<sup>fi</sup>les, to obtain more reference gains, one should consider listing his/her auctions with equal or close durations as others do. As two auctions approach their ends at almost the same pace, gains from bid referencing, especially the reference gains from later bids, can be greater. It has been common that sellers with similar reputation herd together in online auctions. Our results provided an explanation for the practice. Second, reputation has been and still is a major differentiator. This would not be an issue for sellers that already established strong reputation pro<sup>fi</sup>les. However, for less established sellers, the bid timing effect on reputation competition offers another route to avoid direct competition. First, one should try the best they could to post their auctions at a time that strong competitors are not abundant. This is to avoid head-to-head reputation competition. However, if this is not feasible (established sellers are everywhere) or not preferred (a demand peak that nobody could afford to miss), our results further suggest that changing auction duration may be a convenient tactic for less established sellers. As an auction approached its end, the reference auction may be still early in its auction that weakens the reputation competition. Certainly, the downside of such practices is the loss of potential bidders as suggested by the positive correlation between auction duration and the number of bids.

The existence of reputation competition, on the other hand, might not be completely negative. Our bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c model suggested that reputable sellers have brought more bidders for everyone. As a result, posting auctions away from established sellers has its shortcomings: one lost the full <sup>fl</sup>ow of bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c brought in by major sellers. Interestingly, the reputation difference did not matter in determining bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c. As we discussed before, the reputation effect might matter only at the beginning of entry [23] and quickly faded as the <sup>fi</sup>rst several bidders built up trust.

The results of auction heatedness on bid increment suggested that more traf<sup>fi</sup>c did not guarantee higher bid increments. The results seemed to point to bidder rationality where bidders actively curb their enthusiasm on over-bidding, the winner's curse. Another interesting implication of this research is the relevance of our results to the traditional study of auction theory. We have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several literatures in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of jump bidding, which is the practice of bidding more than the minimum bid increase. [46] stated that if the online auctions were indeed private value auctions, then the process through which an auction reached its <sup>fi</sup>nal bid would not matter, or would not be path dependent. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings seem to suggest otherwise, where each new bid increment was a function of its previous reference bid jump. The reference bid jump was calculated using the previous bid, which was a result of the previous bid increment. The path through which all bids entered an auction surely determined the level of bids. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings of path dependency, jointly with bidder rationality for winner's curse, provided further evidences that could support the common value assumption. More interestingly, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings were obtained in a simultaneous auction setting. Value commonality extended beyond single auction. The cross referencing effects have, from a different aspect, provided a more general assumption for the online auction market.

This study contributed to current literature in the following ways:

➢ This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study that explicitly tested bid referencing, instead of price referencing. Our results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the role reference bid played in buyer valuation. We also found that bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c in an auction was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by reference auction traf<sup>fi</sup>c. The two cross-correlations have not been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by extant studies.

➢ We revealed several strategic insights on online auction decisions yet to be witnessed in existing literature.

o Our results suggested that not only bids, but bid increments were also a function of reputation. A good reputation rewarded a seller both higher ending bids [23] and heftier bid increments. o Bid timing had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant mitigating effects on both reference bid and reputation. The notion of lack of seriousness for early bids was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in the context of online auctions. The timing effect offered new tactical spaces for established and less established sellers to compete and respond to each other's moves. The strength and weakness of the added tactical spaces were also analyzed based on our empirical results.

o Reputation was important in bid referencing, but not in determining bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Our bid traf<sup>fi</sup>c model did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any statistical support for reputation measurements. In combination with previous research [23,32], our results seemed to suggest a fading effect of reputation as more bids entered an auction.

➢ Our results on bidder rationality for winner's curse and path dependency of online auctions further supported the common value assumption for large scale English auctions. The crossreferencing perspective of this research could suggest a more general common-value structure for the online auction market.

From the author's point of view, there are several ways future research could improve on and expand this study. First, due to data availability, most studies, including this one, used ebay.com data. Business auction data has been rare. Future research based on business auctions can provide valuable validation for current <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, exactly how bidders process and use reference information should be better analyzed. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of bidder valuation function deserves more exploration. Third, the question of what the optimal timing and duration may be for an online seller is yet to be answered. Analogous to the notion of optimal number of bids for an auction, the optimality of timing and duration should also be better engineered. Fourth, how results from this study carry over to multiple-item auctions could be interesting to auction researchers. Multi-item auctions are inherently more complicated. As [5] stated, a substantial proportion of business auctions were multiple item auctions. Therefore, there are merits in understanding these auctions. Fifth, it would also be interesting to test direct relationship between bid increment and seller revenue or pro<sup>fi</sup>tability.
