# Research Commentary

# Information Technology-Enabled Business Models: A Conceptual Framework and a Coevolution Perspective for Future Research

Arun Rai

Center for Process Innovation and Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30303, arunrai@gsu.edu

Xinlin Tang

Department of Management, College of Business, Florida State University, Tallahassee, Florida 32306, xtang2@fsu.edu

here is growing recognition that firms’ information technology (IT)-enabled business models (i.e., how inter-Tfirm transactions with suppliers, customers, and partners are structured and executed) are a distinctive source of value creation and appropriation. However, the concept of business models’ (BMs) “IT enablement” remains coarse in the information systems and strategic management literatures. Our objectives are to intro duce a framework to elaborate the concept of IT-enabled BMs and to identify areas for future research that will enhance our understanding of the subject. We introduce the idea that two business-to-business (B2B) IT capabilities—dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization—are the mediating execution mechanisms between the strategic intent of interfirm collaboration and the (re)configuration of BMs to both create and appropriate value. We develop the logic that B2B IT capabilities for BM (re)configuration operate at two levels— IT customization at the dyadic relationship level and IT standardization at the interfirm network level—that together provide the complementary IT capabilities for firms to exchange content, govern relationships, and structure interconnections between products and processes with a diverse set of customers, suppliers, and partners. We discuss how these two complementary B2B IT capabilities are pivotal for firms to pursue different sources of value creation and appropriation. We identify how a firm’s governance choices to engage in interfirm collaboration and its interfirm networks coevolve with its B2B IT capabilities as fruitful areas for future research.

Keywords: IT-enabled business model; B2B IT capabilities; interfirm collaboration; governance choices; design elements; value creation; value appropriation; coevolution

## Introduction

The importance of external resources and interfirm collaboration for firms’ competitive success continues to grow dramatically as the competitive context is increasingly characterized by the disaggregation of traditional industries, digital interconnection between products and processes within and across industries, and high interdependence among firms’ competitive (re)actions (Ferrier et al. 2010, Tanriverdi et al. 2010). Peaks—or strategic opportunities—present short windows before morphing into valleys—or positions of losses—as new peaks develop. There is growing recognition that in this context, a firm’s performance is influenced not only by firm-specific factors (e.g., internal resources and administrative structures) and industry characteristics (e.g., resource dependence and clock speed (Mendelson 2000)), but also (or primarily) by its information technology (IT)- enabled business model (BM), which “elucidates how an organization is linked to external stakeholders, and how it engages in economic exchanges with them to create value for all exchange partners.” (Emphasis added; Zott and Amit 2007, p. 181.)

To elaborate, a BM represents the IT-enabled interfirm exchanges through which a focal firm’s strategic intent for interfirm collaborations is executed. Best-practice firms for BMs (e.g., Cisco, Amazon, Priceline, eBay, Procter and Gamble, and Google) excel in developing and leveraging IT capabilities to exchange content with partners, govern different types of interfirm relationships, and structure transactions in novel ways, thereby accruing competitive advantages. Broader empirical evidence also suggests that a firm’s BM affects value creation and appropriation above and beyond firm-specific factors (e.g., product market strategy) and industry factors (e.g., speed of innovation, concentration), thereby making a firm’s BM an important—but understudied—unit of analysis (Zott and Amit 2008).

As neither the sources of value creation and appropriation nor firms’ capabilities are static, firms need to adapt their strategic intentions for interfirm collaboration, develop their portfolio of business-to-business (B2B) IT capabilities to enact these intentions, and leverage B2B IT capabilities to (re)configure their BMs. Accordingly, best-practice firms for BMs have been adept not only at adapting their strategic intentions for interfirm collaboration but also at developing and leveraging B2B IT capabilities to (re)configure BMs. Indeed, firms’ inability to (re)configure their BMs has hamstrung their strategic intent to achieve supply chain efficiencies, develop and commercialize innovations, and realize complementarities with partners’ products and services to the point of even destroying firms that were once industry leaders (Lafley and Charan 2008).

Given BMs’ value creation and appropriation potential, the central organization design question is shifting from a firm’s administrative structure to the IT-enabled structural organization to execute exchanges with external stakeholders (Zott and Amit 2007). A main question raised from this shift is what differentiates firms’ ability to (re)configure BMs to execute their strategic intent for interfirm collaboration. Although there is general recognition of the critical role of IT in enabling BMs (e.g., Amit and Zott 2012, Teece 2010), the concept of BMs’ “IT enablement” has received limited attention in the strategic management and information systems literatures and remains a coarse and largely elusive concept. We advance the viewpoint that the missing links between a firm’s strategic intent for interfirm collaboration and its ability to implement the corresponding BMs are the firm’s B2B IT capabilities to customize exchanges in dyadic relationships and to standardize exchanges across its interfirm network. We examine how the development of these two B2B IT capabilities is affected by a firm’s strategic intent for interfirm collaboration, thereby responding to the critique that the capabilities literature has made an implicit “capabilities first” assumption by overlooking the influence of comparative governance choices (e.g., judgments to engage in types of interfirm collaborations) on the heterogeneity in capabilities across a population of firms (Argyres and Zenger 2012). We also evaluate how these B2B IT capabilities are complementary for exchanging content with partners, governing different types of interfirm relationships, and structuring transactions with external stakeholders, thereby representing foundational IT capabilities to (re)configure BMs for different value creation and appropriation sources.

Our commentary has the following two objectives: (1) introduce a framework identifying firms’ strategic intentions to develop BMs, the B2B IT capabilities at the dyadic and interfirm network levels required to support the design of BMs, and the complementary roles of these two B2B IT capabilities in (re)configuring BMs for different value creation and appropriation sources (Figure 1); and (2) identify avenues for future research at the interstices of B2B IT capabilities with a firm’s governance choices and with a firm’s interfirm networks.

## 1. Strategic Intent for Interfirm Collaboration and Design

## Requirements for Business Models

Recent developments in the strategic management literature suggest that a firm’s strategic intents encompass its managerial judgments—and resulting intentions—not only with respect to product markets but also with respect to structuring interfirm collaborations with partners, suppliers, and customers (Christensen 2001). To elaborate, product market strategy is concerned with the products, customers, and geographies, the generic strategies (e.g., cost leadership, product differentiation) to accompany product market positioning decisions, and entry into, and exit from, product markets. In contrast, a BM is the ITenabled “structural template that describes the organization of a focal firm’s transactions with all of its external constituents in factor and product markets” (Zott and Amit 2008, p. 3). Amit and Zott (2001) and Zott and Amit (2008) argue that in a digitally interconnected world, the organization and structure of transactions—or how a firm’s governance choices to pursue interfirm collaborations are structured and executed—with partners, suppliers, and customers make up a distinct source of value creation and appropriation that differs from a firm’s product market strategy. They support this claim about BMs’ value creation potential with empirical evidence (based on a qualitative case-based analysis of 59 firms and survey/archival data analysis of 190 firms) that BMs do not replace or subsume the well-examined firm- and industry-specific effects on firm performance in past studies (e.g., Hawawini et al. 2003, McGahan and Porter 2002, Rumelt 1991) but are rather an additional source of value.

The above rationale for the broadened conceptualization of a firm’s strategic intent to go beyond product markets and to encompass BMs is being largely fueled by IT innovations that can be combined with other resources (e.g., human capital, process resources) to develop B2B IT capabilities to meet the complex—and diverse—design requirements for BMs. These B2B IT capabilities have changed, and continue to change, how firms develop strategic intentions through comparative governance choices on organizing activities internally or through forms of interfirm collaborations (Arikan and Rai 2011, Jacobides and Winter 2012). Importantly, the heterogeneity in B2B IT capabilities is endogenous to a firm’s comparative governance choices, or its strategic intentions for interfirm collaboration (Argyres et al. 2012).

In proceeding to analyze how a firm’s BMs are enabled by IT, we first recognize that a firm’s portfolio of strategic intentions for interfirm collaboration results in design requirements for structuring interfirm collaboration—or the BM—that need to be enabled by IT and drive investments and firm-specific trajectories in the development of B2B IT capabilities. These BM design requirements pertain to the following three constitutive elements (Amit and Zott 2001): (1) content, or the goods or information exchanged with partners; (2) governance, or the control of information/resources/goods, the exchange’s legal form of organization, and the incentives for participants in the exchange; and (3) structure, or the parties that participate in the exchange and the ways in which these parties and their products/processes are linked.

We identify five key strategic motivations for why firms develop interfirm collaborations, thereby generating distinct BM design requirements (Table 1):

1. When the firm’s interfirm collaboration objective is to achieve supply chain efficiencies, it is concerned about reducing supply chain uncertainty, decreasing costly buffers (e.g., inventory), and decreasing transaction and coordination costs with supply chain partners (Rai et al. 2006). This objective generates BM design requirements to (a) capture, pool, and share supply chain information (e.g., events, plans, and resource positions) with partners (e.g., Malhotra et al. 2005); (b) execute processes (e.g., continuous replenishment) collaboratively with partners (Malhotra et al. 2005, Rai et al. 2012); and (c) restructure transactions with individual partners at a supply chain stage and with partners across supply chain stages (e.g., Klein and Rai 2009, Malhotra et al. 2007).

2. When the firm’s interfirm collaboration objective is to develop market responsiveness, it seeks to scale capacity efficiently in response to fluctuations in factor and product markets. This objective generates BM design requirements to (a) collect, pool, and share real-time demand/supply signals (Gunasekaran et al. 2008); (b) enable mutual adjustment to partners’ supply chain plans and exceptions in their operations as well as reduce relationship-specific investments to lower switching costs (Gosain et al. 2004); and (c) restructure dyadic collaboration when the focus is on the responsiveness of a given relationship for a supply chain activity and multilateral collaboration when the focus is on a set of relationships for supply chain activities.

3. When the firm’s interfirm collaboration objective is to design, develop, and/or commercialize innovative offerings, it needs to combine its knowledge-based resources and capabilities with those of partners to develop products and services and reduce conceptto-customer cycle times (e.g., Dyer and Hatch 2006, Ragatz et al. 1997). This objective generates BM design requirements to (a) share market intelligence, customer requirements, and product and concept ideas with partners; (b) integrate knowledge across firms’ knowledge boundaries (e.g., Carlile 2002) while safeguarding intellectual property (Clemons and Hitt 2004); and (c) enable the digital management of knowledge-intensive collaboration relating to individual partners or a consortia of partners (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Dyer and Nobeoka 2000).

## 2. B2B IT Capabilities to Implement BM Design Requirements

We develop the argument that BM design requirements lead firms to invest in and develop B2B IT capabilities—defined as the focal firm’s ability to manipulate its digital network of information to create, control, and execute interfirm transactions efficiently (Kim and Mahoney 2006). B2B IT capabilities provide firms with the ability to establish digital connections between partners for information sharing and activity coordination, thereby forming the technological foundation to support the design requirements for interfirm collaboration (Malhotra et al. 2005, Rai et al. 2009, Ross et al. 2006, Saraf et al. 2007). We suggest that meeting the design requirements for BMs requires firms to develop two B2B IT capabilities that operate at distinct levels: IT customization at the dyadic interfirm relationship level and IT standardization at the focal firm’s business network level.

## 2.1. Role of Dyadic IT Customization in Enabling BMs

We define dyadic IT customization as a firm’s IT capability to accommodate an interfirm relationship’s idiosyncratic requirements for information sharing (e.g., what to share, how much, how often, with what level of personalization, etc.); governance (e.g., rights to resource attributes, and level of security and privacy); and transaction structure (e.g., collaborative execution of interdependent tasks). Dyadic IT customization represents a firm’s ability to develop build-to-order IT interfaces for a given relationship to exchange custom information; tailor business rules; resolve syntactic and semantic differences with a partner’s databases; and integrate hardware platforms, communication technologies, and applications to work seamlessly with a partner (Broadbent et al. 1999, Turnbull 1991). This capability can be applied to architect digital linkages that accommodate unique integration requirements to transmit, combine, and process data and coordinate activities with a partner (Grover and Saeed 2007, Rai et al. 2006). Examples of dyadic IT customization include a pioneering supply chain visibility system that Apple and Fritz developed for the just-in-time global movement of inventory (Kopczak and Lee 1997); a service-level reporting system that Hewlett-Packard (HP) and United Parcel Service (UPS) developed to monitor specialized shipping, inventory management, and warehousing activities and to foster collaborative learning and mutual adjustment (Lewis et al. 2007); and customized IT processes established between a global logistics leader and its strategic customers to share proprietary information related to plans, resource positions, and finances (Klein and Rai 2009).

With the ability to meet the idiosyncratic exchange requirements of individual partners, dyadic IT customization can help the focal firm achieve quasiintegration with its partners (Subramani and Venkatraman 2003, Venkatraman and Zaheer 1990). Quasi-integration promotes cospecialization, and develops mutual trust and reciprocal interdependence with partners. These attributes are indispensable for a focal firm to search for product/process innovation by exploring synergistic specificity in an interfirm relationship—that is, “the degree to which [it] achieves greater functionality by its components being specific to one another” (Schilling 2000, p. 316).

Since relation-specific investment usually loses its value when transferred to another relationship such that the value from its current use is greater than the value from its next best use (Dyer and Singh 1998), the customized digital linkage between partners can serve as a lock-in mechanism. On the one hand, establishing a customized digital linkage shows the focal firm’s intention to maintain a long-term relationship with a partner (Rai et al. 2006), which may promote partnering firms to jointly invest in and sponsor innovations (Boudreau 2010, Shapiro and Varian 1999). On the other hand, since such a customized digital linkage serving a given relationship cannot be easily transferred to support other relationships, it safeguards against potential opportunistic behavior and involuntary knowledge spillover (Ahuja 2000).

## 2.2. The Role of Network IT Standardization in Enabling BMs

We define network IT standardization as the firm’s ability to leverage modularized IT resources and B2B IT standards to facilitate exchange with its network of suppliers, customers, and partners. By leveraging network IT standardization, firms can establish common processes to exchange data and structure activities with a large number of partners, thereby reducing the need for idiosyncratic IT—and accompanying relationship-specific investments—in interfirm relationships (Malhotra et al. 2007, Ross and Beath 2006). Moreover, network IT standardization reflects the firm’s IT capability to deploy B2B IT standards— proprietary, quasi-proprietary, or open—to remap efficiently both the structure of its interfirm network and the governance of interfirm relationships (Rai and Tang 2010, Tanriverdi et al. 2010). The network IT standardization capability can enable a firm to (a) add, replace, and terminate partnerships, thereby changing the composition and structure of its interfirm network (Gosain et al. 2004, Rai and Tang 2010) and (b) modify the rules of control and collaboration for different types of interfirm relationships, thereby changing the governance of the interfirm network (Rai et al. 2008, Tanriverdi et al. 2010). In contrast to dyadic IT customization, which is the firm’s IT capability to accommodate the idiosyncratic requirements for information exchange and coordination in a dyadic relationship, network IT standardization is the firm’s IT capability to access, mobilize, and leverage resources through different types of partners and relationships in its interfirm network. Network IT standardization reduces the focal firm’s need to dedicate—and manage—unique IT resources for routine coordination with partners, enabling it to commit such unique IT resources to high value-adding information exchange rather than common coordination (“statusing”) information (Malhotra et al. 2005).

As firms are challenged to dynamically reposition in product markets and seize temporary advantages (D’Aveni and Gunther 1994), it is infeasible for firms to access the resources and capabilities for such agile repositioning through a single high-profile partner, a set of uncoordinated dyadic relationships, or even a static network of efficiently managed interfirm relationships (Lavie 2006, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Steinhilber 2009). Instead, such repositioning in product markets requires a firm to adjust its interfirm network—both in structure and governance—so as to access and integrate the resources and capabilities to compete in new product market positions. These requirements imply that a focal firm expands its focus from managing dyadic interfirm relationships and establishing corresponding relationship-specific

B2B IT capabilities to coordinating its interfirm network and establishing corresponding network-wide B2B IT capabilities.

Network IT standardization allows the focal firm to establish digital connections to a group of partners with relative ease and at a low cost. This B2B IT capability is especially applicable to manage those relationships that involve common processes and interoperable products/services across a diverse set of partners. By implementing standardized IT interfaces for these common processes and interoperable products/services, the focal firm is able to broadcast information and achieve scale economies (Tang et al. 2011). Moreover, standardized information allows quick comparison of performance across different partners, helping the focal firm evaluate its partners and learn about effective contract design for a variety of resource attributes (Kim and Mahoney 2005). Using standardized IT interfaces to execute common processes and achieve the interoperability of products/services with a group of partners reduces the focal firm’s dependence on a specific partner, thus making it easy to add, replace, or terminate a relationship whenever necessary (Bae and Gargiulo 2004).

## 3. Leveraging B2B IT Capabilities for (Re)configuring Business Models

Although past research has largely not differentiated between value creation and value appropriation, they can be—and often are—distinct mechanisms that need to be considered in (re)configuring BMs (Lepak et al. 2007). For example, innovation is a key source of value creation, but many firms have failed to capture value from cutting-edge innovations (e.g., Xerox was unable to capture value from its invention of Xerox Alto—the first computer for individual use—while Apple succeeded with the Macintosh that included many of Xerox Alto’s features) (Smith and Alexander 1999). In contrast, although most component technologies for Apple’s iPhone and iPad were in place, Apple bundled these technologies to not only create value by introducing an integrated product to meet customers’ needs but also captured a large portion of the value created by these products. Specifically, Apple managed to keep about 30% of the sales price of its low-end \$499, 16 GB, WiFi-only model of the iPad and about 58% of the sales price of the iPhone 4 (Kraemer et al. 2011). We discuss how dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization provide distinct—and potentially complementary— mechanisms, operating at different levels of analysis, to create and appropriate value.

## 3.1. Value Creation

A BM’s value creation mechanisms are the means to generate value for not only the focal firm but also for all other stakeholders (e.g., customers, suppliers, and other partners). It sets the upper limit for the value the focal firm can capture (Brandenburger and Stuart 1996). There are three main value creation mechanisms: novelty, efficiency, and complementarity (Amit and Zott 2001).

Novelty represents the value created through new ways of conducting economic exchange, which can be achieved by connecting previously unconnected parties, by linking transaction participants in new ways, or by designing new transaction mechanisms (Zott and Amit 2007). Network IT standardization provides a cost-effective way for the focal firm to establish digital linkages with previously unconnected parties and their processes and products/services. By incorporating new partners and their product offerings into its BM, the focal firm can gain access to valuable knowledge and resources that it cannot replicate in a timely manner or obtain elsewhere from existing partners (Shapiro and Varian 1999). It also allows the focal firm to establish connections with a wide range of stakeholders to solicit opinions and ideas, all of which are valuable inputs for the focal firm to generate new ideas for innovations in products and services, production methods, distributing, or marketing (Schumpeter 1934, Tambe et al. 2012). Comparatively, dyadic IT customization allows the focal firm to tailor its digital linkages with individual partners, thus supporting personalized, differentiated connections with transaction participants. As such, dyadic IT customization enables “deep integration” with partners (Kim and Mahoney 2006, Klein and Rai 2009), which helps the focal firm gain rich knowledge about partners’ resources and capabilities and establish innovative mechanisms to collaborate with them.

Efficiency represents the value created through streamlining transactions and coordination activities (Zott and Amit 2007). Network IT standardization provides a standardized interface for the firm to conveniently broadcast up-to-date information to different stakeholders, thereby reducing information asymmetry between buyers and sellers, reducing buyers’ search and bargaining costs, and enhancing both parties’ informed decision making. It also provides the technological basis to establish common interfirm processes, thereby increasing the administrative efficiencies associated with interfirm governance (Ross and Beath 2006). As an example, e-gatematrix—an inflight service provider—broadcasts up-to-date information (e.g., flight schedules, passenger counts, and service schedules) to its network of caterers, suppliers, and distributors and executes a standardized suite of interfirm coordination processes through its digital platform—InflightExchange (Tang et al. 2011). The reduced information asymmetry and common set of processes between the airline and its network of partners generates significant transaction and coordination efficiencies across the in-flight service network. Similarly, Wal-Mart institutionalized a policy requiring its trading partners to comply with the product data exchange standards of its global data network synchronization initiative with the objective of improving data quality, speed-to-shelf time for new products, and purchase accuracy (Kim 2006). In contrast, dyadic IT customization enables the focal firm to implement co-specialized processes with a partner to execute transactions and coordinate interdependencies in the interfirm relationship. It enables the firm to achieve visibility into resource stock and flow as well as to events and exceptions that support the execution of co-specialized processes. Given that co-specialized processes are a form of relation-specific investment that can increase switching costs and mitigate opportunism, dyadic IT customization can also be leveraged to decrease relational risks (Clemons and Kleindorfer 1992, Milgrom and Roberts 1992). For example, Wal-Mart leverages its B2B IT capabilities to customize the sharing of different levels of retail inventory information with trading partners. It shares (and receives) detailed strategic and operational information with key partners like Procter and Gamble (P&G) that enable it to execute co-specialized processes that are not constrained by the one-size-fits-all limitations of common interfirm processes (Grean and Shaw 2002). By leveraging IT capabilities to customize content, governance, and transactions and evolve their cospecialized processes, Wal-Mart’s and P&G’s mutual business has grown tenfold over the last two decades with increased profitability for both.

Complementarity is the value that a firm creates by integrating its assets with its partners’ assets to generate (indirect) network effects. By supporting B2B IT standards to connect digitally with vertical and horizontal firms, network IT standardization expands the focal firm’s search for and access to partners’ complementary assets. By expanding the search scope for and reducing the integration costs with partners’ assets, network IT standardization positions a firm to explore and exploit interfirm complementarities (Tiwana 2008). For example, small independent software vendors (ISVs) that have obtained certification from SAP’s integration and certification centers stating that their products are compatible with SAP’s platform signal to the large installed base of SAP users that the ISVs’ products cocreate value with the SAP platform (Ceccagnoli et al. 2012). These ISVs have realized increased sales relative to ISVs that have not invested in developing network IT standardization to achieve complementarities with SAP’s products across its user base. In contrast, dyadic IT customization enhances the focal firm’s ability to synergistically combine resources and capabilities with individual partners. UPS uses its IT capabilities to configure logistics processes for its customers (e.g., HP) based on their supply chain strategies and requirements as well as the characteristics of their other processes (e.g., manufacturing and marketing) and IT capabilities (Lewis et al. 2007). UPS’s ability to provide differing levels of visibility and configure logistics interfirm processes that accommodate differences across customers, industries, and supply chain structures has enabled it to effectively combine its resources and capabilities with those of its customers, leading firms across key industries to source complex supply chain services from UPS (Rai et al. 2012).

In sum, these three value creation mechanisms are neither orthogonal nor mutually exclusive. A focal firm may combine any two or even all three— enabled by dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization—in a given BM (Zott and Amit 2007). However, these value creation mechanisms specify how value can be created for all stakeholders (including the focal firm, suppliers, customers, etc.) participating in a BM.

## 3.2. Value Appropriation

Drawing on Teece (2010), we identify three value appropriation mechanisms that contrast BMs: bundling, lock-in, and barrier to imitation. We suggest that the two B2B IT capabilities can be applied together to enable each of the three mechanisms.

Bundling is a strategy of selling a collection of products and services for a fixed price (Sirmon et al. 2008). IT capabilities have not only enabled firms to disaggregate products and services and lower the distribution and reproduction costs for information goods (Mithas and Whitaker 2007, Chuang and Sirbu 2000), but have also enabled firms to bundle products and services that align better with the valuations of market segments. The rationale for bundling is that customers’ valuation for a collection of products and services tends to exhibit lower variance per good compared to the valuations for the individual products and services (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999). As uncertainty arising from customers’ valuations of products and services is the enemy of effective pricing and efficient transactions (Myerson and Satterthwaite 1983), the predictive value of effective bundling can enable a firm to appropriate a greater share of the value. However, when market segments differ systematically in their valuations for products and services, simple bundling—or a one-size-fits-all method—is not optimal; rather, the firm needs to offer a menu of different bundles targeted at each market segment (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999, Chrisman et al. 2003).

We suggest that B2B IT capabilities can enable a focal firm to capture value through effective resource bundling across interfirm relationships. First, dyadic IT customization can enable the effective segmentation of customers—a key mechanism through which resource bundles’ value can be protected (Foss and Foss 2005). Dyadic IT customization enables a firm to share and pool partners’ proprietary market intelligence and customer experiences, thereby creating knowledge that can be applied to segment customers more accurately, reduce the risks of adverse sorting, and determine when a product should be sold separately or in a bundle. For example, Amazon and UPS have created bundles that differ in shipping options, thereby enabling these partnering firms to differentiate between customers who are willing to pay for the expedited shipment of Amazon goods through UPS versus those who are unwilling to do so. As another example, content providers like Home Box Office (HBO) and Starz, cable TV companies, online video service providers, such as Hulu, and online marketplaces, including Amazon.com and Apple iTunes, pool their proprietary market intelligence and customer preference resources with individual partners to deliver TV shows through multiple channels so that they can cater to different customers’ watching habits and preferences (Kunstner et al. 2010). Second, when competitors and customers cannot individually price each attribute of a bundle, a firm erects information barriers to ex ante competition (Foss and Foss 2005). Dyadic B2B IT customization can enable idiosyncratic integration among partnering firms’ products and services, thereby increasing the causal ambiguity among bundled attributes as well as the measurement costs related to the valuation of individual attributes. Third, network IT standardization can be used to reduce search and integration costs for resources, thereby increasing the variety of bundles that can be developed and reducing the costs for producing and distributing bundles. Fourth, network IT standardization can be used to develop sophisticated bundles for which the intellectual property rights to specific resource attributes are protected through encapsulation while use rights to these attributes are granted. Good examples include BMs for software-as-a-service for which the price of a bundle of software modules grants customers use rights to those modules while the intellectual property is protected through encapsulation and standardized interfaces that manage interdependencies with other software modules. As such, network IT standardization can increase the value that a BM appropriates by increasing opportunistic agents’ capture costs while decreasing resource owners’ protection costs.

Combining the capabilities of dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization, the focal firm is able to search both deep into individual relationships and across its network of partners for new ideas on how products/services can be bundled and/or tailored for customer segments in unique ways. These capabilities also enable the firm to achieve the necessary integration of complementary assets as we described earlier, thereby erecting additional barriers to imitation from competition. Positioned with a superior ability to explore, develop, and tailor bundles, the focal firm is able to capture greater value.

Lock-in prevents customers and strategic partners from migrating to competitors (Zott and Amit 2007). With this value appropriation mechanism, the focal firm has a higher potential to capture value from its products/services if customers are motivated to engage in repeat transactions and if its strategic partners have incentives to maintain and improve their associations with the focal firm (which may result in both lower opportunity and coordination costs and higher efficiency benefits for the focal firm). Dyadic IT customization allows the focal firm to customize its products/services and processes to serve each customer’s unique needs, thereby generating more value for customers and motivating them to participate in repeat transactions. It also raises switching costs due to relationship-specific investments in these IT systems and associated co-specialized assets that support the exchange processes of the relationship. In contrast, by promoting network effects with partners, network IT standardization raises switching costs as the size of the focal firm’s interfirm network increases because of the network effects that can be realized by suppliers/customers/partners. For example, SAP has leveraged network IT standardization to integrate partners’ offerings while increasing partners’ switching costs by virtue of both the fixed costs incurred by partners to comply with SAP’s standards as well as the indirect network effects arising from the ecosystem that benefit SAP’s partners. In sum, dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization can be leveraged to lock in customers, suppliers, and partners and increase value appropriation.

Barrier to imitation allows the focal firm to enjoy longer periods of time in value appropriation by deterring or delaying competitors from copying its product or service offerings. Firms can enhance their barrier to imitation by seeking strong intellectual property protection (e.g., patent, copyright, business secrets, etc.), establishing and enforcing different types of rights (e.g., use, modify, etc.) for wellspecified resource attributes, or increasing the causal ambiguity of their offerings. Although network IT standardization can be a cost-effective solution to reach a large group of partners, it also bears a higher imitation risk as competitors can simply comply with the same sets of open standard to reach the same group of partners. By adopting a hybrid approach in deploying IT capabilities (i.e., adopting both proprietary and open IT standards and deploying both customized and standardized IT components and digitized processes), the focal firm can combine easyto-access information accessed with standardized IT and process capabilities with hard-to-reach information accessed with customized IT and process capabilities, thus increasing the causal ambiguity of its BM. Such a hybrid approach can mitigate the risk of imitation of the BM and involuntary knowledge spillover from partners, thereby extending the period that a firm can appropriate value from value co-creation activities with partners.

We suggest that dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization are not just distinct—and nonsubstitutive—but are likely to be mutually reinforcing, or complementary, in creating and appropriating value. By being able to customize IT to achieve the desired cospecialization in dyadic relationships and standardizing IT to efficiently execute a common set of processes across a network of interfirm relationships, the benefits of dyadic IT customization are increased with network IT customization, and vice versa.

Recent discourse in the strategic management and information systems literatures suggests that our understanding of the relationship between a firm’s capabilities and its governance choices—including organizing activities internally, sourcing to the market, or establishing various interfirm collaborations—can be elaborated using a coevolution perspective. This perspective focuses on the developmental progression of a firm’s capabilities and its governance choices and recognizes that governance choices are affected by capabilities while driving decisions to invest in developing capabilities. The strategic management and IS literatures also suggest the need to understand how capabilities and a firm’s interfirm networks coevolve. Accordingly, to develop a more nuanced understanding of how B2B IT capabilities at the dyadic relationship and interfirm network levels should be conceived and developed to support value creation and appropriation from BMs, we suggest future research examine the coevolution of a firm’s B2B IT capabilities with its governance choices and its interfirm networks. We identify these particular directions as they are likely to influence and be influenced by B2B IT capabilities and will lead to better integration of the B2B IT capabilities concept (as well as specific B2B IT capabilities) with major theoretical perspectives in strategic management.

## 4.1. Coevolution of B2B IT Capabilities and Interfirm Governance Choices

We developed the logic that a firm’s investments in B2B IT capabilities are influenced by its strategic intent for interfirm collaboration—that is, B2B IT investments are driven by design requirements to establish interfirm collaborations for effective positioning and competition. Given that IT capabilities develop by combining IT resources with other complementary organizational resources and that their development involves path dependencies and learning, the strategic intent to pursue interfirm collaborations can be expected to be influenced by a firm’s preexisting capabilities. As Williamson (1999, p. 1103) notes, “Rather, therefore, than ask the question ‘What is the best generic mode (market, hybrid, firm, or bureau) to organize X?,’ which is the traditional transaction cost query, the question to be put instead is ‘How should firm A—which has preexisting strengths and weaknesses (core competences and disabilities)— organize X?’ ” (italics in original) Some recent work has adopted this additive perspective to combine the capabilities explanation of resources to position and compete in product markets with the organizational economics explanation of sourcing and organizing resources. For example, Lo et al. (2012) find that the strength of firms’ preexisting capabilities affects governance $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ contract’s pricing formats). Future research can adopt this additive perspective to examine how a focal firm’s preexisting B2B IT capabilities at the relationship and network levels affect its formulation and enactment of strategic intent for interfirm collaborations.

By adopting a complementarities perspective to investigate the theoretical overlap between capabilities and organizational economics, some scholars have examined how distinct transaction costs (e.g., communications to source novel services) in dynamic contexts—above and beyond traditional transactional costs in static contexts—can change the relationship between capabilities and a focal firm’s vertical scope decisions. We suggest that by adopting a complementarities perspective to integrate capabilities and organizational economics explanations, future research examine how the effects of unique transaction costs emanating from distinctive industry characteristics and types of interfirm collaborations change how B2B IT capabilities can be leveraged to execute interfirm exchange and thus create and appropriate value. Are there industries and interfirm collaboration contexts in which the two B2B IT capabilities serve as substitutes and others in which they operate as complements for different sources of value creation $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ novelty, efficiency, and complementarity) and value appropriation $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , bundling, lock-in and barrier to imitation)?

We also identify the need for future research to develop a substantive theory to explain the heterogeneity across firms’ B2B IT capabilities at the relationship and network levels. Argyres et al. (2012, p. 1216) caution against relegating the explanation of capability heterogeneity to history and learning over time by stating, “To simply say that heterogeneous capabilities ‘preexist’ the governance choice is not a complete explanation and only leads to an infinite regress.” They also note that past work has focused on macro variables (e.g., routines) but has not integrated the role of agency and rationality, which are central in understanding how managers make comparative governance choices—for example, organizing internally, sourcing to the market, or establishing various forms of interfirm collaborations. It will be fruitful for future research to evaluate how managers make decisions about developing B2B IT capabilities for dyadic customization and network standardization given the processes they use to make comparative governance choices in different industry and transaction contexts.

In creating and appropriating value from BMs, managerial foresight and judgment is not only required for partnering with firms but also for governing the complementary asset and activity bundles accessed through interfirm relationships. Given the influential role of co-specialized investments in capability formation (Williamson 1999), future research can identify the co-specialized investments to accompany the formation of the two B2B IT capabilities—namely, dyadic IT customization at the relationship level and network IT standardization at the business network level. A second route to understanding the emergence of IT-enabled BMs is through the influence of dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization, respectively, in defining and enforcing property rights, thereby affecting transaction costs and the value that is created and appropriated. From a property rights perspective, resources can be conceived as being composed of multiple attributes for which rights to use, consume, obtain income from, and alienate can be defined and enforced (Foss and Foss 2005). Although managers’ entrepreneurial experience antecedes the discovery of opportunities, this experience requires managers to learn about resource attributes—a process laden with transaction costs (Kim and Mahoney 2005). Future research can investigate how managerial learning about property rights to resource attributes is influenced by B2B IT capabilities and how this managerial learning influences the coevolution of the strategic intent for interfirm collaboration and B2B IT capabilities. We suggest future research examine the following questions: How can dyadic IT customization and network IT standardization be combined with other resources and capabilities to contribute to managerial learning about defining and enforcing property rights to resource attribute? How does managerial learning about the costs and risks in defining and enforcing property rights affect the strategic intent for interfirm collaboration, resulting investments in B2B IT capabilities, and the leverage of B2B IT capabilities to capture and appropriate value?

## 4.2. Coevolution of B2B IT Capabilities and Interfirm Networks

We suggest that future research examine how a focal firm’s B2B IT capabilities and its interfirm networks coevolve to affect value creation and appropriation from interfirm relationships. The network perspective of strategy focuses on how interfirm network characteristics affect a firm’s abilities to launch competitive (re)actions and accrue competitive advantages (Gnyawali et al. 2006). The premise advanced by this perspective is that (1) a firm’s ego network characteristics (e.g., centrality) affect its access to information, power, and reputation resources and (2) its ability to leverage these network resources for competitive (re)actions is contingent on the structure of the overall network or its architecture (e.g., density of ties).

Recent work integrating IT capabilities with the network perspective on strategy has shown that a firm’s IT capabilities substitute for brokerage in networks with spare ties and complement closure in networks with dense ties (Chi et al. 2010).

However, there is limited understanding of how a firm’s B2B IT capabilities influence the emergence, evolution, and change of its ego network and the overall network structure and how the structural characteristics of a firm’s ego and overall networks influence the development of B2B IT capabilities and their leverage for value creation and capture over time. How do specific B2B IT capabilities, in combination with other resources and capabilities, enable a firm to undertake actions to create or modify interfirm networks that benefit the firm and potentially influence its overall network structure in the future? As the number of participating firms in an interfirm network grows, the number of ties between firms required to achieve network closure grows exponentially. Because the costs to establish ties for network closure can become prohibitive for individual firms as the number of ties grows exponentially, the benefits of network governance can be expected to decline with a growth in the number of firms and a decline in network closure (Ahuja 2000). How should firms invest in developing B2B IT capabilities to customize exchanges for dyadic relationships and to standardize exchanges across the network given an expanding interfirm network (and the need to compensate for declining benefits of network governance with decreases in network closure)? Also, how can B2B IT capabilities, along with other resources and capabilities, enable a firm to change an interfirm network’s distribution of ties and thus its relative power? Answering these questions will expand our understanding of how a firm’s B2B IT capabilities can enable the firm to create/appropriate value from BMs in the context of the firm’s ego and the broader interfirm networks that change over time.

## Conclusion

We introduce a conceptual framework to expand the concept of IT-enabled BMs, in which two B2B IT capabilities—dyadic IT customization at the relationship level and network IT standardization at the interfirm network level—are proposed to provide the technological capabilities to meet the design requirements for BMs. Drawing on the emerging literature of BMs and the related literatures in strategic management and information systems, we establish logic for how the development of these two B2B IT capabilities is affected by a firm’s strategic intent for interfirm collaboration and the resulting design requirements for exchange content, interfirm governance, and transaction structure. We further investigate how these

B2B IT capabilities are complementary in creating and appropriating value from different sources. Based on our discussion of the conceptual framework, we identify the coevolution of B2B IT capabilities and interfirm governance choices and the coevolution of B2B IT capabilities and network position as two especially fruitful areas for future research on IT-enabled BMs. We hope these ideas will promote further research on IT-enabled BMs.
