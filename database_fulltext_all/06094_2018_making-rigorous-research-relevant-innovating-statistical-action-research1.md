# MAKING RIGOROUS RESEARCH RELEVANT: INNOVATING STATISTICAL ACTION RESEARCH<sup>1</sup>

Alexandra Durcikova University of Oklahoma, 307 West Brooks, AH 305D, Norman, OK 73019-4006 U.S.A. {alex@ou.edu}

Allen S. Lee Virginia Commonwealth University, 301 West Main Street, Richmond, VA 23284-4000 U.S.A. {allenslee@alum.mit.edu}

Susan A. Brown University of Arizona, 1130 E. Helen Street, McClelland Hall 430, Tucson, AZ 85721-0108 U.S.A. {suebrown@eller.arizona.edu}

This paper proposes a new type of action research—statistical action research (AR)—along with our guidelines on how to conduct it and how to evaluate it. Statistical AR provides a new toolkit for our discipline that strengthens the scholarly community by contributing to the recent discussion regarding the collaborative nature of qualitative and quantitative techniques. The major methodological contribution of statistical AR is the introduction and demonstration of the use of statistical hypothesis testing in action research, where this contribution is the first instance of not only statistical AR, but also positivist action research, in the information systems discipline. Our approach to AR addresses, from a positivist perspective, perceived weaknesses of AR. Statistical AR fits comfortably within the framework of canonical AR, with the only difference being that statistical AR takes a positivist perspective rather than an interpretive one. As conducted in this study, statistical AR applies, tests, and advances knowledge validation theory in a knowledge management system (KMS) context. The major practical contribution is illustrating to practitioners how to integrate different methods in action research. A secondary practical contribution consists of turning around an instance of an ineffective KMS, as experienced by an organization, into one that is effective.

Keywords: Action research, statistical action research, research methods, management intervention, positivism, hypothesis testing

## Introduction

How may we conduct action research (AR) so that it benefits from the richness typically associated with qualitative and interpretive research, but additionally embodies the type of rigor typically associated with positivist research? This question drives the inquiry in this paper.

AR was developed independently by Kurt Lewin and the Tavistock Institute.

In both cases, the idea was to apply existing theory to solve practical problems, learn from the experience, and then add to the body of knowledge by modifying the theory or suggesting new theory (Myers 2013, p. 61).

Susman and Evered (1978) built on one of Kurt Lewin’s legacies—his AR “spiral”—by articulating that AR has five stages: diagnosing, action planning, action taking, evaluating, and specifying learning, where the spiral refers to repeating the five stages as needed. AR, however, is deeper than just what the AR spiral would imply; AR has also been described as embodying “a series of commitments to observe and problematize through practice a series of principles for conducting social enquiry” (McTaggart 1996, p. 248).

At the same time, AR has received criticism from positivist researchers who perceive it as not being sufficiently rigorous (Kock et al. 1997). Action researchers have tried to overcome this criticism through the utilization of “classical” action research.<sup>2</sup> Also, action researchers have criticized positivist researchers, whose preferred way of conducting rigorous research involves sample surveys and controlled experiments in an effort to discover causal laws; action researchers argue that surveys and controlled experiments do not allow the researcher to discover nondeterministic or reciprocal relationships (Jönsonn 1991).

How can one overcome such criticisms and, at the same time, benefit from both the qualitative richness traditionally offered by AR and the type of rigor associated with positivist research? Kock et al. (1997) suggested a way to address this through the cyclical implementation of action research as proposed by Susman and Evered. Specifically, Kock et al. argued that the cyclical implementation of action research can overcome the three main criticisms of action research by positivist researchers: contingency of the research findings, low control of the environment, and personal over-involvement.

However, to address such criticisms, rather than only relying on implementing the AR spiral cyclically, we introduce a new form of AR—namely, statistical AR. Using statistical hypothesis testing and sample surveys, it allows a researcher to directly utilize the rigor of positivist research in the action planning and evaluating stages of AR. As a form of positivist research, statistical AR is an option by which to add another dimension to the rigor of AR and affords opportunities for harvesting new insights from the field through hypothesis testing. The accompanying rigor can potentially reduce the number of AR cycles and allow for adding or otherwise changing variables in an already existing theory. Our innovation of statistical AR also allows us not only to avail ourselves of previously developed theory, but also to test it through hypothesis testing with the help of statistical controls. Statistical AR, along with our guidelines on how to conduct it and how to evaluate it, therefore provides a new toolkit for our discipline that contributes to the recent discussion regarding the collaborative nature of qualitative and quantitative techniques (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016).

To illustrate the utility of statistical AR, we conducted a study in the context of knowledge management systems (KMS). In this paper, we demonstrate how to perform statistical AR in a KMS context where we answer practice- and researchrelated questions such as the following: How can a KMS, one that happens to be already implemented in an organization but is ineffective in terms of the number of knowledge submissions, be made effective? In the effort of making the KMS effective, how can we use theory so that it can be deemed relevant? And, in the same effort, how can we test theory so that it can be deemed rigorous? We answer these questions with the help of statistical hypothesis testing performed both before and after the intervention. The statistical hypothesis testing is crucial in our study because, without it, we would not have been able to (1) diagnose the problem (nonsubmissions to the KMS), (2) identify the factors requiring intervention (independent variables in the statistical model) or (3) assess the outcome (impacts on the dependent variables). We derive the hypotheses from knowledge validation theory, which Durcikova and Gray (2009) developed and successfully tested, but did so using only cross-sectional data in a single case setting. In applying their theory to our problem, however, we find that its theorized relationships initially do not hold. Statistical AR allows us to advance this theory by adding a new variable, persuasion, whose moderating effects serve to restore the theorized relationships.

Staying true to a major aim of AR—the aim to foster positive organizational change—we were able to make a major practical contribution in our client organization by significantly improving the number of knowledge submissions to their KMS. A KMS had already been installed, but was ineffective because organizational participants were not making knowledge submissions to it. The inflow of new knowledge significantly influences the legitimacy of a KMS (Lin et al. 2012). Our statistical AR revealed complications pertaining to the information technology’s behavioral impacts. Remedial actions, which ultimately rendered the KMS effective, followed from the use of three behavioral theories (the selections of which explain): attribution theory, persuasion theory, and knowledge validation theory.

This study proceeds as follows. The second (next) section provides a detailed description of statistical AR as a form of positivist AR. The third section provides a detailed description of the organizational setting and how the KMS, as initially implemented, was ineffective in terms of knowledge submission. The fourth section—the heart of this study— provides a walk-through of the five stages of statistical AR in the way we conducted it. In the walk-through, we provide our rationale for and describe our use of attribution theory in the diagnosing stage, persuasion theory in the action planning and action taking stages, and knowledge validation theory across all of the stages. Our discussion covers this study’s contribution to AR, knowledge validation theory, and future research.

## Statistical Action Research as a Form of Positivist Action Research

## A General Description of Action Research

AR has a long history and has developed many schools of thought (Elden and Chisholm 1993), both inside and outside of the information systems (IS) discipline. There is no a priori reason, however, that existing forms of AR are the only legitimate forms of AR. In any case, none of these forms of AR proclaims itself as the sole, correct, mandatory way of doing AR. Rather than select one of these many forms, we choose instead to return to, and adopt, some of the basic ideas that originally motivated the emergence of AR.

AR transcends the mere concatenation of action and research. In action research, the action and the research work hand-inhand. Neither the action nor the research may be properly planned, conducted, or evaluated separately from the other. The quality of the research in AR becomes known not only through its execution of scientific method, but also through its contribution to the success or failure of the action. The success or failure of the action in AR becomes known not only through its impact on the problem experienced by the people in the field, but also through its contribution to the quality of the research. Furthermore, the quality required of the research may depend on the needs of the action, and the quality required of the action may depend on the needs of the research. In other words, what might be considered good quality research in a controlled laboratory experiment could be considered poor quality in action research if it does not contribute to a successful action, and likewise, what might be considered a successful action taken outside of a research context could be considered unsuccessful in action research if it does not further the quality of the research. Thus, AR constitutes a whole that is greater than the sum of its parts. Action research is what emerges from the interaction between the action and the research, not from the action and the research being treated as if they were discrete, independent modules.

Because AR emerges, it is unlike conventional research in that research requirements can and do become known after the research has commenced. An AR intervention can lead to the identification of a new variable for which no instrument has yet been developed to measure. Also, the refutation of a theory in the course of AR could lead to the search for a different or potentially new theory. AR, being conducted in the real world, emerges and evolves in ways that carefully planned laboratory experiments do not.

Adhering to the basic ideas just mentioned, which originally motivated the emergence of AR, can even be considered a better approach to AR than selecting one of the existing approaches. Along these lines, Peter Checkland (Checkland 1999; Checkland and Poulter 2007; Checkland and Scholes 1999) described his work as “soft systems methodology,” but also explicitly considered it to be AR. Checkland performed excellent AR not by following any AR formula, but instead by attending to the basic ideas that originally motivated the emergence of AR. In our AR, we endeavor to do the same.

There is also one very practical reason that we are choosing to attend to the basic ideas of AR rather than selecting one of its ready-to-use, off-the-shelf forms. It is that we are bringing an innovation—statistical hypothesis testing in AR—to IS research. Currently, in the IS literature, there are no examples of published studies that call themselves AR and that conduct statistical hypothesis testing (that specify, for instance, sample sizes and statistical significance); indeed, as detailed in Appendix A, a search of the IS literature for statistical AR identifies no published studies, and a search of the IS literature for the related topic of positivist AR, including qualitative positivist AR, also identifies no published studies. Returning to the basic ideas of AR is therefore helpful in allowing us to introduce not only statistical, but even positivist methods in general, that are not found in existing forms of AR in the information systems discipline.

By way of background, it is useful to review briefly what Myers (2013) and Klein and Myers (1999) have described as the three main types of AR, which can be positivist, interpretive, and critical, as well as to recount what Davison et al. (2012) have noted about the role of theory in AR. Myers derives these three main types of AR from Chua’s (1986) work. These three are, however, not the only types, as recent work has highlighted critical realist (Mingers et al. 2013) and pragmatist (Martela 2015) philosophies that are promising and can augment the types of AR. However, we restrict ourselves to discussing just positivist, interpretive, and critical AR, as covered by Myers.

## First, positivist AR

sees action research as a social experiment and attempts to meet the requirements of positivist social science. Work of this kind is often justified in positivistic terms—AR is seen as a method for testing and refining hypotheses in the real world (Myers 2013, p. 64).

In general,

positivist studies are premised on the existence of a priori fixed relationships within phenomena which are typically investigated with structured instrumentation [where] such studies serve primarily to test theory, in an attempt to increase predictive understanding of phenomena (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991, p. 5).

Positivist research can be quantitative (e.g., by involving statistical hypothesis testing, as in numerous behavioral IS studies) as well as qualitative (e.g., as in the form of positivist case studies as described by Lee (1989), and by Dubé and Paré (2003)). As such, statistical AR would be considered a subset of positivist AR. We also emphasize that Myers was discussing the possibility of positivist AR in general, not just positivist AR in the IS discipline.

Second, there is interpretive AR, which “tends to rely on an underlying interpretive and constructivist epistemology, i.e. social reality is socially constructed” (Myers 2013, p. 64). In general, “the observing social scientist [doing interpretive research] must, among other things, interpret this empirical reality in terms of what it means to the observed people” (Lee 1991, p. 347). Regarding social construction, Lee (1994, p. 151) adds “Berger and Luckmann (1966), in their theory of the social construction of reality, state: ‘Society is a human product. Society is an objective reality. Man is a social product,’” thereby underscoring that interpretivism recognizes not only subjective meaning, but also objective reality.

Myers approvingly quotes Elden and Chisolm (1993) as describing positivist AR as “classical AR” and interpretive AR as “contemporary AR.” This is consistent with the viewpoint that, in its original or “classical” form, AR attempted to implement traditional social science (e.g., involving controlled field experiments), whereas today AR is more often conducted by qualitative researchers who take an interpretive approach.

Third, there is critical AR, which Myers describes as practiced largely within the field of educational research. It is AR that is combined “with the critical social theory of Habermas … [where it] should be participatory and aim for emancipation” (p. 64). Critical AR can have interpretive aspects, but critical action researchers “are distinctive in adopting a more activist view of their role” (ibid., p. 64). Critical AR strives not only to solve immediate problems, but also

must aim towards social justice, involve critical reflection on current practices, question the takenfor-granted assumptions which underlie those practices, and aim towards collective action (ibid., p. 64).

Finally, Davison et al. (2012) are explicit in their endorsement of the use of multiple theories in a single AR study. Whereas action researchers and organizational participants have the option of jointly building a single new theory with which to inform their diagnosing, action planning, action taking, evaluating, and reflecting, Davison et al. provide examples, from both the literature and their own AR, of the use of what they call multiple “instrumental theories” within the same AR study. They describe instrumental theories as those that “include any tools, models, or processes that theorize how work is done or how outcomes are achieved” and they add, “in practice, one or more instrumental theories will facilitate the diagnosis of research problems, planning of interventions, and/or subsequent assessment of the organizational impact of the intervention” (Davison et al. 2012, p. 766). As we later describe in detail, the “instrumental theories” in our AR are attribution theory, persuasion theory, and knowledge validation theory.

A general description of action research also requires some clarification as to how an action research study is not simply a field experiment. Action research does take place in the field and does involve experimentation, but there are at least three major differences between action research and field experiments. First, in action research, the people at the research site (e.g., organizational members) are given the option of being equal research partners who may participate in building or selecting the theory on which the intervention is based. In a field experiment, organizational members are not regarded as co-researchers, and are simply research subjects who do not necessarily have knowledge of the theory being applied. In action research, the organizational members may participate as researchers and researchers may participate as organizational members (Hughes 2008).

Second, action research involves an intervention intended to benefit the people at the research site—specifically, to solve or remedy what they consider to be a problem. In a field experiment, the intervention undertaken refers to changing the value of an independent variable where any theorized, corresponding change in a dependent variable need not benefit the people in the field. Conversely, a successful field experiment can be one that successfully tests a theory in a scientific way, whereas action research must not only test a theory but also have a beneficial impact.

Third, field experiments (and scientific experimentation in general) strive to avoid Hawthorne effects, but in action research, the “systematic generation of Hawthorne-effects” is “precisely what [is] aimed at!” (Jönsonn 1991, p. 389). In other words, the action researcher takes a vested interest, rather than a neutral or impartial stance, in achieving certain outcomes; McKay and Marshall state (2001, p. 47):

One distinguishing feature of AR is, therefore, the active and deliberate self-involvement of the researcher in the context of his/her investigation. Unlike the methods of objectivist science where the researcher is argued to be an impartial spectator on the research context (Chalmers, 1982), the action researcher is viewed as a key participant in the research process, working collaboratively with other concerned and/or affected actors to bring about change in the problem context (Checkland, 1991; Hult and Lennung, 1980).

Our action research manifests all of the three differences between action research and field experiments. However, there is one respect in which there is a similarity between our action research and a field experiment. Braa and Vidgen (1999, p. 30) state, “Field experiments aim at controlling a small number of variables which may then be studied intensively.” Our multivariate statistical model allows for controlling variables statistically (in the manner of a quasiexperimental design (Campbell and Stanley 2015; Cook and Campbell 1976). The use of statistical controls, however, does not detract from the organizational members’ being research partners, from the intervention’s benefitting the organizational members, or from the researchers’ taking a vested interest.

## Statistical Action Research

Although there are, as yet, no published positivist AR studies, including statistical AR, in the IS discipline, there are some IS scholars who recognize that positivist AR exists as a category of AR. Klein and Myers (1999) and Myers (2013), as just mentioned, explicitly recognize it, as do DeLuca and Kock (2007), who list positivist AR first among seven forms of AR (p. 186).

Given the freedom (if not the necessity) to innovate the form to be taken by statistical AR, we take the liberty of adapting aspects of AR that have already been recognized in IS research—in particular, by introducing elements of positivist research to those fundamental aspects of AR that Davison et al. (2004) have distilled and organized from the literature into what they call “canonical action research.” Next, we describe each stage of statistical AR and how it both overlaps with and is different from canonical AR.

First, in the diagnosing stage, one or more researchers scientifically trained in an academic specialty (for instance, the researcher could be a faculty member of a university’s social science department, business school, social work school, etc.) and members of the client organization together identify a problem to be a focus of their joint efforts. According to Davison et al. (2004, p. 73),

The researcher starts the process with a thorough diagnosis of the current organizational situation. While the client may identify one or more problems, the researcher has a responsibility to conduct an independent diagnosis not only so as to confirm the nature of the problem(s), but also to determine its/ their causes.

In our adaptation of Davison et al.’s (2004) framework so that it entertains positivist theorizing, a simple but illustrative example of a theory would involve the relationship between two constructs: an independent variable and a dependent variable, where the former refers to the action to be taken and the latter refers to the desired goal or solution. A straightforward rubric for selecting a theory, therefore, is to identify one in the existing research literature whose independent and dependent variables align with those just identified jointly in the field by the researcher and the client organization. This is a crucial step that is not required in traditional positivist studies where the researcher is often removed from the client company’s environment and poses a theory without first having performed any qualitative field work at the client com pany. In statistical AR, a researcher goes into a company without initially having a theory in mind. The researcher has to allow the theory to “show itself” based on the qualitative work s/he does at the client company. This is different from canonical AR in that, traditionally, it has been interpretive (indeed, as already noted, what Myers refers to as “contemporary AR” is interpretive). However, the use of a positivist theory, while a break from tradition, can be considered entirely compatible with canonical AR; Davison et al. (2004, p. 74) “CAR [canonical AR] theory commonly takes the following form: in situation S … the outcomes X, Y and Z are expected from actions A, B and C,” where these outcomes and these actions can correspond to the dependent and inde pendent variables, respectively. In positivist AR where a statistical model is applied, data are collected in the diagnosing stage, not only to indicate the pre-intervention values of the dependent and independent variables (values that the intervention would strive to change), but also to confirm whether the statistical model is valid—that is, whether it “fits” the local situation in the first place, where the researcher would use standard statistical criteria (such as estimated coefficients that are statistically significant and an acceptable

R<sup>2</sup> value) to indicate a successfully fitted model. The measured pre-intervention value of the dependent value also later serves as the baseline against which the post-intervention value is compared.

Second, in the action planning stage,

a detailed understanding of not only the problem but also the surrounding environment [from the diagnosing stage] is a prerequisite to the determination of an appropriate intervention. The diagnosis will directly inform the planning of actions. Here, the intended sequence of actions needs to be specified (Davison et al. 2004, p. 73).

Naturally, the planning of actions follows from the theory identified in the diagnosing stage, where, in statistical AR, a desired future value of the dependent variable would call for planning a corresponding change in the future value of the independent variable. Any lack of fit of the statistical model, as indicated in the diagnosing stage, could call for not only a revision of the model, but also the planning of actions by which to test the revised model.

Third, the action taking stage involves the intervention. Davison et al. (2004) state, regarding canonical AR,

The intervention might require the assistance of catalytic change agents, for example: a project champion who helps to initiate proceedings; a process or content expert who helps to empower or reassure project members; or an external stakeholder who can shed light on how potential changes will affect other parties (p. 73).

In statistical AR, the intervention takes the form of efforts to change the value of the independent variable so as to bring about a desired value of the dependent variable. Again, this statistical AR stage fits the description of the canonical AR.

Fourth, in the evaluating stage, “after the planned actions are completed, the intervention needs to be evaluated with outcomes being compared to project objectives and expectations” (Davison et al. 2004, p. 73). In statistical AR, the same statistical model that was fitted with pre-intervention data in the diagnosing stage is again fitted, but with post-intervention data in the evaluating stage, where not only a successful fit, but also desired relationships between independent variables and the dependent variable are sought. Both statistical evidence (a comparison of the current value of the dependent variable to its baseline value) and qualitative evidence are used to show whether the intervention had the desired impact.

Finally, the specifying learning stage can involve reformulating or otherwise improving the theory which was earlier used as the basis for planning the action. Where the organizational problem responds to the planned action in ways not expected by the theory, the theory can be reformulated so as to be able to account for such unexpected responses. The specifying learning stage can also involve additional learning or data collection about the organization, its business processes, and problems. The problem can then be diagnosed anew in a second and new diagnosing stage, thereby initiating a new cycle in the AR process.

In summary, statistical AR fits comfortably within the framework of canonical AR, with the only difference being that statistical AR takes a positivist perspective rather than an interpretive one. Table 1 illustrates the canonical AR stages in statistical AR.

## The Setting: The DBL Software Company and its Knowledge Management System

We conducted a study to demonstrate how the principles of statistical AR can be helpful for both research and practice. Our study can be encapsulated as follows. One of the authors of this study was present in an organization, working to identify and solve problems so as to make its knowledge management system (KMS) effective in terms of knowledge submissions. We conducted statistical AR and utilized the rubric for selecting theories as described above (also see Appendix B). Application of this rubric resulted in employing three different theories; the first two were: attribution theory (e.g., Weiner 1986) in the diagnosing stage and persuasion theory (e.g., Hovland et al. 1953) in the action planning and action taking stages. Both theories already had the status of being previously established. The third theory, knowledge validation theory (e.g., Gray and Durcikova 2005-2006) was used in all five stages because the main problem that DBL Software Company faced was the low rate of knowledge submission to their KMS and the confusion around the knowledge validation process. Whereas knowledge validation theory, like attribution theory and persuasion theory, also had the status of being previously established, its validity had only been partially demonstrated. For the purposes of making a contribution to scientific research in the area of KMS, we subjected knowledge validation theory to further empirical testing in an effort to fully demonstrate its validity. This required us to take statistical measurements of its variables both before and after the intervention (i.e., in the action-planning and the evaluating stages, respectively) in order to test whether the values of certain variables actually changed in the theorized directions. The results included not only an improvement in the KMS that the organizational participants themselves considered to be a mark of success, but also an important advancement in the theory about the impact of the validation of knowledge submissions.

Next, we set the stage by describing each of the following: the organization, DBL Software Company, and its business; the help-desk/agent-customer business process; DBL Software Company’s knowledge management system (KMS); and the problem that DBL Software Company experienced in using its implemented KMS.

DBL Software Company is a leading vendor for several widely distributed off-the shelf accounting software packages for individual consumers and small businesses in the United States. The software packages offer functions such as billing, invoicing, accounts receivables, income tax, and payroll. Corporate headquarters and the offices for developing the software packages are located in one state. The software packages are made available for sale online. Two help-desk call centers are located in two other states.

At the time that the AR began (see Appendix C for description of all interviews conducted), DBL Software Company’s number of customers was rapidly increasing, leading DBL Software Company to double the size of its help desk operations to approximately 300 phone agents providing customer support for its software products. When a customer called in, the following process, which we call the help-desk/agentcustomer business process, took place. The agent responding to the customer listened to the customer’s own description of the problem. Next, the agent formulated a description of the problem, using technical vocabulary, whereupon the agent entered both the customer’s natural description and the agent’s own technical description into the ticketing system. After that, the agent would either immediately determine the answer or would need to find a solution by doing a web search, consulting his/her personal notes, or tapping into the knowledge repository of the KMS.

This part of the KMS was designed to work in the following way. Using a web interface, agents could input key terms into the natural-language search engine of the KMS to look up information, procedures, and even solutions with which to address customer queries. The KMS would then output a list of links to articles from the knowledge repository (housing solutions to customer problems) retrieved to match the key terms. If the agent succeeded in finding an answer, the agent would share it with the customer and also note the solution in the ticketing system. In addition, the KMS had authoring capabilities that allowed agents to write up articles describing any new helpful information, procedures, and even solutions, and to directly submit the articles to the knowledge repository so that these articles could subsequently be validated by knowledge champions and subject matter experts (SMEs) (see Table 2 for descriptions of different organizational roles at DBL Software Company). Any article that an agent submitted had to be validated before the knowledge repository would make it available to all other agents.

To increase the ease of use of the KMS and its knowledge repository, it was seamlessly integrated into the tickettracking system that tracked the work tasks assigned to agents. If, however, the agent was unable to resolve the problem, the agent would then have two options: consulting a SME or escalating the ticket to level 2, where the problem would be relegated to specialists and the agent would no longer be responsible for this customer query.

All agents participating in the help-desk/agent-customer business process were well qualified. Prior to being assigned to the help desk, agents were required to have knowledge about accounting, software, hardware, and networking. They were required to have five years of experience in over-the-phone customer service. Upon being assigned, they went through an intensive, paid training session that lasted six weeks. After the training session, the agents took calls where they were supervised by more senior agents or SMEs. Once the agent was comfortable, competent, and knowledgeable (typically two to three weeks), the agent was allowed to handle calls on his or her own.

As implemented, the KMS experienced no technological problems, but initially, it was ineffective in that it was not used in the way it was designed. In particular, its success depended on agents’ submitting their knowledge to it (Lin et al. 2012), but the knowledge submission process was, in practice, so plagued with problems that the agents ended up not making many submissions. Thus the knowledge repository, the key part of any KMS (Fadel and Durcikova 2014), was, in effect, not functioning. In other words, the interactions in the help-desk/agent-customer business process became so problematic as to threaten the KMS with failure. This posed the problem requiring intervention in our AR.

## Statistical Action Research and a Walk-Through of its Five Stages at DBL Software Company

We now present the different stages of statistical AR, including a detailed walk-through of how we conducted each stage of statistical AR at DBL Software Company (see Figure 1).

## Stage One of Statistical AR: Diagnosing

In this first stage of statistical AR, we diagnose the problematic situation in the help-desk/agent-customer business process at DBL Software Company. First, building on current KMS literature, we explain why focusing on knowledge submission is critical for success in any knowledge management effort. Second, making use of comments from interviews, we present the problematic situation from the perspectives of the people who were involved, directly or indirectly, in the helpdesk/agent-customer business process (see Appendix C for a list of the interview participants by role). Third, using the rubric described in the statistical AR section/diagnosing stage, we diagnose, from the perspective of already established theories (attribution theory and knowledge validation theory), what the problems were (see Appendix B for application of the rubric).

Organizations recognize the value of a KMS because it allows them to compete in today’s knowledge-driven economy. Many researchers agree that a KMS cannot deliver value to an organization unless it contains knowledge that is accurate and valuable to its employees (Olivera 2000; Zack 1999). Fadel and Durcikova (2014) argue that there are two required steps to getting high quality knowledge in a KMS: (1) knowledge must be contributed by the members of the community that utilize this knowledge; and (2) the contributed knowledge must be validated to ensure accuracy. Reasons why employees submit knowledge include attitude toward knowledge sharing, subjective norms of knowledge sharing (e.g., Bock et al. 2005), intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (e.g., Kankanhalli et al. 2005), individual motivations, structural capital, cognitive capital, and relational capital (Wasko and Faraj 2005), and knowledge reuse from a KMS to which they then contribute knowledge (e.g., Watson and Hewett 2006). However, even if highly motivated employees perceive the validation process to be too strict or non-transparent, they will stop submitting knowledge (Durcikova and Gray 2009); one reason is that they perceive that submitting knowledge is costly (Bock et al. 2006) and another reason is that the possibility of their submissions being rejected can lead to embarrassment and harm to their reputation (Husted and Michailova 2002). Because the agents at DBL Software Company were already submitting knowledge to their own KMS in a particular way (i.e., a wiki to which only a small number of employees had access), we knew that the agents were willing to make, and capable of making, knowledge submissions, so we focused on how the validation process influences their knowledge submission to the KMS.

As we move to the second and third steps of diagnosing, we consider the perspectives of the agents, the coaches, the subject matter experts, the knowledge champions, a cultural change agent, a project manager, and several managers. Table 2 in the previous section summarizes the different organizational roles at DBL Software Company.

Six months after the implementation of the KMS, senior management at DBL Software Company saw that its agents were not submitting articles to the knowledge repository of the KMS at the rates senior management had anticipated. It was clear that the agents recognized the value of knowledge repositories—as evidenced by the existence of pockets of repositories, outside of the KMS, that agents created throughout the organization. As mentioned, some agents had implemented wikis, but they restricted the wikis so that only a small number of employees could access them. Seeing this, DBL Software Company’s senior management recognized that their agents had the ability to write good quality KMS articles, but senior management wanted all DBL Software Company agents to use a centralized system in order to better facilitate knowledge exchange among all agents. One of the managers expressed the following frustration:

We invested millions of dollars and implemented a state of the art KMS. We designed a validation process for the submitted knowledge, yet agents prefer to use their own wikis that have restricted access. We tried to send emails, fliers, banners to encourage knowledge submission. Nothing is working. [Help Desk Manager 1, who was responsible for managing over 60 agents]

In an effort to improve the level of knowledge submissions to the KMS, senior management removed the best agents from the task of answering phone calls from customers and redirected them to dedicate their time to writing articles as knowledge submissions. These agents became “knowledge champions” and were responsible for writing new articles as well as reading the knowledge submissions written by other agents, editing them, and eventually posting them in the knowledge repository. However, the approach of designating people to focus on this aspect of the KMS and no longer answer customer inquiries over the phone had its drawbacks. First, it was considered to be costly because agents answering of customer inquiries over the phone was supposed to be their primary work. Second, because only two agents were assigned to the role of knowledge champions, the validation process took, on average, 30 days to complete per submission—a duration that, in KMS practice, is considered extremely long in this type of environment. The following comments highlight this issue:

I can write better than 90% of them ever would be able to. But, the question is do they understand what I write? The longer you stay away from the phones, the less you understand their [the agents’] world. [Knowledge Champion]

The coaches and agents saw the knowledge management system as a black hole. Nobody knew who was validating the knowledge. They didn’t know what was happening when they hit the submit button. [Help Desk Manager 1]

It was clear that, overall, the agents had little idea about how the knowledge was being validated or who was validating it. This help desk manager summarized some of the views:

It is interesting what our agents believe about what happens to articles they submit. One of them told me that he believed it was artificial intelligence that checked the validity because he received an email that had some random text in it. Another agent believed it went to the headquarters [in a different state] and the software developers check it. Very few of them know that they check it here. [Help Desk Manager 2]

Also, the agents clearly recognized that there were significant problems with the process.

There is a serious bottleneck once an article is written and submitted. I submitted about [a] hundred articles, and I believe four of them have been published. So, while I understand that a certain percentage of what I submitted was poorly written or incomplete, that is still a horrifically low rate of publication. [Agent 1]

I have submitted many articles and only one was ever taken. I always check with my most respected coworkers before submitting one. Needless to say, I have stopped sending them. When I see improvement on what is accepted, I will be very willing to submit articles. [Agent 2]

In this environment, coaches played a key role. In general, a coach was responsible for eight to ten agents, where agents had the authority to decide who their coach would be if there was an opening in a particular group. Each coach met with agents on a weekly basis in a group session. In these meetings, the coach communicated important information on the product that the group was supporting. In addition, the coach met with agents individually on a monthly basis. In these meetings, employee (agent) evaluations were conducted, recommendations for training were made, and performance goals for the upcoming month were established. Because all messages from senior management to agents flowed through the coaches, the coaches assumed a role in which they represented the larger organization to these employees. For this reason, relationships with coaches were critical for influencing employee behavior not only as it related to agents development or their handling of customer calls but also their knowledge submission to the KMS. A coach’s view of the value of the KMS was mirrored by an agent’s view of the value of the KMS.

In summary, DBL Software Company had tried many interventions to increase knowledge submissions to the KMS (emails, fliers, banners, creation of knowledge champions) yet all these initiatives failed. Based on our observations through interviews with many different types of employees, a pattern appeared showing that employees were attributing their nonsubmission of knowledge to the KMS to different factors: some did not submit because they wanted to follow their coach; others, because of what they thought was happening in the knowledge validation process. Therefore, utilizing the rubric described in the statistical AR/diagnosing stage (and Appendix B), we took advantage of two already published theories—attribution theory and knowledge validation theory—to interpret the data.

Attribution theory is about an individual’s “perceived causes of [his or her, or another individual’s] success and failure in achievement-related situations” (Weiner 1985, p. 549). We emphasize that attribution theory is about what an individual in everyday life attributes to be causes, not what a social scientist attributes to be causes. The theory takes the following perspective (Weiner 1985, pp. 548-549):

[For an individual in his or her everyday life,] it clearly is functional to know why an event has occurred. As Kelley (1971) stated, “The attributor is not simply an attributor, a seeker after knowledge; his latent goal in attaining knowledge is that of effective management of himself and his environment” (p. 22). Once a cause, or causes, are assigned [by the individual], effective management [by that individual] may be possible and a prescription or guide for future action can be suggested. If the prior outcome was a success, then there is likely to be an attempt [by the individual] to reinstate the prior causal network. On the other hand, if the prior outcome or event was undesired—such as exam failure, social rejection, political loss, or economic decline—then there is a strong possibility that there will be an attempt [by this individual] to alter the causes to produce a different (more positive) effect.

From the perspective of attribution theory, all people are “naïve psychologists” who are curious to understand the causes of their own and others’ behavioral outcomes (Harvey and Martinko 2008). Individuals make attributions continually throughout the day in an almost automatic manner. Attributions that an individual makes will shape her emotional and behavioral outcomes, as well as enable her to adapt to the circumstances of her environment in order to avoid problems or to achieve goals (Heider 1958; Weiner 1980, 1986). Research on attribution theory has shown that an individual’s causal attributions affect his or her feelings about past events, expectations about future events, attitudes toward other individuals, reactions to their behavior, and conceptions of self in an effort to improve one’s fortunes (Kelley and Michela 1980, p. 489).

The causal attributions that an individual makes can be classified along three causal dimensions (Weiner 1985): (1) locus, where conditions leading to an outcome such as success or failure are seen as “factors within the person” and “factors within the environment” (p. 551), which are called internal factors and external factors, respectively, (2) stability, where a cause, over time, is relatively constant or variable, and (3) controllability, where a cause is or is not subject to the individual’s volitional control. Next, we identify the external and internal factors in the locus dimension, and we examine the factors in the stability and controllability dimensions for DBL Software Company.

Attribution theory considers examples of internal factors to be an individual’s ability, aptitude, effort, and mood. As for DBL Software Company agents’ not making knowledge submissions to the KMS, we diagnosed the DBL Software Company agents as attributing this outcome to external factors rather than internal factors. This is because if the agents had attributed this outcome (not making knowledge submissions) to internal factors such as their levels of ability, aptitude, effort, and mood, then they would not have made knowledge submissions to their own wikis either. However, as already mentioned, senior management observed, somewhat ruefully, that the agents were indeed making knowledge submissions to their local wikis and not to the KMS. We also observed anger and resentment from the agents regarding their experiences in making knowledge submissions to the KMS. This is significant insofar as anger and resentment are, in general, most frequently associated not with internal factors, but with external factors (Gundlach et al. 2003; Harvey and Martinko 2008). This directs our attention to external factors.

Previous literature on knowledge submissions has identified three categories of what we consider to be external factors that can influence knowledge submissions: operating procedures, the organizational environment, and the KMS itself. We discuss operating procedures and the organizational environment next because they were the focal points of our investigation. Given its secondary role in the organization but important role in the nomological network, we discuss the role of the KMS itself, along with the statistical results, in Appendix D.

As for the first category of external factors, there are the operating procedures associated with making knowledge submissions. There is a theory that is not only specific to knowledge management research, but even identifies certain external factors; it is knowledge validation theory (Durcikova and Gray 2009). It holds that “individuals’ perceptions of three primary validation process characteristics (duration, transparency, and restrictiveness) impact … their contribution behaviors” (ibid., p. 81). The duration (“the time lag between submission of a new contribution and a decision by a reviewer,” ibid., p. 83), the transparency (“the extent to which contributors can observe the validation process in action,”

ibid., p. 83), and the restrictiveness (“overall rejection rate of the validation process,” ibid., p. 83) are all theorized to influence an individual’s contribution behavior to the KMS knowledge repository. Indeed, as evidenced by the preceding quotations, the knowledge submission process at DBL Software Company was marked by an interminable duration (as far as the agents knew, most submissions were still “in process” after being contributed), no transparency (e.g., agents fallaciously believed their submissions were being assessed in a different state or being assessed by artificial intelligence), and extreme restrictiveness (e.g., one agent reported only four accepted submissions out of approximately one hundred submissions). As part of our diagnosis, we measured any impact that duration, transparency, and restrictiveness have on knowledge submissions. We used the same measurement instruments as used by Durcikova and Gray (2009). Selection of knowledge validation theory is in line with the rubric described in the statistical AR/diagnosing section of our paper (also see Appendix B).

Second, as for the organizational environment, a factor that stood out in our fieldwork was the role of the supervisor, significantly referred to as “coach.” Agents were actually allowed to select their coaches. Based on interviews, it appeared that there was a desire among agents to work with individuals who were regarded as highly knowledgeable and well respected in the organization. Many agents even chose to work with coaches whom they had never met, basing their choices on what they heard from other agents. Because there is no theoretical basis for including professional respect for a coach in the knowledge validation theory, we included it as a control variable. Therefore, as part of our diagnosis, we measured any impact of the agents’ professional respect for their coaches on the agents’ making knowledge submissions. We measured professional respect using an instrument developed by Liden and Maslyn (1998).

Having identified the external factors in attribution theory’s locus dimension, we now reexamine them in light of attribution theory’s stability and controllability dimensions. Regarding the stability dimension, all of the external factors were stable from the time that the KMS was implemented throughout the time of our action-research intervention (which we discuss later). Over this period of time, there was no variation in the features of the KMS, in the work of the coaches, in the excessive duration for reviewers to make a decision on agents’ knowledge submissions, in the lack of transparency in the review process, or in the extreme restrictiveness of the acceptance rate for knowledge submissions. Thus, there were no changes in the factors to which DBL Software Company agents attributed their not making knowledge submissions to the KMS. Regarding the controllability dimension, the agents themselves could not change or control any of these factors.

Because the two dimensions—stability and controllability— do not, at this point, add to our diagnosis, it might seem that they do not contribute to our AR. To the contrary, by simply having us consider these two dimensions, attribution theory is usefully drawing our attention to possible points of intervention (i.e., to replace stable conditions with changing conditions and to change factors that agents have no control over, but senior managers do).

The results are shown in Figure 2 (details of data collection and analysis are provided in Appendix D).

The baseline values of all the salient factors are summarized in Table 3, found in the “Stage Four of Statistical AR” section. Regarding an external factor’s influence on knowledge submissions (“submission frequency”), only the measurement of the influence of “respect for coach” was statistically significant (see Appendix D for results that include KMS characteristics). None of the measurements of the influence of the three external factors identified by knowledge validation theory (duration, transparency, and restrictiveness) were statistically significant, thereby indicating, contrary to the theory, that they had no detectable effect on agents’ making knowledge submissions. To the extent that the theory predicts there to be an observable effect, the observation of no effect is a refutation of knowledge submission theory.

This refutation, however, pertains to the situation in which measurements are made at one point in time (a cross sectional picture or snapshot). This is a picture in which variations in duration, transparency, and restrictiveness across different agents, all measured at the same point in time, are theorized to be associated with variations in the frequency of knowledge submissions across these agents, again all measured at the same point in time. In general, in behavioral research involving statistical hypothesis testing, it is a standard practice to make such cross-sectional measurements; indeed, the original study that put forth knowledge validation theory and found these same relationships to be statistically significant, had made all of its measurements cross-sectionally. At the same time, this leaves open, in our diagnosis, the possibility that variations in duration, transparency, and restrictiveness over time (rather than at one point in time) are associated with variation in the frequency of knowledge submissions made over time. In the terminology of positivist research methods, pursuing this possibility would mean applying an experimental stimulus to induce variation in the three independent variables (namely, duration, transparency, and restrictiveness) so that afterward we may observe variation in the dependent variable (namely, submission frequency) as predicted by the theory (namely, knowledge validation theory).

## Stage Two of Statistical AR: Action Planning

Our diagnosis followed from attribution theory’s proposition that what a person attributes to be the causes of “why an event has occurred” (including her own or another person’s behavior) serves, for this individual, as a “guide for future action” (e.g., Weiner 1986). Naturally following from such a diagnosis is the prescription to change what the individual would be attributing to be the causes of the events and behaviors that he or she experiences. However, as the managers at DBL Software Company had already learned, it does not suffice simply to tell people to do things differently; we earlier quoted a manager, who was supervising over 60 agents, as having said: “We tried to send emails, fliers, banners to encourage knowledge submission. Nothing is working.” To plan our actions, we obtain additional assistance from existing research. Earlier, we took advantage of attribution theory and knowledge validation theory; now, we turn to persuasion theory. This allows us to create an intervention that is much more sophisticated than any of the previous interventions that did not follow any theory; the only message management delivered was that there is a central KMS and that the agents should submit knowledge to it.

Persuasion theory is concerned with “human communication designed to influence the autonomous judgments and actions of others” (Simons et al. 2001, p. 7). Dainton and Zelley (2011, p. 122) emphasize that “persuasion is not accidental, nor is it coercive. It is inherently communicational.” Hovland et al. (1953), in conducting pioneering research on “how opinions and beliefs are modified by persuasive communications” (p. 269) found that

most of the results can be viewed as specifying the effects of a communication according to the nature of 1) the communicator (who says it), 2) the communication (what is said), and 3) the audience (to whom it is said) (p. 269).

Hovland et al.’s findings have persisted, been refined by others, and even achieved acceptance to the point of becoming textbook material: In his book Persuasion Theory & Research, which distills the results of decades of research on persuasion, O’Keefe (2002) devotes a chapter each to the same categories used by Hovland et al., which O’Keefe identifies as source factors, message factors, and receiver and context factors.

Regarding the first category of the communicator or source, research in persuasion theory offers a finding relevant to the action of persuading agents that the knowledge validation process at DBL Software Company, contrary to their past experience, is indeed worthy of receiving their submissions: “With a counterattitudinal message, the high-credibility communicator will tend to have a persuasive advantage over the low-credibility source” (O’Keefe 2002, p. 194), where credibility “consists of the judgments made by a perceiver (e.g., a message recipient) concerning the believability of a communicator” (ibid., p. 181) and where credibility, in turn, is marked by the dimensions of expertise and trustworthiness. To overcome the agents’ already established causal attributions regarding the knowledge validation process, it would therefore be helpful for them to receive a message from a high-credibility communicator. It is fortuitous that there were already some people whom the agents regarded with credibility: the coaches. They were already seen as trustworthy by and commanded respect from the agents (which we measured, as Figure 1 indicates). This renders the coaches suitable as sources or communicators for the message that the knowledge validation process was worthy of receiving the agents knowledge submissions.

Regarding the second category of the communication or message, past studies on persuasion “have commonly found that messages with more specific descriptions of the recommended action are more persuasive than those providing general, nonspecific recommendations” (ibid., p. 218). The alternative would be to let the message recipient reach the intended conclusion by herself rather than to specify the conclusion, but “research evidence suggests that persuaders commonly have little to gain (and much to lose) by leaving the message’s conclusion implicit” (ibid., p. 218). O’Keefe adds,

It is not yet clear what might explain this effect, but one possibility is that more specific descriptions of the recommended action enhance the receiver’s behavioral self-efficacy (perceived behavioral control) (p. 181).

We thus plan the action to communicate to agents, in specific terms, to make knowledge submissions to the KMS, where doing so would be good for all concerned.

Regarding the third and final category of the audience or receiver, the past research on persuasion has not yet yielded findings suitable for application in our AR. For instance, “there is not yet any entirely well-established procedure that permits advance identification of persuadable persons, and there is not good evidence about the correlates of general persuasibility” (ibid., p. 242). Also, “there is some indication that persuasibility may be maximized at intermediate levels of self-esteem and at lower levels of intelligence” (ibid., pp. 243- 244), but we consider planning an action to target persons with these characteristics not to be feasible or appropriate.

We intend the action that we are planning, regarding the source and the message, not only to remedy DBL Software Company’s real-world problem in which agents are not making knowledge submissions to their KMS, but also to add a major refinement to theory, namely, knowledge validation theory, which the evidence in the diagnosing stage contested. To make a contribution to knowledge validation theory, we plan to add persuasion as a moderating variable to three relationships: the relationship between restrictiveness and knowledge submission frequency, the relationship between duration and knowledge submission frequency, and the relationship between transparency and knowledge submission frequency.

Our rationale is that persuasion, in altering the agents’ causal attributions regarding restrictiveness, duration, and transparency, would operate by rendering these three relationships discernible by the agents and therefore detectable in our measurements.

## Stage Three of Statistical AR: Action Taking

Here we describe the components of the action/intervention administered by the company to the agents. Figure 3 provides the general overview of the action. It is worth noting that the old persuasion efforts (e.g., banners, emails, flyers per the quotation of Manager 1), just like the new one described below, called for knowledge submissions to the KMS. However, the new action, taking the new form of a calculated persuasion, differed on the following items: (1) communicator/ source characteristics and phased communication; (2) communication/message characteristics and emphasis on the purpose and benefits of the change (i.e., the new KMS, and the roles to be played); and (3) delivery. Each of these differences is described below.

Communicator/source characteristics and phased communication: The project manager of the KMS implementation and the cultural change agent for the organization initiated phased communication involving middle level managers of the business units, SMEs, coaches, and help desk agents. In the first phase, the project manager and the cultural change agent communicated with the middle level managers, who were asked to be the project sponsors in the eyes of the agents. In the second phase, the middle level managers communicated with coaches and SMEs. In the third and final phase, coaches approached the agents and explained the purpose and benefits of the new KMS. As we explained before, coaches were regarded with credibility and were seen as trustworthy by the agents. While the project manager and the cultural change agent initiated the communication (both verbal and email), the message was delivered by coaches and reiterated by the middle level managers. Agents reported that all individuals involved in the intervention (i.e., source factors) were respected and perceived as trustworthy by the agents.

Communication/message characteristics and emphasis on the purpose and benefits of the change: To achieve the goal of the intervention, the message had to provide specific descriptions of the recommended action that was readily comprehensible by each recipient group and had a small number of arguments (a maximum of three bullet points) for each of the following categories: the purpose of the change, the benefits of the change (Weiss et al. 1965), and the roles to be played (Hovland and Mandell 1952). While the confidentiality agreement does not allow us to show the exact verbiage of the message, we can provide the key points the intervention was delivering. The purpose of the change (the new KMS) was twofold: (1) to help the agents do a better job when answering customer queries and (2) to explain the knowledge validation procedures (considered proprietary by DBL Software Company) in detail. The benefits of the change were threefold: (1) easy access to knowledge, (2) access by all agents to the full body of knowledge rather than limited as provided by local wikis, and (3) validated knowledge that ensured that the customers’ data would not get damaged. The roles to be played by the agents were as (1) the users of the knowledge in the KMS and (2) authors of the knowledge.

Delivery: The phased communication plan (explained above) utilized both face-to-face and mass-media (e.g., email) communication. Although face-to-face communication was used to deliver the message the first time, email was used to reinforce the message rather than as the main and only way to deliver the message (as was the case in the past). Face-to-face communication was used during management or team meetings. Most importantly, agents were taken off the phone to attend a meeting that explained the purpose, benefits, and roles. In a help desk environment, an agent’s time is completely scheduled, including bathroom breaks and even time to speak with the action researchers; therefore, the very act of taking agents off the phones for them to be informed of this announcement conveyed out-of-the ordinary significance to everyone at DBL Software Company.

## Stage Four of Statistical AR: Evaluation

Evaluation is the fourth stage of the AR cycle as described by Davison et al. (2012, p. 73). Three months after the intervention, the management team, with the help of one of the authors, evaluated the effect of the intervention and compared it to the baseline. The same survey as before the intervention was given to the 80 employees participating in the intervention. No other unusual organizational initiatives (such as a major new product release) were undertaken at the time of the study. Thus, potentially confounding effects of situational and design factors were mitigated. We received 64 usable responses, representing an 80% response rate. Figure 4 tells a story different from that of Figure 2, while still explaining 23% of the variability in knowledge submission frequency (details of data collection and analysis are provided in Appendix D). The process variables (i.e., restrictiveness, transparency, duration), together with perceived usefulness, now predict submission frequency (whereas prior to the intervention, they did not). Not surprisingly, the rejection rate has a negative impact on submission frequency. However, contrary to expectations, process transparency also has a negative relationship. Respect for coach and ease of use have no detectable direct impact on submission frequency. As we can see in Figure 4, which presents the results of model after the intervention, a shift in attribution occurred. Respect for coach is no longer a predictor of knowledge submission, but rather, the variables identified by knowledge validation theory emerge with both theoretically and measurably significant roles (see Appendix D for results that include system characteristics).

## Stage Five of Statistical AR: Specifying Learning

Specifying learning is the fifth stage of the statistical AR cycle, which addresses learning not only by organizational participants, but also by academic researchers.

For the audience at DBL Software Company, the new knowledge was that the mere purchase of information technology and its introduction into the workplace are not sufficient, in themselves, to implement the new technology, but additional actions required of people in specific roles, along with additional procedures pertaining to knowledge management, also need to be devised, communicated, and adopted. On the one hand, researchers and educators in information systems have long known that information technology is not an appliance, like a washing machine, that simply works after just being plugged in, but instead has behavioral impacts that richly complicate its adoption and transform the social environment around it. On the other hand, practitioners and even senior managers in the everyday world of organizational life do not necessarily know this general lesson at the outset of an information-technology implementation, as evidenced at DBL Software Company with its initially ineffective KMS implementation.

There are specific lessons for practitioners regarding KMS in the “specifying learning” stage of our statistical AR. The results of our study provide evidence that carefully calculated, persuasive messages from management (about the purpose and benefits of the change, and the roles to be played) can be necessary and can have a positive impact on both KMS submission frequency and submission quality. A carefully calculated persuasive message has two key components. First, the communicator is carefully selected based on his or her relationship with the message recipients, and phased communication can be helpful. Second, the message itself needs to be specific with respect to the desired action and also needs to mention the roles to be played and the purpose and benefits of the change. In addition, the message needs to be delivered in a manner that signals its importance in the particular context. In our context, it was taking certain agents off the phone. This persuasive message contributed to doubling the number of good quality submissions that provided the necessary inflow of knowledge so that agents at DBL Software Company could provide better customer service to a fast growing customer base. Companies should pay attention to proper diagnosis of the underlying attributions because the agents’ attributions (not what the management or a consultant thinks these attributions ought to be) play a central role in how the intervention needs to be designed.

For the scholarly audience in the IS discipline, the major methodological lesson learned is that we have demonstrated that statistical AR, one form of AR that combines AR and hypothesis testing, is an altogether feasible form of AR. The second lesson learned is that the original formulation of knowledge submission theory, as presented by Durcikova and Gray (2009), is deficient and needs to be reconfigured by adding a new moderating variable, persuasion, pertaining to the roles to be played and the purpose and benefits of the change. ## Discussion

The main research question of this paper was: How may we conduct action research (AR) so that it benefits from the richness typically associated with qualitative and interpretive research, but additionally embodies the type of rigor typically associated with positivist research? We provided theoretical support and also empirical evidence that this type of AR, named statistical AR, is an answer to this question. We utilized knowledge management, specifically KMS, as a context to demonstrate statistical AR. We leveraged attribution, persuasion, and knowledge validation theories. Figure 6 summarizes the implementation of statistical AR in our paper and shows the phases where each of the three theories was pertinent. Based on our findings, we now discuss methodological contributions to AR and implications for KMS research and practice.

## Implications for AR Methods

The main implication of our study for AR methods is a proof, by example, that positivist AR, including statistical AR, is fully compatible with canonical AR. Our “proof by example” therefore makes the contribution of affirming the constructive role that positivism may play in AR and demonstrating how to expand AR beyond Baskerville’s (1999, p. 25) conceptualization of AR as “parked solidly outside of valid positivist techniques.” This contribution also serves to leave behind any explicit or implicit rejection of positivism, such as

Susman and Evered’s (1978, p. 589) framing of “action research as a corrective to the deficiencies of positivist science”; this is significant insofar as the intellectual legacy of Susman and Evered’s classic study is still evident in the well-known cycle of five stages of AR that it originated and that is embraced by, for example, not only the participatory AR of Baskerville (1999) but also the overall “contemporary AR” described by Myers (2013). Susman and Evered had even gone so far as to contend, “we find all positivist approaches to science … to be deficient in their capacity to generate knowledge for use by members of organizations for solving the problems they face” (p. 585)—a contention that our demonstration of AR’s successful use of positivist statistical hypothesis testing puts to rest.

Worth emphasizing is that our instantiation of AR satisfies the requirement for scientific rigor as articulated by Lee and Hubona (2009), who specifically cover AR. Scientific rigor requires reasoning in the form of what symbolic logic calls “modus tollens,” which is a specific form of the syllogism. Its major premise is “if p is true, then q is true”; the minor premise is “q is not true”; and the conclusion is “therefore, p is not true.” We can apply Lee and Hubona’s framework to our statistical AR. In AR, “p” is the “theory of action T for solving a certain type of organizational problem” (p. 244). In our statistical AR study, the theory of action was knowledge validation theory and “q” is the solving or remedying of the targeted organizational problem (in our case, this was a significant increase in usable knowledge submissions to the KMS at DBL Software Company). We observed, after the AR intervention, that the minor premise was not “not q”— which, according to Lee and Hubona, is evidence that is consistent with the theory being tested, indicating that the theory (as extended with persuasion as a moderating variable) has passed the empirical test posed in statistical AR (but, like all scientific theories, remains open to the possibility of being shown to be invalid in future empirical testing).

Finally, we argue that statistical AR can overcome three main criticisms of AR by positivist researchers (Kock et al. 1997): contingency of the research findings, low control of the environment, and personal over-involvement. The use of preexisting theory and instruments increases the extent to which the research can be replicated and generalized, thereby ameliorating excessive contingency. The low control of the environment was not a problem but actually an advantage in this case: it provided a stable environment without additional factors that could potentially confound the intervention. The use of preexisting positivist theories and instruments provided a safeguard on personal over-involvement. Although Kock et al. suggest that the cyclical nature of AR can overcome these limitations, we demonstrate that they are not, in fact, limitations, and we further offer an alternative that does not require multiple iterations through the cycles.

## Implications for KMS Research

Although the emphasis of this paper is on statistical AR, all AR is required to make contributions to both theory and practice. Our contributions to theory are to theory about KMS. Our primary theoretical contribution in the area of KMS is a test and extension of knowledge validation theory (Durcikova and Gray 2009). According to Whetten (1989), “theoretical insights come from demonstrating how the addition of a new variable significantly alters our understanding of the phenomena by reorganizing our causal maps” (p. 493). Whetten identifies what, how, and why as key components of theoretical development. Knowledge validation theory provided the basis for what factors influenced knowledge contribution. However, our first round of data collection demonstrated that the “what” did not have the expected impact on the dependent variable in this context. We then set out to understand why this was the case. The “why,” which is the most fruitful but also most difficult avenue of theory development (Whetten 1989), was identified by introducing the two additional theories; attribution theory explained why the agents reacted in the ways that they did and persuasion theory explained why they changed their reaction and behavior. This integrated model explained the events we observed at DBL Software Company; it also provides an expanded view of knowledge validation theory that can be tested in other organizations.

## Implications for Practice

This study demonstrates the usefulness of statistical AR in practice. Statistical AR can enable a researcher to utilize and test already existing theories, as described by the rubric in Table 1, and thus provide the solution to the problem and have statistical evidence of the efficacy of the solution. In this way, a researcher, endowed with statistical skills and already versed in the theories of his or her specialization and positioned to seek out additional existing theories, can be an invaluable team member in a statistical AR effort.

This study also highlights the value of understanding the users’ perspective (rather than what management believes to be the users’ perspective) when attempting to turn around an instance of an ineffective system. Understanding attribution, and applying attribution theory, can be valuable in organizational change efforts where attributions made by the intended users of the IS occupy a point along the critical path to system usage and therefore to system success. In addition, this work highlights the role of persuasion as a tool that can facilitate the implementation of systems, where the effectiveness of the persuasion depends on identifying the elements that contribute to a message’s being persuasive in a specific organization. Organizations leveraging our findings will need to determine what constitutes a persuasive message in their environments, including the purpose and benefits of the change, and the roles to be played. The generalizability of our recommendation, for organizations engaging in systems implementation to provide a focus on persuasion, awaits the results of further empirical testing. Furthermore, even if the theory were to survive empirical testing, judgment calls (Lee and Baskerville 2012) must still be made about the extent to which the new setting shares relevant features with the current setting, DBL Software Company.

## Limitations

Statistical AR addresses many of the limitations of conventional AR; however, as theories “show themselves” to the researcher in AR, it is possible that different researchers, with different backgrounds, will identify different theories to address this problem. This could lead to the design of a different intervention and different outcomes. Nevertheless, it does not alter the evidence presented here that statistical AR provides value in IS research. In addition, our data collection and analysis provides support for the revised knowledge validation model in the paper. Thus, despite the fact that different researchers might leverage different theories, we do not believe that would alter the key findings from this work.

There are additional limitations stemming from the context in which we applied statistical AR. Namely, the study was conducted in a single organization with a single KMS and a single intervention. Although it is not uncommon in action research to examine a single organization, its problem, and a solution, the single organizational focus does raise questions regarding the generalizability of the results; however, generalizing results from one organization to other organizations always requires testing the results in those organizations (Lee and Baskerville 2003). Given that one of our goals was to demonstrate the applicability and value of statistical AR, an important assessment of the study is whether or not the intervention worked. In our case, it did. In addition, we did not directly measure persuasion. Instead, we observed the organizational actions that reflected persuasion. Based on these observations, we feel confident that persuasion occurred in the organization.

## Future Research

Our work lays the foundation for a number of important future studies. First, applying statistical AR in different contexts will be an important next step. As Johns (2006) indicates, context can have significant impacts on theorizing. In fact, he suggests that context can be “a shaper of meaning” (p. 388). This is particularly important for designing interventions in statistical AR, as they are largely dependent on the perceptions of the affected parties. If taking people off the phones had not been a signal in this organization, it would not have been nearly as valuable in designing the persuasive message. Further, by acknowledging the organizational context, we could begin to understand if there are times/places when statistical AR is not appropriate.

In our statistical AR, theory shows itself to the researchers. Researchers with different backgrounds and experiences might see other theories that would be equally appropriate for this same context. For example, researchers familiar with expectation confirmation and related theoretical perspectives (e.g., Brown et al. 2014) might have cued in on employee comments regarding expectations of the knowledge validation process. As a result, expectation confirmation theories would have “shown themselves” to the researchers. In this case, an intervention might have been designed around managing expectations. This provides multiple opportunities for developing a richer understanding of how employees can be motivated to perform different behaviors.

Based on our research, we propose that statistical AR is appropriate for more mature fields of study. Edmondson and McManus (2007, p. 1158)

suggest that theory in management research falls along a continuum, from mature to nascent. Mature theory presents well-developed constructs and models that have been studied over time with increasing precision by a variety of scholars, resulting in a body of work consisting of points of broad agreement that represent cumulative knowledge gained. Nascent theory, in contrast, proposes tentative answers to novel questions of how and why, often merely suggesting new connections among phenomena.

Given the need for the theory to show itself to the researcher, it would seem that research areas that are mature will be most well-suited to statistical AR. Well-established theory and previously validated instruments provide a solid foundation for statistical AR. For example, research in employee security behavior (e.g., Jenkins et al. 2013) has attempted to understand the factors that will lead employees to engage in better security practices. Statistical AR could be leveraged to obtain a clear understanding of those factors. Likewise, research in electronic medical record (EMR) adoption could leverage mature theories associated with enterprise systems to understand how to improve the adoption and use of EMR. In contrast, for nascent theoretical domains, other types of AR are likely to be more suitable.

## Conclusion

The objective of this paper was to propose a new type of AR, statistical AR, and provide a proof by example that positivist AR, which includes statistical AR, is fully compatible with canonical AR. Statistical AR represents a new toolkit for the information systems discipline by contributing to the discussions regarding the collaborative nature of qualitative and quantitative techniques. Our approach, which introduced statistical hypothesis testing in AR, satisfies the requirements for scientific rigor as articulated by Lee and Hubona (2009), overcomes the three main criticisms of AR by positivist researchers (Kock et al. 1997). In this paper, we utilized statistical AR to improve knowledge submission to an already implemented but ineffective KMS and were able to advance knowledge validation theory (Durcikova and Gray 2009). Well-established theory and previously validated instruments provide a solid foundation for statistical AR and therefore, research areas that are mature will be well-suited for statistical AR.

We sampled the 80 employees involved in the pilot testing of the intervention. We received 56 usable responses in pre-intervention and 64 in post-intervention, resulting in response rates of 70% and 80% respectively. The data were analyzed using SmartPLS 3 (Ringle et al. 2015). PLS is a structural equation modeling technique that allows us to simultaneously evaluate the data and the theory. It is particularly well-suited for our purposes as it is good for the analysis of small data sets (Chin 1998) and is more suited for predictive applications and theory building than covariance-based structural equation modeling (Gefen et al. 2000). We employed bootstrapping (500 samples) to test the significance of the path coefficients in the model.

Following Podsakoff and Dalton (1987), common method bias was tested via factor analysis. The procedure extracted 7 factors explaining 90% of the variance in the underlying data. No single factor had significant loadings for all items. We also utilized the marker-variable technique (Lindell and Whitney 2001), which offers two alternative approaches for assessing common method bias. First, a marker variable that is theoretically unrelated to at least one variable in the study can be identified and incorporated into the instrument. The correlation between the marker variable and the dependent variable is a reliable estimate of common method bias. Second, the second-smallest positive correlation among the manifest variables provides a conservative estimate for common method bias. Given our study design, we employed the second method. After adjustment for the second smallest positive correlation, all significant correlations remained significant. Thus, common method bias was not deemed a problem for our analysis.

We then conducted confirmatory factor analysis to identify items that loaded poorly on their respective constructs. As a result, we dropped three items: items one and four for perceived rejection rate and item one from transparency. We examined the reliability of the remaining measures using three techniques in PLS. We first examined the item loadings to be sure that they were all above .7. Except for one item (the second item in perceived rejection rate at time 2), the loadings met this criteria. Due to the fact that the remaining reliability analysi demonstrates good reliability (ICR = .75), we retained the items. Second, we examined the internal consistency by evaluating the composite reliability. For all items at both time periods, the values exceeded Nunnally’s (1978) suggested cutoff of .7. Finally, we calculated the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for all items. The lowest value was .60, which exceeds Chin’s (1998) recommended cutoff of .50. Following Ringle et al (2012), this provides ample evidence of the reliability of the constructs.

Discriminant validity was assessed using two techniques. First, an examination of the correlation of items with their respective constructs revealed that the items correlated most strongly with their intended constructs. Second, the square root of the AVE exceeded all interconstruct correlations. Together, these measures demonstrate that the items discriminated adequately across constructs. To assess potential multicollinearity, we calculated the variance inflation factors (VIF). VIF values for all variables for both pre-intervention and post-intervention ranged from 1.0 to 1.97, below the suggested threshold value of 3.3 (Kock and Lynn 2012). This provides evidence that multicollinearity was not a potential confound.

Tables D2, D3, and D4 provide the detailed analysis for the reliability and validity of constructs. Tables D4 and D5 shows the item-total correlations for pre-intervention and post-intervention.

Note: Our literature review resulted in three categories that can influence knowledge submission, namely, operating procedures, the organizational environment, and the KMS itself. We discuss the role of the first two in Stage One of Statistical AR. Here, we discuss the role of the third category given that it is a part of the nomological network and this is how we presented the results to DBL Software Company.

As for the third category, there is an extensive literature on technology acceptance (e.g., Davis 1989; Davis et al. 1989; Venkatesh et al. 2003) that addresses, among other things, an individual’s perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and resultant usage of a system. We note that, based on our interviews, there were no technical problems with the KMS as installed and implemented, including its knowledge submission component. We also observed nothing indicating that DBL Software Company agents blamed features of the KMS for their not making knowledge submissions. However, as part of our diagnosis, we measure certain technology-acceptance relationships, in order to control for any impact they have on making knowledge submissions. We measure perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness using instruments already available from technology acceptance research (e.g., Davis 1989; Davis et al. 1989; Venkatesh et al. 2003) and include them in our hypothesis testing as control variables.
