# Research of information systems: from parochial to international, towards global or glocal?

Maris G. Martinsons

Department of Management, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong, email: mgmaris@cityu.edu.hk

The development over time of the Information Systems Journal and information systems (IS) research in general is examined by applying both quantitative and qualitative data analyses. The evidence suggests that the journal and the IS discipline have both evolved from parochial and national to become international. This evolution has in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the phenomena and contexts being studied and, ultimately, theory and practice. Cultural and institutional differences are major obstacles to globalizing IS research. Based on a trilemma for theoretical development among generality, accuracy and simplicity, glocalization is suggested as an alternative to globalization. The merits of glocal research and education, with globally connected villages, are discussed.

Keywords: research evolution, international, theory development, top-tier journals, multiple research methods

## INTRODUCTION

An ancient Greek philosopher, Heraclitus, reputedly said ‘Nothing is certain except change.’ His statement undoubtedly applies to the Information Systems Journal (ISJ) and research in information systems (IS). Both have changed substantially over the last 25 years.

In examining and commenting on these changes, I found it useful to start by considering how the ISJ founding editors justi<sup>fi</sup>ed their establishment of yet another academic journal. In their initial editorial, Avison and Fitzgerald (1991, p.1) criticized existing IS journals for having an overly narrow concentration. They suggested that too many IS research publications focus narrowly on information technology (IT). Avison and Fitzgerald believed that a major change in emphasis was needed. They argued that IS researchers should broaden their focus beyond science and technology by standing ‘with our backs to the machine and (look) outward to the world at large, considering business and social issues and impacts’ (ibid, p.2).

The founding editors’ belief in an outward view of IS has been increasingly realized on the pages of this journal. ISJ has published more than 200 broadly based ‘qualitative’ studies and a slightly smaller number of ‘quantitative’ studies. These papers have commonly consid ered the context of their research in order to determine the theoretical and practical implications of their <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclusions. An outward orientation and explicit discussion of business and social impacts is now common in IS research papers.

The ISJ founding editors also criticized existing journals for having ‘a national rather than international perspective’. This criticism provides a focus for my research and re<sup>fl</sup>ection here. After examining the evolution of the IS discipline and its namesake journal, I look to the future and consider different paths forward. In doing so, I distinguish between internationalization, where national borders are crossed, and globalization, where the whole world is involved. Finally, I suggest and discuss a promising alternative: glocalization.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS BEFORE THE 1990S: A FRAGMENTED AND PAROCHIAL RESEARCH DISCIPLINE

Avison and Fitzgerald expressed a general hope that their new journal would bring together a ‘fragmented discipline’. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to establish and apply speci<sup>fi</sup>c criteria to evaluate whethe their hope has been realized. However, before the 1990s, many IS researchers, especially those not working in or networked to the USA, found it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to share their research and in sights with a relevant worldwide audience. This is much less of a concern now even as debates continue about the identity, scope and legitimacy of the IS research discipline.

Shortly after ISJ was launched, I was invited by Bill King to be part of an organizing commit tee that aimed to create a professional association for IS researchers. Our committee had a core group of Americans but included members from six continents and with at least 12 different nationalities. In 1994, the Association of Information Systems (AIS) was formally established as a worldwide community. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, the AIS structure acknowledged three geographic regions (Americas, Asia–Paci<sup>fi</sup>c, Europe and Africa).

Together, ISJ and the AIS have helped to defragment the IS discipline. Many relationships among IS researchers have undoubtedly been established and strengthened over time as they contributed to developing and sustaining this journal and/or participated in AIS activities. Mean while, ISJ has accepted and published nearly 400 papers since 1991. Those of us associated with it as editors, authors and reviewers believe that these papers represent high-quality research contributing to our cumulative knowledge of IS.

In addition to criticizing the state of the IS discipline, Avison and Fitzgerald (1991) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve aims for the new journal. The journal has changed over time. In 1994, it was renamed from the Journal of Information Systems to Information Systems Journal. In 2008, its publication frequency was increased from four to six issues per year. Its physical size and appearance have also been altered slightly. Nevertheless, the <sup>fi</sup>ve aims (as stated in the inaugural issue) have remained constant. The <sup>fi</sup>fth aim was to ‘provide an international perspective’.

My focus in the remainder of this article is on this international aim and its broader implica tions. My thesis is that the ISJ has increasingly succeeded in providing this international perspective. My <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that it has evolved considerably from a rather narrow initia concentration on research conducted and reviewed primarily by British scholars. ISJ has be come the most international top-tier IS journal.

In establishing a new journal, Avison and Fitzgerald (1991, p. 1) cited the need to ‘draw together the information systems community’. From my perspective as a young scholar, academ ically trained in North America and Europe yet researching primarily in Asia, the IS community in the early 1990s was certainly fragmented. IS were being studied in schools of engineering, business and library science within universities as well as within multinational corporations such as IBM. This young community of IS researchers was quite parochial and dominated by Americans. Moreover, many IS researchers working beyond the USA and the UK were trained in those two countries. This represented a form of Anglo-American hegemony.

This heritage has left a deep imprint on the IS discipline. The preferred designs and methods for researching different types of IS phenomena tend to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the American academic norms. Positivism and quantitative methods predominate. Admittedly, IS research has evolved to some extent amid calls for greater pluralism (cf. Mingers, 2001; Davison & Martinsons, 2011).

Nevertheless, traditional designs and methods continue to have a privileged status among many senior and in<sup>fl</sup>uential members of the IS community as well as their disciples. Consistent with its aims (Powell, 2015), ISJ has helped to extend the boundaries of top-tier IS research, beyond positivism to interpretive and even critical methods as well as more developmental domains.

The creation and spread of explicit lists to classify journals into tiers have also shaped scholarly research. The establishment of objective performance criteria by universities was part of an effort to make the tenure-granting and promotion process ‘less inequitable and irrational’ (Fashing, 1976, p. 206). Increasingly, universities around the world have followed the American lead and created journal lists. Vastly different value is now given to different publications when it comes to pro motion and tenure decisions. While developing and sustaining relationships with dozens of universities around the globe, I have seen growing numbers of them essentially adopt journal lists from the USA. Almost wholesale adoption has been evident throughout East Asia.

The growing acceptance of a ‘one size <sup>fi</sup>ts all’ logic for journal lists has encouraged and en dorsed a research monoculture whereby ‘particular criteria … assume the status of a universal benchmark of performance’ (Mingers & Wilmott, 2013, p. 1051). Homogenous journal lists tend to limit the scope, trajectory and creativity of scholarship. This endangers intellectual debates while hindering innovative, unconventional and ground-breaking research.

The highest valued publication on nearly all IS journal classi<sup>fi</sup>cation lists is MIS Quarterly. In the early 1990s, it was an almost exclusively American journal. For example, over 90% of both editorial board members and article authors between 1991 and 1993 were Americans working at universities in the USA. Not surprisingly, MISQ articles in that era tended to re<sup>fl</sup>ect American interests in topics such as Executive/Strategic Information Systems and Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cers.

## FROM BRITISH BEGINNINGS TO A TRULY INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL

Information Systems Journal aimed to break the American hegemony. It undeniably helped to internationalize IS by publishing primarily non-American research. Nevertheless, it initially did this mostly by providing a respectable outlet for British IS researchers. Research conducted in Britain was commonly reviewed for publication by others working in British universities.

A total of 36 authors had their work accepted and published in the 1991 issues of the new journal (as detailed in Table 1). Of these, 22 had British af<sup>fi</sup>liations. Among the remaining 14, six were from the USA, while three (all on the same paper) were from Spain. My article (Martinsons, 1991) was the only one in that entire year without any American or European af<sup>fi</sup>liation.

Subsequently, this journal has lived up to its mission and become a highly regarded outlet for IS research. In 2007, the AIS adopted a basket of six top journals based on a recommen dation from the IS Senior Scholars Forum. The inclusion of ISJ in this original Senior Scholars Basket recognized the quality and in<sup>fl</sup>uence that the journal had achieved in less than two decades.

Consistent with its aim of encouraging qualitative research, ISJ has published the highest percentage of qualitative research among the top-tier IS journals. Almost two-thirds of the research articles published by ISJ have used qualitative methods. This compares with less than one-third of these in the other <sup>fi</sup>ve ‘original basket’ journals. Less than 15% of the articles in three American-based journals (MiS Quarterly, Information Systems Research and Journal o Management Information Systems) are qualitative studies. Disappointingly, mixed method studies remain rare, representing less than 5% of all articles across all the original basket journals.

Information Systems Journal has also slowly yet steadily become ‘the’ truly international IS journal. The <sup>fi</sup>rst volume had only one author based in the Asia–Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region. However, dozens of subsequent ISJ articles and some of the most-cited ones have come from Asia and Australia. Its published authors now represent more than 25 countries. The journal’s international aim was reinforced by a special issue in the summer of 2008. It solicited research from and about the world’s most populous country just as China welcomed the rest of the world to the Beijing Olympics.

The evolving composition of the ISJ editors and its editorial board is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Initially, both editors and 19 of the 25 editorial board members were based in the UK. The remaining six were equally divided between Australia, Sweden and the USA. This British dominance of the ISJ ed itorial leadership team has diminished dramatically as shown in Table 2. Its international balance now contrasts starkly with other top-tier IS journals. Remarkably, the three editors-inchief each live and work on a different continent. They are based in China (Hong Kong), the UK (London) and the USA (University Park, Pennsylvania). The 10 senior editors span the same three continents as the editors-in-chief but represent a total of seven different countries on four different continents

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS JOURNALS: MORE INTERNATIONAL BUT NOT GLOBAL

Information Systems Journal has evolved from its British roots to become a much more interna tional journal. The current editors and editorial board members work in 16 different countries. No one country or continent dominates this team. Thus, I believe that ISJ is ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the <sup>fi</sup>fth aim of its founding editors: to ‘provide an international perspective’. The national and continental diversity of ISJ now clearly distinguishes it within the current Senior Scholars’ Basket of top IS journals.

In contrast, the MIS Quarterly remains dominated by Americans (as detailed in Table 3). Its single editor-in-chief and 21 of its 28 senior editors (75%) are af<sup>fi</sup>liated with US universities. Similarly, the vast majority of those publishing in MISQ and other American IS journals have been trained in the USA. More than 95% of MISQ papers have an author with an American PhD or employer.

The mindsets of those authors (and the editors who have accepted their papers) tend to re-<sup>fl</sup>ect American values and beliefs. Meanwhile, their professional work continues to conform to American norms, which differ from those in, for example, northern Europe. This may be seen as the lag effect of a wealthy and savvy society successfully marketing its way of doing things as ‘universal’. It also tends to re<sup>fl</sup>ect a belief that cultural differences at the national and societal levels can be ignored or easily overcome and that the world will soon be globalized.

I will return to this issue when considering the future of IS research and education.

Information Systems Journal is undoubtedly the most international top-tier IS journal. Nevertheless, it must be noted that large parts of the world were not mentioned in the previous para graphs. The ISJ leadership team is highly internationalized, but South America and West Asia are totally unrepresented. Meanwhile, there is only one associate editor (and no editorial board members) from the entire continent of Africa. Overall, the countries without any ISJ editor or editorial board member represent nearly half the world’s people. Similarly, the home countries of all the ISJ authors represent only about 80% of the global population. More than billion people live in countries that do not have anyone who has ever published an ISJ article.

Submissions to ISJ undoubtedly span more countries than those represented by its editorial team and published authors. I have personally reviewed ISJ submissions based on research conducted in ‘exotic’ locations such as Bangladesh, Egypt, Ghana, India, Iran, Malaysia, Tunisia and Vietnam. Nevertheless, the absence of many nations and nationalities highlights the aforementioned difference between internationalization and globalization.

Information Systems Journal is an international journal. Its submission and review activities now cross dozens of national borders. Yet its geographic scope is far from global. We may ask: is this a serious shortcoming?

The lack of a truly global top-tier IS journal may re<sup>fl</sup>ect a disparity in research funding and resources across countries. It may also re<sup>fl</sup>ect a geographic clustering of skilled scholars and highlight the importance of both a critical mass and a local competition to ‘translate’ research skills into outcomes. Alternatively, the lack of a truly global IS journal may re<sup>fl</sup>ect the limited scope of interest in developing world issues by those in the developed world. The latter continue to be the dominant gatekeepers for top-tier IS journals. Remarkably, both alternatives resemble the patterns evident in worldwide economic activities.

## THE GENERAL TREND TOWARDS GLOBALIZATION

Not so long ago, globalization seemed almost inevitable. International trade and foreign investment from 1967 to 2007 increased at about twice the rate of overall economic growth. Financia markets across much of the globe were becoming more integrated, while information and people were moving around the world with ever greater ease. National borders and geographic distances seemed to become steadily less important.

Globalization enables unprecedented economies of scale and the realization of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from comparative advantages. With access to more information and more goods and services, we have the potential to be better informed and to live better lives, at least materially and physiologically. However, this assumes that we can process the additional information and make wise choices in selecting among the ever-widening variety of goods and services.

Globalization also has many negative consequences. These include undermining the sovereignty of governments while also harming regional and local economies. Rising levels of unem ployment and poverty in some parts of the world are almost inevitable when increased interactions heighten the impact of competitive advantages and disadvantages. The invisible hand of the market does not necessarily produce win–win outcomes.

Globalization has raised serious concerns about responsibility and accountability. With powerful economic forces and complex international relations, it has become dif<sup>fi</sup>cult if not impossible to identify the sources and root causes of problems. Who is responsible for the harm that the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis in<sup>fl</sup>icted on individuals, groups and societies? Who is responsible when individuals born and raised in one country are trained to be terrorists in a second country and, subsequently, commit criminal acts that kill people, destroy property and cause widespread panic in yet another country? There are no simple answers.

We do know that globalization was enabled largely by advances in the information technolo gies that underpin IS. Remarkably, the World Wide Web emerged almost at the same time as this journal. Soon afterwards, the internet enabled e-commerce and spawned the dot-com boom.

Many new business models were developed. These were implemented with varying degrees of competence and success. The founders of enterprises such as Amazon and Alibaba became fabulously wealthy. Meanwhile, Steven Jobs literally became a legend in his own lifetime (before his untimely death) as Apple introduced iPods and iTunes, iPhones and iPads.

Although the dot-com boom went bust very early in the current century, our lives have been changed forever. Who among us does not search the internet when we need to quickly <sup>fi</sup>nd out something? Who among us does not expect to communicate with colleagues who are half a world away almost as easily as with those in the next of<sup>fi</sup>ce?

It no longer matters if you are in Manchester or Melbourne, Seattle or Seoul or Toronto or Tokyo. Nearly all of the developed world has inexpensive and reliable access to the internet.

This has enabled trade and commerce regardless of where the buyer and seller or business partners are located. Individuals and start-up enterprises from much of the world can now com pete successfully. They do so not only by making good use of cost-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient labour but also by offering their information, creativity and innovation to the highest bidder.

Thomas Friedman (2005) famously declared that the ‘world was <sup>fl</sup>at’. He argued that lower trade and political barriers combined with technological advances had connected the whole world in an unprecedented way. It became ‘possible to do business, or almost anything else, instantaneously with billions of other people across the planet’ (Friedman, 2005, p.196)

## GLOBALIZATION SINCE 2008: TEMPORARY PAUSE OR TREND REVERSAL?

Much of this globalization took place before 2008. The global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis quickly impaired the <sup>fl</sup>ows of both trade and capital. Subsequently, economic distress has encouraged protectionist tendencies that threaten to limit the <sup>fl</sup>ows of information and people across national borders. The volume of international information <sup>fl</sup>ows has undeniably continued to rise. Its upward trajectory has been driven by the growth of bandwidth along with the rapidly declining prices of both digital hardware and telecommunication services.

However, concerns have increased about information <sup>fl</sup>ows, data privacy and national sovereignty. Dozens of countries, including China and Turkey, have increased state controls on information <sup>fl</sup>ows. European governments have been particularly keen to ensure that foreign companies that collect personal data within their national borders also maintain and protect that data there. American-headquartered multinationals such as Amazon and Google are among those most affected by growing restrictions on international information <sup>fl</sup>ows.

Meanwhile, the opportunities for people to move across national borders are greater than ever. Yet the global percentage of those living outside the country of their birth has not increased in recent decades. According to the United Nations, 97% of the world’s people live in the same country where they were born. Compared with the 2005–2008 period, the number of job-seeking migrants to rich OECD countries in the 2009–2012 period fell sharply, from 4.4 to 2.6 out of 1000 people. The more recent proportion is almost the same as it was in 1960!

Many national governments face popular pressure to restrict immigration. This includes countries with relatively robust economies, such as the USA and UK. These two countries now require airlines to provide detailed information about their passengers before their planes arrive. Even within the European Union (EU), there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences of opinion with re spect to the inevitable trade-off between national security and the data privacy rights of citizens. It may be remarkable but not farfetched for EU citizens from one member state to be barred from another because of con<sup>fl</sup>icting concerns about border security and data privacy.

Restrictions on and protracted processes for crossing national borders have encouraged more migration within countries. Some intranational movement comes from desires to <sup>fl</sup>ee local con<sup>fl</sup>icts. However, most of this movement re<sup>fl</sup>ects urbanization, led by the Chinese and Indians relocating from farms to cities. Remarkably, the urban areas of China are gaining about 20 million residents per year from domestic migration. Meanwhile, between 2009 and 2014 the USA and Europe added only about 1 million people each annually as a result of international migration.

The global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis and gradual reorientation of the Chinese economy have contributed to a ‘new normal’ in international trade. From the 1960s until 2007, international trade grew reliably at double the rate of the global economy. Some economists now refer to this in the past tense as the ‘era of hyperglobalization’. Between 2012 and 2014, international trade grew much more slowly, increasing at roughly the same rate as the world’s total economic activity. Protec tionist policies and changing costs have encouraged many manufacturers to shorten their supply chains and ‘reshore’ production.

This raises more general questions: Has the globalization trend merely paused as a shortterm response to the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis? – or – Are we witnessing the end of globalization? – or – Is this in fact the beginning of a longer-term reversal of the trend towards globalization? This critical issue merits a closer look at globalization and what it really means.

## GLOBALIZATION: FLAT, DEEP, BROAD AND DIRECTIONAL

Globalization may be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the process of international integration arising from the interchange of worldviews, products, ideas and other aspects of culture’ (Albrow & King, 1990). The ultimate result of globalization would be the triumph of the forces that homogenize institutions and cultures throughout the world.

Globalization has been closely associated with integrated governance and connectivity. Con trary to the ‘<sup>fl</sup>at world’ assertion of Friedman (2005) that connectivity has not been evenly or ‘<sup>fl</sup>atly’ distributed across our planet. Some countries (such as the Netherlands, Singapore, South Korea and Switzerland) are much more globally connected than others (such as Burundi, North Korea, Paraguay and Zimbabwe).

Going further, Rugman (2005) has argued that the globalization of business has always been largely a myth. He asserts that rather than operating globally, even the most multinational enterprises ‘think regionally’ and ‘act locally’.

An example of this thinking is the French <sup>fi</sup>rm that allowed its executives to <sup>fl</sup>y in business class only on outbound journeys. The rationale was that the <sup>fl</sup>ights from France to the USA were at night, and the executives needed to have a good night’s sleep in order to be productive the next day. Flights in the opposite direction, from the USA to France, could be completed during daytime and within normal working hours. Thus, economy class seats were considered suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient. The French <sup>fi</sup>rm did not adapt its airplane seating rules for many decades even though a growing amount of its business was being conducted in East Asia and Australia. Getting from Paris to Melbourne or vice versa, for example, involves journeys that take at least an entire day (24 h) between home and hotel. More than half of the <sup>fl</sup>ight time would be outside of working hours. Being confined to economy class seating and service in either direction is difficult to jus: tify if a business aims to have a productive executive at the end of each journey.

The geographic scope of business is undeniably growing as illustrated by the new journeys between Paris and Melbourne. Nevertheless, international and especially intercontinental busi ness remains the exception rather than the norm.

The continued dependence on regional and local business is evident from the recent rise of many Asian enterprises. An e-commerce <sup>fi</sup>rm operating almost exclusively in one country succeeded with the world’s largest initial public offering in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial heart of another country. Alibaba may someday span the globe. For now, its dominance in China was enough to com mand a mind-boggling market valuation in New York. Other dynamic IT-based <sup>fi</sup>rms such as NTT Docomo, Flipkart and Xiaomi are largely unknown beyond their home country.

Conversely, eBay has been successful in many Western countries but failed in China. Its ‘global’ model did not <sup>fi</sup>t the Chinese market, and it could not or would not ‘act locally’ (Ou & Davison, 2009). More than a decade after expanding to China, Amazon has only about a 3% share of the online retail market there, and it is not (very) pro<sup>fi</sup>table. Amazon aims to grow its market share and pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins in China, but will it learn from the eBay experience?

International business continues to account for a small minority of overall economic activity. Meanwhile, most of the business activities that cross national boundaries are conducted in and between three speci<sup>fi</sup>c regions: North America, Western Europe and East Asia. West Asia and the entire continents of Africa and South America are relatively insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant from a global economic perspective. If natural resources are excluded, the inequity becomes even more pronounced.

## THE RESEARCH GLOBALIZATION MYTH

Similar to the mythical globalization of business (Rugman, 2005), the globalization of IS research also remains a myth. The establishment and evolution of ISJ and the AIS provide useful supporting evidence. Research of IS has been internationalized, but not globalized.

To elaborate, it is helpful to go beyond a simple and single-dimensional view of globalization. The International Monetary Fund (2000) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four basic aspects of globalization: trade and transactions, capital and investment movements, migration and movement of people and the dissemination of knowledge. In terms of the dissemination of knowledge, in general, and IS research speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, several dimensions of globalization (adapted from Ghemawat & Altman, 2014) may be considered:

• Depth: how much information or knowledge is <sup>fl</sup>owing across national borders (as opposed to within a single country)

• Breadth: how are information and knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows distributed (do they involve just a few countries or most of the world?)

• Directionality: the ratio of information and knowledge <sup>fl</sup>owing into the country vs. out of it.

In the early 1990s, the top-tier IS research journals were dominated by Americans working in American universities. The USA was the primary national source of reputable IS research. The <sup>fl</sup>ow of leading-edge IS knowledge was largely within the USA or outward from it.

The breadth of international IS knowledge flow was also limited First, IS was a new aca: demic discipline. Second, the major recipients beyond North America were scholars in a small number of countries, including much of Western Europe and Australia. IS knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows were further constrained by language differences and the limited capabilities of communications technology. A little more than a decade ago, Galliers & Meadows (2003, p. 108) still concluded that the IS discipline is marked by ‘a distinct parochialism … with culturally biased research.’

The production and <sup>fl</sup>ow of IS knowledge have both increased greatly in the last 25 years. The number of top-tier journals has increased from 1 or 2 to 6 or 8, while their total numbe of published pages has increased at least <sup>fi</sup>vefold. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, non-Americans now accoun for a much larger share of top-tier IS research. International diversity has increased greatly. ISJ and the AIS deserve much credit for this evolution.

Nevertheless, the intensity of IS research continues to be unevenly distributed around the world. The USA remains the heart of the scholarly community while East Asia has rapidly become a major intellectual force, with research and education hubs in Hong Kong and Singapore. Based on publications in the AIS Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals for the 3-yea period starting in 2011, the world’s top 20 IS research schools included the City University of Hong Kong (no. 2), the National University of Singapore (no. 9) and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (no. 13) along with 16 universities from the USA and one from Canada (McGill). Based on the revised basket of eight well-respected ISJ journals, two European universities (Warwick and Tilburg) are among the top 30 contributors.

The geographic distribution of those publishing in top-tier IS journals resembles the pattern of international business activities; the vast majority of articles in top-tier journals are based on research conducted by those based in North America, East Asia and Western Europe. There is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interaction between these three regions, while many authors in Asia and Europe have educational links to the USA. Not surprisingly, mainstream IS research, similar to mainstream commerce, continues to re<sup>fl</sup>ect American norms.

## THE FUTURE OF IS RESEARCH: GLOBALIZATION OR GLOCALIZATION?

Information systems research has grown greatly in quantity and geographic scope. Assisted by the rapid rise of IT in business and society, the IS research community has become much larger and more international. The <sup>fl</sup>ow of IS knowledge across national borders has increased dramatically. Is there farther to go? I believe so. The key question now is the direction of the journey.

There is a natural inclination towards globalization. Fukuyama (1989) famously argued that ideological evolution is nearing an endpoint. He foresaw the universalization of Western libera democracy as the <sup>fi</sup>nal form of human government. Similarly, Galliers & Meadows (2003, p. 109) argue for ‘a more global stance in our research and publications’. Other advocates of globalization argue that it would unite the whole world of IS research. The term ‘global’ has become a buzzword, being (over)used for titles of many journals and conferences as well as university programmes and courses.

Globalization may be an unrealistic dream or expectation. Decades after the proclamation by Fukuyama (1989), there is little evidence that the alternatives to Western liberal democracy and free-market capitalism will disappear simply or quickly. From Angola to Zimbabwe, and signi<sup>fi</sup> cantly including China and Russia, autocratic regimes and state capitalism continue to thrive. Kleptocrats and oligarchs are likely to remain part of our socio-economic vocabulary.

The American hegemony that has guided international rules and relations is ebbing. The sole superpower after the Cold War has lost its image of invulnerability after setbacks in Afghanistan and Iraq. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the geopolitical shift. This bank was proposed by the Chinese government and will be headquartered in Beijing. Many US allies have ignored American diplomatic pressure to become AIIB members (Rachman, 2015).

More relevant to IS are large disparities in technological sophistication and cultural differences across societies. These deeply embedded differences are unlikely to disappear and are impossi ble to ignore (Davison, 2014). They severely limit the value of a homogeneous approach to IS research. A standard research design may be inappropriate for a global study that covers different types of economies and societies. Findings, explanations and conclusions are unlikely to be valid universally (cf. Martinsons & Westwood, 1997). Indeed, one size does not <sup>fi</sup>t all.

Efforts to build comprehensive theories by incorporating different social, political or economic characteristics as explanatory variables can become messy and complex. For example, a six-country study ended up incorporating all <sup>fi</sup>ve Hofstede/Bond dimensions of culture in order to explain the variations observed in planning and implementing IT-enabled organizational change (Martinsons et al., 2009). Another study found that the Chinese context led to changes in not only the content and process of American-style business process reengineering but also its objectives. This led to a context-dependent international theory (Hempel & Martinsons, 2009). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the value of understanding a speci<sup>fi</sup>c phenomenon in assorted contexts.

Comprehensive and parsimonious theories should be appropriate for similar and/or converging contexts – like large parts of the Western world. Analogous to their ideological evo lution (Fukuyama, 1989), the developed democracies may be nearing an equilibrium endpoin for IS. Individuals have essentially become connected nodes in a widespread network. With the take-up of electronic devices nearing 100%, IT adoption issues may be less important.

Ubiquitous IT, well-developed institutions and infrastructures along with high degrees of political and social stability are characteristic of advanced societies. However, these characteristics are far from universal. Most of the world’s people live in Asia and Africa. Across these continents, inade quate infrastructures as well as ineffective or incomplete institutions remain common.

Consequently, many interesting IS research questions are not global. Factors such as politi cal ideology and local cultures will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the debates and resolutions of issues such as personal data privacy vs. institutional (national) security. Instead of globalization, a more fruitful path for future research and education may be glocalization (Figure 1).

Glocalization is a portmanteau of globalization and localization. It may be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the transformation or adaptation of a product or service speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to meet the needs of a loca market or culture. Roland Robertson (1994) describes glocalization as a natural outcome of dif ferences between social communities and local resistance towards global pressures. At a 1997 conference on ‘Globalization and Indigenous Culture’, Robertson said that glocalization ‘means the co-presence of both universalizing and particularizing tendencies’.

Both McDonald’s and Chinese food restaurants are located throughout much of the world. However, the menus of these restaurants and even their recipes for speci<sup>fi</sup>c foods are not global. They are modi<sup>fi</sup>ed from country to country (and even region to region) in an attempt to appeal to local palates. For example, McDonald’s serves Bulgogi burgers in South Korea, Maharaia Macs in India and assorted rice dishes in Hong Kong and mainland China.

It is easier to globalize material things than to accommodate the preferences and constraints of people. Strategic innovation is commonly limited by human factors rather than technological ones (Martinsons, 1993). For example, a global virtual team can work at any time on any days of the week. However, if it includes devout Muslims, Jews and Christians, there will be resistance to working on Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. This adds to the common dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of arranging meetings across multiple time zones on the other days of the week.

The socio-economic and regulatory environments for producing and disseminating new knowledge represent an even more daunting challenge for IS researchers. Guo (2003) reported that since China joined the World Trade Organization, its regulatory reforms have in fact increased rather than reduced the obstacles for knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows across its national borders. Meanwhile, Downing (1996) suggested that Western communication and media theories commonly assume a stable political economy, which is absent in most of the world.

## GLOCAL RESEARCH OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS

Information systems research can and should capitalize on the connections between modern societies. However, the focus, theories and methods of their research should <sup>fi</sup>t local circumstances. Research agendas would refocus away from the direct transfer of best practices found in developed countries with rule-based societies and economies.

Instead, more studies would focus on the principles and practices that <sup>fi</sup>t the profoundly different conditions of relationship-based societies with high degrees of state control and emerging marke economies. This may involve adapting some ‘best practices’, but more likely uncovering effective indigenous practices and developing new business models. Context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c theories can be ex pected to emerge from such glocal IS research as shown in Table 4.

The development of a relationship-based theory to explain (e-)commerce (Martinsons, 2008) provides an example of glocal research. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects the stark differences between the business environment in China and those found in rule-based economies such as the USA. Publication of this theory together with illustrative cases from China sets the stage for further research to elaborate the theory and test its boundary conditions (cf. Avgerou & Li, 2013; Ou et al., 2014). Subsequent examination can focus on how both relationship-based and rule-based ecommerce evolve. Do they converge? Is one subsumed by the other? Is there a way to integrate them effectively?

Glocal IS research will <sup>fi</sup>nd that some principles, practices and processes are widely applica ble. They will be effective almost everywhere, perhaps with minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. Other principles, practices and processes will vary greatly depending on the context. Rewards for innovation rather than duplication will encourage domestic entrepreneurship and force foreign businesses to accommodate local circumstances, rather than the other way around (Rein, 2014).

We know that some aspects of organizations are more susceptible to local cultural and polit ical in<sup>fl</sup>uences than others (Child, 1981). While work motivation and corporate governance may vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, organizational structures and coordination mechanisms tend to be determined more by common factors. Similarly, we would expect that some IS phenomena would be less in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by contextual factors than others.

Studies of larger-scale IS should consider factors such as political stability and institutiona maturity (Child & Marinova, 2014). Meanwhile, smaller-scale systems may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced more by cultural factors and personal traits. Research designs should thus aim for depth rather than breadth and analyse the phenomenon and its context.

Increased emphasis on emerging markets and less developed countries is almost a certainty. This will require greater consideration of personal relationships and the role of the visible hand – the state. Other visible hands, namely non-state actors, may also be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. New theories of relationship-based interactions at the individual, organizational and societal levels can be expected to emerge from such glocal IS research. This increased attention to the context <sup>fi</sup>ts the outward vision of Avison and Fitzgerald (1991) when they started to edit this journal.

## THEORIES AND METHODS: UNIVERSAL OR CONTEXTUALLY SENSITIVE?

Theory developers face a fundamental trilemma that involves generality, accuracy and simplic ity as depicted in Figure 2.

Social scientists have commonly prioritized generality and simplicity while downplaying accuracy when developing their theories (Weick, 1989). Progress towards glocalization would strengthen the accuracy of research and theories while maintaining their simplicity (or parsimony). However, some generality would be sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced. Simpler and more accurate <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be easier to apply and more effective in practice.

Glocalized IS research would not return us to the parochial and national levels of IS research that were dominant before the 1990s. However, it would be oriented less to the world market and be driven less by quests for ‘universal’ theories. Instead of aiming to publish only in a single set of tiered journals, research results would be communicated to the most relevant audiences. For example, a case study of how IT enhanced the supply chain of a winery would be shared with wine industry stakeholders.

Phenomena-based advances in knowledge that contribute to local, regional and national development would be encouraged and valued. Contributors would thoughtfully select theories, methods and information sources to <sup>fi</sup>t their speci<sup>fi</sup>c research questions and context.

International information and knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows must continue. The cross-fertilization and mutual enrichment of different intellectual communities is imperative. International interactions and collaborations (including comparative studies) will also remain important. After all, many new theories and discoveries are widely applicable, especially if they are adapted to the host context rather than transferred directly from their native context. However, international knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows would have less depth with glocalization than with globalization. Not all <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclusions would be shared with the whole world, because they do not apply globally.

The advancement of universal theories, models and principles would be reoriented to studying their limitations and boundary conditions as well as the contexts beyond their boundaries. This would encourage the generation of complementary theories. For example, a new theory of knowledge sharing may be developed by starting from a Chinese perspective rather than a Western one (cf. Davison et al., 2013). Beyond the quests to advance theories, there would be efforts to develop contextual insights and understandings of IS phenomena.

Glocal IS research would aim to support more localized socio-economic development. For example, research of the internet would not only study its role in connecting the world but also investigate how it can encourage social cohesion and community engagement at local levels. The action design research project that investigated the limited adoption of e-Government in the wicked context of Bangladesh (Gregor et al., 2013) pro vides an example. It demonstrates that signi<sup>fi</sup>cant outcomes are possible despite limited resources.

## CONCLUSION

The modern world is not a global village. Instead, it resembles a set of globally connected local villages. We should not expect one size to <sup>fi</sup>t all. A single solution is unlikely to solve a problem throughout the world. What works in New York or Manchester will not necessarily work in New Delhi or Moscow.

A universal set of theories and best practices is unlikely. There are some globally dominant norms. However, differences in government policies, environmental conditions and societa norms require adaptation. Glocal research and education <sup>fi</sup>t this reality.

Research and education should balance global and local knowledge. If mentors and teachers focus only on local or regional issues and (re)solutions, then our protégés and students will be poorly prepared to compete in and contribute to an increasingly interconnected world. Conversely, a purely global perspective will lead to homogeneous knowledge. Professors and their disciples would be insensitive and ill-equipped to handle the speci<sup>fi</sup>c problems and issues that surround them. It also limits the opportunities to create and sustain competitive advantages.

Activities across the world may be increasingly interconnected, but organizations and their stakeholders are not monolithic, nor should they be. Glocal research and education would re-<sup>fl</sup>ect this reality. Theories and technologies along with information, insights, ideas and innovations would be shared widely. However, theories and technologies would be applied with sensitivity to the context.
