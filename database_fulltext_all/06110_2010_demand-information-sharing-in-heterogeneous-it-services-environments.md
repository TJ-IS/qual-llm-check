# Demand Information Sharing in Heterogeneous IT Services Environments

Sagni ka Sen , T.S. Raghu, and Ajay Vin ze

Abstr act : In an information technology services outsourcing arrangement, variance in demand volume and individual user preferences pose significant challenges to the provider organization in making resource allocation decisions. Such variations affect service levels, especially under fixed resource constraints. We explore the possible role of periodic demand information sharing and subsequent resource-level adjustments as a means of addressing issues arising from demand variation. As information exchange alters the dynamics of the relationship between the customer and provider organizations, incorporating information sharing in service-level agreements requires modifying current pricing schemes. A pricing heuristic is developed and tested under varying levels of information accuracy and granularity. The heuristic is shown to provide better economic welfare for both participants in comparison to the baseline pricing strategies considered. Also, it is shown that information, even at a coarse level of granularity, is very effective in providing stable service levels—a finding that is encouraging for enhanced collaborations between customer and provider organizations in outsourcing arrangements.

Key wor ds and phr as es : heterogeneous demand, information sharing, IT services, pricing.

Ov er t he past few years , the practice of information technology (IT) has evolved into a services orientation [1]. Various IT functions—for example, network, office automation, application services, and so forth—are now seen as “services” that can be subscribed from a third-party provider at prespecified levels of quality [42]. Infrastructure management outsourcing (IMO) is growing at a rate of 20 percent each year and the trend is predicted to continue in the near future [35]. Despite the appeal and such rapid emergence of service-oriented business models, cases of conflicts between customer and provider organizations are frequent [8, 15]. This is evidenced from a rising number of problematic deals in recent service outsourcing arrangements that have led to litigations and deal terminations. An in-depth analysis of several Fortune 500 organizations suggests poor demand management and its subsequent cost and performance implications to be the key contributors of tension between the customer and provider organizations.<sup>1</sup>

The context for this research is support and management of IT infrastructure services, where human resources serve requests arising from normal day-to-day operations of an organization. Usually service-level agreements (SLAs) for most IT service contracts guarantee a specified level of service for each service component (e.g., network uptime, trouble ticket resolution time) applicable for the entire organization. These agreements are augmented with penalty (reward) schemes for not meeting (exceeding) the prespecified service level. Achieving desired service levels in a cost-effective manner requires efficient resource allocation [17]. There are, however, two demandrelated issues that complicate this resource allocation process—variations in demand volume and variations in user preference. First, the user demand pattern for different services may be time varying in nature [26]. Second, variances in user preferences for service quality levels due to diverse functional and operational requirements within an organization exacerbate the complexity of the service environment [37].

Demand heterogeneity (variance in user quality requirement as well as the nonstationary nature of demand) has significant implications for both the customer and the service provider. Wide variance in user quality requirements necessitates service differentiation. For the provider, demand heterogeneity requires mechanism designs to match human resource levels to demand such that there is ample ability to provide customized service to different customer segments while optimizing resource utilization. As prespecification of price and quality levels at the organizational level provides little flexibility to effectively respond to user differences through prioritized resource allocation, it can be reasonably argued that SLA formulation should have provisions to adapt to both the nonstationary nature of demand and user preference variance.

In this paper, we address demand management issues in IT infrastructure services under nonstationary and nonhomogeneous demand patterns. Dynamic (volume-price adjustment) and priority (user-defined urgency of a service) pricing approaches have long been advocated as solutions to demand management issues [1, 2, 18, 32]. In these scenarios, however, the specific conditions under which customers and providers prefer dynamic over fixed price approaches and associated implications are not clear. While price and corresponding quality discrimination is a well-established approach in addressing user preference variance, demand fluctuation can be addressed in two ways—making periodic volume-price adaptations while resource levels are kept constant, or making periodic adjustment to resource levels according to future demand projection. Design of an effective resource allocation mechanism in a heterogeneous demand environment thus entails combining two aspects—price discrimination and either volume-price adaptation or resource-level adjustment.

In a recent study by Sen et al. [38], periodic volume-price adaptations at fixed resource levels has been investigated as a means to address demand heterogeneity issues in IT infrastructure services. Their study illustrates that even though a dynamic pricing scheme always yields socially superior results (in terms of economic welfare), individual organizations might exhibit conflicting preferences toward pricing structure. Such conflicts arise mainly from two factors—the level of heterogeneity in the system and, more importantly, the level of resource utilization. The observation that resource utilization is a key source of conflict in an IT service arrangement leads to significant implications toward sharing demand-related information between customer and provider organizations. Periodic exchange of demand information will enable providers to adjust their resource levels accordingly and achieve steady resource utilization, thus lessening the adverse effects of performance degradation during high-demand periods. Subsequently, the main objective of this paper is to investigate the effectiveness of demand information sharing as a possible mechanism in reducing conflict between organizations engaged in IT service outsourcing. We design and evaluate a dynamic resource adaptation mechanism under varying demand volume and user preference. An incentive-compatible priority pricing scheme based on periodic information exchange between the participants is proposed and tested to address demand heterogeneity issues.

The role of information sharing in interorganizational relationships has been investigated in many other contexts—for example, markets, supply chain literature, security informatics, and so forth. Because the focus of our research is on sharing demand-related information, we primarily build upon previous research in the supply chain management area. Literature in supply chain management, in general, advocates a truthful exchange of demand information across all levels in the supply chain to reduce the “bullwhip effect”—inefficiencies introduced via distorted information regarding demand pattern. Subsequently, a number of policies are in practice—such as electronic data interchange (EDI), vendor-managed inventory (VMI), and automatic replenishment (AR). The intent is to reduce inefficiencies by utilizing actual end demand information to facilitate resource allocation or capacity decisions throughout the members of a supply chain.

In the context of IT infrastructure services, these concepts directly translate to possible adjustment of resource levels based on a demand estimate by the customer. Updating the provider periodically with a future demand estimate will help it in setting resource levels dynamically and more accurately. Such adjustments will also benefit the customer, especially in the case of demand upswings, as there will be no performance degradation. Similar to supply chain contexts, sharing information changes the dynamics of service provisioning and requires the design of an appropriate mechanism so that individual organizational objectives are met. Consequently, demand forecast sharing requires design of appropriate mechanisms for sharing information and subsequent evaluation of its effectiveness in reducing conflicts. Periodic adjustment of resource levels according to demand forecast essentially implies that providers agree to maintain the same level of service for varying demand levels. The efficacy of the mechanism, however, would depend on the accuracy and granularity of the shared information. As such, the performance of the information sharing mechanism needs to be evaluated under varying conditions of information accuracy and granularity.

To summarize, investigating the role of demand information sharing in reducing conflicts between the participants of an IT infrastructure service requires answering two separate but related questions. The first step involves modifying the pricing strategies proposed by Sen et al. [38]—fixed and priority pricing—to incorporate implications of sharing information. A pricing heuristic is developed to address user preference variance and offer differentiated levels of service to different user populations. Subsequently, the impact of information accuracy and granularity on the performance of a pricing heuristic is investigated.

The contribution of our work is threefold. First, we develop insights onto how an information sharing mechanism can be integrated into a pricing mechanism. Second, we develop and validate a pricing heuristic that addresses demand heterogeneity issues. The effectiveness of this heuristic is compared against baseline cases, and is shown to improve both customer and provider welfare. Finally, it is shown that information, even at a coarse level of granularity, is very effective in providing stable service levels—a finding that is encouraging for enhanced collaborations between customer and provider organizations in outsourcing arrangements.

## Related Work

Ther e ar e t wo dist inct but int er elat ed as pects of resource allocation in a heterogeneous demand environment with varying user preferences and demand volume. An effective way to address user preference variance is a tiered pricing approach offering differentiated service levels targeted at distinct user populations [33, 43]. The timevarying nature of demand, however, adds another level of complexity to the process of resource allocation. Volume fluctuation affects service performance, and has been addressed in the IT literature through volume-price adjustments when the resource level is fixed [18, 23, 38]. Interestingly, information sharing in supply chain research has explored periodic resource-level adjustments based on demand forecast as a possible approach to addressing demand uncertainty [5, 7]. A straightforward application of these approaches in an IT context is, however, difficult for several reasons. Demand information sharing in the supply chain literature is product oriented and deals with a single product and its related capacity decisions. In an IT service context such as the current one, usually more than one type of service is provided simultaneously through shared resources. Moreover, there exist multiple user populations with varying quality preferences for the same service type.

Segmentation via differential pricing has long been proposed as a useful strategy to address heterogeneity in customer requirements. While some service providers offer different service grades to different customer organizations, but not to users within the same organization, it is likely that the dual pressure of customer satisfaction and resource cost optimization will eventually replace the “one size fits all” SLAs. The need for appropriate pricing models as a mechanism for balancing the resource–quality trade-off has been recognized as being imminent [3]. Early information systems (IS) research on differential pricing has primarily focused on Internet-related services [4, 18, 39]. More recently, price and resource allocation–related research has taken place in different IT service contexts [2, 22, 30]. A priority pricing mechanism for adaptive resource allocation for computing resources under fixed resource constraints is offered in Gupta et al. [17]. The DifferServe architecture in Blake et al. [4] is based on the idea of classifying Internet traffic (packets) into different behavior aggregates based on diverse quality of service requirements. Multiple quality guarantee and corresponding prices in the telecommunication services context has been developed in Keon and Anandalingam [22] where application type (e.g., voice, data) determines user classes and resources are not shared among the different service classes. A similar concept is employed in Maglaras and Zeevi [30] where level of service is divided into two categories—guaranteed and best effort. For broadband Internet services, multiple performance guarantees in conjunction with a penalty for failure to meet performance level has been shown to be superior to standard uniform pricing approaches [2]. Although the context and setup for these research models vary, they demonstrate the effectiveness of customer segmentation and provide conceptual support to our model in the user differentiation aspect.

One of the earliest works in pricing and congestion control issues in service facilities characterized by customer queues brought about the notion of delay costs in pricing computer services [31]. This notion of delay cost is also implemented in Gupta et al. [17], and its subsequent extension to a real-time database context [23], where delaysensitive users’ requests (queries) are serviced in accordance with their individual preferences. Our research model, being close in setting to Gupta et al. [17] and Konana et al. [23], uses delay cost as an indicator of user quality preference. Hence, different user populations within the customer organization are associated with different delay cost distributions.

Utilizing demand information in capacity decisions has been investigated primarily in the supply chain management area. Industry initiatives such as EDI, VMI, and AR arose from the intent to reduce the inefficiencies (i.e., bullwhip effect) by utilizing actual end demand information to facilitate resource allocation or capacity decisions throughout the members of a supply chain. Researchers in this area have underscored the value of information in increasing supply chain performance. Supply chain management literature has proposed two distinct approaches in regard to information sharing. One stream of research [6, 13, 24] has focused on quantifying the value of information and its subsequent utilization in improving supply chain performance. A second stream of research [7, 9, 25] is concerned with designing coordination mechanisms in the presence of possible opportunistic behavior arising from asymmetric information possessed by different members in a supply chain. A main assumption in these models, however, is single-period contact between the upstream and downstream members. IT service contracts, on the other hand, are mainly geared for building long-term relationships between the provider and customer organizations. Literature in both supply chain and game theory has illustrated the significant role of trust in repeated forecast-sharing games to enhance long-term relations [11, 27]. Accordingly, our model focuses on the use of information with no intentional inaccuracy, and we borrow from the supply chain literature implications of sharing demand information and how pricing strategies should be modified in the presence of such information exchange.

## Research Model

The pr icing s chemes dev eloped in t his paper are applicable to scenarios where service requests arise from normal day-to-day operations within an organization. Specifically, the setting is for support and management of IT infrastructure services such as software installation request, individual computer configuration requests, and troubleshooting. Customer requests for those services are placed in a queue and are served in the order they arrive (traditional fixed price scheme) or according to the user’s perceived level of urgency (proposed priority-based scheme). In both schemes, the customer organization provides future demand estimates to the provider on a periodic basis and resources are adjusted accordingly.<sup>2</sup> The provider’s cost for servicing a request is proportional to the time a resource spends on servicing the request, and unutilized resources are not considered. This is a reasonable assumption since in many service settings providers are able to use any idle time of the resources in servicing other customer organizations. We start with a baseline fixed price scheme in a single-service context and extend it to a multiple-service context (following Sen et al. [38]). Comparisons are provided with the baseline fixed price scheme as well as with no information sharing scenarios.

In the fixed price scheme, the provider states the price and quality level for each of the services rendered. The quality guarantee for a particular service pertains to the entire organization. Since the fixed price single quality guarantee SLAs are the current practice, this serves as the basis of deriving resource requirements and is used in both fixed and priority pricing settings. In the priority pricing scheme, the providers offer a menu of service-level and corresponding prices. Users from the customer organization select the service level according to their individual preferences.

To maintain analytical tractability, we make a few simplifying assumptions. We assume a Poisson arrival process for service requests and an exponential service time (i.e., M/M/n queue discipline, a standard assumption in queuing models). We make no distinction between the human resources in terms of their skills to serve a request. In other words, we assume that each of the servers can serve any request equally efficiently. Also, the requests do not require any intermediate step to be taken by different resources; one request is served by one human resource.

## Resource Requirements

As mentioned briefly before, the fixed price model serves as the basis for determining resources. For an agreed-upon price, the provider’s aim is to maximize profit subject to meeting the expected service level promised to the customer. That is, the probability of serving each request within the maximum allowable time limit must be greater than or equal to the target specified in the SLA. Formally,

$$
p (t <   T) \geq L.\tag{1}
$$

The probability density function of the total waiting time for an M/M/n queue is as given by Gross and Harris [16]:

$$
w (t) = \frac {\mu e ^ {- \mu t} (\lambda - n \mu + W _ {q} (0)) - (1 - W _ {q} (0)) (\lambda - n \mu) \mu e ^ {- (n \mu - \lambda) t}}{(\lambda - (n - 1) \mu)}.\tag{2}
$$

For an optimal or near optimal design, the probability that a customer has zero waiting time is very small. Hence, the term $W _ { q } ( 0 )$ is ignored in w(t).Therefore,

$$
p (t <   T) = \int_ {0} ^ {T} w (t) d t = \frac {\mu (\lambda - n \mu)}{(\lambda - (n - 1) \mu)} \left[ \frac {1}{(n \mu - \lambda)} \left(e ^ {- (n \mu - \lambda) T} - 1\right) - \frac {1}{\mu} \left(e ^ {- \mu T} - 1\right) \right].\tag{3}
$$

When arrival and service rates are known, the probability value depends only on $n ,$ the resource level. Because the average service rate $\mu$ is known to the provider for a particular type of service, the provider aims to optimally choose $n$ based on the demand estimate provided by the customer for each period. Hence, Equation (1) can be rewritten as

$$
L = \frac {\mu (\lambda - n \mu)}{(\lambda - (n - 1) \mu)} \left[ \frac {1}{(n \mu - \lambda)} \left(e ^ {- (n \mu - \lambda) T} - 1\right) - \frac {1}{\mu} \left(e ^ {- \mu T} - 1\right) \right].\tag{4}
$$

The solution of Equation (4) provides us the value of $n ,$ the required number of resources in the fixed price scheme. To keep the comparisons meaningful, the priority pricing scheme also uses the same level of resources.

## Developing a Mechanism for Sharing Demand Information

The task of designing appropriate mechanisms to share information in the fixed and priority pricing schemes essentially translates to arriving at a consensus on servicelevel expectations, prices (for both pricing schemes), penalties for missed service levels (applicable to the provider), and penalties for misinformation (applicable to the customer). As mentioned previously, these aspects are addressed utilizing theory and practice in the supply chain area.

As the premise of our research is facilitating long-term relationships between the customer and providers of IT services arrangements, we do not consider any intentional gaming behavior by the customer and provider organizations. The demand estimate may, however, be inaccurate, mainly because of two reasons. First, forecasting demand for each service type and user profile is not costless for the customer organization. Hence, inaccuracies in information may arise from the customer organization’s attempt to minimize this cost. Second, due to the stochastic nature of the service system, it is often not possible to have a completely accurate demand forecast.

Given shared information on the demand estimate in each period, the provider’s resource allocation in each of those periods is verifiable information upon the realization of actual demand. Seen another way, as the provider has agreed to provide stable service levels upon receiving demand information, there is no incentive for the provider to employ less than necessary resources, and hence provide substandard service.

Based on the information sharing procedure discussed above, essential aspects for modeling the fixed and priority pricing schemes are now formalized.

## Fixed Price Scheme with Information Sharing

The fixed price scheme guarantees a single level of service for each type of service provided. The pricing for each service is based on usage. This is in accordance to the literature on supply chains where per unit prices are charged upon the realization of actual demand [7] (most software licensing models also follow the same practice, including the new software-as-a-service [SaaS] models; even domestic broadband providers are currently exploring such models in the United States [14]). Since information exchange enables the provider to adjust resource levels according to demand, a pay-per-use scheme is more justifiable to the customer as there is no longer the need for providing risk insulation from demand swings by agreeing to a monthly/quarterly/ yearly subscription price based on average demand forecasts.

Given the above pricing scheme, because the customer organization provides the demand estimate, risks arising from incorrect information would be borne by it. In other words, the customer organization compensates the provider for any over/underestimate of demand. Similar practices are observed/suggested to achieve coordination in supply chains; for example, the retailer agreeing to pay a fee up front to the manufacturer to reserve capacity [12], options contracts [7], allocation of over/undercapacity costs to the member responsible for supply chain coordination [10].

In the present context, overestimation of demand results in providers’ employing more than necessary number of (human) resources for the upcoming period. If these resources are entirely dedicated to the customer organization, the latter should pay for the extra resources. However, because in our model only the cost of utilized resources is incorporated in the provider’s profit function (under the assumption that the provider may utilize any unused portion of its resources), overcommitment of resources will not be reflected in the provider’s welfare. Consequently, no penalty schemes are designed for overestimated demand. Underestimated demand, on the other hand, may result in a lower level of resource allocation by the provider, subsequent service degradation, and possibly missing the agreed-upon service level. As argued previously, because the customer organization should bear the risk of estimation error, providers would not be penalized for missing service levels due to inaccurate information. That is, the expected level of service is evaluated ex post<sup>3</sup> when actual demand information is available. The provider would be penalized if and only if it did not achieve this expectation given the demand estimate by the customer.<sup>4</sup>

## Priority Pricing Scheme with Information Sharing

The objective of the priority pricing scheme is to provide differentiated and prioritized service to different user populations in the customer organization based on their delay sensitivities. In other words, in the priority pricing scheme, the provider offers as many levels of service grades as the number of distinct user populations in the customer organization and guarantees to maintain the service levels for each user population (given the accuracy of demand information). Because the objective of the information sharing mechanism is to utilize demand information to alleviate the effect of congestion, prices are a function of the distribution (i.e., relative volume) of users among the priority classes. As such, with a perfect demand estimate, a user’s choice of priority class depends only on his or her valuation of the service (i.e., delay sensitivity), and he or she receives the same level of service irrespective of the total number of users in the system.

There can be two levels of inaccuracies in demand forecast. First, total demand may be over/underestimated as in the fixed pricing scenario. Allocation of risk arising from such inaccuracies follows a similar rationale as in the fixed pricing scheme. Hence, similar schemes for over- and underestimate are applicable here, too. The second level of inaccuracy may arise in the granularity of the demand estimate; even though the overall demand estimate is correct, demand distribution among different user populations may be wrong. The implications for such inaccuracy cannot be easily predicted (this is discussed in more detail later), thus necessitating a computational approach.

## Pricing Schemes

## Fixed Price Model

As discussed above, the fixed pricing model charges the same price $( S _ { F P / R e q } )$ for a particular service (say, video) to all users for each individual request. Requests are served on a first-come, first-served basis in an M/M/n queue. At the beginning of each period, resource levels (n) are set according to Equation (1) based on the customer’s demand estimate.

## Priority Pricing Model

We denote the IT services by a set $( j = 1 , 2 , . . . , J )$ . Different service types ( j ) are associated with varying levels of complexity (e.g., a software installation request could be less complex and less time-consuming compared to a configuration management request). The processing time for a particular service $( 1 / \mu _ { _ j } )$ is a characteristic of the service; more complex services require higher processing times. To accommodate these differences in user preferences, the proposed priority scheme allows users<sup>5</sup> to choose a specific priority level for the request. The provider’s performance is evaluated based on the context (user-assigned priority) of the request. Such prioritization of requests would require price differentiation among the priority classes; in the absence of such differentiation, all users would opt for the highest priority. As such, the price a user pays for a particular request is based on the service type and the economic significance of the request to the user. The latter is captured by offering the user a menu of price priority choices $( k = 1 , 2 , . . . , K )$ for any service request.<sup>6</sup>

Literature in queuing theory has demonstrated the negative externality costs [20, 31, 34] that customers waiting in a queue impose on other customers. Each arriving customer incurs a cost per unit time until the customer’s request is fulfilled. The cost is a function of the perceived urgency of the request to the user at the time of placing the request. The delay cost/time $( \delta _ { _ { i j } } )$ parameter models demand heterogeneity arising from user preference variance. As the value of the delay cost/time varies from one user to another, it provides the rationale for users to choose between priority classes. Although delay cost information is known only to the user, who has no incentive to truthfully reveal this information, it is argued that delay costs can be estimated by observing the user’s past choice behavior [23]. This approach has also been advocated in the context of estimating Internet users’ value of time in Gupta et al. [19].

For each service type ( j ), deriving the prices $( S _ { k } )$ for each individual priority class (given the demand estimates ${ \lambda _ { k } } ^ { 7 }$ provided by the customer) is essentially an optimization problem where providers set the prices to maximize their profits, and the price in a priority class equals the reservation price for an average user in the user population corresponding to the priority class. Formally the problem can be stated as

$$
\operatorname{Max} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(S _ {k} - \frac {C}{\mu}\right) \lambda_ {k}\tag{5}
$$

such that

$$
V _ {k} = S _ {k} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {k} \overline {{\omega}} _ {k} \quad \forall k.\tag{6}
$$

Equation (5) represents the provider’s profit from the service arrangement. The righthand side of Equation (6) represents the average cost of a user in profile k (= price paid + the user’s valuation of the time it took to serve the request). An analytical solution of this optimization problem, though, faces two difficulties. First, for a multiple-service scenario, the characteristics of our particular queuing system are multiclass, multiserver queues with nonpreemptive priorities. Second, charging each distinct user population according to their reservation prices requires knowledge of specific demand function of each of these populations.

Although the queuing theory literature is rich and varied, research on priority queues is relatively sparse because of the inherent complexity and lack of analytical tractability in some situations. A few notable contributions include Jewkes and Stanford [21], Sleptchenko [40], Stanford [41], and Wierman et al. [44]. A key assumption in these studies is that priorities are reflective of job class characteristics. In our research, because users select priorities based on private valuation of delay cost at the time of service request, waiting time estimates from these models cannot be used directly. The service rate in a particular priority class is a complex function of the relative proportion of different service types in that priority class and all other higher-priority classes, thus making a closed-form solution infeasible. Relaxation of the assumption of a stationary demand pattern adds another layer of complexity. Given that closedform solutions for waiting times in the multiple-service setting is not feasible (even under stationary demand patterns), we would need approximations.

Because second-degree price discrimination is not feasible, we used fixed prices as a proxy for the user demand function. The complexity of the waiting time expression and the unavailability of the user demand function render a closed-form analytical expression infeasible and lead us to develop a heuristic as an alternative. Development of this heuristic is based on the fixed service price as well as guided by theory. The fixed service price is chosen as a baseline as this is a more commonly accepted practice. As such, this price can be used as a proxy for the customer organization’s average valuation of the service. Use of the fixed price as a baseline to set prices is an easy first step from both provider and customer perspectives.

Therefore, the heuristic proposes that the price in priority class 2 (for the user population having the medium delay cost/time) is set in such a way that users in this class incur the same overall cost as an average user in the fixed price case.<sup>8</sup> Formally,

$$
S _ {2} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {2} \overline {{w}} _ {2} = S _ {F P / R e q} + \overline {{\delta}} \overline {{w}},\tag{7}
$$

where δ is the weighted average of the delay cost $= \Sigma _ { _ k } ( \lambda _ { _ k } / \Lambda ) \delta _ { _ k } ;$ w is the mean waiting time of an M/M/n queue (no priority) with the total arrival rate $( \Lambda = \Sigma _ { k } \lambda _ { k } )$ into the system.

Once the price in priority class 2 is set, incentive compatibility conditions can be used in setting prices for the other two priority classes. The incentive compatibility conditions in the current context imply that each user’s choice of priority class is a truthful revelation of his or her delay sensitivity. This condition is satisfied when the user incurs the lowest overall cost in the priority class that corresponds to the profile he or she belongs to, and hence is not induced to select a lower priority class to pay lower prices. In a related service context, similar treatments can be found in Maglaras and Zeevi [29]. Formally,

$$
S _ {1} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {1} \overline {{w}} _ {1} <   S _ {2} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {1} \overline {{w}} _ {2}\tag{8}
$$

$$
S _ {2} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {2} \overline {{w}} _ {2} <   S _ {3} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {2} \overline {{w}} _ {3}.\tag{9}
$$

Equation (8) implies that a user belonging to the highest delay cost/time profile (profile 1) incurs a higher total cost when he or she chooses the next lower priority class (class 2), and thus ensures that users coming from this profile choose the appropriate priority class. Equation (9) has similar implications for users in profile 2 not choosing priority 3. It must be noted that similar conditions can be constructed for any two priority classes. However, those conditions are automatically fulfilled once these two hold (this is tested during the simulation experiments as well). Solving Equations (7), (8), and (9) for each service ( j ), we obtain the prices $( S _ { j k } )$ for each service in each priority class:

$$
\begin{array}{c} S _ {j 2} = S _ {F P / R e q _ {j}} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {j} \overline {{w}} _ {j} - \overline {{\delta}} _ {j 2} \overline {{w}} _ {j 2} \\ S _ {j 1} = S _ {j 2} + \overline {{\delta}} _ {j 1} \left(\overline {{w}} _ {j 2} - \overline {{w}} _ {j 1}\right) \\ S _ {j 3} = S _ {j 2} - \overline {{\delta}} _ {j 2} \left(\overline {{w}} _ {j 3} - \overline {{w}} _ {j 2}\right). \end{array}
$$

As the priority pricing scheme is based on a heuristic rather than a closed-form analytical solution, it is now essential to test the quality of the heuristic developed in addition to testing the efficacy of the priority pricing scheme in contrast to the fixed price scheme. The heuristic will be considered good if it yields at least as good as (or better) welfare (customer, provider, and system-wise) as the fixed price scheme given the same demand estimate. In the next two sections, the design of the computational experiments, their results, and implications are discussed.

## Provider and Customer Welfare in the Dynamic and Priority Pricing Models

The effectiveness of the proposed dynamic priority pricing scheme in comparison to the traditional fixed pricing scheme is measured in terms of the economic welfare of the customer and the provider organizations. Welfare expressions for the multipleservice scenario are obtained by summing over all the services.

## Provider Welfare

Provider welfare in the dynamic as well as the fixed price scheme is modeled as the total profit from the service arrangement. In both pricing schemes, $C / \mu$ represents the average cost of servicing a request. For the fixed price scheme, the revenue from a request is simply the subscription price; for the priority pricing scheme, the revenue equals the service price in each priority class times the actual demand in that priority class. Hence, the provider welfare expressions in the fixed and dynamic price settings are, respectively,

$$
W _ {p _ {F P}} = \left(S _ {F P / R e q} - (C / \mu)\right) \lambda\tag{10a}
$$

$$
W _ {p _ {\text { Priority }}} (S, \lambda , \omega) = \sum_ {k} S _ {k} \lambda_ {k} - (C / \mu) \sum_ {k} \lambda_ {k}, \text {   where   } \lambda = \sum_ {k} \lambda_ {k}.\tag{10b}
$$

## Customer Welfare

Customer welfare in both schemes is modeled as the cost incurred by the users through payments for services and expected value of individual delay cost (δ) spent in waiting for service delivery. The total cost incurred by all users in the system is given by ld |w\ (w\ is the mean waiting time). Hence, the customer welfare expressions in the fixed price and dynamic price scenarios are, respectively,

$$
W _ {p _ {F P}} = - \Big (S _ {F P / R e q} + \delta \overline {{\omega}} \Big) \lambda\tag{11a}
$$

and

$$
W _ {c u s t _ {P r i o r i t y}} = - \sum_ {k} \left(S _ {k} + \delta_ {k} \bar {\omega} _ {k}\right) \lambda_ {k}, \text {   where   } \lambda = \sum_ {k} \lambda_ {k}.\tag{11b}
$$

## Mean Waiting Time

Mean waiting time $( \bar { \mathbf { \omega } } _ { j k } )$ estimates are required to compute the values for $S _ { j k }$ . For a single service (J = 1) queue, this waiting time in priority class k is as given by Gross and Harris [16]:

$$
\Rightarrow \overline {{\omega}} _ {k} = \frac {1}{\left(1 - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \alpha_ {i}\right) \left(1 - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k - 1} \alpha_ {i}\right)},\tag{12}
$$

where

$$
\alpha_ {I} = \frac {\lambda_ {I}}{n \mu}, \bar {\omega} _ {0} = \frac {P _ {0} \left(\frac {\lambda}{\mu}\right) ^ {n}}{n ! \left(1 - \frac {\lambda}{n \mu}\right) n \mu}, P _ {0} \left[ \sum_ {l = 0} ^ {n - 1} \frac {1}{l !} \left(\frac {\lambda}{\mu}\right) ^ {l} + \frac {1}{n !} \left(\frac {\lambda}{\mu}\right) ^ {n} \frac {n \mu}{n \mu - \lambda} \right] ^ {- 1}, \lambda = \sum_ {k} \lambda_ {k}.
$$

A key assumption in priority queue models is that priorities are reflective of job class characteristics [21, 40, 44]. In this research, because users select priorities based on private valuation of delay cost at the time of service request, waiting time estimates from these models cannot be used directly. In our system, service rate in a particular priority class is a complex function of the relative proportion of different service types in that priority class and all other higher priority classes; thus a closed-form solution is infeasible. At this stage, the mean waiting time in a single-service setting is given by Equation (12) under assumptions of a stationary demand pattern. Mean waiting time estimation is necessary to get a closed-form solution for prices. Given that a closed-form solution for $\bar { \mathbf { \omega } } _ { j k }$ in the multiple-service setting is not feasible (even under stationary demand patterns), we would need approximations. The approximations can affect the effectiveness of the dynamic scheme in an IT services setting. We develop a simulation setting that is based on the analytical approach outlined in this section to further investigate the performance of the dynamic pricing approach under nonstationary demand scenarios.

## Computational Experiments and Results

A comput at ional appr oach is adopt ed to address the research issues outlined at the beginning of the paper. Because the main objective is to study the effectiveness of the proposed mechanism (fixed and priority pricing with information sharing), conducting simulation experiments enables us to study system performance under various parametric combinations that would not have been possible otherwise. The simulation study is based on data from an outsourcing arrangement between a prominent service provider and a large public organization. This outsourcing arrangement in turn conforms to the general SLA specification guidelines of the industry. The data pertain to a three-year performance record of the provider available from help desk logs. This log provides detailed information about the request start and closure time, resource assignment, steps taken to resolve a request, and user-assigned urgency for a number of services. These data provided the basis for selecting a range of arrival and service rates in our simulation setup. Price parameters in the study are based on price information from this same contract. From the service-related data set, we chose three services—password reset, desktop upgrades, and video and telecom services—as representative services with varying demand and quality requirements. The service rates and arrival patterns of these services were used as the basis for simulation experiments. Furthermore, to enable comparisons, we keep the same settings (in terms of arrival rate, service rate) as in Sen et al. [38] to make meaningful comparisons of the information sharing mechanisms with the corresponding no information sharing scenarios.

## Experimental Factors

The design of the simulation experiments is guided by the research motivations posed at the beginning of the paper. In addition, it is now necessary to test for the quality of the proposed heuristic. The research questions are summarized below:

RQ1: When demand information is shared, is the priority pricing heuristic an effective tool for allocating resources?

RQ2: Does sharing demand information reduce conflicts in preference in provider and customer organizations as observed in Sen et al. [38]?

RQ3: If information sharing and priority pricing heuristics do reduce preference conflict, what is the effect of the information accuracy and granularity on the effectiveness of such mechanism?

Addressing the second question requires that the pricing mechanism with information sharing is implemented under the same system settings (arrival and service rates) as in Sen et al. [38]. We observed that under nonstationary demand, all four service scenarios (75 percent, 80 percent, 85 percent, and 90 percent utilization rates representing varying levels of congestion in the system) resulted in conflicting choice in a single-service setting. Arrival rates from the two extreme cases from the single-service scenario were chosen, where the amount of conflict was at its lowest (75 percent utilization) and highest (90 percent). In a multiple-service setting, two lower utilization rates (75 percent and 80 percent) resulted in conflict, and arrivals rates for both data sets were chosen. If the simulation experiments show that sharing demand-related information is able to reconcile (or at least reduce) the welfare differences, then it establishes the case for using the mechanism proposed in the current paper as a better resource allocation tool under nonstationary demand and multiple-user profiles.

As discussed previously, even in the absence of the intent to behave opportunistically, it is difficult for the customer organization to provide an accurate demand forecast. Given that there will always be a certain level of inaccuracy in the demand forecast, it is essential to test for the performance of the proposed information sharing mechanism (as reflected in welfare and waiting time values) with various levels of accuracy. Three levels of information accuracy are used—high, medium, and low, where the demand forecast is within 5 percent, 15 percent, and 30 percent of the actual demand, respectively.

Granularity in the present context refers to the demand estimate broken down to the level of each individual user profile (applicable to the priority pricing scheme only). As the customer bears the risk of estimation error, over/underestimation of the overall demand has a straightforward implication that the customer is going to suffer, especially in terms of increased delay costs when demand is underestimated. However, in the priority pricing scheme, there is an externality effect on the prices and waiting times among the different user profiles. A wrong estimate in the demand distribution among the three priority classes affects the price calculation and may lead to setting up prices that induce the users to choose inappropriate priority classes. In this case, it is difficult to predict a priori how customers and providers will be affected with such inaccuracies. Consequently, the simulation experiments investigate the efficacy of the proposed pricing mechanisms under an inaccurate demand distribution estimate, keeping the overall estimate accurate. This enables separating the effect of inaccuracies in overall demand (predictable effect) and the inaccuracies in predicting the relative amount of arrivals from each user profile (unpredictable effect).

When the overall demand estimate is accurate, three estimation error scenarios are possible—arrivals in any one of the user profiles are overestimated, leading to underestimation in the other two profiles. These scenarios are referred as Profile102U3U, Profile2O1U3U, and Profile3O1U2U $( ^ { 6 6 } \mathrm { O } ^ { 5 } )$ stands for overestimate and “U” stands for underestimate) in the experiments. All three scenarios are implemented under all levels of information accuracy. In order to generate the inaccurate demand forecast within the priority classes, actual aggregate demand from Sen et al. [38] is used. Arrivals in the priority class for which accuracies are to be introduced are then inflated by using a uniform random sample appropriate for the level of inaccuracy. Because the overall demand is to be kept accurate, demands in the other priority classes are accordingly reduced.

## Results and Discussions

This section provides results of the computational experiments. Overall, system welfare values in both fixed and dynamic/priority pricing schemes indicate that sharing demand-related information improves welfare. The magnitude of this improvement is, though, more pronounced for the customer compared to the provider. Because there are no congestion effects and subsequent service degradation, the customers are better off when resource levels are adjusted based on the demand forecast, even if there may be some inaccuracies in the demand forecast. Providers, on the other hand, benefit more in low-congestion scenarios because they are no longer able to extract the congestion rents during high-traffic periods.

## Quality of the Priority Pricing Heuristic

Table 4 presents welfare values in the fixed and priority pricing schemes, both under accurate information. As argued above, the priority pricing heuristic would be considered good if it leads to at least as good as or better welfare values in comparison to the fixed price scheme. This is indeed observed (Table 4) (because the customer welfare represents costs, it is represented as negative values). In the single-service scenario, both customer and system welfare are significantly higher in the priority pricing scheme; provider welfare though appears to be slightly lower—the difference is so small that it can be considered inconsequential for all practical purposes. In a multiple-service scenario, however, the performance of the heuristic is not as good for the providers. While customer and system welfare are still significantly higher in the priority pricing scheme, providers seem to be better off in the fixed pricing scheme. The risk of inaccuracies in demand estimate is borne by the provider. Even though demand may be accurate at an aggregate level, there may be inaccuracies as to the relative proportion of service requests in each priority class. Having multiple service types introduces greater uncertainty as resources are shared among the services, making the fixed price more attractive for the providers. Despite this, we observe that provider welfare improves significantly from the no information sharing scenarios, thus leading to reduced conflicts as discussed in the next section.

Role of Information Sharing in Reducing Conflicts

Overall, the results demonstrate that sharing demand information closes/reduces the welfare gap as observed in Sen et al. [38].

Single Service. Sharing demand information, as seen from the current results (presented as percent changes from the fixed price scheme in Table 5), has a significant positive effect on the customer organization. Customers are now slightly better off/indifferent in the priority pricing scheme compared to the fixed pricing scheme. Customers are now better off where previously minimum conflict was observed (data set 1) and they are now indifferent where previously maximum conflict was observed (data set 2). Providers are slightly worse off/indifferent in the priority pricing strategy (at all levels of information accuracy and granularity). Providers are now worse off where previously minimum conflict was observed (data set 1) and they are now indifferent where previously maximum conflict was observed (data set 2). From a system welfare perspective, the priority pricing scheme is significantly better statistically, although from a practical standpoint, they would probably be seen as being equivalent (differences are < 0.3 percent). These results are particularly encouraging as they demonstrate that the proposed information sharing and associated pricing mechanism benefit customer organizations even with a lesser degree of heterogeneity (in terms of user profiles, service characteristics, etc). In Sen et al. [38], the customer organization preferred the fixed pricing scheme in single-service settings. This is attributed to the fact that the effect of congestion outweighed the benefit of differentiation (especially since lowest-priority users suffered long delays) as the user population was less diverse. Information sharing, thus, has altered the preference by eliminating the source of conflict between the participants.

Multiple Services. In the multiple-services setting, the conflict of choice, as observed in Sen et al. [38], arises due to the fact that the provider is able to extract the benefit from user segmentation only at higher congestion levels. Sharing demand information reduces the welfare gap compared to the no information sharing scenario. Compared to the single-service setting, however, this pricing mechanism is not as effective in changing the provider’s choice (presented as percent changes from fixed price scheme in Table 6) as they still seem to be better off in the fixed price scheme, although their welfare difference reduces considerably from no information sharing. Customers are better off with the priority pricing scheme when information sharing is practiced.

System welfare improves significantly with the use of the priority pricing scheme. An implication of this observation is that subsidy payments may be utilized to redistribute the benefits accruing from the priority scheme. While sharing information reduces the provider’s welfare gap, subsidy payments from the customers may make them indifferent between the two pricing alternatives. Because the system welfare is higher in the priority scheme, customers will still remain better off even after sharing some of the surplus.

## Effect of Information Accuracy and Granularity

The effect of information accuracy is evaluated by the deviation of provider, customer, and system welfare at varying levels of information accuracy from the accurate information scenario. These are presented as percent changes from priority price with accurate information in Tables 7 and 8. Within the same level of granularity, different levels of information accuracy seem to have little or no effect on the welfare values. Even when the differences are statistically significant, they are practically inconsequential. Granularity of information affects the welfare values of both the customer and the provider (in opposing directions), the magnitude of this effect being greater under the multiple-service setting. Interestingly, the direction in which the welfare values are affected depends on which user profile is overestimated (Figure 2). Overestimating arrivals from the highest/lowest delay cost profile yields higher welfare values to the provider (and hence lower welfare values for the customer). Overestimating arrivals from the medium delay cost profile has precisely the opposite effect. This can be explained as follows. Overestimating arrivals from the highest delay cost profile (i.e., overestimating the number of users potentially choosing priority class 1) leads to higher waiting time estimates, and consequently higher prices. Overestimating arrivals from the lowest delay cost profile (i.e., overestimating number of users potentially choosing priority class 3) essentially leads to charging lower prices. However, as the users are provided actual waiting time estimates, the system self-corrects in the sense that users choose the appropriate priority classes as the actual waiting times are higher than estimated. Consequently, customers suffer from increased delay costs. Overestimating demand in the medium priority level has a different implication altogether. In the heuristic, the medium profile is the anchor such that users belonging to this profile are kept at the same cost level as in fixed pricing. Evidently, overestimation of the number of users in this profile implies loss of opportunity to charge prices close to the reservation price of each individual user profile, leading to lost revenues. The customer benefits at the provider’s expense.

While the results demonstrate that conflicts arising from opposing effects of resource utilization can be reduced by periodically sharing demand information and subsequent resource adjustments, the trade-offs and incentives for the customer and provider organizations must also be discussed. From the perspective of the customer organization, the major commitments are agreeing on monitoring demand patterns and subsequently making the information available to the providers on a periodic basis. In exchange, customers agree on differentiated price as a function of relative demand distribution. Interestingly, information sharing is valuable even when it is not perfect. Even when the demand information is accurate only at a coarse level of granularity, the customer organization would still get better service levels than with traditional fixed price schemes. Therefore, efficiency can be achieved at a relatively low cost.

For the providers, the trade-off comes in terms of the costs associated with periodic resource adjustments. There are costs associated with both ramping up resource levels (when demand increases) as well as reassigning a resource to other services (when demand from a particular customer organization goes down). The benefit for the provider in agreeing to such resource adjustments is that this results in increased welfare, especially as demand variability goes up. As long as the provider’s cost of resource adjustments does not exceed a certain percentage of the total operating cost, the providers benefit from information sharing. This is especially encouraging for the providers as cost considerations for such resource adjustments can be accommodated as part of the negotiations with the customers, and information sharing can still benefit the two entities.

We conducted an additional set of simulation experiments to test the robustness of our results. The first set of simulations was conducted under increased demand fluctuations (we generated another set of data with the same mean, but with 30 percent more variance) and the second set of simulations was conducted with additional scenarios in estimation errors (the new profiles included 10203U, 102U30, and 1U2030). With both sets, our original findings do not change. Moreover, sensitivity analysis results indicate that information sharing becomes more beneficial with increasing demand uncertainty. The results with the new profile sets did not change the preference profiles observed in the main results.

At this point, limitations of the current research must be noted. From a domain perspective, this research is applicable to only those types of services where timeliness is the major performance measure. While these services constitute a large portion of IT outsourcing arrangements currently offered in the marketplace, they do not encompass the entire spectrum of IT services. From a methodological standpoint, the use of estimation errors as percentage values may have skewed the results somewhat for the medium delay cost case. In our model, a majority of the users belong to this profile. Hence, a 5 percent (or 15 percent or 30 percent) overestimate in this profile introduces a larger noise magnitude than an overestimate in the other two user profiles.

## Summary and Conclusion

In t his paper , we explor e t he v alue of s har ing demand information as a resource allocation mechanism for IT infrastructure services. In a prior study, a dynamic priority pricing scheme was proposed as a means to address heterogeneous demand arising from (1) a presence of users with varying quality preference and (2) a change in overall demand volume due to exogenous factors. This mechanism, while shown to be superior to the current practice of fixed price single quality guarantee SLAs, also brought to light the presence of a considerable amount of conflict of interest among the provider and the customer. Following up on the findings from this study, our current research explores the possible role of periodically exchanging demand information in reducing the conflict between the provider and the customer. Based on previous scholarly work, a priority pricing scheme that is incentive compatible with user’s delay costs (i.e., individual valuation for a particular service) is developed and subsequently evaluated. This priority pricing scheme is shown to be an effective mechanism for sharing of demand information.

Findings from this research bear significant implications to the current practice of IT service provisioning. While the trade press and practitioner literature are rife with frequent citations about cases of friction between outsourcing participants and advocate collaboration as a solution, guidelines toward achieving this goal are sparse In this regard, our research proposes ways to enhance SLAs. Extant literature on interorganizational information sharing identifies the perceived costs and benefits of sharing information to be an important motivator for firms to engage in such relationships [28]. Key implications from our research suggest that in an IT services context characterized by heterogeneous demand, sharing demand-related information on a periodic basis reduces inherent conflicts between the customer and provider organizations that may arise due to over/underutilization of resources in nonstationary demand situations. As long as the overall demand estimate is accurate, estimation error in predicting demand distribution among different user profiles has little or no effect on the welfare of customer and provider organizations.

While IT services providers and customers are yet to embrace service models that incorporate information sharing, similar models have been successful in other scenarios. In a study of demand information sharing methods in a high-tech electronics equipment supply chain, it is shown that forecasted demand distributing methods work better than planned demand distributing methods, even in presence of high forecasting error or high demand variability [36]. This is similar to our finding that information sharing is a better resource allocation mechanism compared to volume-price adaptations, as the latter scheme is shown to not be effective for customer organizations with a less heterogeneous user population. In summary, there are significant incentives for both customer and provider organizations engaged in a long-term IT services contract to incorporate demand information sharing in their service agreements. Such a mechanism would help reduce the inherent conflict between the organizations that arise due to over/underutilization of resources in nonstationary and heterogeneous demand environments.
