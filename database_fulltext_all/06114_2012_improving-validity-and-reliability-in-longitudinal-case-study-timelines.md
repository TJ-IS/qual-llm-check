RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Improving validity and reliability in longitudinal case study timelines

Christopher T. Street<sup>1</sup> and Kerry W. Ward<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Hill School of Business, University of Regina, Regina, Canada; <sup>2</sup>College of Information Science and Technology, Peter Kiewit Institute, University of Nebraska – Omaha, Omaha, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Kerry W. Ward, College of Information Science and Technology, Peter Kiewit Institute, University of Nebraska – Omaha, 1110 S. 67th St., Omaha, NE 68182, U.S.A. Tel: 402 554 3369; Fax: 402 554 3284

## Abstract

Management Information Systems researchers rely on longitudinal case studies to investigate a variety of phenomena such as systems development, system implementation, and information systems-related organizational change. However, insufficient attention has been spent on understanding the unique validity and reliability issues related to the timeline that is either explicitly or implicitly required in a longitudinal case study. In this paper, we address three forms of longitudinal timeline validity: time unit validity (which deals with the question of how to segment the timeline – weeks, months, years, etc.), time boundaries validity (which deals with the question of how long the timeline should be), and time period validity (which deals with the issue of which periods should be in the timeline). We also examine timeline reliability, which deals with the question of whether another judge would have assigned the same events to the same sequence, categories, and periods. Techniques to address these forms of longitudinal timeline validity include: matching the unit of time to the pace of change to address time unit validity, use of member checks and formal case study protocol to address time boundaries validity, analysis of archival data to address both time unit and time boundary validity, and the use of triangulation to address timeline reliability. The techniques should be used to design, conduct, and report longitudinal case studies that contain valid and reliable conclusions.

## Validity and reliability in longitudinal case study timelines

The longitudinal case study examines chronological timelines of events or changes in real-world organizational characteristics over time, and is therefore a valuable methodology in information systems research. The longitudinal approach is often essential for studying organizational processes such as IS strategy implementation processes (e.g. Silva & Hirschheim, 2007), the influence of technology on organizational change (e.g. Sabherwal et al, 2001), and organizational downsizing (e.g. Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 2002). While longitudinal case studies are a valuable methodology in IS, both authors and reviewers struggle with the challenge of evaluating and reporting the validity and reliability of case studies (Dube & Pare, 2003). Since the quality of empirical research rests heavily on the validity and reliability of the methodology that is used, uncertainty about what constitutes validity and reliability in longitudinal case study timelines present a significant barrier to IS researchers.

The authors have experienced these struggles firsthand. During a conversation at a recent conference, we discovered that we had received strikingly similar comments from journal reviewers on separate longitudinal case study manuscripts that examined factors influencing IT strategy development in organizations. We had both followed generally accepted methods for developing valid and reliable positivist case studies (e.g. Miles & Huberman, 1994; Yin, 1994; Lee, 1999 among others). However, the reviewers were not questioning the validity and reliability that these well-known references addressed. Instead, the reviewers questioned the validity and reliability of the events and details that made up the sequence in which events were reported, the vigilance in including all the important facts, the relative importance of the events, and their ability to explain an organizational process over time.

After further thought we concluded that longitudinal case study methods might be just different enough from the general case study methodology that they would benefit from additional assessment methods. Specifically, the methodology of longitudinal case studies is distinct from that employed in general case studies because it includes an extra step: a timeline of events or a sequence of changes in research variables across time that is created from the dataset as the basis for the case study narrative.

As we reviewed the well-known references that provide accepted procedures for evaluating validity and reliability in case studies (e.g., Miles & Huberman, 1994; Yin, 1994; Lee, 1999; Dube & Pare, 2003), we found that surprisingly little had been written addressing validity and reliability of the timeline in a longitudinal case study. After reflecting on this further, we concluded that there are three forms of validity and one form of reliability that should be addressed – time unit validity, time boundary validity, time period validity, and longitudinal reliability. Time unit validity deals with the question of ‘how to segment the timeline (weeks, months, years, etc.)?’, time boundary validity deals with the question of ‘how long of a timeline?’, time period validity deals with the question of ‘which periods?’, and longitudinal reliability deals with the question of ‘would another judge have assigned the same events in the same sequence, categories, and periods?’

Developing methods to address these forms of validity and reliability in longitudinal case studies offers practical value to IS researchers who may be challenged to demonstrate the quality of longitudinal case studies within journal page limits. Demonstrating quality in other methods is more highly standardized and researchers benefit from these accepted techniques. For example, the steps to take when assessing the validity of a survey are well defined, and this allows researchers to efficiently measure and report the rigor of a particular study. As Cassell and Symon explain in the introduction to their book on qualitative methods in organizational research, ‘the danger of not producing some sort of [consensus], however, is that judgments are prejudiced by subconscious and stereotypical ideas of what constitutes “good research” ’ (Cassell & Symon, 2005, p. 5).

Consensus around reporting validity and reliability reduces the variability of interpretation, efficiently uses journal space, and allows researchers to dedicate less time convincing reviewers and readers that the research is high quality.

The purpose of this article is to strengthen the quality and rigor of positivist longitudinal case studies by (1) examining validity and reliability issues with respect to the timelines in case studies, and (2) proposing techniques for addressing validity and reliability in future work. Our intent is to add to the case study methodology literature on internal validity in longitudinal studies in a similar way that Lee & Baskerville (2003) did for external validity in cases, Eisenhardt (1989) did for theory building with case studies, and Benbasat et al (1987) did for evaluating case studies.

We feel a word of caution is necessary at this point. It is not our intent to simply develop a checklist that authors must follow and that readers must accept because it is not the checklist that is important but rather a better understanding of the research process. Instead, we propose a framework that includes questions that both researchers and reviewers can ask as they assess the quality and rigor of longitudinal case studies. Our intent is pragmatic in nature, not legalistic. We stress that it is not sufficient for the researcher to merely address these questions; validity and reliability are also judged by reviewers asking whether the proper standards of evidence have been provided. Our goal is to increase the acceptance of longitudinal case studies in IS research by providing a vocabulary and an approach that can be used by authors to convey the validity and reliability related to longitudinal case studies.

We begin by addressing three questions regarding the need for this paper. We then review the existing literature on the longitudinal case study methodology and identify a gap in that literature (i.e., there has been virtually no emphasis on the timeline aspect of longitudinal case studies, and no one has differentiated the validity and reliability of the timeline itself). We then discuss validity and reliability issues as they apply to longitudinal case studies and suggest ways these issues might be addressed. Finally, a real world example is presented that demonstrates the value of addressing the validity and reliability of the timeline in longitudinal case studies.

## APPLYING POSITIVIST RELIABILITY AND VALIDITY MEASURE

Qualitative research exhibits a general absence of quantification and statistical procedures (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). It includes methods such as participant observation, interviews, and document analysis where the intent is to do more than simply count occurrences or classify types of action (Cassell & Symon, 2005). On the basis of Cassell & Symon (2005), we take the position that qualitative research – and case studies as one form of qualitative research – can be viewed from a variety of ontological perspectives, including the positivist perspective, which we take in this paper. Note that our definition of qualitative research differs from some definitions that consider it as a form of naturalistic or interpretive research (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Golafshani, 2003, among others).

Consistent with popular case study references such as Yin (1994) and Eisenhardt (1989), we define the case study as the study of a phenomenon in a real-life context or setting that may involve more than one level and unit of analysis (person, group, organization, technology), and where no interventions or controlled observation that involve manipulation are involved. Longitudinal case studies fit within this general definition, but an additional element is present: chronological time periods or sequences of events are explicitly or implicitly included as an independent variable.

Three questions may arise in the reader’s mind at this point: (1) Are all longitudinal case study researchers concerned with reliability and validity?, (2) Is time really a variable in a longitudinal study?, and (3) Can we really make further significant improvements to research with additional reliability and validity techniques? We address these important questions before going further.

## Concerns over reliability and validity

In this paper, we adopt a positivist perspective. While all researchers are concerned with accuracy and precision, positivist case study researchers refer to these concepts with the terms validity and reliability. This paper will therefore be of primary interest to positivist researchers, but it should also be of interest to other researchers with different perspectives.

We explicitly state our perspective because other perspectives make different assumptions about the nature of reality and knowledge. These differing foundational assumptions may impact the definitions, standards, and applicability of validity and reliability (Burrell & Morgan 1979; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Klein & Myers, 1999; Winter, 2000). How the concepts presented in this paper may apply under other perspectives will be discussed later in the ‘Alternative Perspectives’ section.

Given the complexity of the ontological and epistemological foundations of different perspectives and the difficulties in establishing common definitions for validity and reliability that apply across perspectives, we find it is necessary to focus on the application of validity and reliability to positivist longitudinal case studies. Research involves systematic investigation to establish precise facts and reach accurate conclusions, so researchers are naturally interested in accuracy and precision. Validity and reliability are simply two widely agreed upon terms related to the accuracy and precision of research within the positivist perspective.

## Time as a variable

In longitudinal case studies, changes in variables are examined in relation to the passage of time, so time becomes an additional variable that must be accounted for in a study’s design. When a study reports how an individual, group, or organization has changed between two points in time, the time variable takes on different values. Frameworks, theories, or suppositions involved in the research treat time as a variable that has a theoretical effect, as yet unproven, on something of interest in the study. For example, Watson et al (1998) demonstrated that time has an effect on ERP system implementation skills. Sabherwal et al (2001) showed that time has effect on the extent to which organizations are able to resume normal operations after a large organizational change. Sarkar & Lee (2003) reported that time has interaction effects with strong and committed leadership, open and honest communication, and a balanced and empowered implementation team on successful ERP implementation. These studies treated time as an independent variable acting on the dependent variables ERP implementation skill development, organizational change success, and ERP implementation success, respectively.

We recognize that other perspectives exist suggesting that time may not be a traditional research variable. For example, Schonfeld & Rindskopf (2007) point out that some longitudinal case studies do not explicitly recognize time as a variable within the research design. Time is also regarded by some researchers as a social mental construct (Stengers & Gille, 1997; Nandhakumar, 2002). Because social time is used to keep track of where we are and what we are doing, time passes at different rates and with different salience for each individual (Das 1993). Saunders & Kim (2007) also note that is it is not uncommon for time to be only implicitly reflected in research; time is therefore a ‘silent visitor’.

How the concepts presented in this paper may apply if one takes a different view of time is discussed in the ‘Alternative Perspectives’ section of this paper. At this point we simply observe that time is regarded by some researchers as an independent variable in longitudinal case studies. To the extent that researchers are concerned with validity and reliability, time should be accounted for in a way that is consistent with other variables in the research design.

## Need for additional reliability and validity techniques

There are three reasons why attention should be paid to the methods that are chosen for evaluating and reporting the reliability and validity of timelines in longitudinal case study designs. First, there is a lack of shared understanding of how reliability and validity applies and is evaluated in longitudinal case study timelines. We could find no acknowledged methodological technique for reporting the reliability or validity of the time variable in currently published longitudinal case studies or in methodological references.

Second, longitudinal case studies, particularly those using retrospective accounts, are considered by some researchers and reviewers to lack reliability and validity. Golden (1992, p. 857) launched a vigorous debate on this issue, arguing that the use of retrospective accounts in management research needs to be seriously questioned. Golden’s proposal met with resistance, with some researchers arguing that retrospective reporting in longitudinal case studies is practical and valid when the method is conducted properly (Miller et al, 1997). The suspect reliability and validity of longitudinal methods continues to generate debate. Cox & Hassard (2007) stated that longitudinal designs involving retrospective interviews are still considered as marginally accepted methods by mainstream researchers, noting that Such research receives relatively little attention in either the professional research journals or methodology textbooks (Cox & Hassard, 2007, p. 489). This skepticism suggests a strong need to develop improved methods and ways to assess validity and reliability.

Third, proposing improved methods should provide assurance that any new knowledge arising from the research will be credible, and that practical applications will be valuable. Reliability and validity influence the practical relevance of research by providing a measure of assurance that actions derived from the conclusions are founded in valid science and not in biased, anecdotal, or idiosyncratic observations. It is our contention that developing better techniques for evaluating and reporting reliability and validity in longitudinal case studies will lead to a shared methodological presentation for researchers.

## Limitations of existing case study methods literature

While there is a substantial body of literature on case study methodology, none of the sources that we are aware of address threats to validity and reliability with respect to longitudinal case study data. Table 1 lists several established case study methodology references from the IS and general management literature, and notes the extent to which they provide guidelines regarding the validity and reliability of longitudinal case study timelines. None of these references directly tell us how to determine if a longitudinal case study accurately represents significant events, if it is examined at the appropriate unit of analysis, if it captures the proper order or sequence, or if it demonstrates the validity and reliability of the timelines. Yin (1994) and Eisenhardt (1989) – two references from the management literature that are considered de facto standards in MIS research (Dube & Pare, 2003) – both address concerns of validity and reliability, but neither one addresses these concerns as they apply to longitudinal timeline datasets. Other references from the IS literature (Benbasat et al, 1987; Lee, 1989) also discuss validity and reliability in case studies and the specific importance of longitudinal case studies to IS research, but once again, neither one directly addresses validity and reliability as it applies to timelines.

Sources that directly address the issue of how to conduct longitudinal qualitative, field-based, or case study research discuss the use of timelines to analyze data, but do not address the validity or reliability of these timelines (see, for example, Miles & Huberman, 1994; Langley, 1999; Lee, 1999; see also the edited chapters in Huber & Van de Ven, 1995). Langley (1999) does address the issue of how to best illustrate and make sense of data by using time-oriented data displays, but is silent on how to assess the validity of the presentations or what types of questions to ask when thinking about these concerns. Thus, case study methodology references provide guidelines for collecting longitudinal data, but not for assessing the validity and reliability of timelines created from longitudinal data.

## Review of longitudinal case studies in the information systems literature

Having established that there is a gap in the methodology literature, we reviewed the IS literature between 2005 and the first half of 2011 in order to determine whether (and how) validity and reliability in longitudinal designs were discussed, and whether common conventions had been established in assessing and reporting the validity and reliability of case study timelines. Following recent practice in similar styles of articles (Olk & Griffith, 2004; Boyd et al, 2005), we selected four IS journals to represent high quality empirical research on general MIS topics: European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Research, Journal of Association of Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly. We used two primary inclusion criteria when selecting case study articles: they had to be identifiable as positivist research and the case had to be identifiable as longitudinal. Both authors and a Ph.D. student independently reviewed the literature; a given study was included only if all authors agreed that it met the selection criteria.

To identify appropriate cases, we searched the term ‘case study’ within each journal for 2005 to the first half of 2011 timeframe and then searched for the term in key words, abstract, and document text. This resulted in a total of 181 articles (see the first column of Table 2). To identify whether a case study was positivist, we followed the methodology of Dube & Pare (2003). We first examined each paper to see whether the author(s) self-identified the perspective taken in the case study. When the author(s) did not self-identify, we examined each case study in detail for practices consistent with a positivist perspective (for example, using a priori frameworks, stating formal research hypotheses or propositions, evidence of qualitative or quantitative measures of variables or constructs, explicitly stated purpose of theory testing or theory building, or discussion of validity or reliability in the methodology).

Determining a case studies’ ontological approach was a difficult process. We did not always agree with some authors’ self-identification but chose to use the authors classification when it was explicitly stated. Given our purpose in reviewing how validity and reliability of the timeline was addressed in the cases, we excluded papers that took design science, action research, or other approaches that some may consider more of a method than an approach. We also exclude mixed method papers because of the limited ability to address and present issues related to validity and reliability of both methods. On the basis of these criteria, we identified 46 positivist case study articles, each of which was examined to determine whether it was longitudinal or cross sectional.

Our review of the IS literature found that no conventions exist for the consistent, systematic reporting of validity and reliability in longitudinal case studies, and that reporting of validity and reliability related to the timeline is almost non-existent. This represents a serious omission because the timeline is a foundational aspect of a longitudinal case study, and failing to establish the validity and reliability of the timeline undermines the credibility of the research.

## Assessing timeline validity in longitudinal case studies

Assessing the validity of longitudinal case studies requires examination of three forms of validity that are specifically related to the timeline developed from the case data: time unit validity, time boundaries validity, and time period validity. In the following paragraphs, we define each of these three forms of validity and indicate how they differ from one another. We are not suggesting that these concepts apply equally to every study; and some of the following points may not be applicable, depending on the longitudinal design or research question. Instead, it is useful to consider first if a type of longitudinal validity or reliability is applicable to a specific design; if so, the following suggestions are instructive in demonstrating that the research was conducted with accuracy and rigor.

## Time unit validity

Time unit validity addresses the issue of whether the selected time unit (weeks, months, or years) is appropriately sensitive for capturing changes in the variables it is intended to measure. Time unit validity is an issue of construct validity and addresses measurement error in the time unit. There is a tradeoff between validity and precision (Winter, 2000). Measurement error and inaccurate conclusions can occur if the time unit is too large or too small. There is a point where additional precision is unlikely to result in additional validity and alternatively, not enough precision will mean a failure to capture changes in key variables. The time units must be just large enough to capture or record a particular change in the state of some variable. In terms of assessment, it is important to ask if the results would change significantly if a timeline were analyzed by one unit (e.g., month by month) vs a different unit (e.g., year by year). For example, if a Chief Information Officer (CIO) succession is known or expected to occur roughly every three to four years in a company, framing this process on a quarter-by-quarter basis would likely show a dozen or more periods of stability punctuated by just one or two periods of change. By contrast, framing the process on a year-by-year basis would show a more continuous process of change.

Time unit segmenting, sometimes referred to as ‘temporal bracketing’ (Langley, 1999), involves appropriately collating events and other important characteristics under study into equally sized segments such as weeks, months, or years. According to Langley (1999), these ‘buckets’ represent the time unit of analysis for the exploration and replication of theoretical ideas. In the CIO succession example, the important question is whether a time unit is large enough to completely record the fact that one CIO was succeeded by another. Time unit validity is negatively impacted when the temporal units do not match the pace of change under observation (Abbott, 1990; Pettigrew, 1990).

The key to addressing time unit validity is determining that a particular time unit has the proper resolution and is not favoring or misrepresenting portions of the timeline or changes in variables across the timeline. Often, organizational studies that report processes of punctuated change (e.g., Sabherwal et al, 2001) or paced change (e.g., Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997) depend on showing certain types of changes across certain-sized timeframes. The extent to which the timeframes are properly defined is therefore critical.

Time unit validity can be assessed by addressing the question of whether or not the time unit selected (weeks, quarters, years, decades, etc.) matches the rate of change in the variables in the study. Indications of the rate at which decision-making or reporting cycles occur within the group, organization, or industry being studied provide a suitable starting point. The use of longitudinal data such as annual reports, monthly columns, or newsletters could be used to justify the choice of a given time unit. Or, participants could be asked how often or at what rate important events occur. Some way of establishing that the process under study changes at a rate that is reasonably matched with the time segments in the timeline offers a greater degree of validity than simply leaving the issue to chance or convention.

## Time boundaries validity

Time boundaries validity addresses the issue of whether the beginning and ending points of the total observation window (the ‘window’ of time between $t _ { 0 }$ and $t _ { \mathrm { e n d } } )$ have been set in such a way as to capture all significant events or effects that occurred in the process under study. In other words, did the process truly begin at $t _ { 0 }$ and end at $t _ { \mathrm { e n d } } ?$ This question might seem obvious, but surprisingly few authors discuss timeline boundaries and whether their datasets collect the whole story. Time boundaries validity is a form of content validity indicating the degree to which the domain is accurately reflected in the measure. If the bounded timeframe is not adequately captured, content validity is reduced because the researcher is not fully representing the domain of data in the data collection.

Failure to justify time boundaries creates two threats to validity. First, if the proper starting and ending points for a longitudinal account are not correctly chosen, data censoring can result (Street & Ward 2007). This means that data about an event or variable are not included in a dataset, possibly because data collection methods do not go far enough back in the timeline or are curtailed before a process has been completed. Figure 1 illustrates how different types of data censoring can occur. Second, setting improper boundaries on a timeline and any potential data censoring that might result from those improper boundaries increases the risk of a spoiler effect, where data are inadvertently shaped to support an expected result or relationship between variables.

To see the impact of these two threats to validity, consider a case study that examines the process of developing a capability to manage IT outsourcing relationships in an organization. The researcher designs a longitudinal study where data are collected on five IT outsourcing relationships over a period of 3 years. The empirical timeline in Figure 1 begins in January 2004 and ends in December 2007, perhaps reflecting the period of time that the organization had several outsourcing relationships in place.

Relationships 1 and 2 are completely captured in the observation window, but relationships 3, 4, and 5 are only partially captured. The extent to which this discrepancy matters is, of course, dependent on the type of process being studied and on the research question that is being posed. Even though data may have been collected and questions might have been asked about events that occurred outside the observation window, reporting what occurred within these time boundaries influences the way the results are presented and how well conclusions are supported by observations. For example, relationships 3 and 5 do not likely contribute all the data regarding organizational capabilities in initiating outsourcing, and relationships 4 and 5 do not likely contribute all the data regarding ending such relationships. If the research question involves start-up or termination capabilities, then half or more of this potential dataset may be lost and internal validity – how well certain conditions can be shown to affect subsequent conditions – is negatively affected.

Time boundaries validity can be assessed by considering factors such as (a) the analysis of archival documentation (for example, legal filings, newswire articles, annual reports, etc.), (b) member checks, and (c) the use of a formal protocol for defining the start and end point conditions. All three can be used to set timeline boundaries and to establish when events or important characteristics could be considered to begin and end.

Timelines are valid and of higher quality when researchers can validate that the observation window is wide enough to capture the pertinent data for each variable under study.

In longitudinal case study research, it is advantageous to explain and justify the time boundaries that were chosen and to describe any steps that were taken to increase the validity of the time boundary (e.g. member checks, formal protocol, archival documentation, etc.). Including some form of visual timeline representation similar to that shown in Figure 1 is also helpful. The timeline should display the time period covered by the data collection, along with the presentation of events or processes representing data collection units (again see Figure 1). Any events or processes that extend beyond the data collection timeline need to be identified and the relevance of the events or processes that fall outside the data collection time boundaries should be addressed. It is also important for researchers to indicate why the events outside of the data collection period have little impact on the phenomenon under study (or to recognize that uncollected data outside of the data collection boundary may be a limitation on the study’s findings).

## Time period validity

While time boundaries validity deals with the issue of the beginning and ending points of the total observation window, time period validity deals with the issue of where the observation window is situated. For example, the researcher may decide that the legitimate time boundaries for a certain process might cover a 2-year period, but a decision must still be made about whether the 2-year period should be, say, 2002–2004 or 2003–2005.

Two longitudinal case studies about changes in information technology in organizations – Rindova & Kotha (2001) and Pinsonneault & Kraemer (2002) – illustrate time period validity and how it differs from time boundaries validity. Rindova & Kotha (2001) investigated how organizational form, function, and competitive advantage co-evolved at Internet search engine companies Excite and Yahoo!. The authors showed that between 1994 and 1999 the two companies went through three phases (phase one: search engine; phase two: destination site; phase three: portal), and that the changes within each phase were comparable. The authors then developed explanations about how and why co-evolution between form, function, and advantage occurred (Rindova & Kotha 2001). In conducting their analysis, the authors would have considered whether they had established the correct time boundaries to delineate when the search engine, destination site, and portal phases began and ended (time boundaries validity), and whether the time period selected (1994–1999) was sufficiently representative to accurately characterize the ‘early Internet age’ and therefore support their conclusions (time period validity).

Pinsonneault & Kraemer (2002) studied two mediumsized American cities over a period of 10 years between 1985 and 1995 to examine the role information technology played in organizational downsizing. Their analysis of organizational downsizing involved longitudinal timelines that divided the 10-year period into two phases (1985–1990 and 1990–1995), and reported changes within each period as they related to IS-enabled downsizing. In conducting their analysis, the authors would have considered whether the 1985–1990 and 1990–1995 boundaries represented valid beginning and end points for observing changes in strategy (time boundaries validity), and to what extent the 10-year time period accurately represented the ‘downsizing phase’ for the two cities studied (time period validity).

In historic and retrospective longitudinal designs – where the outcome is known in advance and the risk of a spoiler effect is increased – the potential exists to incorrectly define a period of time as being representative of some process. Rindova & Kotha’s (2001) study of early Internet companies may have produced much different results if the ‘hypercompetitive’ period for Internet firms was extended to the dot-com bust years of 2001–2002. Likewise, Pinsonneault & Kraemer’s (2002) results may have looked much different if their analysis had been extended to 1995–2000 when the details of outsourcing arrangements were becoming better understood. If researchers have prior knowledge of how a process turned out and what the end results were, the risk exists of defining a time period as being representative of the process being studied simply because it fits expectations. Inadvertently shaping the data therefore poses a significant threat to validity and should be addressed.

Archival documentation can be used to indicate how much change typically occurs in an industry or organization, thereby providing an independent view of when a process takes place. In the Pinsonneault and Kraemer example, newspaper or trade magazine articles about downsizing announcements could be reviewed to establish when the IT-related organizational downsizing period best representing the research question was occurring. Thus, there could be a relatively objective determination of when this period occurred in North America. Time-series analysis techniques that indicate volatility or rates of change can also be used to independently justify the periods being examined. An increased or decreased frequency of downsizing announcements, Internet startups, technology adoptions, changes in organizational strategy, etc. will independently signal the beginning or end in some overall change process. Internal validity should be higher, and the risk of spoiler effects should be lower, when the period of time can be judged as being sufficiently representative of the process being studied.

## Assessing timeline reliability in longitudinal case studies

Longitudinal research should ideally be an objective representation of past occurrences (Carroll, 2002). Unfortunately, that goal is not often achieved because events and opinions are subjectively remembered, new reports may surface, old reports may deteriorate or become lost or destroyed, and the context around them vanishes. Events do ‘happen’, however, and longitudinal reliability is the degree to which an empirical methodology can determine and record what those past events were, as well as their characteristics. In other words, longitudinal reliability is the reproducibility of an account of a sequence of events, where the account is considered reliable because it accurately shows the events and the order in which they occurred in a repeatable manner.

Longitudinal reliability complements other data point measures such as inter-rater reliability in much the same way that different types of validity checks complement each other. In assessing validity, it is understood that construct, convergent, and discriminant validity each assess different complementary dimensions of overall accuracy (Lee, 1999). Similarly, inter-rater reliability assesses the reliability of the data as they are coded or drawn from a data source, asking, for example, ‘Would another judge have assigned the same things to the same categories?’ (Carney, 1979, p. 200). Longitudinal reliability, by contrast, assesses the reliability of the empirical timeline as it is created, asking, ‘Would another researcher looking at the same process uncover the same events, in the same sequence and within the same timeframes?’ Reliability in longitudinal data collection can be addressed through triangulation (Ward & Street, 2009), which refers to the verification of data by multiple reference points (Jick, 1979). Triangulation provides a measure of reliability when facts and interpretations from different sources converge on the same event; that data point in time is then seen as more trustworthy because it is indicated by more than one type of data. Theoretically, triangulation of data sources increases reliability because each type of data provides a unique but overlapping perspective on the same phenomenon ( Jick, 1979; Lustick, 1996). In a cross-sectional case, individual data points are triangulated to reliably record what happened, while in longitudinal cases the goal is to triangulate so as to reliably record what happened as well as when it happened.

## A real-life example: constructing a 21-year timeline of organizational change in a health-care company

In this section we present a real-life example of a case study conducted by the lead author. Our intent is to provide a reflexive, critical appraisal of our methodological practice in conducting longitudinal case studies, and to think about how the research could be designed and conducted in a more convincing manner. We first present an overview of the case.

## The case study

An interesting phenomenon occurred in the aftermath of the 2001 dot.com bubble: many internet businesses failed, but others adapted quickly and survived. To gain a better understanding of how successful businesses made the transition, a research project began in 2004, which investigated the practices and routines of an IT-intensive business that survived the dot.com bubble. The goal was to identify how IT was being introduced and used, and how the infrastructure changed over time to identify potential relationships between changes in IT infrastructure and the adaptability of the business during periods of rapid technical change. A single-site retrospective case study method was chosen because of the time-based, process-oriented nature of the research question.

Events covering the period from 1984 to 2005 were examined using multiple data collection techniques. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with all senior managers who were involved throughout the 21-year history. In addition, data from a rich variety of historical sources were collected. The documentation included both internal sources (e.g., internal newsletters) and external sources (e.g., newspaper articles). A total of 219 pages of data were collected to create multiple overlapping perspectives of how the business operated as well as how IT was invested in and used. As part of the analysis a timeline of events, decisions, and operational milestones was created to visually represent how the business perceived and reacted to ‘the dot.com explosion’. Similar to procedures used by Rindova & Kotha (2001) and Chiles et al (2004), this 21-year timeline became the primary ‘results’ of the analysis and the underpinning of the discussion that followed.

In accordance with common practice and standard case study methodology, potential threats to validity and reliability were identified and steps were taken to address each one. The research site was selected according to a pre-existing set of empirical criteria from organizations that we predicted would be influenced in a specific way by the passing of time that differentiated the site from a convenience sample (Yin, 1994). As noted, data were collected from multiple sources to minimize the chance that the information was biased by idiosyncratic viewpoints (Yin, 1994). We developed and followed protocols for data collection and analysis. A qualitative software package (ATLAS-ti) was used to manage and audit the large dataset that was created. We calculated and reported inter-rater coding reliabilities. We sent draft versions of the organizational narratives to key informants for their review and revised them until agreement on accuracy was established (Yin, 1994). Overall, the steps involved in a typical case study were completed, known threats to validity and reliability were addressed and reported, and the case study was thought to be reasonably complete and of sufficient quality and rigor that we sent the manuscript out for formal review.

However, we came to understand (with assistance from reviewers) that even after accounting for the common case study validity and reliability criteria there were still important questions to be asked about validity and reliability. The reviews indicated that the site was acceptable and that we had established the rigor with which the data were collected. However, the reviewers also wanted assurance beyond what was addressed by the existing literature that the timeline constructed from the data was developed and presented accurately. Specific concerns were raised about the validity and reliability of the longitudinal raw data, the analysis method, and the results. In essence, despite our best efforts and reliance on existing references for conducting case studies, the reviewers were asking if the proper events were identified and whether they were presented in the proper order.

In the following paragraphs we refer to parts of the case study briefly described above, point out the parts of the method that we found to be troublesome in retrospect, and propose ways that our future projects could be changed in order to produce increasingly credible results using our proposed language of time unit, time boundary, and time period validity as well as longitudinal reliability.

## Time unit validity

In the case study the data were assembled by year. During the data coding process all the events, important decisions, and various reports were identified by year and then all the data points for each year were collapsed into one ‘time bucket’. For some parts of the timeline this approach was likely acceptable (for example, IT investments and usage in the 1980s and early 1990s were relatively slow to develop). However, during other parts of the timeline, several significant events occurred within a given year that proved difficult to unravel when analyzing the data on an annual basis. For example, within a 1-year period (1997) a major change in senior leadership occurred, the contract with a long-standing IT software supplier was cancelled, and the selection process for a new software provider began. Hence, the case was weak for clearly demonstrating the order of events and their precedents when more than one significant event happened in 1 year (the unit of time chosen in the case study design).

Time unit validity was not a concern until key events took place within the same time unit, at which point it was difficult to convincingly show the influence that cancelling an IT contract and searching for a new supplier had on leadership succession. The validity of the results was limited because the way the data were grouped made it incapable in the practical sense of systematically showing how the order of events and decisions within one time unit influenced one another. If the results were to reliably demonstrate that the CEO was replaced because of an IT crisis, rather than an IT crisis being caused by a change in the CEO, then the time units in the case design should have been selected to systematically capture the change and the sequence of change necessary to draw these conclusions from the data.

## Time boundaries validity

A second issue concerned whether the timeline was sufficient for investigating the question of how IT was introduced, used, and adapted by the franchise company over time. The timeline began when the company was founded, so the potential impact of left-censoring was considered low. A reviewer, however, wanted to know whether the timeline was sufficiently long to draw a valid conclusion about how the company made the transition between software suppliers between 1997 and 2004. If the timeline was not sufficiently long to capture data from, for example, the people who used and were comfortable with the existing software, then the results about the effect of the changeover would not have been very convincing because it could be argued that we had not heard from all the affected individuals and the effects of the changeover were not yet seen. However, by 2004 the changeover was only considered verifiably complete by the franchisor and there was a time boundary validity problem in the results. If the results were to accurately support the conclusion that the company had successfully made the transition by the end of 2004, then it would have been necessary to include data from both the franchisor and the franchisees to make a valid conclusion about what had occurred by the end of 2004. The time boundaries in the research design set the data collection limits in such a way that it was only the franchisor’s opinion – the head office view – that appeared in the results. The conclusions, however, were erroneously attributed to the overall company at a specific point in time.

## Time period validity

In this particular case, the intent of the research question was to understand the effects of IT infrastructure and usage on the adaptability of an organization during events like the dot.com bubble. Since we had the opportunity to document the business from a point before the term ‘Internet’ existed to well after the dot.com bubble ended around 2001, time period validity was not a significant concern because the timeline covered the complete period we were interested in.

In comparison, however, consider a second case study that was part of the larger overall project. Instead of beginning in 1984, the timeline for the second case began in 1997, 2 years after the start of the run-up to the technology bubble, and ended 8 years later in 2005. Because the second timeline begins after significant interest in the Internet had begun, it is not actually possible to draw the same type of conclusions about how organizations adapt to technological change with the same level of validity as we could demonstrate with the first timeline (all else being equal). If the conclusions from this case study were to state that the company adapted in some comparable manner as the other organizations in the project then it would have been necessary to design the study so that the time period captured the proper sequence of years required to examine adaptability to the rise and fall of the Internet boom between approximately 1994 and 2001.

## Longitudinal reliability

Following Yin’s (1994) guidelines, triangulation of data points was used in the case study to demonstrate reliability in the conclusions; individual events in the timeline were represented by two or more data points (e.g. interview transcript excerpt, newspaper article). This approach established the reliability of the data points (we knew what happened), but did little to help establish the reliability of the sequence of the data points (we did not always know when something happened). If the conclusions about the time effects in the study were to be considered reliable, then it would have been necessary to revise the data collection protocols to triangulate the data from a longitudinal perspective (i.e. multiple perspectives on when it happened), as well as from a cross-sectional perspective (i.e. multiple perspectives on what happened) and present a table reporting validity and reliability for the timeline in the case study in a format similar to Table 3. The conclusions as they were originally presented are open to criticism regarding the reliability of the timeline.

## Considerations and limitations in applying these techniques

Since there are several theoretical perspectives in parallel with the positivist tradition, there are also several questions about the applicability of the concepts presented in this paper within other research traditions. As we stated earlier, our belief is that researchers share a belief that systematic research is both precise and accurate. How precision and accuracy is represented changes across the different traditions. A few important considerations and limitations are discussed below.

Researchers who view case studies from a naturalistic or interpretive perspective or who follow other research philosophies (constructivist or critical, etc.) may justifiably conclude that evaluating reliability and validity in case study research is unnecessary or inappropriate. For example, some scholars believe that the foundational assumptions underlying paradigms are so different that the paradigms cannot be compared and would not share methodological standards such as validity and reliability (see for example Burrell & Morgan, 1979). This is consistent with Klein & Myers (1999) who argue that positivist criteria for evaluating a case study along the lines proposed by Yin (1994) or Lee (1989) are inappropriate for evaluating interpretive case study. Walsham (1995), referring back to the work of Van Maanen (1989), indicates that establishing validity in interpretive case studies ‘is as much a matter of rhetorical style and flair as it is of accuracy and care in matters of theory and method’ (p. 79).

Given the complexity of the ontological and epistemological foundations of different paradigms and the difficulties in establishing common definitions for validity and reliability that apply across perspectives, we found it is necessary to focus on the application of validity and reliability within one research tradition. Focusing on the positivist perspective is consistent with Cassell & Symon’s (2005) outline of explaining research methods through reflexivity (being reflective and critical of the practices, commitments, and influence of the method-in-use), and staying within an acknowledged epistemology (realism, positivist, constructivism, critical, post-positivist, etc.). This is also consistent with prior IS literature addressing methodological issues (Lee. 1989:

Straub, 1989; Walsham, 1995, 2006; Klein & Myers, 1999; Petter et al, 2007; Mingers & Walsham, 2010; Myers & Klein, 2011).

In addition to the challenge of describing ways to account for research methods under a particular theoretical perspective, we were faced with a similar crisis of perspective: that time is a concept that that has more than one fundamental nature, and that different – and often opposing – beliefs exist about what time is (Ancona et al, 2001a, b). One classical view, sometimes referred to as mechanistic time (Nandhakumar, 2002) or scientific time (Stengers & Gilles, 1997), is that time exists as a central part of the physical universe and is independent of whether anyone perceives its passage or not. Those who follow this conceptualization of time consider it to be objective. Time passes everywhere at the same rate and with the same effect. It can be broken into meaningful segments or units and measured with high levels of precision (Whitrow, 1979; Mitchell & James, 2001). As mentioned above, an opposing view is that time is a socially constructed artifact (Stengers & Gille, 1997; Nandhakumar, 2002), and is therefore subjective and dependent on human consciousness. It is no accident that the philosophical underpinnings of mechanistic and social time parallel the ontological perspectives of positivism and interpretivism. The objective, measurable nature of mechanistic time parallels the beliefs of positivist researchers while the socially constructed, subjective nature of social time parallels interpretive perspectives.

Consistent with the positivist perspective, we chose to adopt the mechanistic perspective that time is external, universal, and can be measured. Given that time is at least an implied variable in positivist longitudinal case studies and given that time can be measured, establishing the validity and reliability of the timeline measures become an essential element in establishing the overall validity and reliability of the longitudinal case study. The techniques we proposed in this paper are intended to help address this need.
