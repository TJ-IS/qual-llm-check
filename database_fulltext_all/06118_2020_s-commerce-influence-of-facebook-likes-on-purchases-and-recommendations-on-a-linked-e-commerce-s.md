# S-commerce: Influence of Facebook likes on purchases and recommendations on a linked e-commerce site

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Social commerce Facebook likes Social networking site (SNS Social proof Attitude transfer Controlled experiments

## A B S T R A C T

Social networking site (SNS) driven e-commerce, the latest social commerce (s-commerce) phenomenon, gains prominence with the introduction of the call-to-action feature. The call-to-action feature on any sponsored post or advertisement on SNS redirects the user to a linked e-commerce website that ofers the product. Information cues available on the SNS are expected to influence user decision making on the linked e-com merce site. Set in the context of Facebook driven e-commerce, this study explores how likes on Facebook influence user's purchase and recommendation decisions on a linked e-commerce website. Using controlled experiments we find that a higher volume of likes on Facebook leads to a higher likelihood of purchasing and recommending a product on the linked e-commerce site. This efect is found to be mediated by the user's initial product attitude formed on Facebook. An additional analysis examining the strength of the influence reveals that the mere presence of likes is not suficient to impact the user's decision making. In fact, a low volume of likes elicits user behavior similar to absence of likes. The influence is effective only if the number of likes is substantially high. The findings of the study add to the s-commerce literature by establishing the inter-site influence of Facebook likes on user's purchase and post-purchase decisions and providing empirical evidence of the eficacy of SNS-driven e-commerce.

## 1. Introduction

Recently marketers have started leveraging social networking sites (SNSs) not only to promote their products but also to facilitate commercial transactions through the channel, giving a boost to social commerce (s-commerce). S-commerce, the newest paradigm of e-commerce, refers to the convergence of e-commerce and social networking sites that facilitates “the delivery of e-commerce activities and transactions via social media environment” [1]. SNSs such as Facebook, Pinterest, Instagram, etc., have extended their capabilities from merely hosting advertisements to providing avenues to carry out commercial transactions through their sites. The latest call-to-action (e.g., shop now, subscribe now) feature on sponsored posts and advertisements on SNSs is an example of the extended capabilities in this regard. The call-to-action feature redirects users from SNSs to a linked e-commerce site that sells the product, giving rise to SNS-driven e-commerce purchases. Users browsing their accounts on SNSs get exposed to advertisements or sponsored posts about products and services. After an initial evaluation of the products on the SNS they may choose to click on the call-toaction button to visit the linked e-commerce site where they can finally evaluate the product. The initial evaluation of the product on the SNS and the final evaluation of the product on the e-commerce site are expected to be impacted by the information cues available on the SNS.

One of the most common information cues on SNSs is one-click social cue such as like (on Facebook) or love (on Instagram).<sup>1</sup> Although non-lexical or symbolic in nature [2], such one-click social cues convey positively connoted user opinion [3,4] and indicate user preference or interest [5] for the ‘liked’ item. Thus, it can serve as an indirect form of recommendation and act as a source of social influence [6,7]. Facebook like, a common form of one-click social cue, has gained a considerable amount of attention in the academic research recently. This is due to the fact that Facebook is the most popular SNS among individuals and organizations [8.9]. As of 2020. Facebook has more than 1.7 billion daily active users accounting for 37% of global internet users.<sup>2</sup> It is also a popular medium among marketers with more than 86% of marketers in the US using Facebook for advertising in 2019.<sup>3</sup> Due to its popularity in practice, academic research has started showing interest in under standing the role of Facebook likes in users' perceptions and actions, particularly in users' consumption decisions [6,10–16]. However, several gaps still exist in this field of knowledge.

First, the role of Facebook likes in user decision making in inter-site setting such as SNS-driven e-commerce has not been investigated. Most of the existing studies have investigated the efects of likes for intra-site purchases only, i.e., display of likes and users' purchasing decisions are restricted to the same platform, e.g., a retailer's website [10], or a group buying website [6]. However, in reality, users often come across likes on a product on Facebook but make the final purchase decision on a diferent site. Therefore, it is important to examine whether Facebook likes exert any influence in inter-site settings. Second, the findings of prior studies are contradictory with some claiming significant positive efect of likes on users' consumption decisions [6,10,13,17], while some questioning its efectiveness in persuading users (e.g., [14]). Third, most of the previous studies have merely established association between the number of likes and sales [6,15], without providing any evidence about the causal efects of likes on the outcomes. Finally, studies have pri marily looked into the influence of likes on individuals' purchasing behavior only [6,12,15,17], overlooking individuals' social sharing behavior (e.g., spreading word of mouth, recommending a product to others, etc.) which is an important aspect of s-commerce [18,19]. To address these gaps, in this study we investigate the causal efects of likes on Facebook on purchases and product recommendations on a linked e-commerce site, delineating the inter-site influence of likes on user decision making. Thus, this becomes one of the few studies to empirically examine the causal influence of social cues on e-commerce purchases, thereby assessing the value of s-commerce. Also, to the best of our knowledge, this study is a novel attempt to identify the inter-site influence of social cues by simulating the real s-commerce phenomenon of Facebook-driven e-commerce. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions:

In SNS driven e-commerce, how do likes on SNS influence user's product evaluation on the SNS itself? How do likes on SNS influence a user's decision to visit the linked e-commerce site that ofers the product? In SNS driven e-commerce, how do likes on SNS influence a user's final decision to purchase and recommend<sup>4</sup> a product on a linked e-commerce site that ofers the product?

We theorize our study based on the social proof heuristic, a psycho logical phenomenon in which individuals' decisions are impacted by preferences and behaviors of peers [20,21], and the attitude transfer hypothesis [22], a theory that expounds how individuals' attitudes get transferred from one entity to another related entity. Combining the concepts of social proof and attitude transfer we posit that users' initial product attitude on Facebook, their intention to visit the linked ecommerce site, and their final purchase and recommendation decisions on the e-commerce site will be positively shaped by Facebook likes. The conceptual model of our research is presented in Fig. 1.

Using controlled experiments, we substantiate the findings of extant research by establishing a positive causal influence of Facebook likes and the outcomes. We find that likes on Facebook exert a significant and positive inter-site influence on users' likelihood to purchase and recommend a product on the linked e-commerce site. The influence of likes on users' final decisions (to purchase and to recommend) on the ecommerce site are found to be mediated by the initial product attitude formed on Facebook. Additionally, we conduct a post-hoc study to determine whether SNS driven e-commerce provide any considerable leverage to the marketers as compared to direct e-commerce.<sup>5</sup> Hence, we compare the results of our main experiment with the results of a baseline experiment that captures users' likelihood of purchase and likelihood of recommendation when visiting the e-commerce site directly. We find that both outcomes are significantly higher in case of SNS driven e-commerce transactions than direct e-commerce transactions if the volume of likes is substantially high. In case of a low volume of likes, there is no significant diference between SNS driven e-commerce and direct e-commerce.

In terms of theoretical contributions, our study establishes the intersite influence of Facebook likes in shaping users' decision to purchase and recommend a product on a diferent site. Specifically, it demonstrates the causal impact of Facebook likes on users' attitude towards a product not only on the same website (i.e., Facebook), but also on a separate yet related website (i.e., the linked e-commerce site). It also contributes to the literature by combining the concepts of attitude transfer and social proof to explain the inter-site influence of social cues. Further, by comparing SNS driven e-commerce to direct e-commerce purchases, the study provides empirical evidence about the role of s-commerce. It ofers insights on the strength of Facebook likes and add to the literature on likes and other types of one-click social cues. From a practitioner's point of view, our study examines the efectiveness of likes in SNS driven e-commerce and provides implementable insights to marketers who are contemplating to advertise their oferings on SNSs. The findings of the research can help them devise efective means of attracting more trafic to the linked e-commerce sites and maximizing sales.

## 2. Literature review

A thorough review of existing literature reveals the relationship between Facebook likes and users' product evaluation and consumption decisions in diferent online settings. Prior studies have reported a positive association between Facebook likes received on deals on group buying websites and their quantity of sales [6,15], a positive efect of likes on product's quality perception [10], brand evaluation and purchase likelihood [23,24], also and a positive efect of likes on consumers' impulse buying tendency [12]. Studies have also found a positive impact of peer liking behavior on user's consumption decisions for experience products such as online music [13] and movies [17,25]. However, there still exist a few important gaps in extant literature. First, majority of these studies have employed correlational analyses on secondary data to find the association between number of likes and group deal sales [6,26], limiting any causal claim. A few studies that have investigated the causal relationship between likes and users' product perception [10] and tendency of impulse buying [12] through experiments, have done so by restricting the likes and the purchase to take place on the same platform. The inter-site influence of Facebook like is rarely investigated. We intend to address the gaps by conducting a controlled experiment to establish the causal influence of likes received by a product on Facebook and users' intention to buy and re commend the product on a linked e-commerce site. Second, researchers have mainly focused on purchase or sales as the outcomes when studying the influence of likes $[ 5 , 6 , 1 2 , 1 5 , 1 7 ]$ . For instance, some of them have examined the impact of likes on group-deal purchases [6,15], whereas others have checked the efect on Facebook consumerto-consumer and second-hand marketplace purchases [5,12], and movie ticket sales [17]. We notice a lack of attention to identification of the efect of likes on users' social sharing behavior, one of the critical aspects of s-commerce [18]. We intend to advance the research by examining the efect of Facebook likes on users' social sharing behavior in terms of their intention to recommend a product in the linked e commerce site. Finally, although a few studies have underlined the economic value of social media or SNSs as marketing and transactional channels in e-commerce purchases [27–30], there is a paucity of studies that provide empirical evidence of the value of social cues in SNSdriven e-commerce. Also, there is a lack of studies that empirically examine the additional value of s-commerce over e-commerce. In our study, we not only investigate the role of social cues (on SNS) in SNS driven e-commerce but also compare it with direct e-commerce purchases to demonstrate the additional value that SNSs ofer in s-com merce transactions. ## 3. Theoretical background

The theoretical foundation of our study is based on the concepts of social proof [21] and attitude transfer [22]. The central tenet of this study is that a social cue such as Facebook like acts as a social proof that positively impacts users' perceptions and actions including their intersite purchase and recommendation decisions. Further the transference of this influence of likes from Facebook to another site (the linked ecommerce site) is mediated by the initial product attitude formed on Facebook, as expounded by the attitude transfer hypothesis.

## 3.1. Social proof heuristic

Social proof heuristic is a socio-psychological concept that explains the human tendency to follow opinions or actions of others while undertaking any decision. Rooted in the works of Cialdini, social proof is a form of social influence that leads to the tendency of a large group of individuals conforming to a particular choice, also known as herd be havior [21]. Social influence can be either normative or informational. Normative social influence implies conformity to be liked or accepted by others [31], whereas informational social influence occurs under un certainty where we seek information from others in order to decide or act [21]. Thus, social proof is essentially informational social influence that leads to genuine acceptance of others' actions or opinions [32]. Research suggests that the strength of social proof depends on a number of factors, such as the number of information sources, and the nature of the information source. The same information coming from multiple sources is perceived to be more credible and has a higher propensity to be accepted [21]. Social proof becomes stronger if the information is shared by reputed or trustworthy sources or peers rather than unfamiliar sources [20]. For example, studies show that information (e.g., online reviews) shared by reputed sources (reviewers) are of higher quality and exert greater social influence on others [33]. Social proof comes into play in case of online purchases where consumers are uncertain about the quality of the product or the credibility of the marketer. In such cases people seek information from fellow consumers in their network as social proof.

## 3.2. Attitude transfer hypothesis

Individuals' perceptions and attitudes can be transferred from one entity to other related entities. It is referred to as the attitude transfer hypothesis [22]. For instance, attitudes can be transferred from an advertisement (source) to advertised product (target) and parent brand [34], or from retailers' physical stores (source) to their online shopping websites (target) [35]. The attitude transfer hypothesis has been widely used in social psychology for explaining the formation of impression [35,36]. Studies have shown that congruity between the source and the target increases the chance of attitude transfer. When two entities are closely related or the target entity is identified to a previously defined source, attitude transfer is more pronounced. For example, [35] have shown that congruity between a retailer's known ofline store and the online website increases the influence of prior attitudes on consumers evaluation of the website. Our current research context involves the evaluation of a product on two closely related entities: Facebook and a linked e-commerce site. We expect transference of product attitude from Facebook to the linked e-commerce site during product evaluation and subsequent purchase decision. In the context of our study, we expect Facebook like to act as a social proof as it indicates fellow consumers' preference for a product. When users observe a product on Facebook being liked by many of their peers, they are likely to form a favourable attitude towards the product. When they visit the linked ecommerce site, we expect the initial product attitude to be carried over (attitude transfer process) to the e-commerce site and shape their final product attitude. ## 4. Hypotheses development

4.1. Efect of likes on product attitude on Facebook and likelihood of purchase on a linked e-commerce site

According to the social proof heuristic, individual's perceptions and actions are influenced by the opinions and actions of others [20,21]. For example, the efect of e-WoM on sales of movie tickets [37], sales of digital microproducts [38], etc. Facebook likes being a form of peer opinion [6] can act as social proof and influence prospective buyers. The fact that likes are provided by real users, some of them being familiar sources such as friends and acquaintances, further strengthens the influence [20]. The social proof heuristic also suggests that individuals give more credence to the same information generated by multiple sources [21]. Since a user can like a content only once, each like indicates opinion from a unique user. Therefore, a higher number of likes for a product indicates preference of a higher number of individuals, creating a higher degree of social proof. This will translate into a positive attitude towards the product on Facebook. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

A higher degree of social proof, resulting from a higher number of likes is likely to amplify user's tendency to click on the call-to-action button and visit the linked e-commerce site. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1b. A higher volume of likes for a product advertised on Facebook will lead to a higher likelihood of visiting the linked e-commerce site that ofers the product as compared to a lower volume of likes.

Furthermore, studies indicate a positive relationship between social proof and online purchases (e.g., [38]). Therefore, we posit that a higher degree of social proof resulting from a higher volume of likes on Facebook will increase user's propensity to buy the product on the linked e-commerce site. This leads us to the following hypothesis:

H1c. A higher volume of likes for a product advertised on Facebook will lead to a higher likelihood of purchasing the product on a linked e-commerce site as compared to a lower volume of likes.

## 4.2. Efect of Facebook likes on likelihood of product recommendation on a linked e-commerce site

Individuals' intention to recommend a product to their peers is propelled by various social and psychological motivations, such as intention to help others, expressing own choices, or even reduction of one's postpurchase dissonance or anxiety [39]. However, recommendation behavior involves costs such as time and efort expended in providing the recommendation and the risk of reputation loss [39]. Although online channels have reduced the time and efort involved in providing recommendations, the risk of reputation loss still exists. If a receiver is dissatisfied with a purchase resulting from the recommendation, the relationship between the recommender and the receiver sufers [40]. Thus, while recommending, users become cautious about the quality of the product and tend to seek social proof to alleviate the uncertainty involved in the process. Therefore, we can say a higher number of likes on Facebook elicits a higher likelihood of product recommendation among users. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2. A higher volume of likes for a product advertised on Facebook will lead to a higher likelihood of recommending the product on a linked e-commerce site as compared to a lower volume of likes.

## 4.3. Mediating role of product attitude on Facebook

Prior research on attitude transfer has shown that formation of attitude towards an entity is influenced by previously developed attitude towards the same or other related entities [22]. Several studies have applied attitude transfer hypothesis in online settings. For example, studies have demonstrated how initial attitude towards a company's website leads to attitudinal responses towards the company itself [35,36], or how positive attitude towards e-WoM services transfers to mobile-WoM services [41]. Accordingly, in our context, when users visit a linked e-commerce site from Facebook, they carry the initial attitude towards the product formed on Facebook to the e-commerce site. Due to the relatedness of Facebook and the linked e-commerce site. the initial product attitude is expected to influence the formation of the final product attitude on the linked e-commerce site, which in efect is expected to shape users' purchasing and recommending decisions [42]. Thus, we can say that product attitude on Facebook will mediate the inter-site influence of likes on users' likelihood of purchase and likelihood of recommendation through the process of attitude transfer. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3a. Users' attitude towards the product on Facebook will mediate the efect of likes on the likelihood of purchasing the product on a linked e-commerce site.

H3b. Users' attitude towards the product on Facebook will mediate the efect of likes on the likelihood of recommending the product on a linked ecommerce site.

## 5. Research methodology

Existing research has predominantly used secondary data for examining the influence of likes (e.g., [6,15,17]), that has weaker causal claims. To establish causality and increase the accuracy of conceptualization of constructs, we conducted scenario-based controlled experiments, as used in many previous studies (e.g., [43,44]). A randomized controlled experiment is the best method to establish causality as it minimizes the problems created by confounding variables and self selection bias<sup>6</sup> [45]. Also, the scenarios used in the experiments emulate the case of real-life SNS driven e-commerce as closely as possible, evoking responses that are more realistic.

## 5.1. Design of experiment

We created a simulated online shopping scenario where participants were exposed to an advertisement on Facebook with a ‘shop now’ button which redirected them to a linked e-commerce site ofering the product. The number of likes on Facebook was manipulated using two levels: low and high, to test the impact of likes on the outcome variables. The aggregated product rating on the simulated e-commerce site was also manipulated using two levels: low and high. Since aggregated product rating plays an important role in users' purchase decisions [46,47], we examined whether our proposed relationships would hold good under conditions of high and low product rating. This resulted in a 2 (Facebook likes: low and high) x 2 (Aggregated product rating: low and high) full-factorial experimental design. The experiment was conducted in such a manner that each participant went through all four treatment conditions, making it a within-subject design. Within-subject design is more sensitive to the treatment than between-subject design and demands a smaller number of respondents to produce reliable results. To alleviate the problem of demand efect [48], a Latin-square design approach is used. In Latin-square design each subject goes through all the treatments, with the sequence of the treatments being randomized among subjects [49]. Table 2 shows the treatment conditions mapped to diferent combinations of input factors.

Each treatment condition was coupled with a unique product to form a sequence which was varied across groups to ensure randomization. The four products selected for the experiment were: selfie-stick, travel adapter, power bank, and headphones. All the products were chosen to be utilitarian, low-involvement, and gender-neutral in nature to minimize any possible bias. Table 3 shows the Latin-square design resulting from the diferent combinations of products and sequences. The subjects who were randomly assigned to Sequence1 of the experi ment were first shown a scenario with high Facebook likes and high aggregated product rating (T1) for a selfie-stick which was followed by high Facebook likes and low aggregated product rating (T2) for a travel adapter, and so on.

## 5.2. Preparation of stimuli

We conducted two rounds of pre-tests to prepare the stimuli for the experiment, i.e., values required to operationalize the levels of both Facebook likes and aggregated product rating. In the first pre-test 11 doctoral students at a university were asked to indicate the number of likes they would consider suficient to click on the ‘shop now’ button on Facebook and visit the linked e-commerce site for each of the products (selfie-stick, travel adapter, power bank, and headphones). Using these values as threshold we operationalized the low and high number of likes. A number substantially above the threshold was considered as high, whereas a number substantially below it was considered as low. Table 3 presents the threshold value of Facebook likes indicated by the subjects, and the values we used to operationalize low and high likes for each product. We also asked them to report the minimum product rating at which they would consider purchasing each product. The results suggested an aggregated product rating of 3.8 out of 5 to be the threshold A rating below the threshold was considered as low and a rating above the threshold was considered as high. The second round of pre-test, conducted with a separate group of 12 doctoral students, confirmed the values we used for operationalization of Facebook likes (as shown in Table 4).

## 5.3. Subjects

In November 2018 we conducted the main experiment with in dustry professionals who were participants of an executive program in digital marketing at a university. 60 executives were invited for the experiment, and 42 of them completed it. 16 of these 42 respondents were female. The average age of the respondents was 31. They represented a population of Internet users and online shoppers who were familiar with SNSs and e-commerce sites. More than 90% (37 out of 42) were very familiar with online shopping, and around 68% (28 out of 42) respondents were very familiar with the ‘call-to-action’ (shop now) feature of Facebook.

## 5.4. Experimental procedure

The respondents were sent a link to the experiment via an e-mail and were instructed to fill out a scenario-based survey. A lecture on digital marketing, a part of their executive curriculum, preceded the experimental session. The purpose of the experiment, as stated to the participants, was to understand how SNSs facilitated e-commerce transactions. After a brief introduction and sharing of general instructions for filling the survey, the respondents were randomly assigned to one of the four sequences. A scenario was presented to set the context of the experiment which read: “Imagine you are planning to go on a trip within the next few days. Before your travel you need to purchase some travel accessories such as selfie-stick, travel adapter, power bank, and noisecancelling headphone. You have casually browsed a few online shopping sites to check the items but haven't made a purchase yet. Now, you log-in to your Facebook account as a part of your usual routine and notice sponsored advertisements with the items from a linked e-commerce site named The Travel Store which specializes on travel gears and accessories. You notice a ‘shop now’ button on the advertisement which enables you to visit the shopping website directly from Facebook, and there you can purchase the item if you want to do so. You are requested to carefully go through each of the scenarios and answer the following questions.” A fictitious website with a generic name ‘The Travel Store’ was created for the experiment to eliminate any prior attitude towards the website [43]. Also, unknown or fictitious brands were used to insulate the experimental results from the participants' existing bias. Each of the participants were subjected to all treatments presented in a random order as a part of one of the sequences. Fig. A1 and Fig. A2 of Appendix present the layouts for the first treatment condition of Sequence1 i.e., high Facebook likes and high aggregated product rating condition coupled with selfie-stick. Each treatment consisted of two layouts: the first part was a layout of Facebook with an advertisement of the product, and the second part was the linked e-commerce site.

The value of likes on Facebook layout was manipulated using the pre-tested values. After careful inspection of the page, the participants were asked to indicate their agreement to the following statements on a 7- point Likert scale: “I have a favorable attitude toward the product” (1 = “Strongly disagree” to 7 = “Strongly agree”), and “I would click on ‘shop now’ to get redirected to the e-commerce site” (1 = “Strongly disagree” to $7 = ^ { \ast } 8$ trongly agree”). These two statements were used to capture the participants' attitude towards the product on Facebook. Next, the participants visited the layout of the fictitious online store ‘The Travel Store’ where the product was displayed along with information cues such as aggregated product rating, price, brief descrip tion, and the number of customer reviews. The objective was to make the layout look realistic. Only aggregated product rating was varied across scenarios using the respective pre-tested values while controlling for other elements of the layout.

Following each treatment condition, the participants were requested to respond to a set of questions which included the measurement of two dependent variables (DV) pertaining to their behavioral intention: likelihood of purchasing the product and likelihood of recommending the product to their friends. The DVs were measured using standard one-item scales adapted from previous studies $[ 5 0 , 5 1 ]$ ]. The respondents were required to rate their agreement to the statements on $\textsf { a } 7 -$ point Likert scale: “I would purchase this product” $( 1 \ = \ ^ { \mathrm { { \sc ~ 4 } } } \mathrm { { S t r o n g l y } }$ disagree” to 7 = “Strongly agree”), and “I would recommend this product to my friends” (1 = “Strongly disagree” to 7 = “Strongly agree”). Along with the main responses, the participants were asked to answer a set of questions for manipulation check. After four treatment conditions and the corresponding set of questions, a general section was presented to capture the respondents' demographics (age and gender), their familiarity with s-commerce and their susceptibility to informational and normative social influence. Finally, they were debriefed and thanked for their participation. 42 respondents going through 4 treatments resulted in 168 unique data points.

## 8. Discussion and conclusion

## 8.1. Summary of result

SNS driven e-commerce has given rise to the possibility of users' purchase decisions getting impacted by likes on the SNS. In this study, we theorize and test the efect of likes on Facebook on users' product attitude on Facebook, and purchase and recommendation decisions in a linked e-commerce site. The results indicate that Facebook likes exert a positive inter-site influence on users' likelihood of purchase and recommendation on the linked e-commerce site, through the initial pro duct attitude developed on Facebook. Further, as an additional analysis we compare our results with the case of direct transactions on the ecommerce site. We find that the strength of Facebook likes becomes a decisive factor in users' e-commerce purchases and recommendations. Low number of Facebook likes does not demonstrate suficient strength to sway users' decisions. This implies that mere presence of a social cue (Facebook likes) does not provide any extra leverage over direct ecommerce, and the value of SNS-driven e-commerce can truly be reaped if the volume of the social cue on the SNS is significantly high.

We attempt to provide a possible explanation of the observed results using the information integration theory. According to the theory individuals combine several co-acting information from diferent sources to produce a response, in terms of attitudes, preferences, behavior, etc. [56]. Multiple information cues are integrated on the basis of their respective valence (positive or negative) and weight (strong or weak) [57]. Information has a positive valence if its presence strengthens individuals' existing attitude and a negative valence if it contradicts that attitude. The weight relates to the degree of influence. ## 8.2. Contributions to IS research

This study contributes to the IS research in many ways. First, it adds to the s-commerce literature by establishing the inter-site influence of Facebook likes, particularly in a unique setting of SNS-driven e-com merce. Despite discrete anecdotal evidences suggesting the role of Facebook likes in the success of SNS driven online purchases, to the bes of our knowledge, our study is the first one to investigate it by simu lating the phenomenon in a controlled experimental setting. It also expands the existing research that mainly investigates the effects of Facebook likes in intra-site settings, i.e., Facebook likes appearing on same the site where users make their purchases, e.g., group buying sites [6,15] or a retailer's website [10] displaying Facebook likes on an item. In our research we focus on the inter-site influence of Facebook likes that is becoming increasingly relevant with SNSs and e-commerce sites getting heavily interconnected, amplifying the possibility of social cue on SNS to impact users' decisions on linked e-commerce sites. Second, using controlled experiments we successfully establish the causal efects of Facebook likes on users' product attitude, and purchase and re commendation decisions. The results of our study not only corroborate the findings of prior studies that show a positive relationship between Facebook likes and users' perceptions and actions $[ 6 , 1 0 , 1 3 , 1 7 ]$ , but also extends extant research that has predominantly established mere as sociation between Facebook likes and sales $[ 6 , 1 5 , 1 7 ] .$ . Thus, our study also contributes to the broader area of research on the role of SNSs in user decision making [58,59]. Third, we add to the literature of scommerce by establishing Facebook likes as a potent source of socia proof. This expands the existing literature that has primarily considered online reviews and ratings as the dominant forms of social proof [38,60,61]. We also extend the concept of attitude transfer in the do main of s-commerce. Any s-commerce purchase typically involves multiple social sites (e.g., social media, blogs, etc.) apart from the ‘buying and selling’ website. Thus, various information cues from dif ferent sites are expected to shape users' attitude, leading to the possi bility of transference of attitude from one site to another. The attitude transfer hypothesis can help us understand shaping and reshaping of users' product attitude and thereby their final purchase decisions in scommerce that involves myriad social information cues from various websites. Finally, by comparing SNS driven e-commerce to direct ecommerce, our study provides empirical evidence on the value of SNSs in e-commerce purchases. It shows that if suficient in number, social media cues (Facebook likes) can create business value by shaping users decisions in a positive way. The comparison helps in demonstrating the additional value that SNSs bring into s-commerce transactions. Thus, it advances the stream of research that examines the economic value of s commerce, specifically of SNSs as commercial and advertising channels [27,28], contributing to the research on business value of SNSs [62,63]. Our additional analysis ofers deeper understanding about strength of Facebook likes, which is an underexplored area of research. We observe that, similar to conventional forms of e-WoM (online reviews, ratings, etc.) [37], the strength of Facebook likes is also determined by its number, but is only functional after crossing a certain threshold value

## 8.3. Implications for businesses

Our study helps e-tailers with practical insights on how to tailor their social media strategies for enticing more users to their sites and creating positive attitude towards their oferings. First, the study show that Facebook likes, a common social cue, positively influences users' ecommerce decisions including their intention to purchase and recommend a product. E-commerce sites should leverage this type of social cues to their advantage by either integrating Facebook likes into their websites, or by posting advertisements from their business pages on Facebook to garner additional social proof. In this regard, we acknowledge the recent initiative of some of the SNSs (e.g., Instagram) to hide the actual number of likes on posts and show only an approximate number in order to reduce unhealthy social comparison among users and improve users' mental well-being.7 While we appreciate the initiative, it testifies the undeniable social power of likes which should not be missed out by the marketers and s-commerce retailers. There fore, we recommend the SNSs to hide the exact number of likes in case of personal posts only and continue displaying the information on sponsored posts and advertisements. Thus, marketers would be able to enjoy the power of likes for commercial purposes only, without compromising people's mental health. Second, since a higher number of Facebook likes is observed to supplement the efect of aggregated product rating on users' product evaluation, it can benefit products that have low ratings on e-commerce sites. We observe that Facebook likes can somewhat mitigate the unfavourable product attitude formed due to low product ratings. Therefore, for products sufering from the vicious cycle of low aggregated rating and hence low sales, may break the cycle by the leverage provided by a higher number of Facebook likes. However, the number of Facebook likes should be sufficiently high to drive users' attitude and purchase decision significantly. Third, e-tailer expecting more footfall on their websites through SNSs should strive to increase the social validation of their products and proactively seek avenues to garner more likes on their oferings. One of the ways could be by increasing visibility on the SNS, frequently posting advertise ments of their oferings, and soliciting likes from users through ap pealing content or messages. Finally, since we find that a lower numbe of Facebook likes fails to provide extra edge over the “no likes” con dition, products receiving a smaller number of likes on SNSs may not drive enough trafic and, in turn, enough sales on the linked e-com merce site. Marketers are therefore recommended to be judicious abou the selection of products that they intend to display as sponsored post on SNSs. Advertising on SNSs has a significant cost. Thus, marketers should focus only on the products that are highly popular and have the potential to garner a higher number of likes.

## 8.4. Limitations and future research directions

The study has certain limitations that can be addressed through future research. First, we theorize and test our hypotheses for a single type of social cue, i.e., Facebook likes. Our findings can be general izable for other forms of one-click information on other social media sites such as love reaction on Instagram, +1 on Google Plus, etc. Future research can validate our findings in other social media facilitated e commerce transactions, especially Instagram driven e-commerce as Instagram is becoming increasingly popular among users, particularly the millennials, and companies as well. Moreover, as we know that information from multiple sources exert greater social influence it would be interesting to examine how Facebook likes and Instagram love reactions simultaneously influence SNS-driven e-commerce, and whe ther they have diferential impact, i.e., whether the influence of each type of social cue is contingent upon diferent purchase situations (e.g., purchase timing, product involvement, etc.). Second, as with any ex perimental research, the external validity of our study is potentially limited. Using four diferent products in the experiments improve generalizability to some extent. and also counter the carrv-over effects. However, all products that we chose for the experiments were utili tarian and low involvement in nature. Carrying out the experiment with hedonic or high involvement products may generate additional in sights. Also. field experiments on real SNSs and e-commerce sites can be carried out to get insights that are even closer to the reality. Third, additional analysis reveals that a lower number of Facebook likes fail to uplift individuals' likelihood of purchase and recommendation as compared to the baseline (no like) condition, whereas a higher numbe of Facebook likes significantly boost individuals' likelihood of purchase and recommendation. Therefore, a threshold value of likes should exist, above which Facebook likes exhibit considerable power in influencing users' decisions, Future research may identify the threshold value of Facebook likes and provide a more nuanced understanding of its strength. Fourth, in this study we do not hypothesize efects of intention to visit the linked e-commerce site on purchase and recommendatior decisions as the conversion of visit into purchase (and hence post purchase) behavior is not straightforward and depends on many factors including customer type. visit type. individuals' purchasing threshold. etc. [64]. Future research may explore this relationship after taking into account these factors. Finally, this study underlines the value of s commerce by establishing the efects of Facebook likes on e-commerce purchases at an individual level. Researchers may take it one step ahead and ascertain the value of s-commerce at an organization level. Future studies may compare organizational outcomes, such as sales, for e commerce companies before and after they adopt SNS-driven e commerce strategy. This will help us understand whether users' purchase intention, induced by social media cues, actually translates into sales.

In conclusion, this study shows that social cues on SNSs, such as likes on Facebook, if suficient in number, can create business value by afecting users' initial product attitude, their intention to visit a linked ecommerce site, and more importantly, their product purchase and recommendation decisions. Therefore, we can say that Facebook likes do matter. The study makes a novel attempt to establish the inter-site influence of social cues, thus underlining the significance of SNSs in e commerce and substantiating the recent success of s-commerce.
