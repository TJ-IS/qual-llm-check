# Information technology productivity: in search of a definite observation

Effy Oz

Management Division, School of Graduate Professional Studies, The Pennsylvania State University, Great Valley, 30 E. Swedesford Road, Malvern, PA 19355, USA

Received 10 July 2003; received in revised form 22 July 2004; accepted 22 August 2004 Available online 30 September 2004

## Abstract

The perceived phenomenon that huge investments in information technology (IT) over the past four decades have yielded a very small gain in productivity has been dubbed the ‘IT paradox’. Researchers have tried various ways to prove or disprove the paradox. This article highlights the challenges that researchers have faced and proposes a simple theory to explain what seems to be a vanishing contribution of IT to productivity growth. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology; IT paradox; Productivity

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, scores of articles and books have been devoted to the issue of information technology (IT) productivity. More specifically, some of these addressed the IT productivity paradox, the notion that, despite huge investments in IT, the technology has not yielded significant productivity gains. Strong statements were made about IT productivity, from Solow’s [38] ‘You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics’ which he recounted 13 years later [43] to ‘there’s not a shred of evidence to show that people are putting out more because of investments in technology’ a decade later [37]. The issue continues to be debated as is evidenced in the stream of articles for and against Carr’s assertion that IT does not matter [13,14].

The paradox was raised by studies that used production theory. The researchers tested the correlation between IT expenditure and productivity. These found either no positive correlation [4,25] or that the costs exceeded the benefits [28]. The great majority of the studies found that investment in IT did not bring about productivity growth or yielded only a very low increase. The studies and resultant conclusions addressed individual firms, whole industries, and national economies. In the majority of studies, ‘investment in IT’ was defined as national investment in hardware, value of IT ‘stock’ for the sampled years, other national statistics of dollar investments in IT, corporate budgets for hardware and software, or whatever the sampled firms defined in their own accounting as IT expenditures. While many economists used a variety of methods to measure growth in productivity [5,7,9,10,19,23,32–36], many spoke of it in a more general manner. Under the umbrella of ‘productivity’, they have measured profitability, revenue, market value of the firm, return on investment on individual projects, and performance, defined differently as a mix of many of these measurements or a combination thereof. For example, Navarrete and Pick [29] argued that the IT productivity paradox did not exist in the Mexican banking industry during the period 1982–1992. They based their argument on a finding that increased investment in IT was positively associated with the industry’s net profits and return on assets.

Economists distinguish between labor and multifactor productivity. Labor productivity is the ratio of the output of goods and services to the labor hours devoted to producing that output. Multifactor productivity relates output to a combination of inputs used in production, including labor and capital or labor, capital, energy, and materials. It seems that the greater interest of businesses is in labor productivity, especially in developed countries where labor is a relatively expensive resource. Thus, here we addressed studies that attempted to measure the contribution of IT to labor productivity (referred to here as ‘IT productivity’): an increase in the ratio of units of products or services produced to the amount of labor spent on the production of those units due to procurement of IT.

## 2. What has been measured?

While discussions of findings in many studies speak of productivity, many do not measure it but actually measured other things. Some researchers tried to evaluate the ‘value’ of IT. However, value is defined in different ways by different people, and, as Lucas [26] wondered: ‘what is value?’ Even if it were well defined, it is doubtful that we could measure it directly in solid economic terms. Value could be considered as: better decision-making, employee satisfaction, better customer service, etc. Some of these may indirectly contribute to productivity, some may not, and some cannot be measured or deemed to result in a change of productivity.

Some studies [39,41], speak of productivity but measure the relationship between investment in IT and corporate earnings. However, increase in revenue and/ or profit may or may not have to do with investment in IT. In any case, change in revenue or profit is not change in productivity, and the former are poor surrogates for the latter.

A handful of studies attempted to measure the linkage between IT expenditure and real output [6,23], but they were not conclusive despite the effort of the researchers in using detailed data and rigorous quantitative analytical techniques, such as production functions.

Brynjolfsson and Yang [11] surveyed over 150 studies; from these they concluded that, in the 1980s and early 1990s, not only was the link between IT and productivity inconclusive, but also that measuring such a link was practically impossible due to lack of data and use of inadequate analytical methods. They concluded with optimism about both better data and more rigorous methods, but there does not seem to be more detailed data now than when they wrote the article.

## 3. What is information technology?

The answer to the question ‘what is information technology?’ is simple: it is hardware, software, telecommunications, and the services of IT personnel, whether employed in-house or purchased. However, few studies have accounted for all these elements. In fact, a large number have relied on a single element: hardware. The reason is simple: it is the only element on which there are reliable government statistics in the U.S. and some other countries. The strategic planning institute has accumulated data on IS services, which has been used in by Strassman. It is, however, difficult to assess the reliability of those figures. In their 1999 study, Bynjolfsson and Yang did include investment in software and worker training, but assessed firm value (as the stock market valuation) rather than labor productivity.

Estimates of the annual contribution of investment in computers to total product output have exceeded \$ 0.60 output per dollar invested [17,24]. Brynjolfsson and Hitt used Cobb–Douglas production functions to search for return on IT investment. They found that, for the sampled firms (all with 5 year average sales of over \$ 7 billion), the rewards were great: a marginal gain in product of \$ 0.81 for every dollar spent on IT capital and \$ 2.62 on every dollar spent on labor of IT personnel. While the approach—using production functions and marginal product rather than regression functions and profits—may be proper for such research, the study suffers from one shortcoming found in all such studies: partial data; they used only IT capital and IT personnel as ‘IT investment’. Perhaps, this is the reason for the huge return on IT investments. Indeed, their conclusion drew criticism as the greater and growing part of IT over the past two decades was software and IT services, not hardware. IT services include installation of ERP systems by consultants, which usually cost millions of dollars per system. In addition, companies spend huge sums on the implementation of telecommunications, which were not considered in the study. An analysis of data from 800 large firms suggested that the ratio of non hardware expenditure to hardware expenditure was 9– 1 [22]. Thus, up to 90% of the investment in IT was not considered in most of the studies of IT productivity. One exception is a study conducted by Oliner and Sichel [30] in which they calculated the contribution of software and telecommunication to labor productivity but cautioned about the limitations of their calculations: they captured only approximated sources of output growth and they could not explain why growth slowed in the 1970s.

The exclusion of some IT spending data from studies was not due to lack of attention on the part of researchers. Obtaining the figures was practically impossible; spending on software, IT services, and telecommunications has not been collected and tabulated by government agencies or trade organizations in a consistent manner that aids productivity research without controversial manipulation of the data. This is also one reason why many researchers have focused their attention on individual firms rather than national economies. Some companies account for IT investments in a different way than other capital investments. And if researchers cannot find data on actual expenditure, they often rely on budgetary data as a proxy. However, the definition of IT expenditure may vary across firms.

## 4. Where has profitability vanished?

In terms of IT profitability, businesses have experienced what they have happened with any other new technologies. The economics of a new technology follows a simple cycle: economic profit thanks to its use by the particular firm or a small number of businesses, the spread of the technology throughout the industry, the reaping of productivity gains (which is diminishing), a continuous decrease in prices of the products (enabled by the productivity gains), and, eventually, a larger quantity of the products sold for a lower price at a low profit margin. When we get to the end of the cycle, no productivity gains accrue. At this point, businesses must adopt the new technology to survive.

A ‘mature technology’ is one with which workers have become comfortable and which they perceive as an integral part of their work environment. In a similar way, we can speak of the economic maturity of technology, which occur when it moves from a novelty to a necessity. Thus, we should not expect significant or even any productivity gains from a mature technology; it now has no ‘innovation benefits’.

Does any company realize productivity gains when it uses telephones or fax machines? Not now. But early adopters might have seen productivity gains in the first few years of its usage. If today a firm does not use PCs, it will simply not survive.

Two centuries ago, the majority of the workforce consisted of farmers and people who worked with them. Now, in the United States only 2% of working Americans work on farms. They feed Americans better than a century ago; as well as millions in other countries. The reason is better agricultural equipment and better methods. But why should a farmer expect productivity gains from employing a tractor, combine, or computer-controlled sprinkler?

## 5. Research challenges

Robert M. Solow’s statement on productivity statistics raises the question, ‘What statistics?’ Do they really measure productivity and are they meaningful in such inquiry? This is not to say that researchers’ attempts were not worthy or did not show anything about some aspects of IT productivity. On the contrary, they were worthy of appreciation for their continued pursuit despite the difficulties.

Productivity is the efficiency of input. Input may be one of various resources, but the most important is labor. Thus, we use the term ‘productivity’ to mean ‘labor productivity’. To measure the contribution of a certain technology to productivity, we must examine the relationship between units of investment in the technology and the increase of output units over the same period of time. However, using the dollar value of outputs as a proxy for output units severely distorts the picture. In addition, the period over which the study occurs may impact the result: if we measure over the first 3–4 years of using the technology, we may find significant productivity gains because only a few pioneering organizations are using the technology and they may have enjoyed increased revenue as a result of producing an increased number of output units at prices that have not significantly changed. But if the measure takes place over a period of 10–15 years, the prices of the output may have decreased and revenue (and therefore, output dollars) may have not have changed or even decreased.

If it is necessary to measure output dollars, the results would reflect a more accurate picture if the procedure were to perform a cross section study of a large sample of technology users over a short period of time rather than a longitudinal study. This would minimize the distortion of revenue measurement because price changes were smaller. When longitudinal research is done, price inflation must be included. Indeed, some researchers have done so [27]. In virtually all these, the researchers used GDP price deflators. Some accounted for prices change by using the Producer Prices Index or Consumer Price Index. However, such price deflators include a large number of goods and services in their ‘baskets’, and only a fraction of these are the goods and services of the sampled businesses with a smaller portion representing IT goods and services. Thus, the researchers might have underinflated or overinflated prices. The U.S. bureau of economic analysis publishes qualityadjusted deflators for computer prices, but no such deflators are published for other components of IT, such as software, training, and services.

Studying across a large sample for a shorter period of time would yield more accurate results than using deflators which do not really deflate accurately either the specific output of a firm or the prices of the inputs for IT. IT investments are capital expenditures that include items whose prices have not changed at the same rate over the years.

## 5.1. Challenges in measuring IT productivity

There is a certain irony in trying to measure IT productivity. IT has been adopted faster and has probably contributed more productivity in service than in manufacturing sectors. Yet, it is almost impossible to measure productivity gains in service sectors.

## 5.2. Challenges in measuring output

In their study of the relationship between IT expenditures and productivity in service areas, Quinn and Baily concluded that the seemingly minuscule gains in measured productivity were biased because the data were flawed. Thus, how is it possible to measure gains in service units? Service hours are a bad measure because the importance is the service that can be provided in an hour. Although some companies do consider the number of customers served per hour or how the patients seen by a doctor per hour, these ratios can vary due to policy change rather than the technology used.

The task is easier when the product is physical, such as barrels of oil or cubic feet of lumber. In some industries, output can be quantified relatively easily although the output is a service; e.g. in the shipping industry output is measured in miles and ton-miles of truck-load and less-than truck-load but here, the output is a single product. Many businesses do not produce a single item but an array of items and this confounds the task of measuring overall productivity; to avoid use of monetary values, productivity must then be measured separately for each item, and the overall productivity of the business (or industry) must be a weighted average.

Admittedly, even when products seem to be homogenous, there may still be some variations in the products due to different levels of quality. For example, a ton-mile of delivery may be on time or late, and the delivered goods may arrive damaged in 5% or only 1% of the deliveries.

However, output is measured, the use of a monetary value makes interpretation difficult. The number of dollars received for the output has little to do with an organization’s ability to produce more or fewer units. The same barrel of oil may be sold for \$ 24 today, but was sold for \$ 32 several months ago. This does not mean that the producer became less productive. Thus, to measure the impact of IT on productivity, physical output units should be used.

In their efforts, researchers have used deflators to bring output units expressed in dollars to a common base year. But this may not yield real output figures. The study that came very close to using real output in calculating IT productivity was conducted by Allen [1]. Measuring real units of electrical power output per employee in the U.S. electric utility industry, he found that about 5 years after significant IT investment, the output of this industry rose while the number of personnel decreased. As the prices of output in this industry did not change much over those years, the distortion of real output is minimal.

## 5.3. Challenges in measuring input

The output of the lumber industry, the numerator of the ratio, is cubic feet of lumber. The input is IT resources. Apparently, there is no way of using physical units to measure input. In most studies, the researchers used ’computing machinery’ as the input variable. For example, Brynjolfsson et al. [8] used the Bureau of Economic Analysis category ‘Office, Computing and Accounting Machinery’, which consists mainly of computers (and other equipments such as calculators). This is a shortcoming in IT productivity studies. The financial value in the IT ‘basket’ has consistently decreased over the years. Purchased software, software development, and software maintenance have become a larger component of IT for many years. At the least, the IT input figures used in any productivity study must include: computing hardware, telecommunications hardware and software, purchased software, software development, consulting services, and personnel training.

Evidently, IT input is a diverse group of products and services. Researchers must therefore use the aggregate monetary value of such services for each sampled business (or industry) that they include in the study. The problem of using monetary values again surfaces. A computer that cost \$ 2000 5 years ago provided one tenth of the computing power (speed, internal memory, etc.) of a computer that can be purchased today for half the price.

Even when using nominal values of IT expenditures, there is a formidable challenge. Obtaining the figures soon prove to be practically impossible to justify. Few companies or industries in the U.S. or elsewhere maintain expense accounting of these items. One surrogate, or proxy, variable for IT expense can be a company’s total annual IT budget. Again, obtaining such figures is extremely difficult, if not impossible. Many companies regard their investment as confidential. Also the figures are not always available; some of the costs are buried in non-IT accounting or budget line items.

## 6. The issue of quality

Several economists have noted that measuring only productivity gains of new technologies distorts the picture. Even if we do not gain more output, the output of new technology may be better than that of older technology. When using IT, we increase not only the output of products and services, but also their quality. The products are more durable, their mean time between failures (MTBF) is longer, and they have some qualities that their predecessors did not. For example, it would be foolish to compare a 2004 auto with a 1930 car. Thus, to calculate the benefits of car manufacturing technologies we must not only measure how many more cars we can produce now per day than we could with 1930 technology, but also their difference in quality.

The same approach is true when measuring the contribution of IT. But is its contribution to quality computed? This is not easy to answer. There are simply too many intangible elements.

## 7. A simple theory

Companies continue to invest in IT. It is doubtful that executives would risk their careers and continue to make such investments year after year had they not noticed that IT adds some value to their organization (value may not necessarily be in the form of productivity gains). Thus, at least at the microeconomic level, perhaps ‘the proof of the pudding is in the eating’.

Economic historians have more modest expectations of the value of new technology. They are less interested in productivity at the firm level and more in the macro-economic measure of productivity. However, they take a longer view of developments.

David [15,16] compared the seemingly insignificant productivity gains of computers to those of electrical power in the U.S. in the years 1899–1920. He explained the slow incremental productivity gains attributed to electricity with the long-time it took business to adopt electrical power and argued that the spread of computers has been similarly slow. In fact, the diffusion of IT has been significantly faster, especially thanks to the tremendous improvement of computing power and its falling prices [42]. Yet, while economists measured significant productivity increases in individual firms, their measurement of productivity gains from either technology was mixed at the macro-economic level.

Gordon observed that, despite the list of innovations during the first industrial revolution of 1760–1830, little productivity gains have been measured. To judge by recorded productivity figures, the steam engine, the power loom, and other great inventions of that era affected only slightly increased productivity in the 19th century. However, he also observed that the second industrial revolution (1860–1900), in which the electric and internal combustion engines were invented, led to measured productivity growth from 1913–1972.

The discrepancy between a contribution to productivity by IT at the firm level and the lack of contribution at the macro-economic level suggest a model that may explain such ‘lack of productivity statistics’. Our model explains the discrepancies between macro- and micro-economic observations of IT productivity: the lack of measured productivity in macro-economic IT-related productivity along with positive micro-economic IT productivity.

Our proposed model draws on century-old observations. While it may be possible to measure the contribution of a specific technology to productivity gains at individual firms, it may be a daunting task to measure productivity gains attributed to technologies that have matured and are now widespread.

## 7.1. Phase 1: adoption of IT

A firm or a small number of firms adopt a new IT: an innovative piece of hardware, a software application, etc. In some cases, the adopter was also the developer of the IT, in which case the long-term potential for first mover benefits is greater. The technology either supplants existing technologies to efficiently produce the same products or services or enables adopters to produce new products or services. Adopters may enjoy significant increase in productivity.

## 7.2. Phase 2: increased profit

Some of the adopters of new IT experience the ‘bleeding edge’; their investment does not yield productivity gains and profits because of difficulties in adjusting to it. However, other companies experience increase in profit, especially if the technology is proprietary and cannot be easily copied. Still, much of the adopted IT can be acquired by competitors. All the firms that use the technology experience increased productivity. They can produce more with the technology than without it. DosSantos and Peffers [18] proved that, in the banking industry, first movers who invested in ATMs in 1972–1974 enjoyed increased market share and increased income; however, banks that invested in the same technology after that did not enjoy benefits.

## 7.3. Phase 3: new becomes standard

To stay competitive, all firms in an industry must adopt the technology and use it successfully though implementation of early adopters often fails. The IT gradually, or sometimes, quickly, becomes standard. The technology matured: companies are comfortable using it because their employees are skilled enough to use it, and it is widely available through emulation or sale by the developers. The technology becomes ubiquitous. Firms cannot survive without it. In the banking industry, for instance, all banks adopted ATM technology. As of the late 1970s, the technology had become standard and necessary.

Examples of other matured information technologies include Web-based transaction systems and Websearch engines. IT that are maturing and will become standard include supply chain management (SCM) software and radio frequency identification (RFID) devices.

The ubiquity and commoditization of IT are the core of Carr’s assertion that IT is unimportant in corporate strategy [13,14]. Carr’s article and subsequent book do not rely on scientific research. The logic is attractive but his equating of IT to other infrastructure technologies such as the railroads may be flawed. A railroad is a relatively monolithic and static. There are no variations of the technology to allow one company to adopt a different railroad technology from another’s. There has not been significant development in the technology. Information technologies, on the other hand, are practically many different technologies. Almost every month regularly, we witness the emergence of a new technology within IT. It takes time until a particular one becomes ubiquitous and this allows early adopters enough time to use it while their competitors do not, reaping strategic rewards if they succeed.

## 7.4. Phase 4: decreased prices

Firms transform much of their productivity gains into price cuts. They can now afford to offer their products and services for lower prices because the production cost per unit is lower. Once the technology has matured and become standard, the investment may not result in economic gain. In fact, some firms may lose money despite their adoption of IT. Companies that do not adopt the technology are at a disadvantage.

## 7.5. Phase 5: productivity ‘disappears’

If firms measure their productivity gains in cash, they may find that IT did not contribute any productivity growth. The sales (in monetary terms) may actually be lower than before IT adoption, simply because the price of each unit has decreased so much. This is the nominal picture at the individual firm. However, productivity has increased: the firm now produces more (significantly cheaper) units of its products and services. At the macro-economic level, inadequate GDP and other deflators may be too inaccurate in helping reflect the true gains in productivity. As Griliches [20] noted, mismeasurement of output occurs mainly in industries where IT is used most, such as banking, insurance, etc. One might add another product that would not affect the GDP statistics: software. For many years, software was considered an intermediate good, unlike final goods that are included in GDP statistics. Software is produced with the aid of IT. There have been huge increases in productivity over the years in the production of software.

Interestingly, one study that did not purport to measure labor productivity per se produced evidence that IT has increased labor productivity. Brynjolfsson et al. found that as U.S. firms increased their investment in computers, the size of the firms has decreased. Smaller companies may show productivity gains: they can produce their planned quantities at desired quality with fewer workers.

Researchers have considered only hardware as their IT input but accounted for the lag needed to adjust to the new technology; i.e. for the technology to mature. Apparently, IT has produced labor productivity gains at the macro-economic level. At the same time, it might be difficult to see any measurable productivity at individual firms established just before the study. Such companies were established with a smaller labor force than would be needed to produce the same output several years earlier.

When an IT matures and becomes standard, asking ‘why do we lose money although we use IT?’ is akin to asking ‘why do we lose money although we use telephone and fax?’ A firm may be profitable and not very productive (because technology helps it sell something that its competitors cannot sell); or the firm may actually lose money but be more productive than it was a year ago (because now it uses IT that enables it to make more units with less labor).

## 8. Distinguishing between productivity and strategy

Phase 2 of the model highlights an important distinction between pursuing productivity in general and implementing strategic investment in IT. Often, investment has yielded great strategic benefits.

A model developed by Barua et al. [3] showed that a competitor in a duopoly may reap productivity gains and economic profit from being the first to use IT. And, indeed, in many cases the strategic advantage emanated from increased efficiencies of labor and other resources which were now supported and enhanced by the introduction and integration of IT into business processes. However, there is a difference between strategic advantage and productivity.

## 9. The mechanics of the model

What businesses really want to know is whether they can increase production with the same or smaller number of employees as a result of acquiring IT. Thus, the important factors to measure are labor productivity and the contribution of IT to this productivity. Measuring marginal product is a valid approach to measurement of the contribution of a specific input relative to all other inputs, but it does not explain the rate of contribution of the specific input to overall output. More specifically, the main interest is measuring the contribution of IT to gains of labor productivity. The equation in measuring such gains is:

$$
\Delta \mathrm{LP} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} (\mathrm{IT}) + \beta_ {2} (\mathrm{Cap})
$$

where IT is information technology expenditures (adjusted for price changes) including telecommunications, software, hardware, and services, spent in the years $n - d$ to $n + t - d ,$ , inclusive; Cap is total non-IT capital expenditures from year n to n + t, inclusive; LP is labor productivity; and where

$$
\mathrm{LP} = \frac {\text { physical   output(in   units)}}{\text { number   of   employees(or   number   of   labor   hours   weighted   by   hourly   wage)}}
$$

and

$$
\Delta \mathrm{LP} = \mathrm{LP} _ {n + t} - \mathrm{LP} _ {n}
$$

It takes several years for an investment in IT to have an impact on the output. Let us term this number of years d. While Cap is measured as the cumulative values of this variable between year $n \mathrm { ~ + ~ } t$ and year n, IT is measured as the cumulative value of IT between year n + t  d and year $n - d .$ This last condition is due to the time it takes the IT investment to yield fruit due to training and adjustment. The time lag d may differ across industries. For example, Allen argued that d is 5 years in the electricity utility industry.

Ideally, IT would be expressed as several components of real input units, and, therefore, as several independent variables. However, this is practically impossible, because IT consists of numerous different types of hardware, software, etc. with in-house and purchased services.

The figures must be dollar amounts invested in each of the above IT inputs deflated to the base year. This may be a daunting task because there are no reliable statistics from which such deflators can be extracted, let alone the fact that the quality of hardware, software, and other IT inputs has increased over the years. Again, we have the issue of input quality. Comparing 2002 hardware and software with 1995 hardware and software is akin to comparing apples to oranges. Since Cap comprises an investment in a myriad of capital equipment and services, using physical units is impractical.

If it were possible to collect the data for the variables, we could run a multiple regression analysis and calculate the betas. The most important, $\beta _ { 1 }$ , would tell us how much IT investments contributed to labor productivity between the years n and $n + t .$ However, obtaining data appropriate for this analysis is practically impossible. In one attempt [31], the researchers succeeded in collecting data on 12 years of trucking in the U.S. Several thousand dollars were spent on the data and its adjustment for analysis.

Difficulties emerged as soon as the researchers tried to collect data on investment in hardware, software, telecommunications, and IT services. Fewer than 25 of the sample’s 1000 firms responded. An investigation revealed that, in most companies, such data are not readily available.

If researchers had access to such data, the answer to the question ‘Does IT make a significant contribution to labor productivity growth?’ could be answered.

## 10. Conclusion

Studies of IT productivity have produced inconclusive results. They have not provided unequivocal conclusions either at the firm or the national economy level. However, since the evidence around us is that businesses do use IT and have steadily continued to invest in IT more than they have invested in any other means of production since the late 1970s, economists and IT researchers are still in pursuit of a clear answer to the question: is the contribution of IT to productivity significant?

We have enumerated the difficulties in measuring productivity gains attributed to IT. We have highlighted the inaccuracy of measuring firm productivity. Productivity can be accurately measured only in real terms: gain in real output units per real unit of input, a unit of IT. While measuring real output units is possible in some manufacturing industries and some service industries, it is impractical to use real units of IT. Inaccurate measurement and difficulties in access to appropriate data may continue to hamper the work of IT productivity researchers.

One piece of information that has been sorely missed is a definition of IT resources and its pricing. If government agencies (such as the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis) defined this and tracked its price, researchers could better consider the contribution of IT to labor (and multifactor) productivity.

However, even with a reliable ‘IT Price Index’, we may not be able to reach a definitive conclusion on the contribution of IT to productivity. IT helps to change business processes and the relationships between firms and suppliers, etc. The economic contribution of such fundamental changes is not considered in most IT productivity studies.

Corporate boards of directors have long given up expecting a detailed return on investment calculation for investment in IT. They understand that it is infeasible. They approve investments because they believe in the intangible benefits (such as better decision-making and employee satisfaction).

Effy Oz is a professor of management science and information systems at Penn State University, Great Valley, where he teaches courses on IT management and ethical issues in IT. He has authored several textbooks including four editions of Management Information Systems (Course Technology Inc., 1998–2005), Foundations of E-Commerce (Prentice-Hall, 2002) and, Ethics for the Information Age (McGraw-Hill, 1994). He has also published research articles in academic and professional journals, among which are MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Information & Management, Decisions Sciences, Journal of Business Ethics, and Journal of Computer Information Systems. Dr. Oz is a member of the editorial boards of Encyclopedia of Information Systems, Information & Management, and Journal of Global Information Technology Management. He is a frequent speaker at IT conferences, has conducted research on a number of IT topics, and has been quoted in numerous media including Computerworld, MSNBC, and Los Angeles Times.
