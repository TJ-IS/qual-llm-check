# Factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing hospital readmission penalties: Are they really under hospitals' control?

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Readmission penalties Geo-demographics Hospital ownership Case mix index Health information technology

## A B S T R A C T

The Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) established by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) penalizes hospitals that have excessive readmission rates. Research is needed to determine if there are factors that influence readmission penalties but may be out of the control of hospitals. In this study, we compare a set of geo-demographic, hospital, and quality of care characteristics of hospitals penalized by HRRP with those not penalized, and determine these characteristics' association with the likelihood of penalization as well as the extent of penalties. We collected and integrated data from multiple sources such as the HRRP Supplemental Data File, the Hospital Compare database, CMS Impact <sup>fi</sup>les, the HCAHPS survey, and the U.S. census. We followed a two-step estimation procedure. First, we estimated a logistic regression model to <sup>fi</sup>nd the relationship between various characteristics and whether a hospital was penalized. Second, we estimated a linear regression model to find the relationship between these characteristics and the extent of penalties. Results show that both the likelihood and the extent of penalties varied across several characteristics related to geodemographics (such as location in the census division, urban ys, rural location, and racial make-up of the area) hospitals (such as ownership, bed capacity, teaching status, and case mix index), and quality of care (such as quality of communication by doctors and information provided at the time of discharge). Although quality of care characteristics are under hospitals' control and can be improved, geo-demographic and hospital characteristics are likely consistent over time and largely out of the control of hospitals. The study supports the case for a comprehensive revision of HRRP's scoring methodology to calculate readmission penalties.

## 1. Introduction

The issue of hospital readmission has gained prominence in the past few years in the US healthcare system because of its signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic and healthcare costs [1]. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) started the hospital readmissions reduction program (HRRP) in <sup>fi</sup>scal year (FY) 2013 to reduce the frequency of rehospitalization of Medicare patients [2]. Under this program, CMS started levying <sup>fi</sup>nancial penalties against hospitals with excessive readmission rates. In FY 2013 (starting in October 2012), the program covered only three conditions: acute myocardial infarction (AMI), congestive heart failure (CHF), and pneumonia. The list of included conditions, however, has been growing. Penalties for 2015 included two additional conditions – chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and elective hip or knee replacement – and penalties for FY 2017 also include coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery.

For FY 2013, CMS calculated nation-wide expected readmission rates within 30 days of discharge of patients (who had been initially admitted with one of the three conditions) for a period of three years from July 2008 through June 2011. These nation-wide expected rates were compared with the readmission rates at each hospital over the same time period. Before the comparison, the readmission rates for each hospital were adjusted for a small set of patient-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors, such as age, gender, and comorbidities (other ailments), at the time of initial admission. CMS assessed penalties against hospitals whose adjusted readmission rates exceeded the national averages. Approximately two-thirds of hospitals were penalized in FY 2013; these hospitals had to forgo approximately 290 million US dollars [3]. The maximum penalty levied was 1% of hospital's total reimbursement amount for Medicare patients.

Arguments have been forwarded both for and against HRRP. Arguments in favor of the program center around encouraging hospitals to focus on discharge planning, care coordination, and providing better quality of care [3,4]. There have, however, been a number of criticisms regarding the scoring methodology used for decision-making by CMS [5]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it has been criticized for not taking into account the demographic di<sup>f</sup>erences in hospitals' patient loads [4–7]. For example, hospitals caring for socioeconomically disadvantaged patients or those with severe illnesses or with mental and behavioral health issues are more likely to see readmissions and hence are at a greater risk for in curring penalties. Although out of a hospital's direct control, these factors may play a major role in readmissions. The criterion of 30-day readmissions has been criticized too. It has been argued that more than a sign of poor “hospital quality,” 30-day readmissions re<sup>fl</sup>ect a failure of the health care “system” [8]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the criterion re<sup>fl</sup>ects such shortcomings of the healthcare system as discharging patients without providing them with the required information about when and how to access follow-up care, as well as the lack of coordination of such care [8].

In this study, we evaluated a set of geo-demographic, hospital, and quality of care characteristics that may play a role in readmission rates but are not currently considered in HRRP's scoring methodology. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the research questions that we address are:

RQ1. Do the geo-demographic, hospital, and quality of care char acteristics of hospitals penalized by HRRP di<sup>f</sup>er from those not pena lized?

RQ2. Is there an association between these characteristics and the likelihood of penalization as well as the extent of penalties?

Results indicate that underlying reasons for di<sup>f</sup>erences in read mission penalties may indeed exist at several levels – from an individual doctor at a hospital to large census divisions. Some of these factors, such as quality of care characteristics, can be improved over time and hospitals may have an incentive to do so, especially if doing so can help reduce readmission penalties. Research in health information technology (HIT) provides evidence that readmission rates can be lowered by integrating data existing at various levels and sharing it among providers using electronic health records (EHR) and health information exchanges (HIE) [9–14]. However, other factors such as geo-demographics and hospital characteristics are either inherent to a hospital or tied to its location which likely makes them consistent over time and largely out of hospitals' control. However, such data are readily available in EHR and our study strengthens the case for CMS to utilize it in its scoring formula when calculating readmission penalties.

Legislative attempts to change the formula have considered di<sup>f</sup>erences in underlying local patient populations [15]. Although even most of these attempts have been unsuccessful, the 21st Century Cures Act, passed by the U.S. Congress in December 2016, does include a provision to amend the scoring methodology by including socio-economic and community-level characteristics and applying di<sup>f</sup>erential penalties from FY 2019 [16]. This is a good <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the right direction but our study indicates that there are even broader geographical di<sup>f</sup>erences CMS needs to consider.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: in Section 2, we list the characteristics and provide the rationale for studying their association with likelihood and extent of readmission penalties. In Section 3, we describe data collection, variable measurement, and data characteristics. In Section 4, we present our empirical models and the results of the estimation. We provide a discussion of key results and some limitations of the study in Section 5. We conclude in Section 6.

## 2. Conceptual framework

As mentioned earlier, arguments have been forwarded both for and against HRRP. Arguments in favor are focused on quality of care whereas arguments against the program are focused on factors inherent to the hospital such as its characteristics and the area in which it op erates. Thus, we considered characteristics related to geo-demographics, the hospital itself, and the quality of care provided by the hospital that may be associated with the rate of patient readmissions.

Potential regional di<sup>f</sup>erences in the types and complexity of prevalent conditions, motivations and lifestyles of people, and levels of healthcare quality and culture may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the rate of readmissions [17–19]. Hence, we considered the location of each hospital in one of the nine U.S. census divisions. Next, tertiary-level hospitals that cater to patients in more serious conditions and, as a result, may see more readmissions are usually located in larger urban areas. Hence, we considered whether a hospital is located in rural area, large urban area, or another (smaller) urban area [7,20–23]. Several studies have indicated the strong correlation of patient readmission with demographic and socio-economic make-up of the area. In fact, research has shown the underlying role of these factors across a wide variety of situations ranging from targeting high-calorie foods and beverages to ethnic minorities [24] to widespread consumer racial pro<sup>fi</sup>ling in retailing context [25]. Consequently, we considered the racial make-up (the number of white, black, and Hispanic residents) within each hospital's zip code. We also considered the average income levels within each hospital's zip code [26]. Finally, it is conventional wisdom that higher competition pushes a service provider to improve the quality of its service. For this reason, we considered local competition between hospitals [27].

Among hospital characteristics, the ownership of a hospital, say public or private, and the management of a hospital, say for pro<sup>fi</sup>t or non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t, can also have wide-ranging in<sup>fl</sup>uence on a variety of decisions ranging from whether or not the hospital o<sup>f</sup>ers certain types of specialized care to whether or not the hospital will admit a critically-ill patient at all [10]. These decisions, in turn, will in<sup>fl</sup>uence a hospital's readmission rates. Hence, we considered whether a hospital is government-run (by federal, state, local, or district authority), non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t (private, church-run, or run by other entities), or privately-owned (proprietary or physician-owned) [18,21,23].

We considered three other factors that indicate a hospital's size and resources [7,21,28]. First, we considered bed capacity which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the size and nature of a hospital's patient load. Next, hospitals involved in teaching and training of residents are more likely to see a complex set of patients and, as a result, may see higher readmission rates [7,29]. Thus, we considered the teaching status of a hospital. The case mix index (CMI) of a hospital re<sup>fl</sup>ects diversity, clinical complexity, and the need for resources in the population of all the patients in the hospital. Hence, we also considered each hospital's CMI to see the role of the hospital's infrastructure in dealing with complex cases in readmission [23].

Finally, we considered several quality of care characteristics that demonstrate the role of care providers. Higher quality of care should have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on patient outcomes and may decrease the rate of patient readmission [1,30]. We considered patients' perception of the quality of communication by doctors, nurses, and other hospital sta<sup>f</sup> [31,32]. Additionally, we also considered if patients were provided information about their recovery at the time of discharge, and the proportion of patients who understood this information [33]. Our conceptual framework is presented in Fig. 1.

## 3. Data description

## 3.1. Data and variables

We collected data from several di<sup>f</sup>erent sources and integrated them all into a single dataset. We obtained penalty data from publicly available HRRP Supplemental Data File [34]. We used this data to obtain information on whether a hospital was penalized and how much penalty it paid.

We used street addresses of hospitals to determine their census division. There are a total of 9 census divisions – New England, Mid Atlantic, East North Central, West North Central, South Atlantic, East South Central, West South Central, Mountain, and Paci<sup>fi</sup>c [17]. Because the South Atlantic division has the largest number of hospitals (16.3%), it is used as a reference category in analyses. We used CMS Impact <sup>fi</sup>les to identify if a hospital is located in rural area, large urban area, or other (smaller) urban area [20]. Because large urban areas have the largest number of hospitals (39.4%), it is used as a reference category in analyses. We used 2010 U.S. census data to obtain information on the three largest racial groups – white, black, and Hispanic – and household incomes in each zip code [17]. To assess level of competition for each hospital, we set up a catchment radius of 10 miles, and used street addresses to identify neighboring zip codes in this 10-mile radius. To obtain the number of people per hospital in this radius, we divided the population in the area by the number of hospitals. Following the convention in this stream of research, we median-split the data on each of these variables. For each variable, the lower half acted as the reference category for the upper half in the analyses. Table 1 presents a summary of all variables as well as their descriptive statistics.

We used the Hospital Compare database to identify hospital ownership [35]. For easier interpretation, non-teaching hospitals were used as a reference category. Case mix index was split into quartiles – quartile 1 (below 1.254), quartile 2 (between 1.255 and 1.446), quar tile3 (between 1.447 and 1.645), and quartile 4 (above 1.646). For easier interpretation, <sup>fi</sup>rst quartile was used as a reference category.

We used Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) surveys to obtain the quality of care variables [35–37,44]. Patients' perceptions of the quality of communication by doctors, nurses, and other hospital sta<sup>f</sup> were measured using a fourpoint scale with “always,” “usually,” “sometimes,” or “never” options. Following the convention in this stream of research, only the “always” response was used in a binary fashion. All those who checked this option got value 1 while others got value 0. Hospitals were median-split based on the proportion of “always” responses to each of the three questions. Whether patients were provided information about their recovery at the time of discharge or not was measured with a binary variable. Hospitals were median-split based on the proportion of patients indicating they were provided the discharge information. Finally, the proportion of patients who understood this information was captured using a four-point scale with “strongly agree,” “agree,” “disagree,” or “strongly disagree” as the options. Following the convention, only the “strongly agree” response was used in a binary fashion. All those who checked this option got value 1 while others got value 0. Hospitals were median-split based on the proportion of “strongly agree” response.

## 4. Methodology and results

## 4.1. Methodology

We have two sets of hospitals based on whether they were penalized or not. Furthermore, hospitals that were penalized did not all pay the same penalties – penalties ranged between zero and 1%. For this reason, we used a two-step estimation procedure:

## 4.1.1. Step 1: logistic regression

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we estimated a binomial logit model to <sup>fi</sup>nd the relationship between whether or not a hospital was penalized and the characteristics related to each hospital's geo-demographics, its opera tional characteristics, and the quality of care it provides.

Let Probability $( y _ { i } = 1 | \mathbf { x } _ { i } ) = \phi _ { i }$ be the probability of hospital i being penalized. Then.

$$
\mathrm{Logit} (\phi_ {i}) = \alpha + \beta_ {j} \mathbf {G D} _ {i j} + \beta_ {k} \mathbf {H} _ {i k} + \beta_ {l} \mathbf {Q} _ {i l}\tag{1}
$$

where α is the intercept term, $\mathbf { G D } _ { i j }$ is 1 to j vector of geo-demographic characteristics for hospital $i , \beta _ { j }$ is the vector of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for $\mathbf { G D } _ { i j } , \mathbf { H } _ { i k }$ is 1 to k vector of hospital characteristics for hospital $i , \beta _ { k }$ is the vecto of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for $\mathbf { H } _ { i k } , \mathbf { Q } _ { i l }$ is 1 to l vector of quality of care characteristics for hospital $i ,$ and $\pmb { \beta } _ { l }$ is the vector of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for $\mathbf { Q } _ { i l }$

## 4.1.2. Step 2: Linear regression

In the second step, we considered only those hospitals that were penalized $( n = 2 0 9 1 )$ . We estimated a linear regression model to <sup>fi</sup>nd the relationship between the penalty paid by each hospital and various characteristics:

$$
m _ {i} = \lambda + g _ {j} \mathbf {G D} _ {i j} + g _ {k} \mathbf {H} _ {i k} + g _ {l} \mathbf {Q} _ {i l} + e _ {i}\tag{2}
$$

where $m _ { i }$ is the extent of penalties paid by hospital $i , \lambda$ is the intercept term, $\mathbf { G D } _ { i j }$ is 1 to j vector of geo-demographic characteristics for hospital $i , \pmb { g } _ { j }$ is the vector of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for $\mathbf { G D } _ { i j } , \mathbf { H } _ { i k }$ is 1 to k vector of <sup>g</sup>hospital characteristics for hospital $i , g _ { k }$ is the vector of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for $\mathbf { H } _ { i k } , \mathbf { Q } _ { i l } \mathrm { i } s 1$ <sup>g</sup>to l vector of quality of care characteristics for hospital i, is the vector of coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for $\mathbf { Q } _ { i b }$ and $e _ { i }$ <sup>g</sup>is the error term for hospital i.

The census division in which a hospital is located seems to play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role. For example, compared with hospitals in South Atlantic states (e.g., Florida), hospitals in East South Central states (e.g., Alabama) are almost two-third more likely to be penalized.<sup>2</sup> Hospitals in Mountain states (e.g., Colorado), in contrast, are almost 60% less likely to be penalized than those in South Atlantic states. The extent of penalties also varies based on census divisions – hospitals in East South Central states paid almost 0.11% higher penalties on average. Given that the maximum penalty in FY 2013 was capped at 1%, this is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence that translates into thousands of dollars. These di<sup>f</sup>erences could be related to di<sup>f</sup>erences in the kinds and complexity of prevalent conditions, motivations and lifestyles of people, and levels of healthcare quality and culture. This calls for considering deep-rooted regional di<sup>f</sup>erences in adjusting hospitals' readmission rates. As of now, HRRP scoring methodology does not consider such broad geographic characteristics which may imply unjust penalization of some hospitals for years to come.

Urban vs. rural location also plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role [39]. Compared with hospitals in large urban areas, hospitals in both rural and other smaller urban areas are about a third less likely to be penalized; hospitals in smaller urban areas pay about 0.05% lower penalties too compared with hospitals in large urban areas. This could be because tertiary-level hospitals, which cater to patients in more serious conditions who then are more likely to be readmitted, are usually located in large urban areas.

Among demographic characteristics, the population of black residents is especially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Hospitals in zip codes with a larger black population are almost 30% more likely to be penalized and also pay 0.04% higher penalties. Penalties are lower when the per capita household income in the zip code is higher, which could be because of relatively better post-discharge care of patients. This supports the case made by previous research observing that racial and economic background of patients in<sup>fl</sup>uences their health outcomes. Hospitals catering more to uninsured patients and those covered by Medicaid may be relatively less funded to begin with compared with those catering to economically well-o<sup>f</sup> and insured patients. Not accounting for such factors when calculating penalties understates the quality of care provided by such hospitals. Unfairly penalizing them for serving socially disadvantaged patients could hinder patients' access to care and widen health disparities further [20].

Penalties are also lower when the number of patients per hospital in a zip code (i.e., competition) is higher. This could be driven by hospitals raising their level of service when facing more competitors in the catchment area. It should be noted, however, that previous work has shown con<sup>fl</sup>icting results about the association of readmission rates with per capita distribution of general practitioners, specialties, and nursing homes [21,40].

Overall, the results highlight the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role geo-demographics play in readmission penalties. To reiterate, these factors are not only fairly consistent over time but are also out of hospitals' control. A hospital being penalized due to geo-demographic factors is likely to get penalized every year notwithstanding any improvements attempted in other areas.

Among hospital characteristics, ownership plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role perhaps because it a<sup>f</sup>ects incentives and motivations in providing care [10]. For example, compared with voluntary non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals run by private entities, physician-owned hospitals are about three-quarters less likely to be penalized. This could be because some physician-run hospitals are specialized (say, in cardiac care) and such narrow focus may help them provide better patient care for myocardial infarction and congestive heart failure [41,42]. Others may be located predominantly in wealthier geographical areas or may decline to admit serious patient altogether which again helps reduce readmissions

Bed capacity makes a very signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence. Compared with small-capacity hospitals, medium-capacity hospitals are more than twice as likely and large-capacity hospitals are almost three times more likely to be penalized. Large-capacity hospitals also pay almost 0.07% higher penalties. This could be because larger hospitals usually are tertiary referral centers and located in areas with lower socioeconomic indicators. In support of previous research, results reiterate that compared with non-teaching hospitals, major teaching hospitals are more than twice as likely and very major teaching hospitals are almost six times as likely to be penalized.

Level of case mix index plays a surprising but key role as well. Higher CMI is associated with both signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower likelihood of penalization (by 30 to almost 80% as it increases) and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower penalties (by 0.09 to 0.16% as it increases). Because CMI is calculated on the basis of all kinds of cases managed at a hospital, higher CMI indicates a higher proportion of complex cases. Penalties in 2013 were based on three conditions which had relatively low diagnosis-related group (DRG) weights for CMI calculation. Hospitals with a higher CMI are likely to do a better job of managing patients with these conditions. Hospitals with a lower CMI may have limited resources and heart attack may be one of the more serious conditions they manage. They also may have relatively meager resources for transition care. Thus, although one may expect to see higher readmissions for hospitals with a higher CMI, the reverse, in fact, seems to hold. Overall, the results for hospital characteristics point out the need to consider not-soobvious, inherent, and relatively stable factors that are largely out of hospitals' control in the scoring methodology.

Finally, some quality of care characteristics are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant too. As one would expect, a hospital was about 40% less likely to have been penalized when a higher proportion of patients reported that their doctor “always” communicated well. When patients reported that they were provided information by the hospital at the time of discharge about what to do during their recovery, the hospital was about a third less likely to be penalized and also paid about 0.07% lower penalties. The hospital also paid about 0.06% lower penalties when a higher proportion of patients “strongly agreed” that they understood their care when they left. These results suggest that when it comes to delivering quality service, hospitals have room for improvement by focusing on these factors which should be cost-e<sup>f</sup>ective to implement.

Health information technology is well-tailored to address these is sues. Electronic health records (and health information exchanges that facilitate exchange of these records), whose basic purpose is to use technology to improve healthcare quality at the individual and popu lation level, integrate and share all the relevant data at various levels – patient, care provider, hospital, and spatial. At the individual level, besides delivering better quality of care as evidenced by several studies listed earlier, EHR can also improve patient perception of the quality of care received as captured by the HCAHPS survey. Integration of all relevant information including physician orders, nurse assessments, lab results, post-discharge contacts, and follow-up visits and making them available to all providers in real-time can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve quality of care delivered and patients' perception of care received. At the population level, these data need not exist in silos anymore and are in tegrated to present a complete picture about the health of a particular subgroup. CMS can readily obtain the variables highlighted in the study for consideration in the scoring formula used to calculate readmission penalties. As health information technology becomes more prevalent it will become increasingly indefensible for CMS to ignore the key variables making a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in the readmission penalties that providers have to pay.

CMS should go a step further and consider providing hospitals in unfavorable geo-demographic settings with resources and incentives for dealing with a large proportion of “at-risk” individuals. For example, it could help hospitals to develop and utilize their health information technology resources to improve patient discharge process, follow-up with patients over the phone, provide increased out-patient care to patients discharged recently, provide more nurse care when needed, and foster self-care in disease management [26]. CMS could also provide resources to promote health literacy among the target demographics. This could be facilitated by investing in mobile health (mHealth) applications which are emerging rapidly with the potential to become an important tool in extending the population impact of traditional health services [43]. Also, because of similar adoption rates across races and ethnicities, mobile technology has narrowed the ‘digital divide.’ The near-ubiquity of mobile phones means that mHealth tools have the potential to reach populations that have not been accessible by traditional health services, especially underserved patients and those with limited health literacy [43].

Although these results provide insights on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant but overlooked characteristics on readmission penalties, there are also some limitations to our empirical study. First, to avoid any confound with a hospital's e<sup>f</sup>orts at quality improvement in response to previous years' penalties, we considered the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of penalties only. Although geo-demographic characteristics, and to some extent hospital characteristics, are consistent over time, it would be interesting to see if the <sup>fi</sup>ndings remain consistent over the years. With multiple years of data on readmission penalties now available, it would be worthwhile for future researchers to consider multilevel models for repeated measures. Second, we did not consider information about transitional and post-discharge care in this study. Hence, we cannot speak to the role of post-acute care providers and patients' own behavior in readmissions. Finally, our analyses were correlational and we could not ascertain causal relationships.

## 6. Conclusion

Results suggest that HRRP needs strategic revision. Patient readmission is a complex phenomenon and the di<sup>f</sup>erences in readmission penalties are related to characteristics at several levels – from how a physician interacts with a patient to the location of the hospital in a particular census division. Some of these characteristics are arguably under the control of hospitals but others clearly are not. Quality of care characteristics, which can be improved over time, may be a cost-effective way to reduce readmission rates and penalties. Hospitals may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from investing in health information technology tools and setting up task forces which can recommend and help implement best practices. CMS can also motivate hospitals by highlighting the role of quality characteristics when preparing hospital-speci<sup>fi</sup>c reports. Of course, CMS could provide resources to invest in health information technology and mobile health applications to reach the targeted population subgroups. Doing so would help achieve HRRP's underlying objective of getting better care for patients. The role of geo-demographic and hospital characteristics, which are relatively consistent over time, validates the criticism that some hospitals are being penalized for factors largely out of their control. This strengthens the case for CMS to comprehensively revise HRRP's scoring methodology. The

required data can be readily obtained from patients' electronic health records. Legislative attempts to change the formula have considered di<sup>f</sup>erences in underlying local patient populations [15]. Although even most of these attempts have been unsuccessful, the 21st Century Cures Act, passed by the U.S. Congress in December 2016, does include a provision to amend the scoring methodology to include socio-economic and community-level characteristics and apply di<sup>f</sup>erential penalties from FY 2019 [16]. This is a good <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the right direction but our study indicates that there are even broader geographical di<sup>f</sup>erences CMS needs to consider

His research interests include the causes of regional variations in medical spending, the value of new medical technologies, and innovation in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology in dustries.
