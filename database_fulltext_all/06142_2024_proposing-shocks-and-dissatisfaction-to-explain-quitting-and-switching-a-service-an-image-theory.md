# Proposing Shocks and Dissatisfaction to Explain Quitting and Switching a Service: An Image Theory Perspective

## Abstract

This study sheds light on how shocks, or events perceived as jarring, cause users to discontinue a service. We use image theory to develop a model of discontinuation (MOD), which includes five main concepts: shock, script, image violation, service and task dissatisfaction, and search for and evaluation of superior alternatives. MOD explains how two types of user discontinuation behavior— quitting and switching—are formed along seven paths that are either related to contextual or technological shocks or result from dissatisfaction with a task or service. Our first empirical study shows that more than 95% of the 467 ex-users surveyed followed one of these paths when discontinuing media streaming, social networking, and matchmaking services. In the second study, we developed a research model based on the MOD to understand how the psychological factors associated with shocks lead to the intention to discontinue. We evaluate this model with a scenario study in which we present the situation of users of a matchmaking service and present participants with a contextual shock. Data from 201 individuals show that users are likely to develop intentions to quit when they use an engaged script or experience an image violation. Interviews with a panel of practitioners confirmed that MOD provides a useful and applicable approach to understanding users’ quitting and switching behaviors in different contexts. We conclude our paper with a discussion of implications for research and practice.

Keywords: Image Theory, Shocks, Jarring Events, Discontinuation, Switching, Quitting, Service Use, Scenario Study, Categorization Method, Multimethod Study, Context

## 1 Introduction

Declining user numbers of services and platforms, enabled by information systems (IS), continue to be a pressing issue for companies that depend on user subscription or advertising revenue models. For subscription-based service providers (e.g., Netflix, Tinder), the loss of users directly impacts revenue (Clark, 2018), while for platform providers (e.g., Facebook, TikTok), the loss of users indirectly impacts revenue by undermining their ability to attract advertisers and business partners (Maheshwari, 2018). In both cases, discontinuing such services jeopardizes the financial profitability of the firm and its future stock market valuation.

In this research, we propose a novel perspective that examines perceived shocks,<sup>1</sup> which are jarring events. Modern life is full of shocks that can cause users to change their behavior (Meier et al., 2023). When shocks occur, individuals may adjust how they use an IS or service. For example, the breach of trust following the Cambridge Analytica data scandal led to an apparent spike in individuals deciding to stop using Facebook, including Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak (Swan, 2018). Similarly, when the online matchmaker Ashley Madison suffered a cyberattack (Hern, 2015), the resulting data leak caused many users to discontinue using the service (Bradley, 2015). While shocks do not mean that all affected individuals stop using—Facebook and Ashley Madison, for example, remained viable—such shocks may explain why some discontinue using or paying for services, either because they switch to alternative services of the same type or quit the service altogether.

It seems intuitive that negative shocks can lead to discontinuation. Notably, positive shocks can also lead to discontinuation. Consider the example of users of matchmaking services, who might be motivated to quit after meeting a new partner (LeFebvre, 2017). Other status changes, such as securing a new job or becoming a parent, may lead users to pause or quit using media streaming or social networking services (SNS) altogether (Guyard & Kaun, 2018). Shocks such as childbirth, a new relationship, or changes in living arrangements can be important triggers for users to discontinue using the service.

Understanding the relationship between shocks and user discontinuation sheds new light on user behavior. Examining jarring events, which arguably “form the core of what is called ‘experience’” (Morgeson et al., 2015, p. 515) may be a powerful force driving service usage behavior. Shocks can be emergent, are often unanticipated, occur at specific points in time, and broadly impact individuals (Lee et al., 1996). By studying shocks, we move beyond existing IS research, which conceptualizes user attrition by understanding how a jarring event shapes and changes behavior rather than viewing user discontinuance as the result of ongoing experiences of dissatisfaction or stress while using the service (Appendix A). We facilitate a more nuanced understanding of user discontinuance by examining the impact of jarring events on users’ decisions to quit or switch. Specifically, we ask the following research question:

RQ: How do shocks influence a user to discontinue service usage?

To address the research question, we first develop a model of discontinuation (MOD). Drawing on image theory (Beach, 1990), we formulate different pathways that lead to user discontinuation (e.g., quitting or switching). They are triggered either by contextual or technological shocks or by dissatisfaction with the service. We then conduct two empirical studies to demonstrate how MOD analyzes, explains, and predicts user churn. Study 1 confirms that the seven pathways depicted in MOD lead to discontinuation behavior, using data from 451 ex-users of three relevant contexts: social networking, matchmaking, and media streaming services. In Study 2, we developed a research model based on MOD, focusing on the explanatory power of MOD. Extending Study 1, this study focuses on the psychological factors of users that lead to discontinuation intentions following a jarring event. Finally, we conducted an applicability test in each of the three contexts (i.e., social networking, matchmaking, and media streaming services) to demonstrate the relevance of our research and inform implications for practice.

The remainder of our paper is organized as follows. First, we summarize related research, develop our MOD, and discuss the research methods and results for Study 1 and Study 2. We then present the results of our applicability checks, followed by a discussion of contributions, limitations, implications for future research, and practical implications.

## 2 Research on User Discontinuation

The use of IS and services involves four major stages: adoption, usage, discontinuation, and resumption (Maier et al., 2021b). This research is anchored around the discontinuation stage, which has two distinct types: quitting and switching. Discontinuing a service (or “quitting” for short) means that the user stops using a service, such as social networking services (Maier et al., 2015b). This definition implies that they stop performing the tasks associated with that service. Switching a service (or “switching” for short) means that the user switches from one service provider to another and continues to perform the task in the same or an improved way (Bhattacherjee & Park, 2013; Xu et al., 2014). For example, when Facebook users stop using SNS, they do not use Facebook anymore nor do they start using Instagram or another SNS. Rather, they cease their online social networking activities. In contrast, users who engage in switching behavior stop using Facebook but start using another social networking service provider, such as Instagram, to continue using SNS. In this case, they continue their social networking activity. Thus, switching implies that the individual continues to use the same type of service with a different service provider, whereas quitting implies that the individual stops using the service type altogether.

Our review of user discontinuation suggests that different factors drive decisions to quit or switch (Appendix A, Table A1). The current literature on quitting shows that negative beliefs, such as stressrelated beliefs (Maier et al., 2015a, 2015b) or guilt feelings (Turel, 2015), are drivers of discontinuation. All of the beliefs that have been studied as drivers of quitting direct attention to the specific service that might be quit. For example, the perceived stress of using the service is often modeled as influencing the decision to quit. In contrast, the current literature on switching shows that the causes of switching are related to beliefs about the old service, such as dissatisfaction (Bhattacherjee & Park, 2013; Xu et al., 2014), as well as beliefs about the new service, such as relative attractiveness (Liu & Xiao, 2014; Chang et al., 2014).

Previous literature has primarily used theories such as migration theory, the theory of planned behavior, or social cognitive theory to study quitting and switching (see Appendix A, Table A1). These theories are similar in that they assume that behaviors such as quitting and switching are guided by intentions, which in turn result from beliefs (e.g., perceived usefulness, social overload) (Maier et al., 2015a) or affective reactions (e.g., dissatisfaction, exhaustion) derived from using the IS or service (Maier et al., 2015b; Soliman & Tuunainen, 2022). Thus, these theories show that (among other issues) self-perceived and actual IT knowledge (Aggarwal et al., 2015) and negative disconfirmation (Zamani & Pouloudi, 2021) influence discontinuation. The literature review reveals that existing studies have not examined shocks or overlooked how jarring events shape behavior in the personal or broader context. We also reviewed the literature on switching and quitting in the overall context of services from related disciplines, such as marketing (see Appendix A, Table B2), and found that shocks have not been studied in this context. To fill this research gap, we used a different theoretical foundation: image theory.

## 3 Image Theory as a Theoretical Base

Image theory explains how decisions are made in dynamic environments (Beach, 1990); it assumes that individuals do not substantially and thoroughly evaluate different options in real decision processes, and that behavior is preprogrammed in most situations. Image theory suggests that individuals employ a set of strategies that facilitate decision-making (Beach, 1993). For example, in the context of a matchmaking service, image theory suggests that a user does not evaluate each alternative matchmaking service in detail; instead, the user superficially considers only a few alternatives and does not engage in an extensive cognitive selection process that considers all the factors associated with each service.

Image theory suggests that individuals rely on three predefined schematic knowledge structures, called images, to organize their thinking, make decisions, and guide behavior. First, the value image refers to the constituents of the individual’s principles, including values, morals, ethics, and ideals, and thus describes the individual’s beliefs. The value image serves as an imperative for the individual’s behavior. It provides structured criteria that define whether decisions regarding a goal or plan are right or wrong. People develop value image principles to generate potential goals and plans for behavior and to guide behaviors and decisions regarding their response to those goals and plans. Second, the trajectory image refers to the constituents of previously adopted goals and includes an individual’s goals and aspirations, both of which guide behavior. This image concerns what the individual hopes to become and achieve in relation to specific or abstract events. Third, the strategic image refers to the constituents of the various plans adopted to achieve the goals of the trajectory image and concerns the plans and tactics the individual will use to achieve the goals. Each plan is rather abstract and involves a sequence of potential activities that begin with the adoption of a goal and end with the achievement of the goal. Central to these plans are tactics and forecasts, which, by definition, anticipate the future. Forecasts consider what will happen in the future if tactics are executed. While monitoring forecasts concerning the trajectory image’s goals, individuals evaluate the progress toward realizing the vision of the ideal future on the trajectory image. Returning to our earlier example of using a matchmaking platform, individuals hold different ethical and moral values about acceptable ways to use such platforms for matchmaking—for example, individuals must consider whether a potential matchmaking service considers infidelity to be unacceptable (value image); they define their goals for using the platform—for example, in terms of long-term or more casual relationships (trajectory image); and they use different tactics to achieve their goals—for example, sending many or just a few messages to potential matches (strategic image).

These different images shape two successive decisions: adoption decisions and progress decisions. Adoption decisions concern the adoption or rejection of goals or plans as constituents of the trajectory or strategic images. Thus, individuals may eliminate unacceptable potential matchmaking services based on their value image and select the most promising alternative. Progress decisions examine the fit between the predicted future resulting from using a particular matchmaking service and the ideal future defined by the trajectory image. If there is an incompatibility, the individual can reject the service and evaluate an alternative matchmaking service. If there is no acceptable alternative matchmaking service, the individual may reconsider using such a service.

Both of these decisions are made using compatibility and/or profitability tests. The compatibility test refers to acceptance decisions based on the incompatibility between available potential services and personal standards related to and predefined by the three images. If the incompatibility exceeds a rejection threshold, the potential service is rejected. Otherwise, it is adopted. The profitability test describes the individual’s choice among the remaining potential services; the individual decides which potential service is most promising. Thus, the profitability test focuses on relative advantage. Returning to our example, if a matchmaking service violates an image, it is rejected and a new matchmaking service is considered. When an initial decision is made, users assess the likelihood of realizing their values, goals, and plans with respect to the matchmaking service. If no promising service is available, the overall goal of using a matchmaking service may be reconsidered or abandoned.

Image theory postulates that individuals continuously process new information and use it to reevaluate current behaviors but rarely feel compelled to alter their decisions (Beach & Mitchell, 1990). A major proposition of image theory is that shocks can disrupt individuals’ impetus to maintain a behavior. Shocks are jarring events that cause users to consider changing their behavior (Lee et al., 1999; Meier et al., 2023) and break users out of habitual routines and automatic cognitive processing (Morgeson et al., 2015; Morgeson & DeRue, 2006). Shocks can be characterized along several dimensions, such as whether the shock is foreseen, its nature (e.g., contextual shocks include finding a new job or a new partner, whereas technological shocks include privacy breaches or technological failures), and its valence (Holtom et al., 2005; Meier et al., 2023).

Image theory has been successfully applied in business and IS research (Appendix B; Tables B1, B2). To a small extent, image theory has been used in human resource management (Lee & Mitchell, 1994; Maier et al., 2021a), marketing (Nelson, 2004), and technology adoption research. For example, it has been argued that during the adoption stage, one’s intention to use a service is influenced by perceived usefulness (trajectory image) and perceived ease of use (strategic image) (Davis & Venkatesh, 2004; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). We use image theory to focus on shocks to complement current discontinuation research based on social psychology approaches.

## 4 Model of Discontinuation (MOD)

## 4.1 Image Theory and User Discontinuation

The principles of image theory help to explain discontinuation. First, while users draw on image sets from multiple domains (Mitchell & Beach, 1990), we focus on two domains: the technology itself and the context of use, which refers to aspects of work, family, friends, ethics, and leisure. Second, images differ in the degree to which they can be articulated, their level of clarity, and the extent to which they determine the ease and speed of decision-making (Beach, 1990). Third, individuals use images sequentially in the screening process. New information or potential alternatives are compared with the user’s images. Then, individuals compare potential alternatives to the other images in the order of value, trajectory, and strategic images (Beach, 1990). Fourth, there may be situations in which individuals adjust images rather than accept or reject information (Lee & Mitchell, 1994).

Based on these tenets, we posit that different paths lead users to discontinue use. To differentiate among the possible discontinuation paths, we focus on five concepts related to decision-making (Holtom et al., 2005; Lee et al., 1999)—shock, script, image violation, service/task (dis)satisfaction, and search for and evaluation of alternatives—and contextualize and integrate them in user discontinuation research. While an image violation represents the outcome of the compatibility test, the profitability test indicates whether an alternative is superior. Next, we describe the relevant concepts in detail.

A shock is a jarring, technology-related or contextual event that the user perceives and that causes them to reflect on and evaluate their use of the service. Because events vary in criticality, urgency, and duration, not every event should be considered a shock (Morgeson & DeRue, 2006). Some events have a stronger impact than others (Morgeson et al., 2015); an event must be perceived as jarring by an individual to be considered a shock, which can lead to a reevaluation of service usage. Users can view shocks as positive or negative (de Guinea & Webster, 2013; Meier et al., 2023). Positive shocks represent opportunities (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) for personal growth, development, and well-being (Maier et al., 2021a). For example, the positive shock of a new job could lead to personal growth and increased well-being, which could lead a user to stop using a matchmaking service. In contrast, a negative shock may be perceived by users as threatening (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), leading to concerns about personal loss, new constraints, or harm (Maier et al., 2021a). An example of a negative shock would be a date arranged by a matchmaking service that ended badly, which might cause the user to stop using the matchmaking service. Both positive and negative shocks can lead to user discontinuation. Previous research has discussed different shocks (Meier et al., 2023), including technological failures (de Guinea & Webster, 2013), privacy breaches (Acquisti et al., 2006), and management-imposed time pressure to use an unfamiliar service (Eckhardt et al., 2013).

We distinguish between two types of shocks: those rooted in a technology (technological shock) and those in the surrounding context (contextual shock). Technological shocks can result from the misuse of a service or be caused by a service failing to meet user expectations. Similarly, security issues or privacy leaks may cause users to reconsider using a service. In contrast, contextual shocks typically result from changes in conditions or attributes related to tasks that affect user behavior (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). For example, finding a partner may eliminate the need to find others through a matchmaking service.

A script refers to a person’s preexisting plan regarding a behavior (Holtom et al., 2005; Holtom et al., 2008), such as using a service. It reflects preplanned courses of action so that no substantial cognitive deliberation is required to evaluate current and alternative options (Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2005). For example, a person may have a predefined script that requires them to stop using a matchmaking service when they are in a new relationship or to switch to an alternative service when faced with a data leak. Depending on the existence of a user discontinuation script, we distinguish between users with a script and those without.

Image violations occur when a user’s values, goals, or strategies do not match those of the service or those reflected by the shock (Lee et al., 1999), meaning that a gap appears between what is expected and what actually occurs (Louis & Sutton, 2016; Zamani & Pouloudi, 2021). This is the result of the compatibility test. We focus on the situation in which an image violation is perceived as a threat to the self (Bala & Venkatesh, 2015; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). For example, a person with a “high” self-image will likely assume that a matchmaking service will provide them with many quality matches. Suppose such a person does not receive the expected level of attention. In this case, the user may feel anxious about violating their self-image, which may cause them to reconsider using the service. Depending on whether the image is violated, we distinguish between a violated image and a nonviolated image.

Dissatisfaction reflects users’ affective and cognitive evaluations of the overall use experience (Au et al., 2008). Previous research on user dissatisfaction has distinguished between dissatisfaction with the underlying task for which a service is used versus dissatisfaction with the service itself (Appendix A). By separating the task from technological characteristics (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995), we argue that dissatisfaction in the context of user discontinuation should similarly be disaggregated into whether a user is dissatisfied with the task (task dissatisfaction) or the service (service dissatisfaction). Continuing with our example, task dissatisfaction would describe a user no longer interested in pursuing a romantic relationship online. In contrast, service dissatisfaction describes a user who decides that a specific matchmaking service is not useful, too difficult to use, or not fun enough.

The search for and evaluation of alternatives occurs when users search for alternatives to their current service provider and evaluate whether they are superior (Lee et al., 1999). Users may not be able to find acceptable alternatives or they may already be familiar with potential alternative service providers. In either case, alternatives are not evaluated. However, when alternatives that are particularly relevant to switching behavior are identified, users tend to mentally categorize each alternative as either “yes, superior” or “no, not superior.” This is the outcome of the profitability test.

Paths of user discontinuation: Theoretically, 72 possible and unique paths exist. <sup>2</sup> However, only a subset of these paths are viable in any context. First, we argue that shocks only cause behavioral changes when users are driven by a committed script or perceive an image violation. For example, based on image theory, if an individual perceives a data breach as a shocking event in a matchmaking service they are using but do not have a script for how to respond and do not perceive their image to be violated, they will not stop using the service. This example illustrates the underlying image theory argument that studying behavior requires a nuanced, contextual approach that helps identify all plausible pathways relevant to a given shock, script, or image violation.

Second, we propose that contextual shocks cause an individual to lose interest in continuing the task (i.e., quitting), regardless of overall satisfaction or access to superior alternatives. In contrast, in the case of a technological shock, the user may choose to continue the task if a superior alternative exists (i.e., switching). For example, suppose a user of a matchmaking service finds a new partner, reflecting a positive shock, and is no longer interested in dating. In this case, they will quit regardless of their satisfaction with the service or the existence of alternative matchmaking services.

Thus, it is unnecessary to consider whether there are superior alternatives for pathways with a shock that focuses on task dissatisfaction.

Third, in the absence of shock, the presence of an engaged script or image violation is irrelevant. In this case (no shock present), the discontinuance decision depends only on dissatisfaction with the task and/or service and the existence of superior alternatives. Returning to our running example, users may discontinue regardless of shock-related constructs such as scripts or image violations if they become dissatisfied with a matchmaking service. Therefore, we do not consider such paths.

Based on the above considerations (see Appendix C for more details), we derive six plausible shock-induced paths (two for contextual shocks, four for technological shocks) to describe what happens when a user discontinues a service because of a shock, and one no-shock path (divided into three subpaths) to describe what happens when a user discontinues a service in the absence of a shock. These seven paths are summarized in Figure 1 and divided into three sections: (1) contextual shock and user discontinuation, (2) technological shock and user discontinuation, and (3) no-shock discontinuation. We also provide an example for each path in Table 1.

## 4.2 Contextual Shock and User Discontinuation

We consider two paths that may trigger quitting when a contextual shock exists.

Path 1: A contextual shock jolts the user, who then identifies a predefined script that fits the surrounding circumstances. The user interprets this shock—e.g., its nature and valence (Holtom et al., 2005)—and applies existing rules to respond to the change in status (Lee et al., 1999). The script indicates that the best course of action would be to quit (Lee et al., 1999). In Path 1, the predefined script is activated; therefore, quitting results regardless of image violation, dissatisfaction with the task or service, or the evaluation of alternatives (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). This logic implies that the individual does not perform compatibility and profitability tests.

Path 2: A contextual shock jolts the user, who does not find a comparable shock or rule of action in their memory. In contrast to Path 1, since the shock does not map to a predefined script, more extensive cognitive processing is required (Polites & Karahanna, 2013), causing the user to perform the mental deliberations necessary to decide on service (dis)continuance (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Users may adapt the respective images and continue using the service or quit without considering an alternative. This decision results from the user’s reassessment or updated beliefs (Kim, 2009) based on the value, trajectory, and strategic images associated with the service (compatibility test). Thus, the user must then assess whether continuing the service is still a good value fit, whether the use of the service continues to fit the individual’s goals and aspirations (trajectory image), and whether the activities and efforts associated with the service still facilitate accomplishing these goals (strategic image) (Davis & Venkatesh, 2004; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). If the images fit the service use, the user may adjust the image and continue using the service; however, if the compatibility test indicates a poor image fit, quitting may follow. The presence of contextual shocks implies that individuals do not search for and evaluate service alternatives, i.e., they quit without a profitability test.

## 4.3 Technological Shock and User Discontinuation

We consider four paths that may trigger discontinuation following a technological shock.

Path 3: Regardless of whether a user is satisfied with a service, the script associated with a technological shock tells users not to search for alternatives; instead, they quit service use directly due to the shock. There is a match between this experienced shock and an earlier shock, such that an engaged script exists (Lee et al., 1999). When this existing script tells the user not to search for an alternative (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009) or when a profitability test indicates no superior alternative exists, the user quits using the service.

Path 4: Regardless of the user’s satisfaction with the existing service, the technological shock shocks the user and activates a preexisting action plan that stimulates the search for, evaluation of, and switch to an alternative service. In this case, we assume that the user stops using the service associated with the shock and selects the alternative identified by the profitability test.

Path 5: A technological shock jolts a user who does not already have a script that prescribes a response; thus, the user may respond to the shock by adapting an image or changing behavior (Lee et al., 1999). Quitting results when the user perceives an image violation, as indicated by a compatibility test, but does not search for alternatives or evaluate them as superior. Thus, the (dis)continuance decision reflects users’ reassessment of the service and their updated beliefs based on their value, trajectory, and strategic images. The compatibility tests assess whether the user’s goals are still consistent with continued use (trajectory image), whether the activities and efforts associated with the service are still aligned with accomplishing the user’s goals (strategic image), and whether the service use continues to fit the user’s values (value image). If the compatibility test indicates a lack of fit with respect to any of these images—if the profitability test is not performed (e.g., because there is no alternative) or if the profitability test indicates that there is no superior alternative, the user will decide to quit the service.

Path 6: A technological shock does not activate a script that causes the user to search for alternatives. Instead, the shock causes an image violation, as indicated by the user’s compatibility test. Regardless of whether the user is generally satisfied with the current service, this shock motivates the user to search for, evaluate, and select an alternative (Lee & Mitchell, 1991). Because the user is interested in continuing to perform the task, the user searches for and evaluates alternative service providers. Since the shock and the image violation make it impossible for the user to continue using the service, the user performs a profitability test and switches to an alternative, superior service to perform the task.

## 4.4 Discontinuation without Shock

Paths 7: This path is compatible with existing IS research on discontinuation, which does not consider shocks. In this path, the user does not experience a shock and treats service use as routine (Li et al., 2013). When service features are changed or adapted, it can result in a discrepancy between the service and the user’s corresponding value or trajectory images. Such discrepancies lead users to either adjust the image or experience dissatisfaction. This view is consistent with mainstream IS research on service (dis)continuance. We use this body of research to explain the user discontinuation decisions encountered in Path 7. We separate this path into three distinct discontinuation subpaths, all occurring without an obvious shock (Paths 7.1, 7.2, 7.3), and separately consider the task and the service (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995).

In the first subpath, Path 7.1, when users become dissatisfied with a task, we posit that they quit performing it and thus quit using the associated service. There is no need to continue using a service when performing the task is no longer desired. In the second subpath, Path 7.2., suppose a user becomes dissatisfied with a service but remains satisfied with a task. In that case, the user chooses to quit if no alternatives are available. In the third subpath, Path 7.3., if a user is dissatisfied with a service but satisfied with a task and an alternative service is available, the user evaluates alternatives and switches to the most viable service.

## 4.5 Summary

We propose the image theory-based model of discontinuation (MOD), which consists of seven paths that explain why users discontinue a service in different ways. Comparing these paths with previous research on user discontinuation, we find that previous research has largely ignored shocks and focused on the two “discontinue without shock” subpaths (Paths 7.2 and 7.3). For example, research has shown that the user’s stress (Maier et al., 2015b) or feelings of regret (Chang et al., 2014) cause discontinuation. In these cases, research has operationalized these beliefs as shaping an individual’s ongoing evaluation of service use, typically service dissatisfaction, without considering shocks that may cause quitting or switching. We complement these pathways by highlighting the influence of specific jarring events on individuals’ behavior and their ongoing evaluations of a technology or service. Thus, MOD integrates new pathways (Paths 1 through 6) and known pathways (Path 7) to create a unified explanation of user churn.

Two studies are presented to evaluate the model. Study 1 focuses on the applicability of MOD to analyze and explain actual discontinuation behavior. Study 2 tests whether MOD can predict individuals’ discontinuation intentions when faced with a shock.

## 5 Study 1: Retrospectively Analyzing and Explaining Actual Discontinuation Behavior

In the first study, we used MOD to analyze and explain actual discontinuation behavior. We focused on exusers—who have used a service but quit using the service or switched to an alternative—and followed a categorization method (Lee et al., 1999), as we used MOD to categorize the behavior and motivation of exusers according to seven theorized paths.

## 5.1 Research Method

We surveyed the ex-users of three online services: Facebook (social networking), Netflix (media streaming), and Tinder (matchmaking). Collecting data in three contexts allowed us to conceptually replicate our studies and strengthen our ability to evaluate the MOD.

## 5.1.1 Measures

Whenever possible, we used items from previous research (Appendix E). We measured shock using a binary item (yes/no) indicating whether the subject experienced a shock, along with additional yes/no questions revealing whether ex-users experienced specific shocks (e.g., data leaks, family issues). We also included an open-ended question that asked participants to describe the jarring event that caused them to consider discontinuation (Holtom et al., 2005; Lee et al., 1999). We measured script using three yes/no items (Lee et al., 1999), and image violation using four items on a 7-point Likert scale (Bala & Venkatesh, 2015). We measured task/service satisfaction using four items on a 7-point semantical differential scale (Bhattacherjee & Lin, 2015), and search for and evaluation of alternatives using four items on a 7-point Likert scale (Bala & Venkatesh, 2015; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). To evaluate whether ex-users quit or switched, we used a single item reflecting users’ discontinuation behavior: “Please indicate which best describes your behavior concerning <service> with one of three possible responses: ‘I still use <service>,’ ‘I quit using <service>,’ or ‘I have stopped using <service> and switched to use an alternative.’”

## 5.1.2 Sample

We collected data using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), an online data panel service. We recruited US-based participants with a long history of completing high-quality surveys, indicated by more than 1,000 approved HITs (human intelligence tasks) and an overall HIT approval rate of 90%. Our objective was to collect data from ex-users. Therefore, we used a broad HIT title and asked for general data regarding these services, including whether participants were users or ex-users. These screening questions included—for example, in the case of Netflix—a question to test whether participants (1) currently had a Netflix account, (2) previously had a Netflix account, and (3) had closed their account. These screening questions allowed us to identify ex-users. While exusers received surveys with measures relevant to this study, those who were not former users of the service received a survey with a different focus and did not complete surveys with the measures relevant to this paper. This procedure ensured that participants were incentivized to complete the HIT and that they did not have an advantage by reporting that they were exusers. We also included attention checks, such as “Please answer ‘strongly disagree’ for this item,” to eliminate invalid responses and ensure that no participant completed multiple surveys (Lowry et al., 2016).

We navigated participants through the questionnaire so that they could only complete the survey for one service. Our final sample included approximately 150 ex-users for each of the three services. Sample characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

## 5.2.1 Categorization Rules

We used measures from the existing literature as input and applied categorization rules. Drawing on related research using categorization rules (Lee et al., 1999), we applied the following procedures to our data (more information is reported in Appendixes C and D).

Shocks: Participants had to provide appropriate yes/no answers to the overall and detailed shock questions to be included in the sample (Lee et al., 1999). We included an open-ended question in which ex-users could specify the shock in detail (Table 3). Answers to this question were coded independently by two of the authors to determine whether the shock was technological or contextual. We also mapped the coding results to participants’ responses, asking whether the shock was technological or contextual. If ex-users reported more than one specific shock and the shocks were of different types, we used this variable to assess whether the primary reason for discontinuation was due to a contextual or technological shock.

Engaged script: We used responses in a yes/no format. If an individual answered at least one of the questions about engaged scripts with “yes,” it indicated an engaged script.

Image violation: We used four items on a 7-point Likert scale (1-strongly disagree to 7-strongly agree). Using four items with high consistency, a mean value of greater than 4 for all four items indicated a violated image.

Task and service dissatisfaction: Both constructs were measured on a 7-point semantical differential scale (1-very satisfied to 7-very dissatisfied). We calculated mean values for both constructs and categorized ex-users as “dissatisfied” when their mean values were above 4 (indicating task/service dissatisfaction).

Search for and evaluation of alternatives: We also measured the search for and evaluation of alternatives on a 7-point Likert scale. Mean values over 4 indicated that individuals identified another service to be superior.

## 5.2.2 Categorization of User Discontinuation to the Suggested Paths

Based on these categorization rules, we mapped the presence or absence of the five concepts according to ex-users’ previous decisions to discontinue a service. Specifically, we analyzed (1) whether the participants answered “yes” or with a high mean value (i.e., values above 4) to one or more questions related to each concept, (2) whether participants’ other answers contradicted previous responses, and (3) whether the behavior of the participant, in terms of quitting or switching, matched the behavior of the relevant path. ## 5.2.3 Validity of Results

Consistent with IS research using Likert scales, we assessed the content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability, and discriminant validity of our measures (Bagozzi, 1979) (Appendix F has more details). Regarding content validity, we drew on wellaccepted items from previous research and adapted them slightly to fit our three contexts. Concerning indicator reliability, we demonstrated that the indicators explain 50% of the latent variable variance, meaning that the value was at least 0.707 (Carmines & Zeller, 2008). This was fulfilled for all indicators with one exception (script in matchmaking service: 0.696). Since this one exception was only slightly under the threshold, we did not remove it to retain content validity (Hulland, 1999). To evaluate construct reliability, we used composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). CR should be higher than 0.7, and AVE should be higher than 0.5. We found that these criteria were fulfilled for all constructs in each domain. We calculated the AVE’s square root to evaluate discriminant validity and compared it to the bivariate correlations. These square root values were greater than the corresponding construct correlations (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hulland, 1999), indicating sufficient discriminant validity. Finally, we used several techniques to ensure that common method bias (CMB) was not distorting the data (see Appendix F).

Based on the categorizations described, we present the results of our three studies: media streaming, social networking, and dating services (Figure 2). We then compare our findings with those reported in previous research.<sup>3</sup>

Media streaming service: Our discontinuation model categorized 157 out of 164 (95.7%) ex-users of the media streaming service. Of these ex-users, 81 quit their use and 76 switched to other alternatives. The user discontinuation decisions for 130 of these ex-users were grounded in shocks (47 contextual and 83 technological shocks) and 27 decisions resulted from dissatisfaction (no shock). Notably, while Path 4 was the most common switching path taken in all three studies, the media streaming service study was the only study in which the largest number of ex-users were categorized into a switching path, and the only study in which quitters and switchers were almost evenly divided. None of the exusers followed Path 3. The seven ex-users who could not be categorized did not face a specific shock or were dissatisfied with the task or service. Therefore, they could not be categorized.

Social networking service: We categorized 148 out of 151 (98.0%) ex-users of the SNS to one of our seven paths. Of these ex-users, 122 participants quit the SNS, and 26 switched to an alternative SNS. 101 ex-users discontinued in response to a shock (60 contextual and 41 technological shocks) and 47 discontinued for other reasons. Most users quit rather than switched because of contextual shocks (Paths 1 and 2) or dissatisfaction with the task (Path 7.1). Three ex-users discontinued for reasons not explained by our paths, indicating struggles with addiction to SNS use. Previous research indicates that pathological use creates an augmented view of a service (Turel et al., 2011). In any case, individuals struggling with addiction likely do not correspond to the basis of rational decision-making presumed by image theory and should be considered separately.

Matchmaking service: Our paths categorized 146 of the 152 responses (96.1%) from ex-users of the matchmaking service. Of these ex-users, 120 quit using matchmaking services and 26 switched to other alternatives. 101 ex-users discontinued in response to a shock (75 contextual and 26 technological shocks) and 45 ex-users discontinued because they became dissatisfied with the task or service. As in the SNS study, most ex-users quit rather than switched; in this study, Paths 1, 2, and 7.1 provided the most common reasons for quitting. The majority of ex-users quit as a result of contextual shock (Paths 1 and 2), and no ex-user followed Path 3, meaning that no ex-user was confronted with a technological shock that initiated a script instructing the user to quit using a matchmaking service, regardless of their satisfaction with it. ## 5.2.6 Robustness Checks

We used a two-step approach to assess robustness. First, our survey included two questions (“To what extent was this event critical for you in the long term” and “To what extent was this an important event for you?”) that critically focused on the event (Morgeson & DeRue, 2006). Using a 7-point Likert scale, we found that all participants in our three surveys reported that the indicated shock was critical for them, as indicated by a mean value greater than 4. Second, following recent research suggestions, we checked whether our thresholds were robust (Mattke et al., 2022). Thus, we modified the thresholds of the categorization rules. For example, the main analysis used scores above 4 to indicate dissatisfaction. We reran the analysis using a slightly higher threshold. We also reran the analyses with slightly different thresholds for image violation and alternative search and evaluation. The results are similar to the main results. We conclude that the results are stable across different thresholds.

## 5.3 Summary

Study 1 shows that MOD can be used to analyze and explain ex-users’ actual discontinuation behavior in the context of media streaming, social networking, and matchmaking services. We were able to categorize 451 of the 467 ex-users surveyed into one of the seven paths suggested by MOD. We demonstrate that the surveyed ex-users largely followed one of the seven different MOD paths when quitting or switching services. The ex-users’ decisions were grounded in (contextual, technological) shocks or dissatisfaction. Study 1 included ex-users only and we took a retrospective approach: the subjects were asked to recall the discontinuation and shocks. To further our understanding, we conducted Study 2 to evaluate whether shocks and image theory can also predict current users’ discontinuation intentions.

## 6 Study 2: Predicting Users’ Intentions to Discontinue with MOD

Study 2 used MOD to predict the behavioral intentions to discontinue the use of a service. In this study, we decided to focus on matchmaking services because recent evidence indicates that users of matchmaking services often experience jarring events (Hughes, 2020). For parsimony, Study 2 focused on a specific contextual shock situation. We used MOD to develop a research model to predict discontinuation intentions, conduct a scenario-based study, and evaluate the model using partial least squares (PLS), a componentbased structural equation modeling method (SEM).

## 6.1 Research Model Development

According to MOD, a contextual shock can cause individuals to discontinue use. Based on Study 1, quitting is the most likely discontinuation behavior. MOD proposes that a contextual shock is the starting point for two divergent paths, Path 1 and Path 2. These paths differ according to whether the individual already has an engaged script related to the perceived contextual shock. Based on these two paths, we framed three hypotheses that predict quitting intentions when the user of a matchmaking service experiences a contextual shock (Figure 3).

MOD Path 1 theorizes the existence of an engaged script that prescribes quitting behavior. For example, according to the examples presented in Table 1, an individual with experience using a matchmaking service may already have preexisting plans for a certain response to the specific contextual shock of finding a new girlfriend. This plan might include that they will stop using the service in response to having a new girlfriend.

Theoretically, an individual interprets a contextual shock in terms of its nature and valence (Holtom et al., 2005) or based on previous experiences (McCrae & Costa, 1986). When a user has a script, based on earlier assessments during a similar shock, the user applies existing rules (Lee et al., 1999). The script then is responsible for a sequence of activities (Polites & Karahanna, 2013), which may include quitting the service. When such a shock occurs, the engaged script directly triggers the execution of these plans, meaning that quitting intentions are likely to be high. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1: An engaged script is positively associated with quitting intentions.

MOD Path 2 theorizes that individuals decide whether the contextual shock violates their image without an engaged script. Consider again the examples in Table 1. An individual may have a specific image that prohibits infidelity. Therefore, after finding a new girlfriend, this individual would stop using the matchmaking service because continuing to use it would be an image violation.

Theoretically, this path shows that after perceiving a contextual shock, individuals use the compatibility test (Beach & Mitchell, 1990) to assess whether using the service is still a good fit with their values in terms of their goals and aspirations (trajectory image) and whether the activities and efforts associated with the service still facilitate these goals and thus fit the individual’s strategic image (Davis & Venkatesh, 2004; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). In situations where the service does not fit their goals, e.g., as the service is considered stressful or distracts the individual from important goals (Tarafdar et al., 2020), the individual perceives an image violation and might develop high quitting intentions.

However, we assume that the influence of image violations on quitting intentions also depends on whether the individual has a script. In situations new to the user, they collect new experiences, assess the information, and start thinking about how to react without having sequences of activities in mind (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). The absence of a script lets the individual base their behavior on actual assessments. This logic implies that image violations are most salient for developing quitting intentions when no engaged script is present. Complementing this argument and according to Path 1 of MOD, scripts entail activities (Polites & Karahanna, 2013) and this occurs regardless of further assessments. This logic implies that engaged scripts directly trigger quitting intentions, regardless of whether users’ images are violated. Therefore, one would expect the influence of image violations on quitting intentions to be weaker in situations with an engaged script. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: Image violations are positively associated with quitting intentions.

H3: The influence of image violations on quitting intentions is moderated by engaged scripts such that the influence is weaker when there is an engaged script.

## 6.2 Research Model Evaluation

We conducted a scenario-based study (see Appendix G for details) to test our research model based on the matchmaking service Ashley Madison. We presented our study participants with a jarring event and captured their assessment of the situation to predict their discontinuation intention regarding the matchmaking service. We set up an Amazon MTurk study with the goal of recruiting study participants who were currently using or had used matchmaking services in the past or were familiar with Ashley Madison. To identify such participants, we used screening questions similar to those in Study 1. Overall, we collected data from 201 individuals. Most of our study participants were male (57.2%), the mean age was 39.5 years, 29.1% were between 20 and 29, 28.6% were between 30 and 39, 25.1% were between 40 and 49, 10.9% were between 50 and 59, and 6.3% were 69 years or older. The measures included in the quantitative study were similar to those used in Study 1. We used the partial least squares (PLS) method and SmartPLS 3 (Ringle et al., 2014) to evaluate the research model. We used PLS because it is suitable for studying new theoretical relationships regarding the consequences of shocks on IS use behavior and ensures high statistical power.

## 6.2.1 Measurement Model

Like in Study 1, we evaluated the measurement model’s content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability, and discriminant validity (Bagozzi, 1979). For the second-order control variable of dissatisfaction, which consists of task and service dissatisfaction, we followed existent guidelines (Wright et al., 2012) and also assessed the extent of common method bias (CMB) using different tests to verify that CMB likely did not impact our results (see Appendix G). Content validity is ensured because our study is based on well-known measurement items (similar to Study 1, Appendix E). Concerning indicator reliability, all loadings were at least 0.707 (Carmines & Zeller, 2008) and highly significant (Appendix G). In terms of construct reliability (Fornell & Larcker, 1981), AVE was higher than 0.5 and CR was higher than 0.7 (Appendix G). Regarding discriminant validity (Campbell & Fiske, 1959), the square root of AVE (Appendix G) was greater than the corresponding construct correlations (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hulland, 1999). The absolute HTMT<sub>0.85</sub> criterion also indicated that discriminant validity was not an issue.

## 6.2.2 Structural Model

We refer to the coefficient of determination (R²), the significance levels of each path coefficient, the moderation effects, and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) to evaluate the structural model. Figure 3 indicates that our model explained

31.1% of individuals’ quitting intentions variance. The results show that engaged scripts and image violations jointly explained a significant 31.1% variance in quitting intention, suggesting the explanatory power of MOD.

## 7 Applicability Check

Our research aims to ensure good communication between research and practice, avoid the sciencepractice divide, and demonstrate the relevance of the results. Therefore, we conducted an applicability test with a panel of practitioners from the same three service contexts as in Study 1. We recruited eight practitioners from a social networking site, two media streaming services, and a matchmaking service, consisting of two members of each organization’s customer relations department. Practitioners were given a one-page summary of MOD (Figure 2 and core definitions) (Rosemann & Vessey, 2008). We briefly summarize our findings below. The interview script can be found in Appendix H and the detailed findings can be found in Appendix I.

All of our interview partners considered it important to understand why users quit using a service or switched to an alternative. They also applauded our identification of different paths because they offer a high degree of accessibility. The suitability of the model’s findings in terms of providing solutions for the challenge of user discontinuation, for example, was confirmed to some degree by the practitioners. Collectively, our eight interview partners confirmed that MOD is applicable and relevant.

We also used the applicability check to complement our findings since most but not all ex-users in Study 1 followed our paths. The practitioners mentioned additional reasons that might lead individuals to discontinue the service. These included problems with blurred work and personal boundaries, high intrusiveness of other users (context: matchmaking service), low barriers for discontinuation, addictive use behavior (context: social networking service), or reduced face-to-face contact with friends (context: media streaming service).

## 8 Discussion

This study sheds light on how shocks, perceived as jarring events, cause users to discontinue the use of a service. Based on image theory (Beach, 1990), we articulated a model of discontinuation (MOD) that explains how quitting or switching aligns along seven paths tied either to contextual or technological shocks or to becoming dissatisfied with a task or service. Based on two studies, we posit that MOD can be used to analyze, explain, and predict discontinuation. In Study 1, we surveyed ex-users from three contexts and concluded that MOD can be used to analyze and explain actual discontinuation behavior. In Study 2, we show that MOD also predicts discontinuation intentions. We further verified MOD’s applicability by discussing our findings with practitioners. Thus, Study 1 shows that MOD can be used as a theory to analyze and explain discontinuation behavior, while Study 2 shows that MOD can also be used to predict discontinuation behavior. ## 8.1 Research Contributions

We provide a theory-driven understanding of user discontinuation that integrates prior knowledge and argue that not only dissatisfaction but also shocks, as identified in this paper, might cause individuals to discontinue using a service. Specifically, we contribute by confirming that at least seven equifinal paths lead to discontinuation behavior. This equifinal characteristic is vital as it carves out the need for individual-centric research beyond focusing on theoretical concepts such as stress (Maier et al., 2015b).

Related to that, MOD explains how shocks trigger users to switch or quit using a service, even if they are generally satisfied with the service. This finding is important because dissatisfaction is typically cited as the main reason for discontinuation. We extend existing work that has not yet explored the importance of shocks as potentially primary drivers of discontinuation. MOD shows that shocks, whether positive (i.e., starting a new relationship) or negative (i.e., system breakdown), trigger discontinuation. While it seems intuitive that negative shocks affect user behavior, we lay the groundwork for studying how positive events are perceived as jarring and cause discontinuance.

MOD also provides insight into the difference between technology-related and contextual shocks in terms of their impact on discontinuation. MOD argues that contextual shocks only trigger quitting behavior, whereas technology-related shocks may lead to switching or quitting. In these pathways, searching for and evaluating appropriate alternative services determines whether a user switches or quits. Our findings in all three contexts we studied indicate that a technological shock leads most users to switch. Our explanation for this pattern is that individuals interested in performing a task seek alternatives despite the technological shock (Xu et al., 2014). Moreover, our findings suggest that both types of shocks vary according to the use domain: technological shocks are more relevant for media streaming services, while contextual shocks are more relevant for matchmaking services and SNS. Such findings offer helpful information for developing a contextualized understanding of why individuals quit or switch services. These findings suggest that we can harvest a fresh understanding of discontinuation by considering shocks and users’ responses to them. Future work could help probe boundary conditions that shape how technological shocks shape users’ responses, such as technological change, economic conditions, and political polarization (Sarker et al., 2019; Wade et al., 2020).

We also provide theory to explain how predefined engaged scripts shape discontinuation decisions by determining the impact of shocks. When users with preexisting actionable scripts experience a shock, MOD suggests that the script becomes a roadmap for ensuing actions—be it continuing, quitting, or switching to an alternative service. This contributes to research focusing on the cognitive aspects of discontinuation and shows that when shocks activate scripts, no substantial cognitive deliberations are necessary (Beach, 1990) for discontinuation. Related to that, existing research shows that there are differences between habit and scripts, as scripts are knowledge structures behind habits (Abelson, 1981), meaning that habits are built when individuals have routines that are formed based on scripts (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). While research has indicated that discontinuation is grounded in the absence of a habit of using the service, we extend this research in two directions. First, we also show that scripts, which are rather general knowledge structures, influence use behavior beyond habits. Second, the scripts studied in our studies are knowledge structures related to discontinuation. In contrast, habit, as studied in previous research, is related to behavior (e.g., system use), reflecting that the reference point is different between habits and scripts.

MOD introduces the concept of image violation to discontinuation research. Specifically, image violation is relevant to this behavior only when individuals experience a jarring event without predefined engaged scripts (e.g., Paths 2, 5, and 6). We see in our three contexts that image violation leads to discontinuation behavior. On the contrary, we assume that for all other paths, it is irrelevant whether the user’s image is violated. This fact becomes visible in our results, as individuals following the other paths have image violations that can be lower or higher than the mean (e.g., Path 1 or 4). Such findings are consistent with recent individual-level IS research (Mattke et al., 2021; Pflügner et al., in press) and lead us to suspect that there is an asymmetry in the concepts, such as image violation, that are relevant for discontinuation, as they must have a specific characteristic for one path (e.g., high image violation in Path 2) and a different characteristic for another path (e.g., irrelevant in Path 1).

As seen in the literature review on discontinuation, dissatisfaction is among the most common reasons. Apart from focusing on jarring events, we integrated such dissatisfaction in MOD. We thereby extend prior work on discontinuation by clarifying the difference between the roles of task and service in the decision to either quit or switch.

Finally, we also contribute by translating an exemplary path (here Path 2) into a research model testing whether it is helpful to predict quitting intentions. Findings suggest that dissatisfaction is sometimes irrelevant when the user experiences a jarring event. Additional research is needed to better understand the interplay between specific (e.g., caused by jarring events) and ongoing (e.g., reflected in dissatisfaction) experiences. Further, our findings also show complex relationships between engaged scripts, image violations, and quitting intentions. On the one hand, there are two direct effects of engaged scripts and image violations on quitting interactions; on the other hand, engaged scripts also moderate the effects of image violations on quitting intentions. This confirms that there are dependencies between concepts included in MOD (e.g., image violations are only relevant when individuals have no engaged script) that deserve further investigation.

## 8.2 Limitations

It is important to note the limitations of this research. First, we were not able to categorize all ex-users who discontinued without experiencing a shock, e.g., addicted users. Future research could focus on specific groups of individuals to explain why and how these groups discontinue their use. Second, IS research has revealed that if users experience a specific jarring event repeatedly, they may get used to it (e.g., habituation effect) or may become hypersensitive (e.g., sensitization effect) (Weinert et al., 2022). Research has also pointed out that shocks and jarring events can change the way people respond to surveys (Prommegger et al., 2021). As we collected data in the pre-COVID-19 period and before GDPR was implemented, it is important to note that our respondents made decisions about quitting in a very different context. However, while the specific jarring events that might cause users to gain experience or learn to handle different jarring events likely differ, there is no reason to think that the broad paths, scripts, and logic described no longer apply. Rather, we suspect that users continue to rely on similar processes to respond to shocks and jarring events. For instance, if individuals now have more engaged scripts, discontinuation might be more frequently guided by individuals’ engaged scripts than by image violations. This may increase the need to replicate the study by also considering specific kinds of shocks or the frequency with which shocks occur, e.g., habituation or sensitization effects. Finally, in Study 2, we used a scenario with a specific jarring event. There is a need for further research focusing on other events.

## 8.3 Directions for Future Research and Implications for Practice-Focused Research

This paper lays the groundwork for research that examines how shocks and different types of dissatisfaction lead to discontinuation. While we highlight the innovative aspect that shocks are central to user behavior and point to multiple pathways, future research could build on these findings. Among other things, it might be useful to hypothesize and test whether and how the variables used in MOD (e.g., engaged scripts, image violation, dissatisfaction) differ for each path and whether these differences are statistically significant. We also develop a specific agenda for future research directions (Figure 4), which we present next.

A useful direction for future research might involve examining the implications of different kinds of shocks, scripts, and image violations regarding how users evaluate alternatives (FR-1 in Figure 4). For example, it would be interesting to explore whether shocks, e.g., those related to privacy or security breaches, could lead to users transferring feelings of distrust to the next service they use. Research on such “emotional halos” (Minge & Thüring, 2018) could offer rich insight into helping users acclimate to using new services.

Another direction for future research would be to further develop each path (FR-2 in Figure 4). Our research in Study 1 provides a unified framework for switching and quitting by articulating different paths that lead to user discontinuation and complements this foundation by focusing on two specific paths in Study 2. Future research could develop additional specific models for each path presented in our framework, allowing them to be used in a predictive rather than retrospective manner. For example, many variables exist, including engaged scripts, whose causes and consequences have not yet been studied in the IS context. A possible starting point might be to examine how negative experiences resulting from a shock distort existing positive evaluations.

Although we focus on discontinuation, we assume that some individuals may continue using a service even after a shock that triggers an engaged script. In other words, our research does not imply that every individual who takes one of these paths necessarily becomes an ex-user (FR-3 in Figure 4). Reasons for continued use include, for example, mandatory use, the lack of suitable alternatives for necessary tasks, and long-term contracts. Future research might explain why some but not all users quit or switch after experiencing a shock or becoming dissatisfied. Another aspect that may influence whether a shock leads to discontinuation is related to image violation. While image violation is a specific reason for quitting or switching, we did not investigate which image is violated. Investigating whether different types of image violation have different effects on IS discontinuation may be worthwhile.

Our results show that MOD can be used to study user discontinuation in different contexts. While we examined media streaming, social networking, and matchmaking services, future research might further develop and test MOD in other contexts to identify boundary conditions for MOD (FR-4 in Figure 4). For example, while it is common knowledge that cultural issues influence service acceptance (Srite & Karahanna, 2006; Zhang & Maruping, 2008), it may be fruitful to adapt MOD to articulate why and how cultural values influence user discontinuation and path selection.

Also, although our studies included empirical data and interviews from both free (e.g., SNS) and paid (e.g., media streaming service) services, future research might study how cost differences (Kohli et al., 2004) influence discontinuation decisions. Similarly, individuals’ specific paths may be best suited to a particular type of service. For example, while technological shocks were most relevant for the media streaming service, dissatisfaction was least relevant in this context. This finding suggests the need for future research that identifies factors that assess the relative importance of shocks and general (dis)satisfaction in different contexts. Thus, future research could examine boundary conditions that moderate the translation of shocks or dissatisfaction into user discontinuation and study whether scripts or image violations are more relevant to discontinuation in different contexts.

Our practitioner panel also suggested several avenues for future research. They prioritized research that develops intervention mechanisms that can mitigate the impact of shocks on the service provider-user relationship and were particularly interested in the topic of how to re-engage users after discontinuation (FR-5 in Figure 4). Specifically, how might services or platforms “reconvince” or “re-earn” the trust of former users? More specifically, are there certain types of ex-users—e.g., those who follow certain paths—who are more likely than others to re-engage with a service? Such research is important because a loss of user trust can ultimately affect a company’s ability to attract and retain customers, as well as its stock market valuation (Mavis et al., 2018).

## 8.4 Practical Implications

Our findings provide service providers such as Facebook, Netflix, or Amazon Prime with plausible explanations for why users quit or switch services. Such insights are important because they help services understand that the steps they take to increase satisfaction, and thus customer loyalty, may not yield the expected increases in retention rates. For example, media streaming services have invested heavily in content creation strategies to attract and retain subscribers. While new content may make the service attractive, without preventing or managing the impact of shocks, our findings suggest that service providers may not realize the expected returns from such investments.

In parallel with improved content or customer service strategies, our research suggests that service providers should prepare plans to mitigate the impact of at least two types of shocks (contextual vs. technological) that can lead to switching or quitting behavior. Our findings provide service providers with readily accessible configurations and pathways for understanding why contextual shocks cause customers to churn or switch services. For example, contextual shocks related to politics and political events have been cited as a reason that users quit social media services. To mitigate the impact of such shocks, our work suggests that social media services need to be aware of how the broader social and political context affects perceptions of their service. In doing so, they should monitor external actors such as regulators and evaluate how the content of the platform, linked to the broader context, shapes users’ perceptions of the service provider. In addition, by considering MOD, our work helps service providers identify possible avenues and opportunities to develop appropriate mitigation strategies to stem discontinuation resulting from such contextual shocks.

Our findings underscore the need for service providers to pay close attention to technological shocks. While service providers may not be able to predict when a jarring event will occur, some categories of events, such as service outages or data breaches, appear to occur with reasonable regularity. While many companies have disaster recovery plans, our work suggests that service providers would be well-served to take a nuanced approach that considers the many different paths that lead to quitting and switching. In developing such plans, MOD suggests that service providers should consider their specific context and user base to effectively identify the most plausible paths to abandonment. Through effective planning, we believe that service providers will be better able to manage the impact of technological shocks on their ability to retain customers.

## 9 Conclusion

Our research introduces the model of discontinuation as a new theoretical lens for understanding user discontinuation. MOD articulates seven paths that lead to user discontinuation: Six paths are motivated by technological or contextual shocks and one path results from dissatisfaction, which previous research has identified as a cause of user discontinuation. In contrast to previous research, which accounts for only a small percentage of discontinuation motivations, our model accounts for the motivations of almost 97% of the exusers we studied. Our second study shows that MOD can also be used to predict discontinuation intentions based on concepts such as engaged scripts and image violations. Our research, based on image theory and shocks, offers a novel view of user discontinuation rooted in specific, discrete shocking events rather than in the ongoing experience (e.g., dissatisfaction, stress) of using a service. The proposed model of discontinuation provides a useful means for analyzing, explaining, and predicting discontinuation.

## Appendix D: Categorization Rules and the Steps We Performed to Generate Our Results

Previous research using image theory and the five concepts (shock, script, image violation, (dis)satisfaction, search for and evaluation of superior alternatives) define some categorization rules (Donnelly & Quirin, 2006; Lee et al., 1999), which explain how the authors coded measurement items that allowed individuals to be categorized into different paths.

To analyze whether a shock was present, previous research (Donnelly & Quirin, 2006; Lee et al., 1999) checked whether at least one question was answered appropriately with “yes,” indicating a shock. Our research aligns with that approach but also included an initial step that checked whether the responses were consistent (e.g., to examine whether the free-text field/open-ended questions correlated with the yes/no questions). To analyze whether a script is present, previous research (Donnelly & Quirin, 2006; Lee et al., 1999) has checked whether at least one of the used questions was answered appropriately with “yes.” We also did this. Previous research has used several questions for image violation and assumed that an image violation was present when at least one question had an appropriate response. In contrast to measuring shocks and script, we also identified other research measures to analyze image violation in these contexts, and therefore align with Bala and Venkatesh (2015). We calculated mean values relating to these questions and used them to determine whether an image violation was present (mean value > 4) or absent (otherwise). We used the same procedure to measure the search for and evaluation of alternatives.

Apart from validating this scenario study, we checked for common method bias (CMB). Overall, we used three different tests to determine the extent of CMB. We first ran Harman’s single-factor test, which indicates whether the majority of the variance can be explained by one single factor, showing that only 31.5% of the data variance is explained by one factor. Second, we included a marker variable (“I love chocolate”) in our research model. This variable should not be significantly correlated with other constructs included in our study. Following the technique suggested by Lindell and Whitney (2001), and the recommendation of Chin et al. (2012) to a priori choose a theoretically unrelated marker variable, we used the second-smallest positive correlation among the constructs of our model and the marker variable (0.03). We then developed a CMB-adjusted correlation matrix to examine the structural relationships in the research model (Malhotra et al., 2006). Results indicate that the directions and significance levels of the paths remained unchanged. Moreover, our results indicate that adding the marker variable does not include an R² value of more than two percent. Third, we used the procedure of examining the correlation matrix as specified by Pavlou et al. (2003). Extremely high correlations (r > 0.90) indicate CMB, but our correlation matrix did not indicate such high correlations.

## Appendix I: Results of the Applicability Check

We next focus on the three aspects that are central to each applicability check in terms of importance, accessibility, and suitability. Afterward, we present additional explanations from our interview partner that might be useful to understand why we could not categorize all ex-users into our paths.

Importance: All our interview partners considered it very important to understand why users quit using a service or switched to an alternative. A media streaming service practitioner reported that there is “a separate department that analyzes whether poor audio or video quality contributes to why customers stop watching a series or cancel their subscriptions … so we have a real need to understand discontinuation behaviors.” An SNS practitioner commented that “[we] observed that active users of that previously famous and widely used social networking site quitting or migrating to Facebook caused its ruin … we are keen on knowing what motivates our users to discontinue use.” One of our matchmaking service practitioners stated, “with the emergence of matchmaking services like Tinder, a flood of cancellations challenged us ... since our business model depends on monthly payments, we rely on a low number of terminations.”

Accessibility: The practitioners applauded our identification of different paths because they offer a high degree of comprehensibility. One practitioner reported that “the different paths illustrate that there are multiple ways [that users] quit and switch and that the paths depend on the user’s disposition, preferences, and experiences … such that one event might have different consequences for different users.” Another practitioner commented that “it is nice to see that one can summarize and state the main points in a few sentences after looking at your figures.” The matchmaking service practitioners were pleased that ex-users were asked why they quit using the service. They told us that women often reported that men are too intrusive on the sites and want to exchange phone numbers or pictures in the first message instead of flirting or making conversation with them.

Suitability: The suitability of the model’s findings in terms of providing solutions for the challenge of user discontinuation, for example, was confirmed to some degree by the practitioners. There might be different ways to adapt the different paths we studied in an organizational context. One practitioner noted that “this was the first time I saw such a structured path explaining quitting and switching, which is indeed the first step towards understanding how to prevent our users from canceling.” Another participant stated: “I see now that we can’t prevent quitting if the user isn’t interested in our task anymore, but we might still be able to win back a user or prevent her from switching if she’s still interested in our task.”

Additional insights into the findings: We also used the applicability check to complement our findings since most but not all ex-users in Study 1 followed our paths. The practitioners from the matchmaking service explained that customers often quit because they feel that other users are too intrusive. For example, one practitioner explained that although “we have a policy against providing personal information such as email addresses or phone numbers during the first few messages ... we’ve gotten feedback that some customers develop workarounds, for example, by writing phone numbers using words instead of numbers. This was perceived as intrusive by some customers and led to them quitting the service.” Another practitioner added: “We also remove customers that break the rules by, for example, sending detailed personal information in the first few messages.”

Practitioners from the matchmaking and SNS sites related similar stories about problems with blurred work and personal boundaries on the sites. A practitioner from a matchmaking service told us that “customers sometimes complain that they were matched with colleagues, supervisors, or friends through our service … which was never their intention since they were not interested in dating these people.” Similarly, the SNS practitioners confirmed that individuals sometimes discontinue use in response to contact from work colleagues on the site.

Another explanation invoked by a practitioner from the media streaming service was that “customers think that series and movies are useful as a common topic when talking to friends, but binge-watching can also lead to less face-to-face contact with friends … which ex-customers have reported as a reason for quitting.” Similarly, a practitioner from the SNS added that “some of our ex-users report that they want to regain control over their personal lives and so stop using services like SNS … they either stop using them because they feel addicted and are trying to address that or they just avoid using services in certain areas of their lives.” Practitioners reported that it is difficult to compare pathological and nonpathological use since pathological users do not necessarily use the same rational decision-making strategies. Thus, this population is likely subject to different motivations and should be investigated separately.

Finally, both the media streaming and SNS practitioners reported that several individuals moved back and forth between using and not using a service. SNS practitioners surmise that this is perhaps “due to the low barriers for quitting or switching.” A media streaming practitioner pointed out a different type of intermittent use, stating that streaming “seems to be more important during the winter months; we see increases during these months and sometimes see users who had accounts during previous winters rejoin.”

Tim’s research has been published in all major IS journals and conference proceedings and has been cited over 10,000 times.
