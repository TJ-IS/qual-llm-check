# Genre Combinations: A Window into Dynamic Communication Practices

CARSTEN ØSTERLUND

CARSTEN ØSTERLUND is an Assistant Professor at the School of Information Studies at Syracuse University. He earned a Ph.D. in Management from Massachusetts Institute of Technology and is a former student of UC Berkeley, University of Århus, and University of Copenhagen, Denmark. His research explores the organization, creation, and use of documents in distributed work environments where people’s daily practices are characterized by high mobility. His work has appeared in, among others, Information Society Journal, Computer Supported Cooperative Work: The Journal of Collaborative Computing, and Journal for Information Studies. He has also been affiliated with the Work Practice and Technology Group at Xerox Palo Alto Research Center.

ABSTRACT: The notion of a genre system typically connotes sequences of interrelated communicative genres. This paper suggests that we can find other types of relationships among genres. Data from a field study in a large emergency room illustrate how doctors, nurses, and clerical staff routinely combine document genres not only in sequences but also in various accumulations achieved through proximity and movement. The combinations of genres add flexibility to the emergency room staff’s genre use and allow them to employ individual genres for several purposes. The data allow us to explore how organizational members manage the tension between a need for continuity in communicative practices and a need for flexibility in managing a jumble of paper-based and digital information systems. In addition, it demonstrates how end users often tinker with genres’ media and form in the process of altering combinations among specific genres.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: boundary objects, communication genres, documenting work, genre combinations, medical informatics, practice theory, qualitative methods.

MODERN ORGANIZATIONS TYPICALLY EMPLOY a large array of information systems (IS), some digital, others paper-based or wall mounted. State-of-the-art digital media often coexist with systems introduced decades ago [47]. Yet we must acknowledge that most contemporary media and IS studies implicitly focus on one system at a time [32, p. 100]. Rarely do studies account for the multiple coexisting systems allowing organizational members to combine multiple systems drawn from different media [41]. This has led to calls for research focusing on how organizational members routinely relate many different media as part of their communicative actions [24, 25, 32]. One can hypothesize that any one system may not possess the needed utility; rather, it is the collective synergies of multiple systems drawing on different media that provide utility [32, p. 111]. A better conceptualization of relations among multiple systems is likely to help managers depict the process of implementing new media or phasing out old systems. Furthermore, interactions among systems may help account for contradictory and unintended consequences of IS [42, 44].

Nowhere does the conundrum of multiple interlaced systems ring louder than in health-care settings. In most hospitals, one finds a hodgepodge of IS; some new and many old. In a typical emergency room (ER), for instance, the doctors have one electronic record system developed by a local physician with a knack for computers, while the nurses have recently implemented another system bought from a large vendor. The administrative system is an older legacy system, as are parts of the laboratory system. In addition to different systems, doctors, nurses, and clerical workers in the ER interact daily with a large number of preprinted forms, paper-based records organized in large folders, whiteboards, e-mail systems, and so forth.

Where the health-care industry may be extreme in the assortment of entwined IS at play, one finds multiple coexisting systems in most other organizational settings. Lessons learned about the relations among systems in health care are likely to benefit research and practice in many other IS domains. For instance, the present study depicts important relations among IS by following a nurse flow sheet as it moves through an emergency department. Along the way, doctors and nurses combine the flow sheet with many other IS. For each combination, the socially situated purpose of the flow sheet alters in the eyes of the user. Some elements are brought to attention, others ignored. A similar perspective could be applied to other technologies, such as the use of a Black-Berry. As consultants with a BlackBerry in hand move across various departments in their home and customer organizations, they are likely to encounter many IS. Before leaving the office, they download documents to their BlackBerry from their corporate intranet. While visiting a customer, they may hold the device up against the screen of a customer’s desktop system to compare notes. At the next site, they struggle to read a customer’s document that was e-mailed to the BlackBerry. Back in the office, they turn off the device to avoid interruptions while writing a memo using a desktop computer. Each interaction with another system changes the relevance of the media. What may be considered peripheral elements in one combination could become the center of attention in another situation.

Successful accomplishment of a project often requires the combination of information from many of these systems [34, 35]. The literature on boundary objects further illustrates how people from different departments and organizations utilize multiple systems as an integral part of their boundary-spanning activities [1, 11, 18, 29, 30, 40].

A growing body of research in organizational communication and IS research draws on the notion of genre to study how media are used in organizational communication, and with what consequences. Most of these studies focus on individual genres [9, 20, 23, 38, 57]. Some studies, however, explore the interdependencies among multiple genres. Typically, these studies describe those relations among genres as sequentially organized [10, 22, 56].

The present paper builds on a similar genre lens to examine how doctors, nurses, and clerical workers structure their use of multiple genres. Drawing on an ethnographic field study in a large teaching hospital and related clinics, the data reported in this paper focus narrowly on the genre combinations associated with one genre, a nurse flow sheet, in the hospital’s ER. What was uncovered was that sequentially organized genres represent only one of several ways in which doctors and nurses combine genres into larger genre systems.

## Genre and Practice

OVER THE PAST TWO DECADES, THE NOTION of “genre” has made its way into the organizational communication and IS literature [5]. This body of work largely draws on a pragmatic and practice-oriented view of genre as pioneered by Bazerman [10], Bakhtin [6, 7], Miller [33], and Swales [50]. Where traditional conceptions of genre have mostly been a taxonomy-oriented endeavor, referring to regularities of form and content to categorize spoken and written discourse, recent theories approach genres as the classification of communicative or rhetorical practices. Genres are classified as they emerge from the pragmatic perspective (rather than syntactic or semantic) of everyday interactions. This approach insists that the “de facto” genres, which we have names for in everyday language, tell us something theoretically important about communication. In effect, it seeks to explicate the knowledge that practice creates [33, p. 155]. Along these lines, Yates [54] and Yates and Orlikowski [55] define a genre as typified communicative action invoked in response to a recurrent situation. In pragmatic terms, people engage genres to accomplish social actions in particular situations, characterized by a particular purpose, content, form, time, place, and set of participants.

Central to a practice-based genre theory is the premise that human communication is suspended in a tension between continuity and change. The perceived fixity of communicative genres is integral to discourse communities striving to structure reliable day-to-day interactions. At the same time, communication and genres are constantly changing, as new electronic media emerge, daily practices evolve, and social relationships change [58]. Bakhtin describes these opposing tendencies as centripetal and centrifugal forces that are at work on any communicative act, in any medium [7]. This tension often poses a problem for genre studies emphasizing genres as the centripetal force in this equation, offering some fixity with their socially recognized purpose, content, form, time, place, and participant expectations. In these situations, it becomes difficult to articulate how a genre can be associated with more than one purpose and content expectation. As suggested by Bergquist and Ljungberg [12] and Miller [33], it is not uncommon for genres to have collections of communicative purposes. To address this issue, let us turn to how the literature describes relations among genres.

## Relations Among Genres

Studies focusing on relations among genres fall into two groups: those studying genre relations in relatively stable environments, and those studying genre relations in situations characterized by change. In the former case, one finds two key terms attending to the relations among multiple genres in stable situations: genre set and genre system [10, 22, 56]. A genre set represents the full range of genre types invoked by a particular discourse community. For instance, one can talk about the genre set that ER doctors employ in the course of their work. An ER doctor produces only a limited number of document types that are related but distinct from each other.

The genre set, however, signifies only one side of a multiparty interaction. For example, the ER doctors’ documents usually refer to information provided by the laboratory and radiology department; the ER nurses write their discharge instructions to the patient based on the ER doctors’ discharge summaries. The notion of genre system characterizes this full set of genres that instantiates the participation of all the parties [10, p. 98]. In this case, the genre system is the full interaction of events enacted as patients move through the ER. More generally, genre systems are interrelated genres that interact with each other in specific settings. Within this broader definition of genre systems, Bazerman and others have tended to envision this full set of genres as sequentially organized. In Bazerman’s words, “Only a limited range of genres may appropriately follow upon another in particular settings, because the success conditions of the actions of each require various states of affairs to exist” [10, p. 98]. Such a sequential perspective is clearly articulated in Yates and Orlikowski’s adoption of the concept, where a genre system is “a series of genres comprising a social activity and enacted by all the parties involved” [56, p. 14]. In short, the genre system concept proves helpful when studying how multiple groups engage many genres to coordinate their activities. Nevertheless, we could expect that sequentially organized genres only represent one among several types of generic intertextual occurrences in organizational settings.

In situations of change, studies of genre relations typically focus on the transition of paper-based genres to computerized systems [9, 20, 23, 38, 57]. This process is often described as a mixing of existing genres in an effort to merge socially recognized genres with the capabilities of electronic media. Yet those studies rarely explore how this mixing takes place and what specific relations among genres are involved. Yates and Sumner [58], for instance, show how software designers rarely begin designs from a blank screen, but modify and combine existing genres at their disposal. In the process of combining the form and purposes of two or more genres, a new genre may emerge and become accepted by members of the community. The affordances and constraints associated with the materiality of a media are central to this mixing of genres. Electronic systems tend to be considered more flexible than traditional media, and thus afford combinations among genres not previously conceivable [58].

In short, when it comes to stable and continuous situations, Bazerman and others have argued that we find relations among genres organized into sets associated with specific discourse communities, and when more than one community is involved, genres are sequentially organized. During change, we learn that materiality and technical features of a particular media play an important role in how designers and users mix existing genres. Yet one could expect many other types of intertextuality and genre combinations beyond sets and sequences. In our exploration, the existing literature gives us direction.

## Combining Genres

The practice-oriented perspective driving contemporary genre studies suggests that any further exploration of relations among genres takes communicative actions as its point of departure. It is the relations among genres that emerge from people’s daily routines that offer theoretically important insights. Here, the notion of “genre combinations” better signifies a practice-oriented emphasis than simply referring to relations among genres. It helps us focus on how people variously combine genres to accomplish social actions. Studying “relations among genres” connotes an abstract analytical perspective, whereas “genre combinations” draws attention to people’s practices and how they do and do not bring some genres together as part of their routine activities. The focus then becomes who combines genres, when, where, and for what purpose.

The social, situated, and conflictual nature of the practice allows us to build a dynamic perspective on genre combinations. First, studies of genre combinations may explain how a genre can be a force of continuity in a community but still be associated with more than one purpose and content expectation. Genre combinations may not necessarily be part of the creation of new genres but could demarcate a range of genre expectations associated with different but stable situations. What are approached as central content, times, places, and participants in one combination may be peripheral in another. This does not mean that genre combinations do not serve as stable and socially recognized communicative actions. Organizational members read genres and genre combinations to find coherent sets of center–periphery relations corresponding to the practice in which they are engaged [16]. Yet, what may be considered peripheral elements of a document in one combination may be swept to the center of attention in another situation, and vice versa.

One can make the same point by articulating the relation between genre combinations and genre systems. A genre combination can be part of a genre system if it is a typified communicative action that is part of the full interaction, and characterized by a particular purpose, content, form, time, place, and set of participants. As one genre can serve several genre combinations, a genre system can be made up of several subsystems, each with its own set of typified communicative action.

Second, we must approach genre combinations as creating a tension between continuity and change—between centripetal and centrifugal forces. In other words, genre combinations are potentially both stable and unstable entities.

Third, we can expect the media of a genre, including its technical features, to offer both constraints and affordances with regard to the types of combinations appealing to a discourse community. Digital media offer flexibilities and constraints in terms of who can combine what genres, when, and where.

## Research Setting and Methods

TO EXPLORE THE GENRE COMBINATIONS ENACTED by the doctors and nurses of an urban ER, I draw my empirical case from a 15-month, multisite ethnographic study, where I followed pediatric nurses, doctors, and secretaries in their daily work (see Table 1). The hospital is regarded among the best teaching institutions in the United States, and the ER receives approximately 50,000 patient visits annually. To protect the privacy of both health-care providers and patients, the names, dates, institutional identifiers, and sometimes the gender of informants have been changed. Approximately 2,000 hours were spent in five primary care clinics, one ER, and two hospital wards, focusing specifically on the document-related practices associated with patients’ care as they move within and across health-care settings. These documenting practices include recording, distribution, sorting, reading, and interpreting, involving various note cards, preprinted forms, online record systems, flagging systems, racks, binders, and whiteboards. My unit of analysis was the work practices of doctors and nurses in documenting patients care. Documents saturate doctors’ and nurses’ work. Few activities do not involve the recording, sorting, or reading of documents. Patients’ histories are taken with a document in hand, and the physical exam usually involves simultaneous recording of the observations. Discussions and interactions between colleagues unfold around one or more IS. When moving around a setting, doctors and nurses often move not from patient to patient but rather from document pile to IS to document pile.

The data collection and analysis adhered to principles for conducting interpretive research in IS as outlined by Klein and Myers [27]. Specifically, qualitative ethnographic methods guided my collection and analysis, both of the empirical observations of everyday work practices, and of informal and semistructured interviews with participants regarding their intentions and perceptions of practice and documents [2, 51, 52]. Practice-oriented theories framed my understanding of how doctors and nurses produce and consume documents, and directed my analysis of transcribed interviews, collected documents, and handwritten and typed field notes [8, 30, 31, 37, 45, 49]. In addition, genre-oriented analytical techniques helped address the research questions.

In an effort to triangulate the findings, I structured my observations, interviews, field notes, and analysis of communicative practices around four points of view. I followed: (1) people (i.e., ER staff members), (2) the task (i.e., the patient), (3) the artifact (i.e., the document), and (4) the conflict (e.g., critical incidences). Within each of these four perspectives, I mapped the purpose, content, form, time, place, and participants of the communicative practices as they evolved from that particular point of view. I developed inductive descriptions of genres and genre systems as typified communicative action invoked in response to recurrent situations. The strategy allowed me to map the communicative practices from the perspective of (1) the individual doctors and nurses’ actions, (2) the patients moving through the department, (3) the document genres coming in contact with several people and places, and (4) the conflicts emerging around the various IS. In the latter case, I analyzed critical incidents involving negotiations or power struggles among participants to change or alter particular genres, genre combinations, or genre systems [39]. The ER staff meeting described below illustrates such a critical incident. During the field observations, I attended most work-oriented meetings and later coded field notes and interviews for such critical incidents.

I use the notion of document genre to signify the multiview process through which genres were uncovered within these dynamic work environments, encompassing many points of view on the communicative actions. In addition, the notion of a physical document highlights the importance of a genre’s material form (i.e., medium) and the constraints and affordances this may impose on the practice.

In an attempt to limit the scope of the present paper, I focus on the corpus of document genres utilized in the ER and, in particular, on one genre, a nurse flow sheet. The dynamic nature of the ER makes it a good example, allowing one to crystallize genre combinations taking place within a limited time frame. In addition, all the patients admitted to the hospital moved through the ER. The nurses’ flow sheets represent a genre used in all patients’ care. I found comparable use of genres in all the other settings involved in this study. Even within the ER, one finds different document genres consulted, depending on a patient’s illness and where the patient is coming from. For instance, a trauma patient brought to the hospital straight from an accident involves more documentation than an infant with bronchiolitis. For the purpose of this paper, I selected the genres used when an infant girl, Emily, was sent to the ER by her primary care clinic. I have chosen this case as a representative of a genre system repeated countless times on a daily basis during the winter months. The case is qualitatively representative in the sense that it reflects the majority of documenting practices by ER staff.

## Results

THE PRESENTATION OF RESULTS HAS TWO SECTIONS. First, I identify two distinct types of document combinations among the ER staff members: sequential and accumulative, where the latter is achieved through either proximity or movement. These two combinations are empirically derived and characterize distinct but not exclusive communicative practices. One document can be combined in both ways. Second, I present a vignette from an ER staff meeting illustrating how doctors, nurses, and clerical workers continuously rework genre combinations in the ER. Each empirical section is followed by an analytical summary.

## Combining Genres Through Sequence

One finds several sequentially organized genre systems in the ER. Some follow the specific work flow of individual occupational groups or teams; others track various patient trajectories through the department. The genre sequence associated with Emily’s care, an infant with bronchiolitis, represents the majority of cases seen in the ER and is summarized in Figure 1.

Emily has been sick for several days and is sent to the ER by her primary care doctor. Ahead of Emily’s arrival, the doctor faxed an “expect sheet” summarizing her history and his recommendations. The triage nurse working at the entrance to the ER picks up the expect sheet and places it in a bin with other expect sheets. Half an hour later, Emily and her parents arrive at the triage desk. The triage nurse grabs a preprinted form known as an “ER flow sheet” and starts recording Emily’s history. The triage nurse sends Emily’s mother to register in the hospital’s administrative IS. Meanwhile, a triage nurse assistant examines Emily and enters her vital signs in the flow sheet.

After a long wait, a nurse calls Emily into the main ER. The nurse brings her to an exam room, signs Emily up on a large whiteboard, and assigns a doctor and a nurse to Emily. The whiteboard summarizes all patients and staff currently in the ER. After another wait, the nurse assigned to Emily enters the exam room. She takes Emily’s history, examines her, and records everything in the flow sheet initiated in triage.

When the doctor finds time to examine Emily, he uses his own document genre, the “temporary note,” to summarize his work. In another genre, an “order sheet,” he records the medication, tests, and other activities to be carried out by the nurse. After midnight, the ER doctor, assisted by a senior physician, decides to admit Emily. With his temporary note in hand, the doctor writes up Emily’s history in an electronic record system, the ER chart, which is subsequently signed off by the senior physician. The nurse draws from her own notes in the flow sheet to write up an admission sheet that she sends off to the inpatient ward where Emily will spend the next two days. In short, the ER staff enacts a document genre sequence.

These eight document genres constitute a highly institutionalized genre sequence. Each genre within the sequence comprises a coordinated, interconnected set of communicative actions associated with a specific purpose, content, form, time, place, and group of participants for each interaction; the sequence taken as a whole composes a similar set. Each genre appropriately follows upon the previous genres, because the success conditions of the practices associated with a genre require various states of affairs to be established by the previous genres. As an example, the ER doctor will not initiate a temporary note before the chart nurse has assigned Emily to him on the whiteboard. Across the different genres in the sequence, the level of intertextuality varies. For instance, when initiating a flow sheet, the triage nurse may or may not draw information from the primary care doctor’s expect sheet. In contrast, the ER doctor typically bases the ER chart on information recorded in his temporary note. Likewise, the nurse fills out the admission sheet with the flow sheet in hand.

The genre sequence accomplishes the highly complex interaction, involving several different professional groups, coordinating and recording Emily’s care as she moves through the ER. From an organizational standpoint we learn much about the collaboration and distribution of tasks in ERs by examining this sequentially organized genre system. Nevertheless, the sequential organization of genres constitutes only one type of genre combination found in the ER. To illustrate these other types of combinations, the following analysis will focus on one of the eight document genres described above—the nurses’ ER flow sheet.

## Combining Genres Through Accumulation

The second type of genre combination identified involves the accumulation of documents, which allows the users to make assessments about a single patient’s status and the status of a system involving various groupings of patients and staff members. For instance, a doctor’s accumulation of specific flow sheets, test results, and provider notes on an individual patient allows the doctor to make important decisions about diagnosis and care. Doctors and nurses achieve accumulation through proximity or movement among documents.

## Accumulation Achieved Through Proximity

Doctors, nurses, and administrators combine all the various document genres outlined in Emily’s case in electronic and paper-based folders, records, piles, bins, and racks, some with documents of the same genre, and others with diverse genres. The nurses flow sheet is a good example.

One finds Emily’s flow sheet in four different locations in the ER. First, the triage nurses place it in a rack outside the triage examination room with flow sheets of other newly arrived patients. The triage nurses sort these flow sheets according to how urgent they perceive each patient’s case to be in relation to the other newly arrived cases. In doing so, the rack serves as a tracking device for newly arrived patients and an itinerary for the triage nurses’ unfolding work. Second, Emily’s flow sheet is moved to the gray rack in the main ER which holds all the flow sheets on patients ready to enter the main ER. Again, the ER staff arranges the sheets according to the patients perceived urgency, as well as other work flow concerns, such as by what team the patients should be seen (e.g., urgent or nonurgent care, orthopedic, etc.). This adds up to a tracking device of the waiting room and an itinerary for future activities. Who should the nurses call in next and how fast should the ER staff work to open up new examination rooms? One evening a nurse flips through the flow sheets placed in the gray rack, mumbling: “Vomiting and fever, vomiting and fever, vomiting and fever. They all seem to be vomiting with fever tonight. I only look at the section specifying their chief complaint,” she explains to the ethnographer. “Only if the kid seems really sick do I look over vital signs.”

The nurse picks out a couple of flow sheets from the left-hand row of the rack and places them on the right-hand side. The two patients are now in line for a room in the nonurgent ambulatory side and not the main ER. Depending on workload and how swiftly each team works, the nurse moves patients between teams such as the urgent and nonurgent side.

Third, when Emily finally enters the main ER, her flow sheet is moved to a bin outside her exam room. Here, it is combined with other documents pertaining to her care—for instance, the order sheet, test result printouts, and so on. In combination with these other document genres, the flow sheet serves as an itinerary and coordination device for the collective activities of the nurses and doctors caring for Emily.

Fourth, the flow sheet ends up in Emily’s medical record, chronologically compiling all her encounters with the hospital. Each institution keeps its own unique organization of the record, reflecting not the patient’s care per se, as much as the work practices of staff members, and the organizational design and needs of the institution. An infant starts out with an empty medical record and, depending on the child’s health, it fills up slowly or quickly. Doctors and nurses often assess a patient’s general health status simply on the thickness of the medical record: “Oh, she is a three binder.”

At this point, one may wonder if combinations only take place among paper-based documents as in the case of the flow sheet. Yet one finds numerous examples of combinations among electronic and paper-based documents. ER doctors with access to the hospital’s online system combine current and past ER charts, test results, and so on, in much the same way as they flip through a paper-based medical record. At any hour, one can observe interns and residents bent over computer terminals with a number of temporary notes, test results, and other documents spread around the keyboard as they work on their online ER charts.

The physical layout of a department plays an important role in the accumulation of electronic and paper documents through proximity. The ER functions around three document-dense areas—the charting room, the triage desk, and the registration desk. Flow sheets can be found in each of these places in addition to a host of other electronic, paper-based, and wall-mounted genres. In the charting room, the doctors constantly scan the whiteboard, the gray rack, residents’ ER charts, and the temporary notes spread around the terminals before deciding which patient to see next or which resident to assist.

Compared to the sequentially organized combinations, genre accumulation achieved through proximity plays an equally central role in coordinating work practices in the ER. Doctors and nurses do not randomly pick and choose which flow sheets they combine. These are highly institutionalized practices that have stood the test of time. Each accumulation of flow sheets is associated with a specific purpose, content, form, time and place of interaction, and set of users. Some of these accumulations narrowly focus on Emily’s care, while others facilitate the coordination of various patient and staff populations.

## Accumulation Achieved Through Movement

The physical layout of the ER and the particular mix of media do not allow doctors and nurses to achieve all desired accumulations through proximity. Documents distributed across space require the staff to move from document to document in order to realize a specific combination. In these situations, the accumulation is not laid out in front of providers, but instead must take place in the doctors’ and nurses’ heads as they move from document to document. Some of these accumulations fulfill central functions and constitute routine practices in the ER.

ER rounds illustrate the process. Five to six times a day the senior doctors and a few nurses gather in front of the whiteboard. In a highly institutionalized pattern, they then walk not from patient to patient, but from the whiteboard to the registration desk, the chart room, and bins of documents throughout the ER, including the gray rack, bins with expect sheets, flow sheets, order sheets, or patient medical records. The doctors and nurses do not arbitrarily zigzag through the department glancing over random documents. Rather, they strive to compile documents that give them a sense of the status of the entire ER patient population. In comparison, ER rounds allow the staff members to track not only patients in the waiting room, as accomplished by the gray rack, but also the patients currently receiving care. As they skim each document involved in the rounds, the senior doctors and nurses focus their attention on the sections allowing them to establish a shared understanding of the overall patient flow through the ER. They glean information such as: How many patients are currently in the waiting room? How far along are patients in the individual exam rooms? What exam rooms can be freed up quickly? How many patients are in need of urgent attention? Should Emily receive extra attention to move her case along or do other patients have more pressing needs? What doctors and nurses need help? In the words of a senior doctor, “Number one, you should pay attention to flow [during rounds]—who are the sick kids and whether the interns, residents, and fellows have their patients seen or need various levels of help and instructions.”

Details on individual patient cases as described in, for instance, the flow sheets are only discussed to the degree they inform the doctors and nurses about the overall flow of patients through the ER. Patients are not examined but team members may ask a patient if they have already had X-rays taken or had other procedures, all of which may affect the length of their stay in the ER.

This accumulation of documents has a clear and broadly accepted purpose in the ER. The team strives to prioritize patient care and staff allocations to facilitate overall patient flow and avoid bottlenecks. The accumulations achieved through the team’s movements during rounds play a central role in coordinating work in the ER, assigning tasks to various people, and prioritizing Emily’s case in relation to the other patients currently in the ER. As with the other genre combinations, ER rounds allow the doctors and nurses to accomplish particular actions associated with a purpose, content, form, and specific times, places, and participants. In this example, the accumulation achieved through movement deals with the management of the ER as a system. Movements, however, can also focus on individual patient’s care. For instance, subspecialists routinely walk from IS to IS in the ER and beyond to learn all there is to know about one patient’s care. Primary care doctors attending to their own hospitalized patients walk from department to department to glean information about their patient’s progress from various distributed IS.

## Analytical Summary

Emily’s flow sheet is combined six times in the course of her trajectory through the ER, as summarized in Table 2. In each of these combinations, the socially recognized purpose, content, form, time, place, and participants change.

The flow sheet is associated with the ER nurses and Emily (i.e., “who”) when it is part of a sequentially organized genre combination tracking Emily’s trajectory through the ER. One would never see a physician add information to an ER flow sheet. The socially recognized purpose of the genre (“what”) is to record and track the nurses’ care of one particular patient (e.g., Emily). The nurses use the flow sheet genre every time and place (“when” and “where”) they interact with Emily. The content expectations are clearly defined (“what”) and embedded in the layout of the format (how). Well-defined sections each outline distinct content expectations, such as patient information and history, vital signs/interventions, mechanism of injury, diagnostic studies, discharge score, discharge observations, and discharge teaching.

As the flow sheets are repeatedly combined and recombined, the content and format of the physical documents obviously stay the same. Yet, as indicated in Table 2, the genre expectations change. What are considered central content, times, places, and participants in one combination become peripheral as doctors and nurses engage the flow sheet in other combinations. For instance, when Emily’s flow sheet is placed in the gray rack, it is read in relation to other flow sheets in the rack. The participants are no longer just the nurses and Emily but all patients in the waiting room (“who”), and the purpose changes from the tracking of ER nurses’ care for Emily to the process of tracking and prioritizing patients in the waiting room. The users are no longer ER nurses but also doctors and clerical staff who will rarely look at the details of the information captured on the sheet but are simply trying to access the needs of the current patient body in the waiting room in relation to one another (“what”). In short, all six genre combinations constitute typified communicative actions invoked in response to recurrent situations in the ER. Doctors and nurses engage each combination to accomplish a social action in a particular situation, characterized by a particular purpose, content, form, time, place, and set of participants. Moreover, they can all be seen as part of the larger ER genre system, defined by the full interaction and interrelated genres that instantiate the participation of all the parties central to the ER work system.

Extending Bazerman [10] and Yates and Orlikowski [55, 56], we can argue that these six genre combinations constitute a more detailed and full history of the communicative events guiding Emily’s care. The intertextual relations are not only organized sequentially but also through accumulation achieved by proximity and movement. Each genre combination constitutes another subpurpose in the overall genre system and the individual genres. For each combination, the socially recognized type of interaction changes without necessarily removing the purpose of the individual genre or other genre combinations. For instance, while Emily’s flow sheet is located in the bin outside her exam room, the nurses and doctors assigned to her care use it to coordinate their activities. Simultaneously, it can become part of the ER rounds genre combination. In this way, doctors and nurses routinely and swiftly alternate between different readings of a document genre depending on the combination that fits their current practices. What may have been considered peripheral content, times, places, and participants at one point in time may become the center of attention or vice versa.

## ER Meeting Case: Changing Genre Combinations

Genre combinations and expectations about their purpose, content, form, time, place, and participants are the subject of ongoing negotiations and alterations that monopolize most departmental staff meetings, whether in the ER, inpatient wards, or the primary care clinics. When a genre changes or a new one is introduced, the alteration inevitably leads to changes in both genre combinations and what materiality of a genre best supports the various combinations. In this section, I describe one typical ER staff meeting, followed by an analytical section. In this ER meeting, five nurses, two secretaries, and two physicians discuss, among other things, how best to call attention to new orders so as to improve coordination among doctors and nurses.

In the ER library, the nursing manager, Joyce, starts the ER staff meeting. As an experiment, the department recently installed red and green flags mounted on the door frame of the red team’s patient rooms. The doctors promoted the initiative. Usually when doctors write orders to nurses, they place their orders in the bins found outside each patient room. In the red team, the doctors are supposed to flip out a red or green flag, thereby signaling to the nurses that they have an urgent or “emergent” order pending. However, the flags do not seem to have had the intended effect. Frequently, red flags are displayed for a long time without anyone seemingly taking notice.

The discussion first turns to whether it is reasonable to make the orders, and thus nurses’ work, visible to everyone. Clara, a more senior nurse, describes how she missed a red flag the other day. The patient’s family finally called it to her attention.

“I felt awful!” Clara concludes. The other nurses all agree. The flags make them feel overexposed by having pending work tasks made visible not only to colleagues but also to the patients’ families.

John, a senior physician, attempts to justify the system. It is difficult, he argues, to find people, even doctors: “At night I give everybody [i.e., doctors] a phone. Then we can reach each other all the time and don’t have to go hunting. I receive calls in the restroom.” William, an ER doctor, suggests that the core problem is not that people cannot find each other but that the nurses should move around less. They should spend more time in the patients’ rooms. William argues, “The nurses spend a lot of time in front of the whiteboard, while the docs run around like headless chickens.” Clara points out that the house officers spend a considerable amount of time in the charting room. William insists, “Often it’s impossible to find out when one nurse has taken over from another nurse. We need to get people back into the patient rooms where they belong, not sitting in front of the whiteboard or in the glass box [charting room].”

As in all other meetings, discussion of one document genre quickly leads to its relation to other document genres and how a combination of several document genres may alleviate the pending problem. Sharon suggests that they place the flags on the whiteboard. The group discusses whether the whiteboard is already too cluttered. Jimmy, a secretary, brings up an idea, proposing that they allocate a slot for each team on the whiteboard where a magnetic flag would indicate pending orders. If they rotate the information on the board 90 degrees, they would make space for such fields. The suggestion is soon pushed aside by yet another idea from William, the doctor. He proposes placing all the orders in racks under the whiteboard. Each team should have their own order box. The senior physicians and the chart nurses can see if a team is falling behind on their orders or if there are any urgent orders pending, he explains. They would not have to walk from door to door on rounds, William suggests, and ends his contribution by arguing that such a change would help nurses detect when colleagues need help. John likes the idea and expresses the often-heard technological dream, “What we need is a patient tracking system like all modern hospitals. One that will show you flags and time of order, labs [laboratory results], everything.” Clara responds, “Give us a hundred years and we will have it.”

Changing the combinations of document genres often leads to undesired consequences for other carefully combined document genres. As a response to William, Joyce tersely points out that bringing the orders to the whiteboard would further pull the patient chart apart: “You need the order with the flow sheet,” says Joyce. William agrees. “As it is, the chart is already spread all over the ER,” he says. The secretary manager and Jimmy nod their heads in agreement. They know the problem. Daily they gather the charts when patients leave the ER to sort and send the assembled documents to the records department.

The conversation moves to the issue of communication and liability. Sharon articulates a sentiment often heard among nurses: she worries that the flags become “a substitute for communication.” Nurses strongly feel that when doctors communicate an order with a flag, and not through paper and face-to-face interaction, nurses lose the option to question and give input on an order. In their daily work, nurses check the calculations of doctors’ orders, suggest alternate possibilities, or simply want to know the rationale behind a particular order so as to be able to answer patients’ or their families’ questions. The flags bring them a step further away from care decisions.

William insists on the need for flags. “We need to find a more efficient way to handle orders—handle them in a timely manner.” He says that it is a legal matter. “If somebody gets hurt, lawyers will sift through the entire system and records and look at how effective it is.” Clara and Sharon, restless in their chairs, get a little defensive. They do not find the system to be as defective as William paints it. “Anyway, it’s the nurses’ responsibility when the doctors have written their orders,” Sharon suggests. William disagrees: “The physician is always responsible. If the nurse does not take his order, he should have done it himself.” Joyce seems surprised: “I always thought that when the doctor writes the order, he pushes the responsibility to the nurse.” William responds, “No, it’s always the physician’s responsibility. I have only seen two cases where nurses were held responsible. If they intentionally ignore the order or if they give the wrong medication or dose.” A brief silence follows. Jimmy doodles on an abandoned flow sheet.

Joyce takes steps to end the meeting. She suggests that they should let the experiment with the red team flags go on for a bit longer, saying, “It has not really been tested yet.” She stresses that the flags should not be a substitute for communication among nurses and doctors. John adds his two cents: “The flags are just a substitute until the day we all have phones operated into our ears.” Joyce closes the conversation by suggesting that they look into the possibility of adding “order boxes” under the whiteboard. With many issues raised and few resolved, the group leaves.

## Analytical Summary

At first glace, the meeting stands out as a typical unorganized workplace meeting, which, in fact, is how many ER staff members feel and why some do not bother to show up. Nevertheless, the jagged course of the dialogue points to the multiple concerns that staff members have in the combination and use of their many document genres. To ensure coordination among doctors, nurses, and secretaries, these groups continuously tweak the combinations of their many document genres and their purposes, content, form, time, place, and participants.

One can divide the meeting into four phases, each emphasizing a particular aspect of the ER genre system. First, the nurses call attention to the new order flags. The doctors have worked hard to introduce this seemingly simple IS as an accepted genre to accomplish particular actions (i.e., to communicate an order), characterized by a particular purpose, content, and so on. Paraphrasing Yates and Orlikowski’s [56] genre definition, the doctors are struggling to establish a typified communicative action invoked in response to recurrent situations in the ER. Yet, as the nurses point out, the participants (“who”) of the new flag genre may not be limited to the doctors and nurses. By their conspicuous position mounted on the patient room door frames, the flags inevitably include patients and their relatives as participants in this new genre.

The nurses are concerned about the patients’ active involvement in the nurses’ communication with doctors.

Second, the doctors invoke the original purpose of the order flags: to free doctors from having to find nurses when giving an order (“what”). They want to change their interaction from a synchronous face-to-face interaction to an asynchronous interaction mediated by the flags and the order sheet. The younger doctor, William, suggests that the problem is not that patients have access to the flags but that the nurses prefer to spend time in document-dense areas around the whiteboard and not in the patients rooms.

Third, all the meeting participants attempt to solve the tension by envisioning changes to the combination, form, location, and participants involved in the order and related genres. For instance, various combinations with the whiteboard are discussed, which would involve changes to the layout (“how”) of the whiteboard and possibly also the flags. Another option is to combine all the order sheets in bins under the whiteboard (“where”). This would change the genre expectations from being a tool to coordinate between a doctor and a nurse caring for one patient, to involving all the nurses and doctors (“who”) in monitoring how busy each team in the ER is (“what”) at any given time (“when”). Yet bringing the order sheet to the whiteboard would break up the highly valued interaction when the flow sheet and the order sheet are both in hand. Neither doctors nor nurses are sure if they are willing to give up such an important combination. The choice is between a document combination facilitating care for individual patients versus a document combination helping to coordinate among multiple staff members caring for a group of patients.

Finally, taking apart all these genre expectations brings the discussion to the core of the relationship between doctors and nurses and their division of labor: who is ultimately responsible for the order and who would get penalized if the communication breaks down? The nurses worry that by turning the communication about orders into an asynchronous interaction, they will lose an important role in patient treatment. In short, as doctors and nurses negotiate the genre expectations and combinations associated with the order sheet, they also debate the social relations governing their responsibilities, power structures, and communicative practices.

Thus the ER staff cannot introduce a new genre or make changes to an existing one without renegotiating its socially recognized purpose and the social order on which the communication builds. Discussions of a genre’s possible combinations with numerous other document genres are central to these negotiations.

## Discussion

## Genre Systems and Genre Combinations

THE DEFINITION OF A GENRE SYSTEM articulated by Bazerman [10] and later by Yates and Orlikowski [56] falls into two parts, as noted above: a genre system is (1) the full set of genres, the full event involving all participants in a social system; and (2) a sequentially organized set of genres. I will address these in turn.

Figure 2. Combination Through Sequence and Accumulation for the Nurse Flow Sheet Genre

First, consistent with prior studies [10, 22, 56], the six flow sheet combinations all play a central role if one wants to understand the full communicative interaction involved in Emily’s care and trajectory through the ER. The six genre combinations each constitute typified communicative actions evolved in response to recurrent situations. Thus, they represent relatively stable and institutionalized communicative practices in the ER genre system.

Second, the notion of genre combinations, however, challenges the assumption that sequential organized genres constitute the only possible dimension in a genre system. Combinations through accumulation achieved by proximity and movement among documents highlight other important structures of the ER’s genre system as summarized in Figure 2. The sequencing and the accumulation of the flow sheet with other ER document genres are both integral to the overall work flow in the department and Emily’s care. The six flow sheet combinations do, to some extent, follow upon one another because success combinations require various states of affairs to exist. Previous combinations often establish such conditions. For instance, the combination of flow sheets in the triage rack requires that the triage nurses first initiate the flow sheets upon the patient’s arrival. In turn, the triage rack predisposes the gray rack. The former involves a prioritized examination of the patients arriving in the ER, which in turn allows doctors and nurses in the main ER to sort the waiting room patients flow sheets in the gray rack based on the urgency of their condition. Yet some combinations take place at the same time. There is nothing preventing Emily’s flow sheet from being involved in the senior doctors’ and nurses’ ER rounds and at the same time being combined by the nurse and doctor in charge of Emily’s immediate care. A single unidirectional sequence does not seem to serve as the best image of the ER genre system. Rather, the genre system stands out as a loosely organized sequence with several parallel tracks, which branch out and narrow at different stages depending on the particular structure of accumulation and sequences. As in the ER case, those are complex processes. Figure 2 highlights only the combinations involving the nurses flow sheets. Many other combinations coexist.

## Continuity and Change

Genre combination opens a window into the dynamics of continuity and change in organizational communication and IS use [43, 44]. The existing genre literature demonstrates that radical change associated with new system development typically involves a mixing of existing genres [9, 20, 23]. Present research suggests that gradual change may be better described through studying the genre combinations that organizational members use for multiple purposes. These members adapt genre expectations as the environment changes or requires solutions to new situations. The intertextual readings associated with genre combinations can, in many situations, be established without changing socially recognized expectations associated with an individual genre. This allows genres with a long history to remain viable as doctors and nurses continuously adapt combinations to emergent needs. Not surprisingly, in most medical settings, one finds several generations of IS living side by side. Old genres continue their existence as ongoing changes to genre combinations allow them to stay workable. The ER meeting illustrates how these processes often involve ongoing negotiations among the various groups participating in the genre system. The system evolves as the staff adds new genres, reworks sequences and accumulations, or changes the media of old genres, thereby opening up possibilities for new combinations. The introduction of even simple genres, such as the flags, may send ripples throughout the department’s genre system, potentially affecting several other genres through new or altered combinations.

## Media and Context

The ways in which doctors and nurses compile documents by either proximity or movement highlight the close relations between the media of a genre and its context. Media choice plays a central role in what types of genre combinations are possible or desirable for particular organizational settings [4, 14, 28, 38, 48, 53]. In the ER meeting, we see how the staff reacts to the material form of the flags. This leads to discussions about what affordances and constraints each media offers in regard to genre combinations and expectations. For instance, should the flags be made of plastic and mounted on the patient room door frames (and therefore near the bin with the flow sheet), or turned into magnets and combined with the whiteboard? Changes in media or contextual factors can have direct implications for the combinations afforded by the genre.

Digital media adds great flexibility to the times and places of genre combinations, although it may also alter the types of combinations possible. Envision the flow sheets in an electronic format that allows doctors and nurses to make various combinations of patients. This electronic flow sheet would resemble a virtual gray rack, bin, and triage desk, all in one place—their desktop. The electronic media would obviously overcome the “one-place-at-a-time” limitation of paper documents. Yet, one can only imagine the many questions put forward during prolonged ER meetings. Those inquiries would likely include: Would the electronic flow sheet alter the genre’s participants, combinations, and purposes? What is the impact on other genres? What changes might follow in work routines and the division of labor?

For the ER staff to address these questions, and establish new combinations among electronic genres, would require some degree of technical skill and training. One rarely finds such skills widely disseminated in health-care settings. In the ER meeting, we notice that discussions of electronic media and digitally facilitated combinations are conspicuously absent. Only the attending doctor, Paul, articulates a couple of technology dreams, which his colleagues instantly push aside as unrealizable (i.e., “give us a hundred years”) or simply ignore. In order to realize combinations involving digital media, the ER staff would have to collaborate closely with members of the hospital’s IS department. IS staff would most likely have to play a central role in ER staff meetings and beyond. It is there that genre combinations are discussed. If meeting participants cannot judge what types of combinations are realistic, those combinations are likely to get dismissed without much consideration. The involvement of IS staff would also require major organizational changes in the relations between the IS department and the rest of the hospital. In the hospital discussed here, the IS staff worked in a trailer on the fringes of the hospital grounds. They could not have been more marginal to the daily workings of the hospital and its many genres and genre combinations.

## Contributions and Implications

THIS STUDY IDENTIFIES TWO TYPES of genre combinations that the ER staff used to enact their communicative activities—sequence and accumulation. These combinations were derived from routine work practices among the staff members in an ER, and all stood out as central to the staff’s daily communication and coordination. The analysis of genre combinations demonstrates that sequentially organized genres only constitute one possible dimension of a genre system. We have to look beyond sequence if we want to understand the relations among multiple IS of various media. Accumulated genre combinations achieved through proximity and movement among documents highlight other important structures of the ER’s genre system. The notion of genre combination allows us to explore how organizational members manage the tension between continuity in genre expectations and the need for flexibility and change. The combination of genres allows the ER staff to associate more than one purpose with a genre. To accomplish such combinations, the research suggests that organizational members must consider both the affordances and constraints that context and media place on the involved genres.

## Limitations

The present research is not without limitations. It relies on a single in-depth ethnographic study, thereby subjecting it to a set of well-known limitations, most notably restricted empirical generalizability. The study focuses on genre combinations in the health-care industry associated with a large number of industry-specific characteristics that affect IS use [19, 21]. Moreover, the hospital involved in this study stands out by being U.S.-based and broadly recognized as one of the world’s leading teaching hospitals. The hospital also runs its own medical informatics research center. Nevertheless, the IS use observed was comparable to practices I have encountered in other U.S. and European hospital systems. Finally, the data presented here gives prevalence to paper-based IS. This largely reflects the empirical reality of this and most other health-care settings. In short, conclusions and generalizations can only be drawn from this study with these limitations in mind. Future research would benefit from studying genre combinations in other industries and settings more saturated by electronic IS. Despite these limitations, the research offers important contributions to the IT and organizational literature, and implications for practice.

## Implications for Research

The implications of IT systems have long been examined one system at a time, with few studies attempting to conceptualize the interactions among multiple systems [24, 25]. The characteristics of any one system may not possess the needed utility; rather, it is the collective synergies of multiple systems drawing on different media that provide utility [32, p. 111]. The conceptual apparatus articulating the relations among multiple IS represents this paper’s most important contribution to research. Through an extension of a practice-based genre theory the present research suggests that a broad approach to document genre combinations can shed light on communication practices that are often obscure to novices of an organizational environment.

Future research studies can draw on the data-gathering approach presented in this paper. Figure 3 summarizes the general steps structuring such a document genre combination inquiry. A hermeneutic process structures the overall research endeavor, where the research moves from investigating the whole communicative practice to its parts and back to the whole [13, 27]. This process is repeated countless times through the data-gathering and analysis phases of the research. What constitutes the whole communication practice and its parts depends on the specific research question under investigation. In the present study, information sharing in the ER involving doctors and nurses represented the whole and the use of individual genre parts. If the study had focused on the information sharing across primary care clinics, ER, and inpatient floors, the whole would have to be adjusted to match this broader investigation.

As depicted in Figure 3, it takes more than following the mere people, task, or documents to develop a thick description of document genres and their combinations utilized in a given environment. It is the triangulation of the four perspectives that allows the researcher to develop rich understandings of communication practices and their organizational context. As illustrated by past practice-based studies, it is often helpful to follow the producers and users of documents in an effort to understand their use of specific systems, both in specific situations and in the context of the whole work system. Likewise, following the task is important. In the present study, the process of caring for Emily in the ER represented the task at hand for the doctors and nurses. A sole focus on this process (i.e., the task), however, would only have revealed the sequential combination of document genres. The goal is to capture the accumulation of documents involved, following the documents through the department and seeing those trajectories in the context of the patients’ trajectories and the work process of the doctors and the nurses, respectively. Finally, following the conflict often allows the researcher to understand situations leading toward or undergoing change as illustrated by the ER staff meeting vignette. As the researcher follows the people, tasks, documents, and conflicts, it is important to ask the same broad questions. As summarized in Figure 3, the researcher should map the particular purposes, content, form, time, place, and set of participants of a document genre from each point of view. Answers to these questions could be gathered and analyzed using a variation of Table 1, thus allowing the researcher to summarize a genre and its combinations. Figures 1 and 2, likewise, offer guidelines to how the researcher inductively can build maps of the emergent genres and their various combinations.

The present research also demonstrates how organizational members can associate a document genre with more than one purpose. The flexibility afforded by multiple combinations of an individual genre allows people to seamlessly switch between different uses of a document. What may be considered peripheral elements of a document in one combination may be swept to the center of attention in another situation and vice versa. This finding contributes to the broader practice-oriented IS literature [8, 26, 30, 31, 37, 45, 49].

First, researchers have long contended that end users often do not use IS as those systems’ designers intended [36, 44, 46]. The notion of genre combinations allows us to further articulate how these unintended uses can coexist with a genre’s original purpose and become part of institutionalized practices. Organizational members develop genre combinations in order to serve needs and purposes not envisioned or intended by system developers.

Second, the present research supports the recent literature on boundary spanning and boundary objects [1, 11, 18, 29, 30, 40], and helps explain how one IS can serve heterogeneous groups with radically different goals and purposes. The expect sheet, flow sheet, or whiteboard genres serve multiple audiences, bridging boundaries between several organizations and occupations that are, at times, driven by divergent goals. By developing accepted combinations of genres, any of these groups can use the existing IS to serve its own purposes and goals.

## Implications for Practice

Managers and systems designers will profit from better understanding the relations among the many different IS routinely used by organizational members. The notion of genre combinations offers important insight into how people, through their daily work practices, relate IS of various media to one another. An understanding of such dynamics can help managers (1) visualize current and projected communication practices, (2) describe and detect emergent communicative practices, and (3) forecast end users’ acceptance of new IS.

First, the paradigm of genre systems and their various combinations allows managers to map existing communicative practices within and across organizational units. Such a process involves describing existing and emerging communicative actions invoked in response to recurrent situations. For each IS genre and its combination, one can depict the particular purpose, content, form, time, place, and set of participants. The framework adds to the existing stock of system development tools such as business process flow charts and “swim lane diagrams” [3]. One can depict work systems as not only sequentially organized but also involving multiple accumulations, which ties IS use closely to the media involved, the social organization of the setting, and the physical layout of the workplace.

Second, genre combinations can serve as a starting point for identifying possible changes in a work system. Emergent practice often takes root in genre combination. As demonstrated in the ER staff meeting, genre combinations can accommodate changes in the system or absorb new technologies. Genre combinations do not take as much coordination as designing and implementing a new stand-alone communicative genre. The existence of partly outdated IS will often be accommodated by genre combinations, thereby adding new purposes, times, places, and participants to the genre use. A careful study of genre combinations offers a window into how communities of practice develop “work-arounds” to buffer themselves from outdated or overly restraining canonical business processes. Ideas for new system designs often emerge from such noncanonical communicative practices [15, 17].

Third, genre combination can likewise offer insights on potential resistance to new IS. A well-mapped genre system may offer a checklist for managers considering new systems or planning on implementing new technology. Will the new system go against deep-rooted genre expectations and unsettle social relations? Likewise, if carefully combined with existing genres, the implementation of a new system may become more appealing to organizational members, and thus add capabilities without eliminating old practices too quickly.
