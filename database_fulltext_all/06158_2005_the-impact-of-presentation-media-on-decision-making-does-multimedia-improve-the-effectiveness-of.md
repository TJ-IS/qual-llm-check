# The impact of presentation media on decision making: does multimedia improve the effectiveness of feedback?

Kai H. Lim<sup>a,\*</sup>, Marcus J. O’Connor<sup>b</sup>, William E. Remus<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Systems, Faculty of Business, City University of

Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, Hong Kong

<sup>b</sup>University of Sydney, Sydney, Australia

<sup>c</sup>University of Hawaii, Honolulu, HI, USA

Received 8 July 2001; received in revised form 14 August 2002; accepted 27 December 2003 Available online 9 April 2004

## Abstract

This paper reports an experiment that examines the impact of presentation media on the effectiveness of feedback information in a decision-making task. The study was based on control theory and resource-matching theory (RMT). A laboratory experiment with 72 participants was conducted in the context of providing feedback when using a decision support system. Consistent with hypotheses derived from theory, when negative feedback is delivered using non-vivid (textual (TEXT)) messages, it induces a higher level of subsequent task performance than when it is delivered using vivid (multimedia (MM)) messages. On the other hand, contrary to the expectation from Control theory, negative feedback, in general, does not lead to a higher level of subsequent task performance. The implications of the findings on multimedia are discussed. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Multimedia; Decision making; Control theory; Feedback; Resource-matching theory; Vividness effect; Multimedia instruction; Decision support systems; Human/computer interaction

## 1. Introduction

Multimedia (MM) technology is a multi-million dollar industry. It is being rapidly applied to many areas of education, business, and government. Yet, in the area of computer-supported decision-making tasks, we know little about how multimedia differs from the text version of the same information. Thus, applicable theory needs to be developed and tested. Of particular interest is the effectiveness of feedback when used in computer-supported tasks. Specifically, the objective of this study was to investigate the effectiveness of multimedia information over textual (TEXT) information when used to provide feedback in decision making. The Cognitive Technology Group at Vanderbilt [7] defines multimedia as the ‘‘linkage of text, sound, video, graphics, and the computer in such a way that the user’s access to these media becomes non-linear and virtually instantaneous.’ Two good examples of multimedia information presentation can be found at http://abcnews.go.com and http://pbskids.org/lions/songs.

Conventional computer-based feedback generates textual feedback messages based on the user’s task performance. Such messages are often impersonal and do not capture the recipient’s attention. Multimedia technology presents an opportunity to enhance the feedback. On the other hand, the rich information conveyed using multimedia and the ‘‘slickness’’ of the presentation might distract recipient’s attention from the main message, thus decreasing the effectiveness of the feedback.

As multimedia production is still expensive and multimedia software requires increasingly powerful and expensive hardware, it is useful to determine the situations in which investments in such technology are beneficial [20]. More specifically, although the literature provides some preliminary guidelines on the use of multimedia, these are not specific enough to be useful recommendations. In this paper, we apply the theories from the feedback and vividness effect literatures to the task of providing decision feedback in DSS. Several predictions were generated and tested in a laboratory experiment. We also attempted to provide some specific guidelines and recommendation on the effective use of multimedia for feedback and training purposes. This is in line with the current literature on the effective use of multimedia in an organizational decision-making context (e.g. [15,21–23]).

## 2. Theory development

Here, we apply theories about feedback and vividness effect to providing feedback on performance.

## 2.1. Feedback sign

Managers have long recognized feedback about individuals’ task performance as an important factor that promotes the overall growth and effectiveness of an organization. Research has also consistently shown that feedback has strong positive effects on both individuals and groups [2,12,19,27,32,34,37].

A number of theories have been developed to account for the relationship between feedback sign (positive versus negative) and subsequent task performance (e.g. social cognitive theory [4]; control theory [8]; goal setting theory [25]). Among these, control theory is more applicable to the decision-making domain and hence will be discussed in detail.

Control theory postulates that the discrepancy between the feedback that people receive and their internal standards form the basis for their subsequent reactions. The theory states that people naturally desire to eliminate or reduce the feedback-internal standard discrepancy by attempting to attain the standard. Specially, when a negative feedback is given (i.e. signaling that performance is below the standard), control theory predicts that effort will typically be increased. On the other hand, when positive feedback is given (i.e. signaling that performance meets or exceeds the standard), control theory predicts that effort is typically reduced or maintained.<sup>1</sup> Several studies provided empirical support for this theory [1,6,18,31].

## 2.2. Vividness effect

Nisbett and Ross [28] described ‘‘vivid’’ information as ‘‘ . . . likely to attract and hold our attention and to excite the imagination to the extent that it is (a) emotionally interesting; (b) concrete and imageryprovoking; and (c) proximate in a sensory, temporal, or spatial way.’’ It is intuitive that vividly presented information has impact and is persuasive. Advertisers operate on this assumption in designing ads to sell their products.

The literature on vividness effect, in general, suggests a positive relationship between vividness and effectiveness of the messages [16,30]. However, the experimental literature supporting this relationship is at best weak [40]. In an attempt to explain this, Keller and Block [17] proposed a comprehensive theory called resource-matching theory (RMT), which suggests that vivid messages are more effective only under certain conditions.

According to RMT, the effectiveness of a message is maximized when the cognitive resources allocated to elaborate the message matches the cognitive resources required for the task. The theory argues that the allocation of too few resources results in impoverished elaboration and comprehension, thus limiting the effectiveness of the feedback. On the other hand, if too many resources are used, the excess resources may be used to generate counter-arguments or irrelevant associations to the message. As a result, the effectiveness of the feedback message may be undermined.<sup>2</sup>

RMT assumes that vivid information is less resource demanding than non-vivid information. Conceptually, this assumption is reasonable. For example, Nisbett and Ross argued that vivid information is (1) more likely to attract and hold attention and excite imagination; (2) more emotionally interesting; and (3) provokes images. Also, in contrasting video with textual information, researchers of the Cognitive Technology Group at Vanderbilt argued that video allows a more vivid representation of events than text. Video is dynamic, visual, and spatial. Therefore, it allows people to form mental pictures of problem situations more easily. Lim [24] also argued that when information is conveyed vividly (such as using multimedia presentation) people need not extrapolate meaning artificially. Instead, meaning is directly perceived from the phenomenon, thus reducing the cognitive effort required.

In summary, RMT predicts that when the cognitive resources allocated to process messages are low, vivid messages are more effective than non-vivid messages. On the other hand, it predicts that when the cognitive resources allocated are high, non-vivid messages are more effective.

## 2.3. The vividness effect and multimedia

Different media have different capabilities in presenting information vividly. Video, for example, preserves many of the non-verbal cues of the com municator, making the information and message more persuasive.

The multimedia literature attributes the benefits of multimedia to its four unique characteristics: complementary cues, rich language, authentic context, and interactivity. Of these, only rich language contributes directly to the vividness of a presentation. The richness of a medium refers to the range of symbol systems available to convey information. Every symbol system has it strengths and limitations. For example, while conventional wisdom suggests that ‘‘a picture is worth a thousand words,’’ pictures cannot convey the meaning of conditional events or causes (i.e. if, nevertheless, or because). Pictures do not entail any logical connections [38]. Another advantage of multimedia presentation is that the vividness focuses and engages individuals’ attentions [10], presumably, because vivid information required less cognitive effort.

By combining the computer with video medium, multimedia technology brings together the symbolic and processing capabilities of various media and thus creates a new and rich symbol system of communication. By combining various media, multimedia is capable of making the messages salient and vivid.

## 2.4. Hypotheses

We designed the experiment to test the effect of multimedia, when used to provide feedback on decision task performance. Control theory predicts that individuals have a natural desire to reduce or eliminate any discrepancy between feedback and their internal standards. When feedback indicates that one’s performance is below standard (negative feedback), a person attempts to reduce the discrepancy by increasing his or her effort on subsequent work. As a result, the task performance improves<sup>3</sup>. On the other hand, when feedback indicates that one has met or exceeded the standard (positive feedback), a person’s effort on subsequent work will remain constant. Based on this, we hypothesize:

H1. Negative feedback leads to a higher level of subsequent task performance than positive feedback.

Since negative feedback indicates a standard-performance discrepancy, negative feedback incites the message recipient to think about the cause of the discrepancy and attempt to find ways to reduce or eliminate the discrepancy. All these induce cognitive effort. On the other hand, positive feedback indicates that the recipient has been ‘‘on the right track.’’ As such, positive feedback induces less cognitive effort.

Resource-matching theory predicts that the effectiveness of a feedback message depends on whether the cognitive resources required to process the message match the cognitive effort expended in processing it.

Since vivid messages require less cognitive resources, they are more effective when a low level of effort is expended. However, when a high level of effort is expended, non-vivid messages are more effective than vivid messages. See Rook [35,36] for empirical support to this argument.

Negative messages inherently induce a higher level of cognitive effort to process and elaborate than do positive messages. Taken together with RMT, this suggests that when negative feedback is given, a non-vivid message is more effective than a vivid message. Therefore,

H2a. For negative feedback, non-vivid messages are more effective than vivid messages.

Since positive feedback induces a lower level of processing effort, RMT predicts that a vivid message is more effective. However, positive feedback indicates that no change is needed. Thus, regardless of the vividness and of message, any changes in subsequent task performance may be marginal. Therefore,

H2b. For positive feedback, non-vivid messages are as effective as vivid messages.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Participants

The 72 participants in the experiment were undergraduate students at a large public university. They were recruited from a junior level course required of all business students. Participation was rewarded with points towards the final course grade.

## 3.2. The experimental task

The experimental task is based on Holt et al. [14] model of the production-scheduling problem. This involves deciding how many units to produce, given an uncertain future demand and the knowledge of current work force size, productivity, and inventory level. The problem was selected because it is managerially relevant and has been calibrated with actual data [13]. The optimal rules for this problem are discussed in Remus [33].

The equation modeling the production-scheduling decision is:

production decision

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & = b _ {1 0} + b _ {1 1} \times (\text { units   that   could   be   produced   by } \\ & \quad \text { work   force }) - b _ {1 2} \times (\text { inventory   on   hand }) \\ & \quad + (b _ {1 2} + b _ {1 3} + b _ {1 4}) \times (\text { forecast   demand }) \end{array}
$$

The coefficients used in this equation were estimated by Holt et al. [13] for the production-scheduling decision at Pittsburgh Plate Glass. Their coefficients and related mathematics are shown in the Appendix A. The randomness added to the task was normally distributed with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of 133. Thus, although there was a decision rule governing the optimal solution, there are some random elements built into the model, much as in the real world.

In this experiment, the equation provided a standard for judging each participant’s decision performance. In our analysis, we examined each participant’s accuracy (as measured by the absolute value of the difference between the participant’s decision and the one given by the above equation, i.e. absolute error).

We wanted to be sure that the participants would do a competent job in making their decisions. To assure this, we provided three categories of information feedback: simple outcome, task information, and cognitive feedback messages.

(1) Simple outcome feedback (obtaining the actual value of a particular decision made) is the most naturally occurring feedback and it was provided following each decision. This mirrors real world production decision making, in which, at the end of each production cycle, decision makers learn whether their decision resulted in surplus, shortage, or no change in inventory. Therefore, after each decision, the participants were given the optimal decision and their error rate (e.g. 15% from the optimal) for the decision.

(2) Task information feedback is information about the true structure of the task. As in Balzer et al. [3], the optimal cue weights (shown in the Appendix A) were provided for the decision tasks, i.e. the participants were told the optimal weights for each of the factors. This task information feedback was given to the participants after they performed the first 20 decisions.

Both outcome feedback and task informational feedback are controlled factors. All participants, regardless of their experimental condition, received outcome feedback after each decision trial and information feedback at the end of the 20th trial. This was to ensure that the participants could competently perform the task and, at the same time, it provided realism to the task context.

(3) Cognitive feedback is information about the participant’s cognitive strategy, including the relationship between optimal cues and the participant’s judgments. In our experiment, participants were shown their cue weights for the last 20 trials and told what they should do to improve their decision accuracy (e.g. pay less attention to the inventory but more attention to the changes in demand). Half of the participants received cognitive feedback in a textual format while the other half received them in a multimedia format. Task and cognitive feedback simulate the real-world situation in which production planners occasionally receive feedback from their superiors on their task performance.

The experimental manipulation occurred at the cognitive feedback level. Depending on their perfor mance, the subjects received either positive or negative cognitive feedback. Further, half of the subjects were randomly given the cognitive feedback using multimedia information while the others were given feedback using textual information.

## 3.3. Research design

The research design focuses on two dimensions. One is feedback sign (either a positive or negative message). The other is the vividness of the feedback messages; the two levels are: high vividness (provided by multimedia) and low vividness (provided using text).

The nature of the feedback message provided was related to the participant’s actual task performance. The system evaluated participants’ performances after the first 20 trials.<sup>4</sup> As part of the feedback, participants were told the optimal decision weights and, as part of the cognitive feedback, their own decision weights over the last 20 trials were given. A sample message display for the feedback is shown in Fig. 1.

Also, each feedback variable had a button assigned to it; when this was ‘‘clicked,’’ the display would explain the meaning of that feedback measure. These explanations were two to three sentences evaluations of the participant’s performance; e.g. a positive message could be: ‘‘You are right on the target in emphasizing the relative importance of the inventory level.’ An example of a negative message is ‘‘You have placed no emphasis on the CPE. You should pay more attention to the CPE forecast. Remember, the optimal score is 56%.’’ Based on [29], we considered solutions within 10% of the optimal to be good and positive feedback was then given.

The participants were also randomly assigned to receive the feedback in different media. In the textual treatment, all the feedback was presented in text form. In the multimedia treatment, video clips of an ‘‘expert’’<sup>5</sup> giving advice were played in the center lower half of the screen on top of the text messages. The video and textual messages used identical words. Fig. 2 shows the corresponding interface for the multimedia condition.

In the multimedia treatment, the video feedback messages were delivered by one of the co-authors, teaching the statistics course in which the participants were enrolled during the experiment. Each of the feedback messages was filmed three times. The one with the clearest voice, most natural facial expression, and body gesture was selected and digitized for use.

In all treatments, the participants performed 20 trials of the decision-making task followed by feedback. Following this feedback, the participants made another 20 decisions.

## 3.4. Dependent variable

The only dependent variable in the study is the postfeedback decision accuracy (in the last 20 decision trials). It is measured by the absolute value of the difference between the participant’s and the optimal decision: the smaller the number, the better the decision.

## 3.5. Procedures

The participants were trained in the use of the software immediately before beginning the experiment. The participants were told that they would be weighing cues in making production-planning deci sions and that they would be provided with feedback on their decision accuracy to aid them in improving their decisions. The context for the decisions was given and the variables explained. The experimenter demonstrated the software and the participants then made two practice decisions to end the session.

Visual Basic software running on personal computers was used to display data for the experimental task and gather the participants’ decisions. Participants first viewed the demand forecast (composite planning estimate, CPE), inventory level, and the maximum amount that the current work force could produce; the screen showing these is displayed as Fig. 3. The participants then had to decide how many units to produce. When making their decisions, they used a mouse to move the slider and ‘‘click’’ to record the value. Following the decision, the value of the optimal decision was displayed (based on the optimal values of $b _ { 1 0 } , \ b _ { 1 1 } , \ b _ { 1 2 } , \ b _ { 1 3 } .$ , and $b _ { 1 4 }$ shown in the Appendix $\mathbf { A } )$

The participants performed 20 decisions before receiving the cognitive feedback manipulation (either positive-MM, positive-TEXT, negative-MM, or negative-TEXT). After this, they made another 20 decisions.The variables used for making the decisions were generated using the random number function in Visual Basic; the generator was seeded with the participant’s social security number. The average time taken to complete all the tasks was less than 1 h.

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Manipulation checks

## 4.1.1. Before receiving feedback, were the participants equal across treatments?

When performing the decision task prior to the feedback manipulation, there should be no significant differences between the participants in the MM versus TEXT treatments since the experimental manipulation had not yet occurred. The absolute errors of the participants in the decision task across the two treatments were not significantly different $( t ( 7 0 ) = 0 . 5 1 5 .$ $P < 0 . 5 9 1 )$ . Thus, we found no evidence that participants in the two treatments differed in performance in the decision task prior to receiving the feedback manipulation.

## 4.1.2. Did the feedback improve the participant’s performance?

To evaluate the participant’s performance before and after the feedback manipulation, we used t-tests pairing participant pre- and post-feedback performances. Absolute error improved from 158 to 139 $( t ( 7 1 ) = 2 . 6 5 , P < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The tests therefore found evidence of improved participant performance on the decision-making task.

## 4.2. Results

A two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the data. The first dimension was vividness level (textual or multimedia). The second dimension was the sign of the feedback message; positive or negative, based on the participant’s performance (the post-feedback decision accuracy). Pre-feedback decision accuracy was used as a covariate in the analysis.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

In the experiment, we observed that for participants who did not perform well initially and therefore received negative feedback, text messages (non-vivid) were more effective than multimedia messages (vivid). Specifically, for the participants who received negative feedback, non-vivid messages led to higher level of subsequent task performance than vivid messages. This result is consistent with the prediction of RMT.

On the other hand, our results show that for participants who performed well initially and thus received positive feedback, text messages were as effective as multimedia messages. Positive feedback indicates to the recipient of the message that his/her performance was satisfactory and that no change was needed. As such, regardless of the vividness of the message, the change on subsequent task performance, if any, caused by the feedback was marginal.

Finally, control theory suggests that people have a natural inclination to reduce the discrepancies between their internally set standard and their performance outcome. However we found that, when compared to positive feedback, negative feedback does not lead to a higher level of subsequent task performance. One plausible reason for this inconsistency is that the effect of feedback sign might be more complicated than expected. For example, Podsakoff and Farh [31] proposed that feedback messages consist of two elements: informational and motivational. The informational element allows the decision maker to assess his/her immediate past performance and provides cues on how to improve future performance. Depending on whether the feedback is positive or negative, the motivational element can either be an encouraging or discouraging one. Control theory only focuses on the information element of feedback.

From the motivational perspective, social learning theory [5] suggests that positive feedback that indicates success or high performance enhances self-efficacy, which in turn induces a higher level of motivation and expended effort and ultimately leads to better task performance. Conversely, negative feedback that suggests failure or low performance diminishes self-efficacy. Therefore, in our case, the motivational element of feedback might have countered the effect of the information element of feedback, which could be the reason that we did not observe a significant effect on feedback sign.

## 5.1. Limitations

Several limitations were noted:

1. Regrettably, we did not include a manipulation check of how our participants actually perceived the expert (was he credible?). However, our participants behaved as predicted. Therefore, arguably, they must have believed that the expert was credible.

2. As a cross-sectional study, the observations only reflect a one-shot view of the phenomenon. Possibly, the relationship between task performance and feedback is a bi-directional, i.e. task performance outcome could affect the next round of feedback received.

3. We did not test the learning style of the participants, who were probably, like most college students, verbal learners. However, we do not attempt to generalize our findings beyond managers/decision makers who have received college education.

## 5.2. Contribution

This study applied and tested control theory in the domain of decision support systems. Although well known in the decision and organizational literature, the theory has never been applied and tested in the DSS domain. Also, the findings should not be limited to decision-making tasks but be generalizable to other structured task context or to computer-based training in general.

Along the same line, another theoretical contribution is that it draws on resource-matching theory, which was developed in the marketing domain, and applies to the DSS domain.

Most importantly, this study shows that the use of a rich medium may not only be a waste of resources but may be counter-productive. From a broader perspective, the study also points out that there might be other situations in which rich media should not be used.

In terms of practical implications, the study also presents specific design guidelines for providing feedback for decision support systems. Despite the hype surrounding multimedia, designers should be careful in using it for providing feedback.

Appendix A. The decomposition of the production scheduling decision

The experimental tasks are based on the Holt et al. [14] model of the production scheduling problem.

The production scheduling problem involves both a decision task (deciding how much to produce) and forecasting task (forecasting product demand). The managerial intent is to make good forecasts and decisions thereby minimizing the average cost as computed by a quadratic function. The cost is a function of changes in work force level, overtime/idle time costs, and departures from the ideal level of inventory. The coefficients of quadratic cost function used in the experiment was found by Holt et al. ([13], p. 163) except that, following Moskowitz and Miller [26], only the quadratic cost terms were used. The resulting set of optimal rules is discussed in Remus [33].The production problem was characterized by Holt et al. [13] with the following equation:

production decision

$$
\begin{array}{l} = b _ {1 0} + b _ {1 1} \times (\text { work   force   last   month }) + b _ {1 2} \\ \quad \times (\text { the   current   month's   forecast }) + b _ {1 3} \\ \quad \times (\text { the   forecast   for   next   month }) + b _ {1 4} \\ \quad \times (\text { the   forecast   for   2   months   ahead }) - b _ {1 2} \\ \quad \times (\text { inventory   on   hand }). \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In this experiment, we decomposed the production problem into two tasks, the first task was forecasting demand and the second task was deciding how much to produce given the forecast. In the first task, the forecast was made based on three other forecasts, we termed the resulting forecast a composite planning estimate (CPE) of future demand. The equation representing the composite planning estimate task is as follows:

$$
\text { composite   planning   estimate } = \frac {(b _ {1 2} \times (\text { the   current   month's   forecast }) + b _ {1 3} \times (\text { the   forecast   for   next   month }) + b _ {1 4} \times (\text { the   forecast   for   2   months   ahead }))}{(b _ {1 2} + b _ {1 3} + b _ {1 4})}\tag{2}
$$

The problem is deciding how many units to produce and workers to employ given uncertain future demand and knowledge of current work force size, productivity, and inventory level. The production scheduling problem was selected because it is a managerially relevant problem and it has been calibrated with actual data ([13], p. 163). This task was decomposed into two experimental tasks, a forecasting and decision-making task as is described below.

After the forecasting task was complete, the decision of how many units to produce can be made using the work force size, inventory level, and composite planning estimate:

production decision

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & = b _ {1 0} + b _ {1 1} \times (\text { work   force   last   month }) - b _ {1 2} \\ & \quad \times (\text { inventory   on   hand }) + (b _ {1 2} + b _ {1 3} + b _ {1 4}) \\ & \quad \times (\text { composite   planning   estimate }) \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

To make the decision task clearer to the participants, we did not provide the work force level directly but instead provided the units that could be produced by the work force last month. That is, we multiplied the work force level by the worker’s productivity and also divided the coefficient $b _ { 1 1 }$ by the worker’s productivity to offset the just described multiplication. In this way, all variables were dimensioned in units. Thus, the above equation can be restated as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { production   decision } = b _ {1 0} + \left(\frac {b _ {1 1}}{k}\right) \\ \text {(units that could be produced by work force last month)} \\ - b _ {1 2} \times (\text { inventory   on   hand }) + (b _ {1 2} + b _ {1 3} + b _ {1 4}) \\ \times (\text { composite   planning   estimate }) \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

where k is the worker productivity. In the equation shown in the main body of the paper $b _ { 1 1 } / k$ is replaced by $b _ { 1 1 } ^ { \prime }$ . we also needed the inventory equation:

current inventory level

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & = \text { last   periods   inventory } + \text { production } \\ & - \text { demand } \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

and an equation that calculated cost of non-optimal production levels as well as non-optimal levels of inventory:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { cost } = C _ {1} \times (\text { production } \\ \quad - \text { worker   productivity } \times \text { work   force   size }) ^ {2} \\ \quad + C _ {2} \times (\text { inventory } \\ \quad - \text { desired   optimal   inventory   level }) ^ {2} \end{array}\tag{6}
$$
