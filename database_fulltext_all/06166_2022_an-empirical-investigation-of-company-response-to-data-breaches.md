# AN EMPIRICAL INVESTIGATION OF COMPANY RESPONSE TO DATA BREACHES<sup>1</sup>

Hamid Reza Nikkhah Department of Information and Process Management, Bentley University Waltham, MA U.S.A. {hnikkhah@bentley.edu}

Varun Grover Information Systems Department, Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas Fayetteville, AR U.S.A. {vgrover@uark.edu}

Companies may face serious adverse consequences as a result of a data breach event. To repair the potential damage to relationships with stakeholders after data breaches, companies adopt a variety of response strategies. However, the effects of these response strategies on the behavior of stakeholders after a data breach are unclear; differences in response times may also affect these outcomes, depending on the notification laws that apply to each company. As part of a multimethod study, we first identified the adopted response strategies in Study 1 based on content analysis of the response letters issued by publicly traded U.S. companies (n = 204) following data breaches; these strategies include any combination of the following: corrective action, apology, and compensation. We also found that breached companies may remain silent and adopt a “no action” strategy. In Studies 2 and 3, we examined the effects of various response strategies and response times on the predominant stakeholders affected by data breaches: customers and investors. In Study 2, we focused on customers and present a moderated-moderated-mediation model based on the expectancy violation theory. To test this model, we designed a factorial survey with 15 different conditions (n = 811). In Study 3, we focused on investors and conducted an event study (n = 166) to examine their reactions to company responses to data breaches. The results indicate the presence of moderating effects of certain response strategies; surprisingly, we did not find compensation to be more effective than apology. The magnitude of the moderating effects of response strategies is contingent upon response time. We also found that the negative effects of data breaches disappear after six months. We interpret the results and provide implications for research and practice.

Keywords: Data breach, cybersecurity, response strategy, response time, data breach notification laws, multimethod, factorial survey, event study

## Introduction

Despite considerable investment in cybersecurity, data breaches have grown exponentially. The number of exposed records reached 30 billion in 2020, exceeding the previous 15 years combined (Glenny, 2021), which created severe consequences for organizations such as reputation and revenue loss (D’Arcy et al., 2020). According to a study by IBM, the average cost of a data breach in the United States in 2020 was \$8.6 million (IBM, 2020). Prior research has indicated that breaches also affect the various stakeholders of these companies, arousing negative reactions from investors and customers (Choi et al., 2016; Gwebu et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2017; Yayla & Hu, 2011).

To alleviate negative consequences and recover damaged relationships with customers and investors, many companies have created a response plan for data breach events. Security experts have acknowledged the importance of a sophisticated timely response in the aftermath of a data breach event to help companies improve their impaired relationships with customers and investors (Barnes, 2021; Hamilton, 2019). Research has also affirmed the effectiveness of a company response in decreasing negative reactions to crisis events, including data breaches (Goode et al., 2017; Hoehle et al., 2022; Mansor & KaderAli, 2017; Tsarenko & Tojib, 2015). Although many companies recognize the importance of a response, each responds to data breaches differently. For example, Nvidia only provided protective recommendations to those affected by its data breach in 2015. Boeing adopted a different strategy in 2017; it apologized to and compensated its affected customers in addition to providing protective recommendations. Notwithstanding various responses to data breach events, the efficacy of these responses in decreasing the negative outcomes of a data breach remains unknown.

Further, prior studies have indicated that customers and investors may react differently to companies’ responses (e.g., Rasoulian et al., 2017), and that it is therefore unclear whether data breach response strategies have the same effect on these two stakeholders. Consequently, our first research objective is to investigate the repertoire of response strategies adopted by breached companies and examine their effects on customers and investors. Evaluating the outcomes of various response strategies is crucial for companies seeking to employ the most effective response strategies for their stakeholders. It also contributes to crisis response management research by improving the understanding of the strategies adopted by companies in data breach crises.

Another facet of data breach response that companies should address after an incident pertains to the laws and regulations that the government imposes on breached companies. Data breach notification laws in the United States require breached companies to notify affected people. Whereas some states demand an immediate response after a breach (e.g., California and Illinois), others allow notifications to be sent 30-45 days later (e.g., Florida and Ohio).<sup>2</sup> Aside from the legal time frame of notification, security experts have emphasized the importance of response time and recommended announcing a data breach response as soon as possible (Hawthorn, 2016; Matteson, 2017). If response time influences response strategy potency, breached companies should make greater efforts to expedite the delivery of their breach responses; otherwise, there is no need to allocate more resources to increase the speed of adopting and publishing a response strategy. However, prior research has not considered the role of response time and whether response time affects the efficacy of response strategies. Thus, the second objective of this research is to examine the effect of response time on the impact of various response strategies.

To fulfill our research objectives, we conducted three empirical studies. In Study 1, we investigate breached companies’ response strategies (n = 204) to identify the different patterns of adopted response strategies. We use the expectancy violation theory (EVT) to argue that the dissatisfaction stemming from data breaches drives customers’ and investors’ reactions. We then present a moderated-moderated-mediation model (Hayes, 2018) based on EVT to examine whether various response strategies can moderate the effect of dissatisfaction on negative reactions and whether the moderating effects of such strategies depend on response time. In Study 2, we conducted a factorial survey (n = 811) with 15 different conditions, and in Study 3, we conducted an event study (n = 166) to examine the effects of response strategies and response times on customers and investors, respectively.

This research makes several theoretical contributions. First, our research contributes to cybersecurity and crisis management research by outlining various actual responses to data breaches based on company-issued letters following data breaches. We reviewed these letters and created a profile of various combinations of response strategies. Second, this research extends service recovery and cybersecurity research, given that little research exists that investigates customers and investors in the same study with respect to companies responses to data security service failures. We examine customers’ and investors’ reactions to various response strategies, which has critical implications for managers in adopting response strategies that effectively influence stakeholders and decrease data breach costs. Finally, this study is among the first to investigate response time based on data breach notification laws; it extends cybersecurity and crisis management research by delineating the role of time in the aftermath of a data breach crisis and discerning the contingency of response strategies on response time.

## Background and Theoretical Foundations

In this section, we first review crisis response strategies and discuss how prior research examines the major organizational responses to crises. We then describe EVT to argue how data breaches violate individuals’ expectations, leading to negative behaviors. We also review prior data breach studies and discuss the main findings, followed by content analysis (Study 1) to determine the actual responses that are salient in the context of data breach crises.

## Crisis Response Strategies

A crisis often represents a fundamental threat to the stability of an organization, including its image, legitimacy, profitability, and survival (Ulmer & Sellnow, 2002). Therefore, crisis management is vital for any organization in order to maintain its stability and current position in the market. Prior studies have argued that crisis management uses different rhetorical, impression management, and accountgiving concepts to identify an appropriate response to protect the organization’s image during and after a crisis (Allen & Caillouet, 1994; Benoit, 1994; Caillouet & Allen, 1996; Coombs, 1995, 1998). As part of crisis management, organizations that face a crisis should respond quickly because stakeholders and media demand immediate, thorough, and qualified responses (Hegner et al., 2016). Crisis management research has emphasized that crises almost always provoke a bad impression of the focal organization and cause stakeholders to engage in negative behaviors. For instance, Wangenheim (2005) found that a crisis can cause customers to be unhappy and spread negative word of mouth (WOM)— that is, negative communication with others about the organization and its products—which can then draw potential new customers away from the organization. However, when an appropriate strategy is adopted after a crisis, the subsequent negative outcomes can be considerably decreased (Borah et al., 2020; Grégoire et al., 2018).

Response strategies are the resources that help organizations protect or repair their image after a crisis (Coombs, 1998). Crisis management research has enumerated several response strategies with different tactics that aid organizations during and after a crisis. In this regard, Coombs (1998) classified various response strategies and presented a defensiveaccommodative response continuum based on a company accepting responsibility for a crisis and attempting to repair the resulting damage. These responses can also be categorized based on their tactics and level of remorse into defensive strategies, moderate strategies, and accommodative strategies (Gwebu et al., 2018). ## Expectancy Violation Theory

We use EVT to understand individuals’ emotions and reactions following a data breach crisis. EVT was originally developed by Burgoon and Jones (1976) to explain how individuals perceive and interpret violations of their personal space (Bevan et al., 2014). According to EVT, humans hold expectations that characterize and frame their interactions with others; subsequently, they behave according to these expectations (Afifi & Metts, 1998; Bevan et al., 2014; Burgoon, 1993). An expectation is a consistent pattern of predictable behavior and should be considered in relation to a specific individual, context, and relationship (Burgoon, 1993). Expectations can also be understood according to the interactional content and relational subtext (Burgoon & Walther, 1990). Whereas content schemas are receiver-stored knowledge about objects and events, relational schemas are the receiver’s expectations for the ways people relate to one another (Housel & Acker, 1979).

According to EVT, breaking assumed norms, such as information privacy, violates expectations and influences relationships (Afifi & Metts, 1998, p. 368). In particular, Afifi and Metts (1998) defined expectation violation as behavior that a receiver notices as being different from the behavioral display that the individual expected. Burgoon (1978) argued that any behavior that falls outside of a range of expected behaviors produces cognitive arousal and triggers an interpretationevaluation sequence that individuals can use to tackle negative outcomes. Expectations that are far from the expected range of behaviors are salient and therefore provoke immediate and significant changes in relationships (Afifi & Metts, 1998). Thus, violating basic expectations in any relationship produces strong negative perceptions and, subsequently, negative actions.

EVT has been used to investigate the impact of organizational behaviors on stakeholders. In this research domain, researchers have discussed the organizational behaviors that fall outside of expected behaviors and have examined how these expectancy violations affect individuals’ perceptions and behaviors. For example, Cho et al. (2021) studied the public’s reaction to an organization whose sustainable development violated the public’s expectations and found that this expectancy violation negatively affected evaluations of the organization (i.e., credibility, attitude, and supportive behavioral intention) more so than increased expectancy conformity. Lee and Chung (2018) applied EVT to their study of corporate social responsibility (CSR) communication and found that negative emotional visuals and company-cause fit influence stakeholders’ memory of CSR information. In the data breach context, Gwebu et al. (2018) used EVT to explain how an organization’s failure to protect its data violated stakeholder expectancy, causing negative reactions in the stock market.

Data breaches reveal customers’ private information; thus, prior research has argued that violating information privacy is an example of confounding individuals’ expectations (Afifi & Metts, 1998, p. 368). Prior research has also discussed data security as one of the basic expectations of stakeholders (e.g., Ball, 2001; Carroll, 1991) violated by data breaches (Rasoulian et al., 2017). EVT expounds upon the damage caused to relationships by violating these expectations and describes the consequential behaviors, demonstrating the appropriateness of EVT for this study and the data breach context.

## Data Breach Consequences and Responses

Consumer behavior research has extensively discussed how individuals negatively react to a service failure (e.g., Borah et al., 2020; Day et al., 1981; Grégoire et al., 2018). Prior research has noted that a data breach is a type of service failure (e.g., Goode et al. 2017; Rasoulian et al. 2017) because it compromises data security, which is a component of service quality (Lewis & Mitchell, 1990; Yang & Fang, 2004). In fact, a data breach constitutes unauthorized access to an organization’s information that results from a compromise in information security. Data breaches possess salient characteristics that embody a major threat to company survival, which differentiates them from other types of service failures. For instance, data breaches can affect a large number of individuals representing several stakeholder groups. The breach may be publicized in diverse media, and such incidents cannot be easily isolated and repaired (Rasoulian et al., 2017) because the data are in the possession of cybercriminals and ultimately out of company control. Data breaches violate individuals’ privacy (Malhotra & Malhotra, 2011) and imperil other aspects of affected individuals’ lives, especially when personal identification or other sensitive information is stolen. Thus, due to these characteristics, stakeholders exhibit significantly negative behaviors in the event of a data breach. For example, customers may seek to switch to another company or spread negative WOM (Choi et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2017), and investors may sell the breached company’s stock, resulting in a decline in stock price (Malhotra & Malhotra, 2011; Yayla & Hu, 2011).

To better understand the prior findings of data breach research, we conducted a review of 71 studies from various business journals. We also reviewed service recovery studies to better understand response strategies following service failures.<sup>3</sup> These studies argue for the effectiveness of various strategies such as apology, appreciation, compensation, and overcompensation (e.g., Noone, 2012; Puzakova et al., 2013; Wirtz & Mattila, 2004; You et al., 2020). We found that there is limited knowledge about what the single most effective response strategy is after an information protection service failure and data breach.

Studies about the effects of data breach response strategies are sparse, and, although these few studies shed light on the importance of response strategies, they suffer from several limitations. First, it is unknown whether breached companies adopt a single strategy or a combination of strategies. The repertoire of strategies examined is limited, and their relative effectiveness is not clear. Second, breached companies issue notification letters to their stakeholders at different times based on the data breach notification laws in effect in each state. However, prior studies have not investigated the role of response time in data breach events and whether response time influences the effectiveness of response strategies. Finally, prior research has suggested that response strategies may affect customers and investors differently (Marcus & Goodman, 1991; Rasoulian et al., 2017) because while customers pursue event-specific satisfaction (Andaleeb & Conway, 2006), investors are loss-averse and seek long-term returns (Barberis & Huang, 2001; Fama, 1998). However, it has not been empirically examined and no prior study has investigated these two stakeholders simultaneously to evaluate whether differences in their respective reactions to data breach responses exist. Thus, to accurately represent the strategies used in practice and examine their efficacy regarding customers and investors, we conducted content analysis of official data breach response letters from breached companies.

## Study 1: Content Analysis of Data Breach Response Letters

To better understand data breach response strategies used by organizations, we investigated companies’ reactions following data breaches. We found two different dates in relation to a data breach event: the “announcement date” and the “response date.” The announcement date is when the breach events are announced to the public, and the response date is when the breached company issues an official notification letter (that includes response strategies) to stakeholders and legal parties. These two dates may be the same if the breached company publicly announces its breach and includes its response strategies (e.g., compensation) in its announcement. However, there are several cases in which there is no response date or where the response date occurs after the announcement date (e.g., when a breached company issues a response letter after a third party announces the data breach).

First, as permitted by data breach notification laws, breached companies may delay their response to data breaches, but the breach may be announced in the meantime by a third party (e.g., FBI, security forums, hacking groups). Second, breached companies may adopt a “no action” strategy, which refers to the organization’s attempt to avoid problems by remaining silent and refraining from officially responding to the crisis (Chang et al., 2015). This strategy is usually unveiled when a breached company intentionally decides not to announce a data breach after an incident occurs, and the announcement is made by a third party. Finally, a data breach may be announced by a third party while the company remains unaware of the event entirely and is unintentionally silent. In this research, we recognize these last two cases as “no action” because the breached company remains silent, whether intended or not.

To identify the response strategies adopted by companies after data breaches, we conducted content analysis of data breach notification letters. We first collected information on 7,250 data breaches from various sources, such as Privacy Rights Clearinghouse, Databreaches.net, PHIPrivacy.net, and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. We then removed the announcements that were (1) ambiguous, (2) from a non-U.S. or not-for-profit firm, and (3) not publicly traded. We were left with 496 unique events from 2005-2018 that included only publicly traded U.S. organizations. The dataset incorporates different information about data breaches, such as announcement dates.

We then attempted to find the official response letters that the breached companies in our sample provided. Because there is no centralized source in which to find the letters, we downloaded them from various sources, such as the websites of attorneys general,<sup>4</sup> the websites of the breached companies, security blogs, and online news websites. We finally used 204 data breach announcements with respect to official response letters from publicly traded companies that faced data breach incidents. We found that 41 companies adopted a “no action” strategy, including those that did not respond, although they were legally obligated to respond, as well as those that were intentionally or unintentionally silent while a third party announced their breach.<sup>5</sup> We read each response letter from the remaining companies that responded promptly and analyzed their content to identify the adopted strategies (Appendix A shows an illustrative letter). We then coded<sup>6</sup> the adopted strategies based on the response strategies from Table 1.

These findings provide several insights into data breach response strategies in recent years. First, we found that the breached companies did not use defensive strategies: when response letters required by data breach notification laws were issued, companies accepted that a data breach occurred rather than defending it (e.g., denying or attacking the accuser). We also found that breached companies did not adopt moderate strategies alone but rather used moderate strategies in conjunction with accommodative strategies. Accommodative strategies are the main strategies used by breached companies with a higher level of remorse and responsibility (see Table 1) and can be broken down into specific strategies (i.e., corrective action, apology, compensation). We found that all breached companies adopted a corrective strategy, which was used either alone or combined with apology and compensation (Table 2).

Finally, whereas prior research has suggested that corrective actions represent companies’ primary efforts to minimize the negative consequences of crises and attempt to prevent similar events in the future (Coombs, 1998), we found that in data breach events, corrective actions following data breaches require actions from customers as well. For example, breached companies often ask customers to replace their credit cards or change their passwords; in the meantime, the company implements new security measures and hires security professionals. Further, we found that even though compensation is included in the apology response type in the classification of response strategies (Coombs, 1998), some breached companies offered compensation in response letters without apologizing to the affected customers. As a result, we separate apology from compensation as two different response strategies in this research.

## Research Model and Hypothesis

We present a moderated-moderated-mediation model based on prior discussions of crisis response strategies, the content analysis of data breach response letters, and EVT (Figure 1). In such a model, the moderation of the mediation effect by one variable is contingent upon a second moderator, which represents a three-way interaction effect (Hayes, 2018). Figure 1 illustrates our model, which includes moderating effects of response strategies and response times on customers’ and investors’ behaviors separately.

Consistent with EVT framing, we first argue that dissatisfaction stemming from data disclosure mediates the effect of information protection expectancy violation on customers behaviors. Prior research on consumer complaining behaviors has found that when customers become dissatisfied with a company, they take punitive actions against it (for a review, see İstanbulluoğlu et al., 2017). Accordingly, we incorporate the likelihood of switching behavior and negative WOM in the model (Figure 1) because they are common punitive behaviors resulting from a data breach (Choi et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2017). We also discuss investors’ negative behaviors in the stock market following data breach announcements, which leads to negative cumulative abnormal return (CAR).<sup>7</sup> CAR is the sum of the difference between the actual and the expected normal return over the event period (Yayla & Hu, 2011). We then discuss how the effect of the response strategies identified in Study 1 can moderate the magnitude of the mediation effect (Muller et al., 2005; Preacher et al., 2007) and the effect of announcements on CAR.

In addition to the response strategies found in the content analysis of response letters, some organizations do not respond to the data breaches but, instead, attempt to cover up the incident. Therefore, we incorporate “no action” as another breached organization response strategy to compare its effect with the effects of other adopted strategies. Additionally, companies respond to data breaches at different points in time: some companies respond immediately, while others significantly delay their notifications to the stakeholders. We thus propose that response time affects the strength of the moderating effect of response strategies and include this in the research model (Figure 1).

## Behavior of Customers and Investors Following a Data Breach

According to the logic of EVT, when a communicator violates expectancies to a degree that exceeds the receiver’s sensitivity, the violation is posited to intensify the receiver’s arousal (Burgoon & Hale, 1988). If violations lead to the disclosure of sensitive and private information, cognitive beliefs that change or terminate the relationship are immediately aroused in the receiver (Afifi & Metts, 1998). The social norm in any customer-provider relationship is that the private information of each party is not revealed by the other party. Accordingly, when customers provide personal information to companies as a requirement for purchasing products or receiving services, they expect that the organizations will stringently protect their information. In a data breach incident, when customers discover that their information has been disclosed and criminals such as hackers have access to it, negative emotions regarding the breached companies will be aroused within them. In this regard, Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2010) categorized emotions surrounding IT events based on opportunity, threat, perceived lack of control over expected consequences, and perceived control over expected consequences. When an event is perceived as a threat and there is a lack of control over its expected consequences, emotions of “loss” are aroused (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010). Data breach events arouse these emotions because customers perceive these events as threats and they are unable to control the consequences of disclosing their information—potentially to cybercriminals. Along with emotions of loss, research on customer complaining behavior has consistently found that dissatisfaction is prompted by negative feelings toward a company when a service failure occurs (Day & Landon, 1977; İstanbulluoğlu et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2010). A data breach is an example of a service failure (Goode et al., 2017) because data breaches violate customers’ expectations that companies will protect their information. Thus, customers become dissatisfied with their relationship with the breached company.

Research on customer-provider relationships has demonstrated that dissatisfaction affects post-event behaviors. For example, Zeelenberg and Pieters (2004) found that when customers are dissatisfied with a company in any way, they exhibit punitive actions, such as switching to another company and spreading negative WOM. Thus, customers show negative behaviors after unauthorized information disclosure. For example, after revealing that 50 million Facebook users’ private information was collected in 2014 by a political data firm associated with Donald Trump’s 2016 presidential campaign, an online campaign with #DeleteFacebook spread negative WOM and encouraged people to delete their Facebook accounts (Montrose, 2018). Consistent with EVT, we posit that when a data breach incident violates customers’ expectations about company data protection, dissatisfaction as an emotion of loss is aroused and ultimately prompts customers to switch to another company and spread negative WOM. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Dissatisfaction mediates the relationship between customers’ perceived expectancy violation and customers’ behavior (i.e., switching behavior and negative WOM).

Investors use all available information to value a company in the stock market, and when new information about a company emerges, investors revalue the company accordingly (Yayla & Hu, 2011). Investors have limited knowledge of companies’ internal functions; thus, announcements help investors form beliefs about a company (Teo et al., 2016). When a data breach occurs and an announcement is made, a message is conveyed to the public that the company was incapable of protecting organizational information against security threats. The company’s inability to protect against security attacks and information loss signals unexpected company behavior to investors, thereby creating uncertainty. Therefore, investors react negatively because they expect expensive lawsuits and a negative public image as a result of data breaches and subsequent financial losses (Gwebu et al., 2018). Investors also perceive data breach events as costs that substantially affect a company’s ability to earn profit in the short term (Yayla & Hu, 2011). Due to the negative perception of a breached company shortly after the data breach event, the change in the firm value would be negative and investors would thus sell their stocks. As a result of investors’ selling behavior, the reaction of the stock market to data breaches becomes negative. Prior research has also consistently found a negative effect of data breach announcements on investors behavior in the stock market (Malhotra & Malhotra, 2011; Martin et al., 2017; Yayla & Hu, 2011). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2: Data breach announcements cause investors to behave negatively toward the breached companies in the stock market and decrease CAR.

## Moderating Role of Response Strategies

Prior research has investigated the effects of response strategies following crises and found that accommodative response strategies impact perceptions and behaviors after a crisis. For instance, Chang et al. (2015) found that response strategies have positive effects on customers’ perceptions and behaviors and that accommodative strategies can decrease the likelihood of customers engaging in negative WOM. In the context of an information privacy violation, Bansal and Zahedi (2015) investigated company responses that can repair trust after online privacy violations and found that apology as an accommodative strategy affects repaired trust more so than “no action” strategies.

In addition to customers, investors are influenced by crises and subsequent company actions. In this regard, behavioral finance research has investigated investors’ decision-making with respect to company responses. This research enumerates several characteristics specific to investors that differentiate them from other stakeholders. According to the axioms of utility theory, investors are (1) completely rational, (2) able to deal with complex choices, (3) risk-averse, and (4) wealthmaximizing (Nagy & Obenberger, 1994). Recent studies have also argued that investors’ behavior can be irrational (e.g., herd behavior) when access to real-time information is limited and there is insufficient time for deliberation (Jackson & Orr, 2019; Nigam et al., 2018). This irrational behavior can be triggered by crisis events such as terrorist attacks, earthquakes (Brounen & Derwall, 2010), and data breaches (Martin et al., 2017; Yayla & Hu, 2011), which are characterized by high uncertainty and inadequate information (Jackson & Orr, 2019). In light of irrational behavior and data breach events, prior research has argued that investors can have emotional reactions to data breaches (Malhotra & Malhotra, 2011).

Although investors’ decision-making may be affected by crisis events, research has found that company statements can manipulate investors’ decision-making behavior. Positive or favorable information decreases uncertainty in the stock market and leads to stock price increases (Nigam et al., 2018). When breached companies issue response letters that include favorable information about accommodative strategies, they signal the highest level of remorse in order to retain their customers. Being that investors avoid uncertainty, customer retention ensures more stable future financial performance and lower associated costs (Anderson & Mansi, 2009). Thus, investors reward company responses that have positive sentiments and signal stable future financial performance (Connelly et al., 2011; Teo et al., 2016).

Given the aforementioned discussion of customers and investors, we argue that given the same level of stakeholder dissatisfaction following a data breach, companies that are responsive to the incident by expressing remorse and accepting responsibility experience a lower level of negative outcomes than breached companies that are apathetic. This is primarily because accepting responsibility as an accommodative strategy was found to be the optimal communication strategy (Dean, 2004). Therefore, companies can decrease the negative outcomes of data breaches by employing accommodative strategies (i.e., corrective action, apology, and compensation), which will be superior to “no action” strategies. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3: Accommodative response strategies (i.e., corrective action, apology, compensation, or a combination thereof) negatively moderate the relationship between dissatisfaction and customers’ behavior (i.e., switching behavior and negative WOM), such that when these responses are provided, the relationship becomes weaker.

H4: Accommodative response strategies (i.e., corrective action, apology, compensation, or a combination thereof) negatively moderate the relationship between data breach announcement and CAR, such that when these responses are provided, the relationship becomes weaker.

Among the various crisis response strategies (Table 1), compensation is the only tangible benefit that a firm can offer to its stakeholders to restore losses from a data breach (Davidow, 2003). Tangible benefits are often more easily perceived, and offering compensation demonstrates a higher level of remorse and accountability. Compensation also promotes distributive justice—i.e., the appropriateness of the outcomes discerned by stakeholders after a data breach (Rasoulian et al., 2017). Compensation has been posited to offset customers’ total cost (Grewal et al., 2008) and has been found to be the best strategy compared to others (e.g., Borah et al., 2020). In the data breach context, Goode et al., (2017) investigated the Sony PlayStation Network data breach and found that offering compensation after data breaches can significantly positively influence individuals’ behaviors. Based on this discussion, we believe that compensation is more effective than apology and corrective action in decreasing the negative consequences of data breaches. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H5: The moderating effect of compensation is stronger than (a) corrective action and (b) apology on the relationship between dissatisfaction and customers’ behavior (i.e., switching behavior and negative WOM).

H6: The moderating effect of compensation is stronger than (a) corrective action and (b) apology on the relationship between data breach announcement and CAR.

## Moderating Role of Response Time

Since 2002, data breach notification laws have been enacted irregularly and have changed over time in the United States. In general, these laws require breached companies to notify their stakeholders about a breach and explain the type of disclosed information. Although the laws in many states share some core similarities, state legislators have decided to pass laws that best protect the interests of stakeholders in their respective states. As a result, some states have more stringent laws and demand more severe penalties for violations. For example, whereas California requires breached companies to immediately respond to data breaches, other states permit them to delay their responses. For instance, Florida requires breached organizations to inform their stakeholders within 30 days after a data breach incident, and Alabama and Ohio require the same action within 45 days. In addition to the response delay officially considered in the data breach notification laws of many states, some organizations do not respond to their data breaches and attempt to hide such events from public scrutiny. For example, Uber was hacked in 2016, and the personal information of 57 million customers and drivers was stolen by cybercriminals. Uber did not respond to the data breach, instead paying the hackers \$100,000 to cover up the incident (Chappell, 2018). However, security experts highly recommend that breached organizations respond to data breaches as soon as possible to boost the efficacy of their response strategies and mitigate negative reactions to the incidents (Hawthorn, 2016; Matteson, 2017). Research has also emphasized that the response time to these crises should be as short as possible. For instance, Coombs (2006) argued that crisis managers should inform stakeholders immediately about what they can to do to protect themselves. Ulmer and Sellnow (2002) maintained that organizations should act quickly to illustrate in good faith its intention to follow through on the pledges it communicates. If companies do not respond to these crises immediately, another party, such as journalists, competitors, or security groups, may reveal the data breach<sup>8</sup> through social media, thereby increasing the negative aftereffects. In the world of public perception, the first mover has the advantage of maintaining control over the narrative (Temin, 2015).

We believe that, when a response strategy is provided with a delay after the announcement, it may give the impression that the breached company provided the response only after becoming aware of customers’ dissatisfaction. By contrast, when a breached company responds quickly, it demonstrates good faith, honesty, and a higher level of remorse, and also demonstrates that the company is more prepared to recover from the failure. Therefore, although response strategies can weaken the relationship between negative emotions about the data breach and negative behaviors, their efficacy is subject to the timing of the response letters issued by the breached companies. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H7: Response time impacts the moderating effect of accommodative response strategies on the relationship between dissatisfaction and customers’ behavior (i.e., switching behavior and negative WOM), such that early accommodative responses affect that relationship more than late accommodative responses.

H8: Response time impacts the moderating effect of accommodative response strategies on the relationship between data breach announcement and CAR, such that early accommodative responses affect that relationship more than late accommodative responses.

## Methodology

In this research, we examine customers’ and investors behaviors with respect to different data breach response characteristics. Given the two stakeholders with different dynamics involved in the model, we adopted a multimethod approach by conducting two studies. In Study 2, consistent with prior security and privacy research (e.g., Vance et al., 2015), we used the factorial survey method to examine how customers would react to response strategies and response times. In Study 3, we conducted an event study to understand investors’ behaviors. An event study is a well-established method for examining investors’ behaviors and is appropriate for analyzing the stock market’s reaction following a data breach (e.g., Martin et al., 2017; Yayla & Hu, 2011).

## Study 2 (Factorial Survey)

A factorial survey method is an advanced approach to the scenario survey. Its objective is to “uncover the social and individual structures of human judgments of social objects” (Wallander, 2009, p. 505). The factorial survey method differs from typical scenario-based surveys in that the description of scenarios is experimentally manipulated and varied (Vance et al., 2015). Prior research has shown how the factorial survey integrates experimental and traditional survey methods to provide a unique research method (Vance et al., 2015). The factorial survey includes dimensions of research interest that can involve several levels—similar to treatments of the experiment method. In this method, the dimensions and levels are converted into textual scenarios to provide real-world situations for judgment and decision-making (Taylor, 2006).

The factorial survey also uses a statistical random sampling method like traditional surveys; however, a full combination of all dimensions and levels is possible (Vance et al., 2015). The full factorial avoids the issue of multicollinearity because the levels are orthogonal with correlations at or near zero (Jasso, 2006). Orthogonality enables us to distinguish among the different effects of response strategies and response times on stakeholders’ behaviors. While traditional experimental designs support a limited dimension with a few levels each before becoming impractically complex, factorial surveys do not have such limitations (Rossi & Anderson, 1982; Vance et al., 2015). In the factorial survey, each participant receives a dimension from several combined levels and gives a response based on the level (treatment) that was manipulated in that dimension. Because many companies adopt a combination of responses after a data breach (Table 2), the factorial survey is an appropriate method for this research because it systematically tests different combinations of responses. The participants of this study randomly received different dimensions with different levels based on response strategies and response times.

## Scenario Design

To create our factorial survey, we provided two sets of scenarios to describe a data breach event (Scenario A) and the response strategy and response time (Scenario B), as shown in Appendix B. We used scenarios that “present subjects with written descriptions of realistic situations and then request responses on a number of rating scales that measure the dependent variables of interest” (Trevino, 1992, pp. 127-128). First, we asked participants to provide the name of a company that they actually use and for which they need to provide personal information to receive the company’s service or product. Then, we showed the participants Scenario A to allow them to imagine that a data breach happened in the company they mentioned earlier and that their personally identifiable information (PII) was stolen<sup>9</sup> as a result (see Appendix B). Subsequently, the participants answered the survey items regarding the independent variables of the research model. Afterward, we provided Scenario B, which described the response strategy and response time (see Appendix B). After reading Scenario B, the participants answered the items regarding the dependent variables of the model. Because we used the factorial survey, a full combination of all factors was possible. Thus, we considered a full combination of corrective action, apology, compensation, and response time, plus the control group (no action), which resulted in a series of $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 + 1 = 1 7$ between-subjects factorial survey. However, because we needed to analyze the effects of response times only in conjunction with response strategies, we eliminated two conditions of response times (their direct effects) in our scenario design and, finally, used 15 groups for further analyses. We provided different Scenario Bs (Appendix B) for 14 combinations of response strategies and response times and examined the “no action” strategy by not presenting Scenario B. The participants were randomly assigned to one of these 15 groups. We also used age, gender, internet experience, data breach experience, and data breach media exposure as control variables because their importance has been acknowledged in prior information disclosure research (e.g., Malhotra et al., 2004). We adapted the scales for the constructs in the model from prior literature, as described in Appendix C.

## Data Collection Procedures

Our data collection followed a systematic approach that constitutes three phases: pretest, pilot study, and primary study. In the pretest phase, we solicited feedback on the survey from five information systems (IS) researchers with Ph.D. degrees. They provided several suggestions about our scenarios and the clarity of our survey items. We also found that the manipulations were not salient in the scenarios, and the manipulation check for response time did not pass. Therefore, we conducted a verbal protocol and reviewed the questions one by one to ensure their clarity. Then, we revised the scenarios and made the survey items clearer and more appropriate to the data breach context based on the suggestions and verbal protocol. After revising the factorial survey, we sent the survey back to the five IS researchers. This time, they noted that the scenarios and survey items were much clearer, and the manipulation check was also successful. We then conducted a pilot study with 110 respondents who were similar to our respondents in the main study. We used the pilot study to conduct preliminary analyses such as reliability and validity of constructs, clarity of scenarios, and manipulation checks. In this stage, the response time manipulation check required us to adjust the early response time for the primary study from 48 to 24 hours.

For the primary study, we initially collected 1,000 responses from CloudResearch by monetarily incentivizing U.S. respondents. Participants of this study were required to be adults over 18 years of age who provide personal information to purchase products or services from retail outlets. We removed those responses that did not correctly answer the security or manipulation questions and those that were completed in a very short time (i.e., less than five minutes). Finally, the sample size of the primary study data was 811 for further analyses, which was enough to reach a power of 0.9, with a medium-size effect and 0.05 significance level, for each of the 15 groups of the factorial survey (Liang et al., 2019). Demographics of the primary study participants (Appendix D) show a sufficiently equal spread of gender, age, marital status, internet experience, income, and education. We also found that 85% of respondents had personally experienced at least one data breach event.<sup>10</sup>

## Study 2 (Factorial Survey) Results

We estimated the structural model with partial least squares (PLS) and used Smart-PLS 3.2 (Ringle et al., 2015) to conduct analyses in two stages. In the first stage, we tested whether the measures used as the operationalization of the model constructs were reliable and valid (the measurement model). After establishing this, we proceeded to the second stage and estimated the path coefficients of the research model (the structural model). Finally, we compared path coefficients across multiple groups using multigroup analysis (MGA). We report the results of these stages in the subsequent sections.

Before testing the structural model, we conducted several preliminary analyses, such as manipulation checks, common method variance (CMV), and the measurement model (Appendix E). The results confirm the adequacy of the measurement model (Appendix E). We then analyzed the base relationships of the model with the “no action” (control) group. As discussed earlier, a response to the data breach event was not provided in the “no action” group so that we could examine the impact of various response strategies. Table 3 shows the results of testing the base relationships of the model with reasonable explanatory power (R<sup>2</sup> <sub>Switching</sub> <sub>Behavior</sub> = 0.29 and R<sup>2</sup> <sub>Negative</sub> <sub>WOM</sub> = 0.25). Table 3 shows that perceived expectancy violation $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 4 3 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ positively affects dissatisfaction. Also, customers’ dissatisfaction with a company after a data breach increases switching behavior $( \beta = 0 . 5 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and negative WOM (β = 0.43, p < 0.001). Although we found that perceived expectancy violation does not directly affect switching behavior and negative WOM (Table 3), it indirectly increases (β = 0.23, p < 0.001) switching behavior and (β = 0.19, p < 0.001) negative WOM through dissatisfaction (Table 4). Thus, our results indicate that dissatisfaction fully mediates the relationship among perceived expectancy violation, switching behavior, and negative WOM, supporting H1. We also found that gender $( \beta \ : = \ : 0 . 0 8 , \ : p \ : < \ : 0 . 0 5 )$ and internet experience (β = −0.19, p < 0.05) affect negative WOM. Data breach experience increases (β = 0.06, p < 0.01) switching behavior and (β = 0.05, p < 0.05) negative WOM, and data breach media exposure (β = 0.11, p < 0.01) influences negative WOM. The results indicate that the effects of age on the outcome variables are not significant.

## Multigroup Analysis

We conducted an MGA to test the moderating effects of our study because prior research has argued that MGA is the appropriate method to estimate moderating effects when the moderator variable is discrete (Eberl, 2010; Henseler, 2007; Sarstedt et al., 2011). In MGA, the discrete variable divides the sample data into a group of subsamples, and in each separate subsample, the same PLS path model is analyzed (Eberl, 2010; Henseler, 2007). The moderators of our model are discrete variables, leading us to analyze the PLS path model using MGA. We divided our sample data into groups of subsamples based on different data breach response strategies and response times. However, the interpretations of PLS MGA results can be misleading if PLS confounds the difference in path coefficients with differences in latent construct composition (Carte & Russell, 2003). Thus, multigroup comparisons require the establishment of measurement invariance to ensure the validity of results (Millsap, 2011). If a significant difference exists in the variance of constructs between two groups, the difference in path coefficients is due to differences in the contents of constructs and thereby misrepresents the results. Thus, we followed Henseler et al.’s (2016) measurement invariance of composite models (MICOM) approach, which requires the analysis of three components: (1) configural invariance, (2) compositional invariance, and (3) the equality of composite mean values and variances. Before conducting the MGA, we tested MICOM for all two-by-two groups based on our hypotheses testing. We also tested the difference in item loadings. The results confirm the invariance of composite models.<sup>11</sup>

We then conducted the MGA by comparing different groups of response strategies and response times. Table 5 shows the effect of dissatisfaction on the outcome variables in each group, and Table 6 shows the MGA results. To test H3 and the moderating effect of accommodative strategies, we combined all groups of corrective action, apology, and compensation. Table 5 shows that the effect of dissatisfaction on both switching behavior and negative WOM in the accommodative response strategies condition is lower than in the “no action” condition. Table 6 shows that this difference in values of the dissatisfaction effect (no action vs accommodative strategies) is significant. In other words, accommodative response strategies decrease the effect of dissatisfaction on switching behavior and negative WOM. Thus, H3 is supported. The “compensation only” response significantly decreases the effect of dissatisfaction on customers’ behaviors (Tables 5 and 6). The “corrective action only” response does not moderate the relationship between dissatisfaction and customers’ behaviors, but “apology only,” like “compensation only,” negatively moderates the effect of dissatisfaction on switching behavior and negative WOM. Table 6 shows that the moderating effect of “compensation only” is not stronger than “apology only.” Thus, H5a is supported, but H5b is not supported.

## Study 2 (Factorial Survey) Post Hoc Analysis

We conducted several post hoc analyses to dig deeper into the effect of response strategies and response times on switching behavior and negative WOM. First, we investigated whether the response strategies had an additive effect and whether their combination had a more positive effect than when they are provided separately. To do so, we examined the moderating impact of the response strategy that included the combination of apology, corrective action, and compensation. The results<sup>12</sup> show that the combination of all response strategies does not have an additive effect, and the effect of compensation or apology alone is no less than when they are combined. Second, being that “apology only” and “compensation only” strategies have negative moderating effects, we examined whether these moderating effects depended on response time. We examined the moderating effects of “apology only” and “compensation only” with early and late response times and found that the effect of these response strategies is significant only when they are provided early.

## Study 3 (Event Study)

## Methodology

We evaluated the effects of data breach response strategies and response times on investors’ behavior in the stock market (i.e., abnormal stock returns) by conducting an event study. An event study adopts the efficient market hypothesis, which states that a stock price at a particular point in time reflects all relevant information up to that time (Fama, 1998). Prior research has conducted event studies to examine investors’ behaviors following data breaches (e.g., Martin et al., 2017; Yayla & Hu, 2011). We followed the convention estimation method, as described in Appendix F.

## Data Collection

To analyze the relationship between data breaches and stock returns, we used the dataset of data breaches and official response letters collected in Study 1. However, the event study is subject to confounding events. Because the hypotheses of this research require the estimation of longer event windows, we controlled for an array of confounding events from around −1 to +35, including dividend declarations, contract signings, earnings information, mergers, acquisitions, and utilizing new technologies (e.g., big data, cloud computing). We used the Lexis-Nexis business news database to identify the confounding events and dropped 38 announcements accordingly. Finally, we used 166 data breach announcements in conjunction with the corresponding response letters for further analysis.

## Study 3 (Event Study) Results

In the event study, the stock market reaction is measured by CAR. Thus, we used Eventus® (Cowan Research, 2007) to calculate CARs in this research (see Appendix F for the estimation method). Figure 2 shows that the mean CAR remains negative beyond 30 days of the data breach announcement day. The analyses of data breach notification letters also indicate that many breached companies respond to data breaches several days after the announcements of data breaches. Therefore, we estimate CARs within longer event windows in addition to short event windows.

Table 7 shows the results of the event study estimation for the full sample size with several different event windows, mean CAR, and precision-weighted CAAR.<sup>13</sup> According to Table 7, the mean CAR is negative for the event windows, which confirms the negative effect of data breach announcements on stock prices. Specifically, for one day before and one day after the data breach announcement (−1, 1), the mean CAR is −0.78%. Figure 2 shows that the mean CAR remains negative beyond 30 days of the data breach announcement day. We then investigated the longevity of the announcements’ negative effects by analyzing longer event windows. The results show that the mean of the CAR for (−1, 32) event window is −0.73%, confirming the longevity of the negative effect of data breach announcements. Thus, because the mean CAR is negative from day 0 to day 35, H2 is supported.

## Analysis of Effect by Response Strategies

We investigated the effect of response strategies on the relationship between data breach announcements and stock prices using the analysis of subsamples. To test whether accommodative response strategies are effective, we divided the sample into (1) events in which the announcements include the breached company’s response (accommodative strategy), and (2) events in which the announcement includes no response from the breached company (no action). As a result, to accurately capture the effect of response strategies at the time of the announcement, we only estimated shorter windows. We further divided the events with accommodative strategies into subsamples based on the type of strategy (i.e., corrective action, compensation, and apology) to compare them with the “no action” strategy. Finally, we also estimated two nonparametric tests—the rank test (Corrado, 1989) and the jackknife test (Giaccotto & Sfiridis, 1996)—because prior research has recommended that nonparametric tests be estimated for sample sizes below 50 in event studies (Yayla & Hu, 2011), which is consistent with the size of our subsamples. Table 8 shows that when a data breach of a company is announced but the breached company does not provide any response on the day of the announcement (no action strategy), the mean CAR (−1, 1) is −0.96%.

## Analysis of Effect by Response Time

To investigate the effect of response time on the efficacy of response strategies, we divided the sample into the responses derived from the data breach announcements (early responses) and the responses provided after data breach announcements (late responses). Table 9 shows the descriptive statistics of late response time.

The full sample results (Table 7 and Figure 2) show that a data breach announcement’s negative effect on the mean CAR has longevity and remains negative even after 30 days. However, we tested whether early responses are more effective and have shorter negative effects. To compare the means of the CARs between early and late responses, we estimated longer event windows (−1, 30), (−1, 32), and (−1, 35) because the maximum late response day is day 30 (Table 9). To this end, we divided the sample into two subsamples (early responses and late responses) and compared them with the “no action” group. Figure 3 shows that the event windows (−1, 30), (−1, 32), and (−1, 35) incorporate early response events as well as late responses that were delivered on different days after the data breach announcements.

Table 10 shows that, whereas the mean CAR for early response events is small and insignificant, the mean CAR for late responses is large and significant. For example, the mean CAR for late responses (−1, 35) is −2.64%. Because the mean CARs for “no action” and late responses are negative and significant (Table 8 and 10) but the mean CAR for early responses is insignificant (Table 10), we can conclude that early responses decrease the negative effect of announcements on CAR more than late responses do. Response time affects the effectiveness of accommodative response strategies. Thus, H8 is supported.

## Study 3 (Event Study) Post Hoc Analysis and Robustness Test

Based on the lack of empirical research on the longevity of the data breach effect, we conducted a long-term event study to understand how long the negative effects of data breaches last. The conventional approach to conducting a long-term event study is to estimate buy-and-hold abnormal return (BHAR); however, prior research has noted that calendartime abnormal return (CTAR) is a superior approach (Barua & Mani, 2018; Mitchell & Stafford, 2000). Consequently, we used both BHAR and CTAR to conduct the long-term event study (Appendix G), in which we found that the negative effects of data breaches last for only six months. These results may suggest that people are no longer concerned about the effects of a data breach effects after six months have passed since the event.<sup>14</sup>

## Discussion

The number of records exposed by data breaches is on the rise and breached companies adopt varying response strategies after data breach events. In this multimethod research, we applied EVT to explain how stakeholders’ expectations of companies’ data protection are violated by data breaches and how this violation leads to adverse effects.

We then sought to examine whether response strategies and response times affect stakeholders’ behaviors. Table 12 shows the summary of our hypothesis testing. These findings, along with the results of the post hoc analyses, provide new insights into research and practice, as discussed next.

## Implications for Research

This research provides several implications for research based on the findings of our data analyses. First, this research contributes to information security literature by examining whether the different stakeholders of a company react differently to a security phenomenon. Prior research has noted that customers and investors may react differently to a data breach response because they have different goals and decision-making behaviors (Jackson & Orr, 2019; Nigam et al., 2018; Rasoulian et al., 2017). Investors consider the public image of a company after a data breach (Gwebu et al., 2018; Yayla & Hu, 2011), whereas customers worry about their personal data loss and possible corresponding adverse consequences (Choi et al., 2016; Goode et al., 2017). While investors value the breached company in the stock market based on collective perceptions, customers make decisions about breached companies individually to protect their interests. However, despite these differences in goals and decision-making behaviors, in this research, we found that customers and investors are similarly influenced by response strategies and response times. The results of the short-term event study show that, within a few days after a data breach response, the stock market reacts differently to each of the various response strategies in a manner that is consistent with the reaction of customers.

The correspondence between customers’ and investors reactions to data breach response strategies suggests that, although responses take different paths to influence customers and investors, they achieve the corporate objective of decreasing the customers’ and investors negative behaviors. Whereas customers consider an accommodative response as a company’s expression of responsibility, acceptance, and remorse, investors consider such a response as favorable information that decreases uncertainty in the stock market. Additionally, many investors either are also customers of the breached company (Malhotra & Malhotra, 2011) or have experienced data breaches and responses as customers of other companies, leading them to perceive and evaluate response strategies in a manner similar to customers.

Second, EVT argues that after expectation violations, individuals act negatively, depending on the severity of the violation (Burgoon & Jones, 1976). We found that expectation violation owing to data breaches leads to dissatisfaction in customers’ relationships with the breached company. Prior research has also contended that when stakeholders become dissatisfied with a company, they take punitive actions against that company (Day et al., 1981).

Although this is reflected in our study, we found that the negative consequences of dissatisfaction caused by data breaches do not last long. Specifically, the findings of our long-term event study show that the negative consequences of data breaches remain only for six months. This corresponds well with a case study of a Target data breach that found that the perceptions of Target’s brand returned to the pre-data-breach level six months following the event (Dubé, 2016).

We also examined the breached companies’ annual revenues in the breach years that manifest customers’ behavior after data breaches. Whereas prior studies noted that data breaches decrease revenues (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2020), we found that data breaches do not significantly affect annual revenues, ultimately suggesting that a customer’s intended reaction in the short term and the actual reaction in the long term may not be aligned. Our findings suggest that the negative effects of data breaches on stakeholders are substantial only for a short time frame.

Shortly following a data breach, stakeholders decide whether to act against the breached company. This decision can be understood from two perspectives. First, EVT discusses how when a behavior violates the expectation, it triggers cognitive arousal, and individuals follow an interpretation-evaluation sequence to decide on negative actions to pursue (Burgoon, 1978). Second, complaining behavior research argues that dissatisfied individuals must make physical and cognitive efforts to take negative actions, which impacts their decisions about whether to take such actions (e.g., İstanbulluoğlu et al., 2017). We found that although the negative impacts of data breaches are considerable, company responses can positively intervene in this decision-making process—but only if the stakeholders evaluate the response as a signal of remorse and regret. The content analysis of response letters in this research shows that accommodative strategies are the crux of data breach responses, and breached companies use either one strategy or a combination of corrective action, apology, and compensation strategies. While some prior research has found that accommodative strategies are not influential on investors (e.g.,

Gwebu et al., 2018), we discovered that accommodative strategies can affect both customers and investors. However, this effectiveness depends on the type of accommodative strategy: while corrective action is not effective, apology and compensation affect both customers and investors.

Third, the information privacy literature constantly argues that compensation and providing monetary incentives can lure individuals to disclose sensitive information, even at the risk of losing such information (e.g., Dinev et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2011). While the information privacy literature has asserted the effectiveness of compensation before information loss, this research found that compensation can also be effective after information loss by keeping the company’s position in the stock market and reducing the likelihood of customers’ switching behaviors and negative WOM. However, although prior research has argued that compensation is the most effective response strategy after a service failure (Davidow, 2003; Puzakova et al., 2013), we found that compensation is not more effective than apology when a company is responding to a data breach incident. The apology and compensation strategies are different in two ways: (1) while apology expresses concern for victims, compensation offers the affected customers something to offset the suffering (Coombs & Holladay, 2008); (2) apology enhances the interactional justice perception, but compensation increases the distributive justice perception (Smith et al., 1999). Regardless of these differences, the findings of this research demonstrate that neither of these strategies outweighs the other. Additionally, we found that the combination of all three response strategies (corrective action, apology, and compensation) is not more impactful than compensation and apology alone. Adding more responses accordingly adds more costs—but does not considerably increase the positive effects. These findings suggest that customers and investors simply want to see that the breached company is regretful, which explains why the provision of financial compensation is not more influential than an apology.

Finally, this research investigates the impact of response time (that occurs within the parameters of notification laws) after a data breach incident. Prior crisis management studies have indicated that companies should respond to a crisis in the shortest possible time (e.g., Coombs, 2006), but these studies have not examined this in the context of data breaches. This research found that response time plays a vital role after a data breach event and that the efficacy of response strategies on customer and investor behavior depends heavily on response time. The findings show that the moderating effects of accommodative responses are significant when breached companies provide responses immediately. We found that accommodative strategies lose their potency if companies employ these strategies one month after the announcement of data breaches. These results suggest that response time is perceived by customers as another signal that the breached company cares about the customers and is determined to minimize their losses. Although data breach notification laws allow breached companies to delay their response notifications, when companies provide response notifications late, customers infer that the breached company is apathetic about the incident. Stakeholders may also perceive that the breached company is incapable of making a quick, responsive decision about the data breach or that it is delegating the response to external forces, such as media and regulations. These findings also suggest that response expediency is not substitutable with response strategies: providing a more comprehensive form of response later is not more effective than a single response strategy provided at an earlier time.

## Implications for Practice

This study has several implications that can assist managers in choosing the right strategic plan. First, some companies remain silent and do not respond to data breaches; we found that this strategy is harmful to companies. Results show that breached companies should take responsibility for what happened to their customers’ information and opt for accommodative strategies. However, when choosing an accommodative strategy plan, managers should be cautious about the type of strategy they adopt. We suggest that managers should not employ “corrective action only,” and should instead, at the very least, use this strategy with other forms of accommodative responses. While we found that one fifth of companies included “corrective action only” in their response letters on the announcement date (Table 2), this strategy is not effective for retaining customers and preventing affected people from commenting negatively about the breached company. We found that it also adversely affects the company’s current stock price in the market.

Second, although our findings show that apology and compensation can be sufficient for sustaining stakeholders and preventing the spread of negative WOM after a data breach incident, each of these strategies requires different resources (Coombs, 1998). This study suggests that apology is as effective as compensation and can help companies decrease the negative consequences of a data breach event using a response strategy that requires fewer resources (i.e., apology). These findings are especially important for companies with smaller budgets. However, the effectiveness of apology in our study relates to only two stakeholders (i.e., customers and investors), and managers might need to use other strategies to influence other stakeholders’ behaviors. Thus, this research suggests that managers should include an apology in their notification letters when responding to data breaches.

Finally, the findings of this study emphasize that communication with affected stakeholders is important but that this communication should take place as soon as possible after a data breach. The results of this study demonstrate that accommodative strategies affect customers and investors only when breached companies respond immediately to the incident. Although data breach notification laws in some states require breached companies to notify affected customers prior to 45 days following the incident, this research suggests that breached companies should respond much earlier. Otherwise, accommodative responses would not be potent enough to prevent customers and investors from taking negative actions. The findings regarding response times may also be helpful for legislators; when revising data breach notification laws, legislators should consider that customers and investors expect companies to respond quickly to data breaches. Any response notification after 45 days will not be effective, even if it is within the parameters of the state laws.

## Limitations and Future Research

There are some limitations of this study that provide opportunities for future research. First, we only examined accommodative strategies and contrasted them with “no action” strategies because they were the only ones that breached companies adopted. However, future researchers might examine specific defensive strategies (e.g., attacking the accuser) and moderate strategies (e.g., justification) to examine whether these are more effective than accommodative strategies. Future researchers could also investigate why a company may adopt a particular strategy (e.g., apology) and the challenges that it faces in doing so. Another facet of adopting response strategies is stakeholder notification of breach and response strategies, but the most effective communication channel for informing stakeholders remains unknown. Future researchers could examine different media (e.g., social media, the company’s website, TV, newspapers) to identify avenues for notifying customers. Because delayed responses have adverse negative effects, future researchers could investigate how to shorten the discovery time of data breaches and decrease companies’ response times. Further, the breached data in our study scenario were credit card information, and we provided both free credit card monitoring and purchase discounts in the factorial survey to examine compensation. Future researchers could examine different kinds of sensitive information and separate types of compensation to understand their relative effectiveness.

Moreover, although we checked CMV with two methods, our factorial survey may have still suffered from CMV because the participants rated the items of the scenarios at the same point in time. Therefore, future data breach researchers using factorial surveys could avoid CMV by, for example, temporally separating the measurement of the predictors and outcome variables. We also examined customers’ intentions after a data breach; however, these intentions might not lead to behaviors. Future researchers could examine customers’ behaviors after a data breach incident. We also only sampled publicly traded firms in the United States. Future studies would benefit from expanding the study to a global context.

## Conclusion

Despite considerable investment in information security, companies face data breaches and subsequently lose many customers and investors. Breached companies adopt various response strategies to mitigate the negative consequences of data breaches. In this research, we conducted content analysis of actual response letters to identify the response strategies that breached companies adopted. We then studied the extent to which these strategies mitigated adverse consequences of the data breach on two stakeholders: customers and investors. Using a factorial survey with 15 conditions (n = 811) and an event study (n = 166), we examined the adverse effects of the breach on customers and investors respectively, and then analyzed how response strategies and times affect these relationships. Our results indicate that data breaches violate stakeholders’ expectations, which increases dissatisfaction and ultimately leads to the intention of leaving or spreading negative WOM about the company as well as a negative reaction in the stock market. However, companies can positively manipulate customers’ intentions and investors’ behaviors through accommodative response strategies. Whereas we found that strategies that took corrective actions were not effective, strategies that showed regret (apology) were just as effective as strategies that provided monetary benefits (compensation). These results were remarkably consistent across the two types of stakeholders. Importantly, the immediacy of response, regardless of the data breach laws, increases the impact of these strategies.

## Measurement Items

We adapted the items of perceived expectancy violation from Affifi and Metts (1998) and those of dissatisfaction from Bhattacherje (2001). We borrowed the scales of switching behavior and negative WOM from Choi et al. (2016) and Martin et al. (2017), respectively. We believe that average customers are not aware of the details of data breach notification laws. Therefore, they do not know what state requires immediate, 30- day, or 45-day response. Moreover, these laws are for companies, not customers, and many customers might not even know the location (state) of the company that they purchase from. Thus, we believe that it is just a perception of whether a response is late, even legally. We measured response time by providing early response time (24 hours) and late response time (30 days) in textual factors. We initially chose 48 hours for early response time because practice and research recommend an immediate response to the crisis (Coombs, 2006; Hawthorn, 2016; Matteson, 2017) but the results of pilot study analyses showed that response time manipulation check did not pass successfully because the respondents did not perceive 48 hours as an early response time. Thus, we changed the response time to 24 hours for primary study, and the manipulation check passed successfully. We also chose 30 days for a long response time because it is the minimum response time among the states that require 30 to 45 days to respond. We provided different descriptions for each of the accommodative response strategies (corrective action, apology, and compensation) in the scenarios and combined the descriptions to make 14 factors of our factorial survey. All items are 7-point Likert scales. Table C1 shows the measurement items and Table C2 shows the manipulation check questions.

The results show that the risk of CMV is low, but that CMV remains a possibility given that these tests cannot rule out this threat. We then proceeded with checking the manipulations of the scenarios. To check the manipulation of response time, we asked the respondents if the response time was late. ## Appendix F

## Event Study Estimation Method

The common approach in event studies is to first compute stock returns on or around the date of the event under consideration. Following the convention of event studies (e.g., Yayla & Hu, 2011; Martin et al., 2017), we used the equal-weighted market index return as the market return in our model and set the estimation period to 120 days, starting at t = -130 days and ending at t = -10 days, where t = 0 days represents the event date. However, it is important to make a clarification on the security event date. From the investors’ perspective, the meaningful event date for a firm-specific security breach is the day when the breach becomes public knowledge, which is not necessarily the date when the breach actually occurred (Yayla & Hu, 2011). Therefore, we carefully choose the appropriate event date for each security breach incident based on when it is announced publicly. Data breaches are announced to the public either by third parties (e.g., security forums) or by the breached companies (e.g., response letters). It is also possible that both third parties and breached companies announce data breaches, but the first public announcement should be considered in the event study. Thus, for each data breach event, we compared the announcement date on Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (that includes third-party announcements) with the announcement date of the breached company and used the earlier date in case of difference. We estimated the daily stock return for each firm using the CRSP stock price dataset over the window of (-130, -10). We calculated the abnormal returns for shorter windows (-1, 0), (-1, 1), (-1, 2), and (-1, 3). The logic behind these event windows is that the stock market may have “pre-announcement” information about the data breach and may react before the market closes a day before the public data breach announcement (Hovav et al., 2017). As we test the hypothesis about response time, we also calculated the abnormal returns for longer windows (-1, 30), (-1, 32), and (-1, 35).

## Post Hoc Analysis

The common approach to analyzing long-term abnormal returns is using buy-and-hold abnormal returns (BHAR). However, some studies argue that BHARs may amplify underperformance by combining single-period returns or they do not sufficiently account for potential crosssectional dependence in returns, providing misleading results (e.g., Barua & Mani, 2018; Mitchell & Stafford, 2000). These studies suggest calendar-time abnormal returns (CTAR) as an alternative method. Thus, we estimate long-term abnormal returns with both BHAR and CTAR methods using the market model. As we previously examined the short-term effect of data breaches in the one-month period, we set the starting estimation period of long-term abnormal returns to three months and increased it to the subsequent three months until one year. We also removed the confounding events for each longer period as the way we did for the short-term event study, resulting in different sample sizes. Table G1 shows the results of the long-term event study and indicates that the results of BHAR and CTAR are consistent. The results show that after six months the negative effects of data breaches diminish.

## Robustness Tests

We conducted several robustness tests to ensure the quality of the findings. While we followed prior security event studies (e.g., Martin et al., 2017) to estimate the main analyses with the market model, we conducted the Fama-French three-factor model and Fama-Frenchmomentum four-factor model as alternative specifications.
