# The complementarity and substitutability relationships between information technology and benefits and duration of unemployment

Winston T. Lin <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Ta-Wei (Daniel) Kao <sup>b,1</sup>, Chia-Ching Chou <sup>a,1</sup>, Raj Sharman <sup>a,1</sup>, N.C. Simpson <sup>a,1</sup>

Keywords: Partial adjustment theory Constant or time-varying speeds of partial adjustment The Box–Tidwell transformation Unemployment benefits Duration of unemployment Nonlinear seemingly unrelated regressions

## a b s t r a c t

This paper aims to analyze the complementarity and substitutability relationships between information technology (IT) and unemployment benefits (B) and duration of unemployment (D), two important policy and macroeconomic variables, by using the partial adjustment (PA) valuation (PAV) approaches where the speeds of PA are fixed and time-varying. The proposed eight research models derived from the PAV approaches based on the theory of PA are tted into a country-level panel data set covering the period from 1993 to 2008 for a sample of 12 countries and are estimated by the seemingly unrelated regressions (SUR) and nonlinear SUR (NLSUR) methods. The findings include: (i) the speeds of PA are dynamic and variable rather than constant, causing the fluctuations of the performance index over time; (ii) among the three production factors used, the traditional (non-IT) capital (K ) is found to exert the greatest impact upon the desired (true) output and, consequently, upon the performance index; (iii) B or D or joint B and D has the power to shift the speeds of PA in the countries considered; (iv) the impacts of B, D, and the combination of B and D upon the actual (observed) output and, hence, upon the business value of IT vary from country to country; and (v) all in all, the empirical evidence strongly suggests that the government policy of B, D, or B and D combined is good for some advanced developed countries (e.g., US and CN) but may be ineffective for some newly developed nations (e.g., Greece and Norway) in the presence of IT, and that the policy of joint B and D is good for all four advanced developed countries and five (Australia, Denmark, Norway, Portugal, and Switzerland) of the eight newly developed economies in the absence of IT.

## 1. Introduction

In the past decades, information technology (IT) has become one of the most prominent factors that impacts the firm's or country's productivity and performance. The term IT in its modern sense first appeared in an article published in the Harvard Business Review in 1958, in which the authors commented that, “the new technology does not yet have a single established name. We shall call it information technology.” Abundant research on the business value of IT has emerged since then, both at the firm and country levels. Numerous firm level studies [4,6,11,17,18,27, and others] claim that IT impacts the firm's performance positively and the IT productivity paradox has disappeared, where the productivity paradox is defined as the apparent lack of productivity improvements that are expected to occur as a result of IT investment; in other words, it means the lack of a substantial and statistically significant contribution of IT investment to firm output at the firm level or to aggregate output at the country level [cf. 6,28]. Especially, [28] provides a conflicting opinion suggesting that the productivity paradox remains as an unpleasant phenomenon.

At the country level, the debates over the productivity paradox are even more severe. Some researches [e.g., 12] argue that the productivity paradox exists only in developing countries, while some others [7,23,25,26] demonstrate that the productivity paradox exists in both developing and developed countries. These conclusions provide important managerial implications for investors, governments and decisionmakers as IT investment is expected to increase over time [6,18,23,25, 27–30].

During an economic recovery and an expansion, there is a misconception that benefits and duration of unemployment are harmful to the country's economic recovery and growth, thereby are not effective national policies; and they are actually the hurdles of economic recovery and growth. During an economic downturn and a recession (e.g. the most recent one taking place in 2008–2009), the misconception gets even more popular. Decision makers, managers, and ordinary people as well are concerned with the increasing unemployment rate, declining housing prices, scary stock prices, and paltry yields on bank accounts, etc. The media constantly reports how many Americans are looking for work and that payrolls, stock prices, and GDP growth point to another contraction, etc. The Congress has passed the bills of extending jobless benefits. No other U.S. post-war recession than the most recent one has been so prolonged and severe. Indeed, every recession causes a lot of pains, job losses, and the financial crisis. Mired in the stress of recession, people get desperate and overly sensitive to lengthened benefits and duration of unemployment.

Nevertheless, no one seems to question whether unemployment benefits (denoted by B hereafter) and/or the duration of unemployment (denoted by D hereafter) may have the potential positive or adverse impacts upon the country's GDP and, hence, the value of IT. Such an important economic issue should be taken seriously as a topic of research inquiry. The present study aims to bridge the gap by pursuing this important research topic.

In substance, this study concentrates on the complementarity and substitutability relationships between two well-familiar country characteristics (B and D) and IT within the framework of the partial adjustment methodologies. Here, a complementarity (a substitutability) relationship between any two variables (say, X1 and X2) is defined as a phenomenon in which the presence of X2 strengthens (weakens) the country's performance or the value of X1, measured by a performance metric (e.g., APR in Eq. (9) below) and, consequently, X1 and X2 are complementary (substitutable), and vice versa.

More specifically, the objective of this study is to analyze the relationships among IT, B, and D, and the effects of B, D, or B and D on the country's performance as measured by the performance index (ratio) when IT is present. In other words, the objective of this research is to answer three research questions, namely, (i) Does IT investment improve or worsen the country's performance as measured by the average performance ratio called APR in the presence or absence of B or D or B and D together? (ii) Does B, D or joint B and D have a positive or adverse (a negative) impact upon the country's performance as measured by APR in the presence of IT? (iii) Does B, D, or B and D combined strengthen or weaken the country's performance as measured by APR in the absence of IT? These research questions are critically important to an economy regardless of whether the economy is in a depression or an expansion era, amid the fact that IT value and impact research at the firm level is abundant but, in contrast, knowledge accumulation of IT value and impact at the country level is poor [7,23,29]. To achieve the objective, we apply the partial adjustment valuation (PAV) approaches with constant and time-varying speeds of partial adjustment.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 conducts a literature review; Section 3 describes the theory of partial adjustment (PA), the PAV approaches, the performance measure, the research models, and the estimation methods. Section 4 discusses the variables and the set of country-level panel data used, while Section 5 presents the various specification and validity tests, the empirical results needed to answer the three research questions, a detailed discussion of the results to answer the research questions, implications for policy makers and for academic researchers, and the links of the study to DSS. Section 6 concludes the paper with some remarks and managerial decision-making benefits of this study.

## 2. Literature review

As mentioned above, numerous researches have investigated the value of IT and held conflicting opinions on the so-called productivity paradox phenomenon. The studies can be classified into two major groups: one group focuses on IT and productivity [6,12,13,30]; and the other on IT and productive (technical) efficiency [7,20,23–25,27]. These researchers undertake either a firm- or a country-level analysis; and conflicting conclusions have been reached towards the existence of the productivity paradox, as we discussed in the preceding Section.

Meanwhile, previous studies apply different methodologies. Some [6,12,18] of them have applied the traditional regression-analysis approach; many others have used the data envelopment analysis (DEA)

approach; and still other studies [7,17,23,27,28] are based on the time-varying stochastic production frontier (SPF) approach. Moreover, Lin at el. [25] and Lin and Kao [26] proposed the one-equation and the two-equation PAV approach with constant and time-varying speeds of partial adjustment, respectively, to evaluating and measuring the value of IT. Though different approaches have been employed, there are only a few studies [e.g., 7] that concern the factors that influence the business value of IT.

In a narrow sense, IT value can be measured by technical efficiency (TE) associated with the SPF approaches or by the performance index (PI) associated with the PAV approaches. In a broad sense, IT value can be expanded to include the TE (or PI) change index that is the ratio of the productivity growth index (or total factor productivity index) to the technological progress index [24]. Moreover, according to Steil et al. [39] and Miozzo and Walsh [32], IT induces technological change and innovation which in turn stimulate economic growth, globalization, and international competitiveness. Having reviewed some selected firm-level studies of IT value published in the 1990–2002 period and selected industry- and country- level studies of IT returns during the 1987–2001 period, Dedrick et al. [14] have found the relationship between IT and productivity and, hence, economic performance, and concluded that the productivity paradox has been effectively refuted.

In contrast, however, the firm- and country- level studies of [28] (which used the same set of data as used in [e.g., 6,18,27]), [7,23,28, etc.] have concluded that the productivity paradox remains in large U.S. firms and in a country regardless of whether it is a developed or developing country.

The aim of this study is to investigate the impacts of the country's two essential macroeconomic unemployment factors, namely, unemployment benefits (B) and the duration of unemployment (D), upon the business value of IT, and vice versa.

To fulfil the goal, we apply the two-equation PAV approach with time-varying speeds of adjustment [26] and, simultaneously, compare to the one-equation PAV approach with constant speeds of adjustment [25]. The two-equation PAV approach is featured by several merits. First, like the one-equation PAV approach, the two-equation PAV approach has a solid theoretical foundation since it is based on the theory of partial adjustment.

Second, the two-equation PAV approach is appropriate for the present study investigating the influence of B and D upon the business value of IT. We base this point on the scientific literature and on convincing explanations. (i) On the basis of the scientific literature, the two-equation PAV approach is featured by several merits as compared with other methods such as DEA and SPF: (a) DEA is mathematical programming-oriented, lacks theoretical foundation, and is deterministic and nonparametric; thus, unlike DEA, the PAV approach is stochastic and parametric as SPF. (b) The two-equation PAV approach with dynamic and variable speeds of adjustment is particularly suitable for research topics engaged in PA towards a desired (maximum) target (e.g., PA towards maximum output in this research, PA towards target capital structure, etc.), but both DEA and SPF are not. (ii) On the basis of convincing explanations, the PAV approach with dynamic and variable speeds of adjustment is constituted by two equations (see Eqs. (3) and (4) below) to evaluate and measure the performance of the country and, hence, the value of IT; and the performance is influenced by the desired (maximum) output through the first equation where IT capital appears, and is also impacted by the changes in the observed (actual) output via the second equation where B, or D, or both B and D appear.

Third, the PAV approaches (one-equation and two-equation) and their accompanied (built-in) performance measures are easy to apply since, unlike the SPF approach, the PAV approach with either constant or time-varying speeds of partial adjustment does not involve a halfnormally distributed random inefficiency variable. Simply because the SPF involves a half-normally distributed random inefficiency variable, its two-stage estimation procedure is complicated and may fail if the so-called exception condition (wrong skewness) prevails [28]; in contrast, since the PAV approaches do not involve a half-normally distributed random inefficiency variable, they are immune from the estimation difficulty associated with SPF and their estimation processes are greatly simplified. Moreover, the PAV approaches offer an additional performance measure, $A S A _ { j }$ (see Eq. (5) below).

Unemployment, job search theory, and their companions (notably, B and D) have drawn much attention of wisdom for a long time. In particular, job search theory has been studied for decades [e.g. 14,33–35,37]. Nonetheless, both job search and D have become critical issues and been hotly debated before the start of the global financial crisis in 2008 [1,2,31,33–35,37,38, among others]. With the growing pain of the unemployment rate, the U.S. Congress and government have extended unemployment benefits several times, but the action seems to fail to encourage jobless people to actively seek jobs and the unemployment rate did not decrease fast enough as expected [10]. This evidence corresponds to the study of [31] suggesting that the higher unemployment insurance has a negative effect on the probability of leaving unemployment.

Since unemployment situations and unemployment compensation systems differ from country to country, unemployment has been investigated from different perspectives, including the impacts of unemployment and related compensation or insurance on D [2,19, et al.], the effects of B and the business cycle on D [2], the influence of extended B on D [22], and the impacts of B and D upon forecasting stock returns.

However, virtually all of previous research focuses only on either B or D. The impacts of B and D upon the business value of IT, and vice versa, have been overlooked and, consequently, missed from the literature. This research attempts to close the gap.

## 3. Theory, research methodology, performance measures, research models and estimation methods

3.1. The theory of partial adjustment (PA) and the partial adjustment valuation (PAV) approach with constant speeds of adjustment [25]

The theory of partial adjustment was originally introduced by Nerlove [36]. It was subsequently applied and extended by numerous authors [25,26, and others]. The theory states that the observed output of a decision making unit $j ( D M U _ { j } )$ at time t is assumed to be equal to a weighted average of the desired output at current time t and the actual output at previous time t−1, with weights of $\dot { \delta } _ { j }$ (the constant speed of partial adjustment) and $1 - \delta _ { j } ,$ respectively. In symbol, the theory is described by

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {j t} = \delta_ {j} Y _ {j t} ^ {*} + (1 - \delta_ {j}) Y _ {j, t - 1} \text {   or,   equivalently }, \\ Y _ {j t} - Y _ {j, t - 1} = \delta_ {j} \Big (Y _ {j t} ^ {*} - Y _ {j, t - 1} \Big), 0 <   \delta_ {j} <   1, j = 1, 2,..., m \text {   and   } t = 1, 2,..., n \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { j t }$ is the actual (observed) output of DMU (country j in this research) at time t, $Y _ { j , t - 1 }$ is the actual (observed) output of DMU at time $t - 1 , Y _ { j t } ^ { * }$ is the desired (ideal, maximum) output of DMU at time t, and δ is the constant speed of partial adjustment of country j. Accordingly, the speed of adjustment represents the ratio of the change in the actual output to the change in the desired output in any time period and, therefore, it must lie between zero and one. In one extreme case, if the speed of adjustment is equal to zero, then the actual output at time t equals the actual output at t−1. In another extreme case, if the speed of adjustment is equal to one, then the actual output is equal to the desired output; in other words, there is no gap between the actual output and the desired output and it is said to be fully adjusted. But these two extreme cases are rare in reality, due primarily to the technological conditions. Consequently, the value of the speed of adjustment cannot be negative or greater than 1; hence, “the theory of partial adjustment” is named.

Eq. (1) can be generalized by adding the traditional error $u _ { j t }$ to the right-hand side, so we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {j t} - Y _ {j, t - 1} = \delta_ {j} f (\mathbf {X} _ {j t}; \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {j}) - \delta_ {j} Y _ {j, t - 1} + u _ {j t}, j = 1,..., m \text { and } t \\ = 1,..., n \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where the desired (true or maximum) output is not directly observable but can be quantified in practice by a production function, as suggested by Lin et al. [25], which is consistent with the practice in the SPF approach; the production function is denoted by $f ( { \pmb X } _ { j t } ; { \pmb \beta } _ { j } )$ in which $X _ { j t }$ is a vector of production factors including traditional capita $K _ { j t }$ , traditional labor $L _ { j t } ,$ and IT capita $I _ { j t }$ thereby, $\pmb { X } _ { j t } = ( K _ { j t } , L _ { j t } )$ when $I _ { j t }$ is absent and $\pmb { X } _ { j t } = ( K _ { j t } , L _ { j t } , I _ { j t } )$ when $I _ { j t }$ is present; $\beta _ { j }$ is a vector of unknown coefficients; and u<sub>jt</sub> is the traditional uncontrollable random error assumed to be distributed according to $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } )$ .

There are several well-known production functions, including the Cobbs–Douglas (CD) production function and the Box–Cox (BC) and Box–Tidwell (BT) transformations [3]. Mathematically, as the two transformation parameters involved in BT tend to be equal, BT becomes identical with BC; as both the two transformation parameters of BT tend to be zero, the limit of BT is CD; and as the only transformation parameter of BC tends to be zero, BC collapses to CD [also see 3,23]. Because of the mathematical reasons just provided and the empirical justifications to be explained in Subsection 3.5 below, we employ the BT function to quantify the true (desired, expected, or maximum) output in our research models.

Eq. (2) combined with the built-in performance measures as stated below has been referred to as the one-equation PAV approach with constant speeds of adjustment to evaluating and measuring the business value of IT.

## 3.2. The PAV approach with time-varying speeds of adjustment [26]

Both the original theory of PA and the one-equation PAV approach assume constant speeds of PA over time, implying that the speed of PA is not affected by any unobserved or observed macroeconomic and microeconomic factors. To achieve the goal of the study requires us to consider unemployment benefits $\left( B _ { j t } \right)$ and the duration of unemployment $( D _ { j t } )$ (i.e., two important macroeconomic policy factors or country characteristics) using the two-equation PAV approach with timevarying speeds of PA over time [26]. That is, we apply the generalized (two-equation) PAV approach given as follows<sup>2</sup>:

$$
Y _ {j t} - Y _ {j, t - 1} = \delta_ {j t} f (\boldsymbol {X} _ {j t}; \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {j}) - \delta_ {j t} Y _ {j, t - 1} + u _ {j t}\tag{3}
$$

$$
\delta_ {j t} = g \left(\mathbf {Z} _ {j t}; \boldsymbol {\pi} _ {j}\right), 0 <   \delta_ {j t} <   1, j = 1, \dots , m \text { and } t = 1, \dots , n\tag{4}
$$

where $\pmb { Z } _ { j t } = ( B _ { j t } , D _ { j t } )$ , and $\pmb { \pi } _ { j }$ represents a vector of unknown coefficients. The estimated average speed of adjustment of country j can be obtained by

$$
A S A _ {j} = \sum_ {t} \hat {\delta} _ {j t} / n, \quad j = 1, \dots , m\tag{5}
$$

where $\hat { \delta } _ { j t }$ is the estimate of $\delta _ { j t } .$

The relationships of $B _ { j t } , D _ { j t } ,$ , and IT are analyzed by treating $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t }$ as country characteristic factors that affect the speeds of adjustment in four cases, each case with two or three factors: Case 1: without $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t } ,$ which is the case of constant speeds of adjustment to be used and served as the base of comparison with other three cases; Case $2 \colon B _ { j t }$ is the only source of the dynamic and variable speeds of adjustment; Case 3: $D _ { j t }$ is the only source; and Case 4: both $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t }$ enter Eq. (4).

## 3.3. The performance measures

As proposed by Lin at el. [25], the performance metric can be represented by the product of the speed of adjustment $( \delta _ { j t } = \delta _ { j } \mathrm { i f }$ constant) and the desired output, that is, $P M _ { j t } = \delta _ { j t } f ( X _ { j t } ; \beta _ { j } )$ , which has the same physical units as the desired output, actual output, and production factors denoted by $X _ { j t } .$ This performance measure is affected by (i) the estimate of the speed of adjustment $\delta _ { j t } ,$ , (ii) the functional form of $f ( { \pmb X } _ { j t } ; { \pmb \beta } _ { j } )$ , (iii) the production factors $\dot { X _ { j t } }$ in the production function, (iv) the components of $\mathbf { \nabla } _ { Z _ { j t } , \mathrm { ~ } } ( \mathrm { v } )$ the functional form of $g ( Z _ { j t } ; \pmb { \pi } _ { j } )$ , and (vi) the estimates of unknown coefficients and $\beta _ { j } .$ . Any change of the six factors would change the value of the performance measure. The value of the performance measure is non-negative and tends to be zero when the speed of adjustment (fixed or dynamic) approaches to zero.

The estimated value $( P V _ { j t } )$ of $P M _ { j t }$ is represented by

$$
P V _ {j t} = \hat {\delta} _ {j t} f (\boldsymbol {X} _ {j t}; \hat {\boldsymbol {\beta}} _ {j})\tag{6}
$$

where $\hat { \delta } _ { j t }$ and $\hat { \beta } _ { i }$ are the estimates of $\delta _ { j t }$ and $\beta _ { j } ,$ , respectively.

Furthermore, in order to compare the performance of DMUs, the average performance value of j (APV ) can be calculated by

$$
A P V _ {j} = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {n} P V _ {j t} / n, j = 1, 2, \dots , m\tag{7}
$$

Both $P V _ { j t }$ and $A P V _ { j }$ are monetary gain as $P M _ { j t } .$ . Now, $P V _ { j t }$ can be transferred into a performance ratio (PR, or index) by dividing the corresponding actual output denoted by Y<sup>Δ</sup>, that is:

$$
P R _ {j t} = P V _ {j t} / Y _ {j t} ^ {\Delta},\tag{8}
$$

where $Y _ { j t } ^ { \Delta } { = } \ln Y _ { j t }$ for $\mathrm { C D } , = ( Y _ { j t } ^ { \gamma _ { * } } - 1 ) / { \gamma ^ { * } }$ for BC, and $= ( Y _ { j t } ^ { \lambda * } - 1 ) / \lambda ^ { * }$ for BT [25].

Then the average performance index (ratio) is given by

$$
A P R _ {j} = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {n} P R _ {j t} / n, j = 1, 2, \dots , m\tag{9}
$$

It is clear that $P R _ { j t }$ and APR lies between zero and one. In particular, $P R _ { j t }$ and APR facilitate a convenient comparison with the performance measures from other approaches (e.g., DEA and SPF).

## 3.4. Research models

As mentioned above, the desired output is represented by the production function $f ( { \pmb X } _ { j t } ; \beta _ { j }$ )which is specified to be the BT transformation production function, yielding a total of eight (8) research models. Under the BT specification, the desired output in Eq. (3) is $f ( X _ { j t } ; \beta _ { j } ) = \beta _ { j 0 } .$ 十 $\begin{array} { r } { \beta _ { j 1 } \left( \frac { K _ { j t } ^ { \theta } - 1 } { \theta } \right) + \beta _ { j 2 } \left( \frac { L _ { j t } ^ { \theta } - 1 } { \theta } \right) + \beta _ { j 3 } \left( \frac { I _ { j t } ^ { \theta } - 1 } { \theta } \right) } \end{array}$ for three factors and $f ( X _ { j t } ; \beta _ { j } ) =$ $\begin{array} { r } { \beta _ { j 0 } + \beta _ { j 1 } \left( \frac { K _ { j t } ^ { \theta } - 1 } { \theta } \right) + \beta _ { j 2 } \left( \frac { L _ { j t } ^ { \theta } - 1 } { \theta } \right) } \end{array}$ for two factors. The eight research models are grouped into four cases, as stated above. Case 1 consists of Models 1 and 2 which are based on the PAV approach with constant speeds of adiustment [25] due to the fact that both $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t }$ are absent, while Models 3–8 in Cases 2, 3, and 4 are based on the PAV approach with dynamic (and variable) speeds of adjustment [26]. The eight models in four cases described as follows are needed to achieve our research objective.

Model 1. Case 1, two-factor, constant speeds $( \delta _ { j t } = \pi _ { 0 j } ,$ for all t) without $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t }$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {Y _ {j t} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} - \frac {Y _ {j , t - 1} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} = \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {0 j} + \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {1 j} \left(\frac {K _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) \\ \qquad + \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {2 j} \left(\frac {L _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) - \pi_ {0 j} \left(\frac {Y _ {j , t - 1} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda}\right) + u _ {j t} \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

Model 2. Case 1, three-factor, constant speeds $( \delta _ { j t } = \pi _ { 0 j } ,$ for all t) without $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t }$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \frac {Y _ {j t} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} - \frac {Y _ {j , t - 1} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} = \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {0 j} + \pi_ {j 0} \beta_ {1 j} \left(\frac {K _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) + \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {2 j} \left(\frac {I _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) \\ + \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {3 j} \left(\frac {I _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) - \pi_ {0 j} \left(\frac {Y _ {j , t - 1} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda}\right) + u _ {j t} \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

Model 3. Case 2, two-factor, dynamic and variable speeds with $B _ { j t }$ and $\delta _ { j t } = \pi _ { 0 j } + \pi _ { 1 j } \ln B _ { j t }$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {Y _ {j t} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} - \frac {Y _ {j , t - 1} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} = \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {0 j} + \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {1 j} \left(\frac {K _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) + \pi_ {0 j} \beta_ {2 j} \left(\frac {L _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) \\ \qquad + \pi_ {1 j} \beta_ {0 j} \ln B _ {j t} + \pi_ {1 j} \beta_ {1 j} \ln B _ {j t} \left(\frac {K _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) \\ \qquad + \pi_ {1 j} \beta_ {2 j} \ln B _ {j t} \left(\frac {L _ {j t} ^ {\theta} - 1}{\theta}\right) - (\pi_ {0 j} + \pi_ {1 j} \ln B _ {j t}) \left(\frac {Y _ {j , t - 1} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda}\right) + u _ {j t} \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

3.5. Justifications for the deployment of the Box–Tidwell transformation function

The mathematical justifications given in Subsection 3.1 above for deploying BT to quantify the unobserved desired output have been confirmed empirically. The estimates, $\boldsymbol { \cdot } \boldsymbol { \lambda } ^ { * }$ and $\boldsymbol { \theta } _ { \mathrm { ~ , ~ } } ^ { \ast }$ of the two transformation parameters contained in the BT function, obtained by $\operatorname { E q . }$ (18), are (0.0935, 0.0772) and (0.0448, 0.0280) for the two-factor and three-factor models, respectively. Because the estimates are neither identical nor 0, the BT transformation production function is certainly more appropriate than both CD and BC for this research. In addition, BT is known to be the generalization of both BC and CD. Thus, specifying the true output as BT is consistent with the mathematical and empirical justifications.

## 3.6. Estimation methods

While Models 1 and 2 with constant speeds of PA are just-identified, Models 3–8 are obviously over-identified. According to Zellner [41] and Kmenta [21], when the disturbances in different equations for different countries are highly correlated, the standard seemingly unrelated regression (SUR, Zellner [41]) and the nonlinear seemingly unrelated regression (NLSUR) estimators are more efficient than the ordinary least-squares (OLS) and the nonlinear least squares (NLS) or the nonlinear maximum-likelihood (NML) method. Here, we are exactly facing the situation in which the disturbances of the equations in each model are highly correlated (with many correlations lying in the range from

0.60 to 0.83.). Thus, Models 1 and 2 with constant speeds of PA and Models 3–8 with dynamic and variable speeds of PA were, respectively, estimated by SUR and NLSUR.

## 4. Data and sources

## 4.1. Selection of countries

This study uses a sample of twelve countries for the period from 1993 to 2008, yielding a panel data set consisting of 192 observations. The twelve countries are: Canada (CN), France (FR), the United Kingdom (UK), the United States (US), Australia (AS), Belgium (BG), Denmark (DM), Greece (GR), Norway (NW), Portugal (PT), Spain (SP), and Switzerland (SZ). One major reason why we chose these countries is the availability of the data on $B _ { j t }$ (the benefits) and $D _ { j t }$ (the duration). Another reason is the stage of their economic developments. The chosen countries have the data on $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t }$ available with the economic development either in the advanced developed or in the newly developed stage. These countries are divided into two groups according to their income criteria. The first four countries (CN, FR, UK, and US) are classified as advanced developed countries and the rest of eight countries as newly developed countries. Such classification offers a possible comparison across countries according to the stages of the economic development level.

The time period that spanned 16 years from 1993 to 2008 was selected simply because the data on B and D were available for the 12 sample countries when we undertook our empirical work at the beginning of 2012. Therefore, the sample was not chosen to circumvent the Great Recession of 2008.

## 4.2. Variables and data

According to the research models, we need these variables to conduct empirical work: (i) capital; (ii) labor; (iii) IT investment on computer software, hardware, and other office equipment; (iv) IS (information systems) staffing costs; (v) unemployment benefits; and (vi) the duration of unemployment. Then, the production factors are (i) ordinary capital $( K _ { j t } )$ = capital minus IT capital; (ii) ordinary labor $\left( L _ { j t } \right)$ = labor minus IS staffing costs; and (iii) IT capital $( I _ { j t } ) = \Pi$ investment $+ 3 ^ { * }$ IS staffing costs, where 3 is usually called the multiplier applied to each and every year of the time period to construct the $I _ { j t }$ series [6,23,25–28]. The macroeconomic factors are (i) unemployment benefits $\left( B _ { j t } \right)$ which are annual unemployment compensation costs and (ii) the duration of unemployment $( D _ { j t } )$ which is the average duration of unemployment measured in months. The dependent variable $( Y _ { j t } )$ is represented by the valueadded created by the input factors and, therefore, is measured by the gross domestic product (GDP) of a country. All the data used have been transformed into 2000 million U.S. dollars.

## 4.3. Data sources

The data needed are collected from several sources. The data on IS staffing costs and IT investment were secured from Digital Planet 2002–2010. The remainder was retrieved from The OECD Statistics, The United Nations Common Database, and Eurostat Database (http:// epp.eurostat.ec.europa.eu).

## 5. Specification and validity tests,<sup>3</sup> results, and discussion

## 5.1. The Chow test for structural breaks

To determine whether there exist structural breaks in the internet bubble period (1999, 2000, and 2001) and 2008, we perform the

Chow [8] test. We find no significant structural breaks that exist in our dataset. Consequently, we are not surprised that a dummy included for 2008 is insignificant.

## 5.2. Specification tests: the White and Breusch–Pagan tests

Here, we undertake the White [40] test and the Lagrange multiplier (LM) test proposed by Breusch and Pagan [5] to see if the random errors in our research models are homoscedastic (H ) or heteroskedastic $\left( H _ { 1 } \right)$ The test results clearly indicate that the random errors in each country's equation are homoscedastic for all eight models.

5.3. The validity and reliability tests to evaluate model parameter magnitude differences: the likelihood ratio (LR), Wald, and LM tests

In this subsection, we evaluate the validity and reliability of the research models by conducting the LR, Wald, and LM tests [9,15,16,22,23]. We are interested in testing the hypothesis composed of the null hypothesis $\left( H _ { 0 } \right)$ : model parameter magnitude differences do not exist versus the alternative hypothesis $( H _ { 1 } ) \colon$ : model parameter magnitude differences do exist. Stated alternatively, we would like to determine if the differences in the parameters associated with any pair of models differ significantly from zero $\left( H _ { 1 } \right)$ or not $\left( H _ { 0 } \right)$ . The test results strongly suggest that $H _ { 0 }$ should be rejected.

5.6. Discussions in detail: the answers to the three research questions

This section focuses on answering the three research questions. In the first place, Table 1 demonstrates the contribution of IT investment in the country as measured by $A P R _ { j }$ in the absence or presence of $B _ { j t }$ $D _ { j t } ,$ or both $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t } .$ In other words, Table 1 serves to answer the first research question: When comparing the production process in which IT capital $\left( I _ { j t } \right)$ is present along with non-IT capital $( K _ { j t } )$ and traditional labor $\left( L _ { j t } \right)$ with one where $I _ { j t }$ is absent, does IT improve (I) or worsen (W) the country's APR without or under the impacts of $B _ { j t }$ or $D _ { j t }$ or joint $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t } ?$ There are four situations characterized by the four cases. Here, we summarize the main points based on the results given in Table 1 according to the four cases.

(i) Case 1 in the absence of B and D: Model 2 vs. Model 1 indicating whether IT does improve (I) or worsen (W) the country's APR<sub>j</sub> in the absence of $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t } .$ In Table 1, after taking into account the effect of IT on the country's $A P R _ { j }$ , the average APR of the whole sample group (AVGW) has increased 12.56%, meaning that on average IT investment did contribute to the country's $A P R _ { j } .$ Interestingly, when comparing other two country groups, we find that the AVG of advanced developed countries has increased 31.95%, while the AVG of newly developed economies has increased just 5.78% which is much smaller than the AVG of the advanced developed countries and is also below the average APR of the whole sample. In other words, collectively, IT investment has exerted a larger positive effect on the APR of advanced developed countries than that of newly developed countries, suggesting that collectively, the advanced developed country group is more productively efficient than the newly developed country group in the absence of B and D.

Individually, the tale differs while looking at the effect of IT investment on the APR of individual advanced developed and newly developed countries. We find that all the countries in the group of advanced developed countries composed of CN, FR, UK, and US have enjoyed improvements in APR (17.83, 40.96, 55.28, and 16.01, respectively). In contrast, among the newly developed nations, IT has caused $A P R _ { j }$ to worsen in AS, GR, and SP (−1.59, −20.81, and −1.15, respectively), suggesting that AS, GR, and SP in the group of newly developed countries have suffered from the productivity paradox in the absence of $B _ { j t }$ and $D _ { j t } .$

(ii) Case 2 in the presence of B only: Model 4 vs. Model 3 suggesting whether IT does improve (I) or worsen (W) the country's APR in the presence of B only. In comparing with Case 1, B improves the AVG of $A P R _ { j } ^ { \prime } s$ for the group of advanced developed countries (from 31.95% to 43.93%), and also improves the AVG of APR 's for the group of newly developed countries (from 5.78% to 11.38%), implying that collectively, $B _ { j t }$ and IT are complementary for both the advanced and the newly developed groups. They are also complementary for the two groups combined because the AVGW also improves from 12.56% in the absence of B to 20.14% in the presence of $B _ { j t } .$ . Individually, in the presence of $B _ { j t } ,$ IT still has shown a positive impact on the APR of the four advanced developed countries, CN, FR, UK, and US (26.60, 29.65, 71.40, and 73.93, respectively); and in the presence of $B _ { j t } ,$ the impact of IT on the APR of GR (−18.91%) remains negative. Thus, $B _ { j t }$ and IT in GR is substitutable; but, in other eleven countries, $B _ { j t }$ and IT are complementary, implying that in the presence of $B _ { j t }$ , the productivity paradox remains in GR but disappears in AS and SP along with other nine countries (i.e., CN, FR, UK, US, BG, DM, NW, PT, and SZ).

(iii) Case 3 in the presence of D only: Model 6 vs. Model 5 indicating whether IT does improve (I) or worsen (W) the country's APR in the presence of $D _ { j t }$ only. The situation in this case is quite different from Case 2. In the presence of $D _ { j t } ,$ , IT contributes to the AVGW slightly higher than Case 1 but much lower than Case 2 (13.18 vs. 12.56 and 20.14); and IT in Case 3 contributes to the AVG of the advanced developed countries less than in Case 1 and Case 2 (18.55 vs. 31.95 and 43.93). In contrast, IT in Case 3 contributes to the AVG of the newly developed economies greater than in Case 1 but slightly less than in Case 2 (10.76 vs. 5.78 and 11.38). In other words, collectively, $D _ { j t }$ and IT are complementary for both the advanced and newly developed groups, so are for the whole sample. Now, we turn to the effect of IT investment in the presence of $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { D } } _ { j t }$ on the individual country's APR<sub>j</sub>. It appears that D and IT are substitutable for GR, SP, and SZ, and are complementary for other nine countries. Thus, in the presence of D only, the productivity paradox does exist in GR, SP, and SZ.

## 5.7. More on the dynamic patterns of the speeds of partial adjustment

Fig. 1 shows explicitly the dynamic patterns of the speed of PA with the impacts of B, D, or joint B and D, i.e., based on the eight research models, for each country.<sup>5</sup> We can group them with similar patterns. For example, CN and BG exhibit a pattern of increasing fluctuations over time based on the eight models, meaning that at the very beginning when B, D or joint B and D was implemented, the speeds of PA did not change; but they started fluctuating more and more after the year of 2000; in other words, the government policy of B, D, or joint B and D has played a greater role of shifting the speeds of PA after 2000.

Another pattern is found in US and PT where the fluctuating patterns of different models converge at the year of 2000, and then diverge after 2000, caused by B, D, or joint B and D. A third pattern is discovered for UK and SP where the speeds of PA fluctuate over time in the absence of IT, but the pattern is stabilized in the presence of IT. Moreover, for FR, and SP, a very stable pattern over time is found. Most countries have a flexion point around 2000 but reveal quite varied patterns.

Furthermore, Fig. 1 clearly indicates that in most countries, the speeds of PA from the three-factor models are more nonlinear than their counterparts from the two-factor models, regardless of whether B, D, or joint B and D is implemented, with AS, GR, SP, and SZ being the exceptions. The implication is that the presence of IT accelerates the speeds of PA in at least two-third of the countries considered.

## 5.8. Implications

Implications for policy makers and academic researchers differ. These include, but are not limited to, the following.

(i) Implications for policy makers:

(a) The PA theory, PAV approaches, research models, built-in performance index, estimation methods, panel dataset, and empirical evidence represent a system that provides enhanced decision support. (b) The results imply that B, D, and joint B and D can be three effective national policies; as such, prudent choice among them is of crucial importance in government decision-making processes. (c) The findings that B, D, and B and D combined have the power to slow or accelerate the speeds of PA and, hence, affect the performance index points to the need to exercise these national policies effectively and carefully in order for them to exert positive impact upon the output and performance index. (d) The results also imply that the choice among the three national policies differ in the presence and absence of IT; therefore, the IT investment becomes a decision-making component needed to make B, D, or B and D combined effective. (e) In the presence of B, D, or joint B and D, the allocation among $K _ { j t } , L _ { j t } ,$ and $I _ { j t } ,$ , is relevant in view of the finding that $K _ { j t }$ exerts the greatest impact upon the output and performance index.

(ii) Implications for academic researchers:

(a) The importance of choosing an appropriate production frontier is implied by the two-equation PAV approach, e.g., between BT if the substitution and complement relations of inputs are neglected (in this study) and CES (constant elasticity of substitution [7]) if the relations among the three inputs can't be ignored (an extension to this study); then in the latter situation, an in-depth analysis of the complementarity and substitutability relationships created by IT and unemployment factors becomes even more complicated but is more interesting. (b) A second implication for researchers is that the present study can be moved a step forward by analyzing the relationships among performance index change, technological progress, and productivity growth (measured by the total factor productivity) in the presence or absence of B, D, or joint B and D. (c) Broad firm-level applications of the PAV approach in which the speeds of PA are dynamic are possible [35]. For example, the chief executive officer (CEO) pay can be investigated by using the decision support system as applied in this research. In other words, the PAV approaches (with constant and dynamic PA speeds) can be applied to bridge a gap created by such issues as: what are the PA speeds of actual (observed) towards desired (maximum) compensation? Whether or not the PA speeds are constant or dynamic over time? And whether or not the firm's performance is positively affected by the PA speeds and desired compensation so that the actual compensation is justified. Of course, in applying the two-equation PAV approach, B and D will be replaced by other variables appropriate for the CEO pay problem. These are ongoing research projects of the first author with an advanced Ph.D. candidate.

## 6. Concluding remarks and managerial decision-making benefits of this research

This country-level study investigated whether the (inter)relationships among information technology (IT), unemployment benefits (B), and unemployment duration (D) are complementary or substitutable, as judged by the speed of partial adjustment (PA) and the performance ratio (index) which is the desired (maximum) output multiplied by the speed of PA.

The methodology used is known as the PA valuation (PAV) approaches with constant and time-varying speeds of partial adjustment. The PAV approaches are noted for having strong theoretical foundation, ease of applications, and built-in performance measures. They are especially appropriate for this research.

The objective of the study was transformed into three major research questions and the answers to the questions were pursued. The empirical evidence related to the answers to the research questions reveals that the (inter)relationships among IT, B, and D are a complex issue and differ from country to country and from different levels of economic development.

The complex (inter)relationships imply that prudent exercises of national policies as to the implements of IT, B, and D are critical to promoting recovery and boosting economic growth. The evidence also implies that the policies related to IT, B, and D are more effective for the advanced than for the newly developed countries and that the practice of joint B and D is more powerful than B or D standing alone in influencing the business value of IT and curing the disease of the productivity paradox and, hence, the country's performance. Thus, there are important implications for policy makers and academic researchers; and the links of this work to DSS are strong.

It is noted that as a byproduct, the data do not support the notion that the productivity paradox exists only in developing countries and reject the widely shared view that the productivity paradox has disappeared since the early 1990s. The byproduct is consistent with the conclusions reached by [7,23].

Finally, we point out that, though this research takes the country as the level of study, there are managerial decision-making benefits of this research for managers and firms. The following are just some of them. First, this research offers a comprehensive application of the abovestated decision support system (Subsection 5.9). The system consists of a series of instruments of enhanced decision making and is well applicable for firm-level studies. The third extension proposed to investigate the firm's CEO pay (Section 5.8) is a witness.

Second, it fallows that the outcomes of this work yield in the way of aid to managerial decision making. For example, in the economic environment with B and D, one outcome indicates that ordinary capital or non-IT capital $\left( K _ { j t } \right)$ is the greatest contributor of the output and performance index, followed by ordinary labor $\left( L _ { j t } \right)$ and IT capital $\left( I _ { j t } \right)$ We believe this outcome equally applies to the firm under the same economic environment. Thus, the outcome is an aid to managerial decision making of the resources allocation among $K _ { j t } , L _ { j t } ,$ and $I _ { j t } .$ Take another example. In the economy characterized by B and D, IT and B and D combined were found to be substitutable (see US and CN with Case 4 in Table 1), meaning the existence of the IT productivity paradox. Under this situation, IT investment may be considered crucial for the firm's survival, competitiveness and growth, the decision support system used in the research becomes an aid to the managerial decision making of IT investment.
