# DIGITIZATION OF TRANSACTION TERMS WITHIN TCE:STRONG SMART CONTRACT AS A NEW MODE OFTRANSACTION GOVERNANCE<sup>1</sup>

Hanna Halaburda and Natalia Levina New York University, Leonard N. Stern School of Business, New York, NY, U.S.A {hh66@stern.nyu.edu} {nl28@stern.nyu.edu}

Semi Min University of Minnesota – Twin Cities, Carlson School of Management, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {minxx146@umn.edu}

We use transaction cost economics (TCE) to define the “digitization of transaction terms” shift parameter that describes the institutional changes associated with increased digitization in society. We then draw on legal scholarship to analyze how strong smart contracts, which refer to agreements with automatic execution and enforcement that are not reversible by courts, rely on a new level of digitization of transaction terms. Specifically, these contracts may rely on standard digital infrastructures such as blockchain systems that guarantee automatic execution and non-reversibility. Strong smart contracts represent a distinct mode of transaction governance compared to markets, hierarchies, or hybrids. This is because each classic governance mode is distinguished by how ex post adaptation is handled—through public courts, managerial fiat, or both. In contrast, strong smart contracts prevent ex post adaptation altogether. We propose that when strong smart contracts can be fully specified, they may dominate other governance modes based on certain trade-offs. These trade-offs include weighing the benefits of avoiding the holdup problem and lowering contract enforcement costs against the downsides of high ex ante specification costs and the elimination of flexibility to make ex post adjustments in a changing environment. Our discussion elaborates on which institutional conditions can further facilitate this institutional shift.

Keywords: Smart contracts, blockchain, transaction cost economics, firm boundaries, shift parameter, digitization

## Introduction

Digital technology has enabled significant institutional shifts in preferred modes of transaction governance. Seminal papers by Malone et al. (1987), Gurbaxani and Whang (1991), and Clemons et al. (1993) unpacked the nature of the anticipated changes before they fully unfolded in society. These papers combined theoretical insights from transaction cost economics (TCE) and related theories with a deep understanding of the nature of digital technology. This theoretical work allowed information systems scholars to understand and anticipate major institutional shifts at a time when the actual scope of the impact of digitization on transaction governance was still very limited.

In this paper, we take a similar approach to understanding the potential role of smart contracts in relation to other modes of transaction governance. Specifically, we draw on TCE and provide a nuanced understanding of technology to unpack how smart contracts might reshape transaction governance. Moreover, while authors of prior seminal papers theorized the transition from analog to digital technologies, today we must take a more nuanced approach to theorize the differences between older and newer digital technologies.

We conceptualize strong smart contracts as a theoretical concept enabled by digital infrastructure—at the moment, specifically by blockchain technologies. We draw on legal scholarship to define this concept as a novel form of transaction governance and conceptualize how it can reshape the institutional environment within TCE theory. TCE is uniquely suited to this theorizing because it offers a framework for analyzing the societal-level institutional change in preferred modes of transaction governance.

We use the TCE concept of shift parameters to integrate seminal IS papers and argue that the infrastructure for supporting strong smart contacts constitutes another level of societal digitization. Moreover, this new level of what we term the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter must be supported by a law regime change that treats strong smart contracts as non-reversible. The key outcome of our analysis is that this form of contracting constitutes a new transaction governance mode that can replace other modes of governance (market, hybrid, or firm) for some transactions. Specifically, strong smart contracts can safeguard transactions with some (nontrivial) degree of asset specificity against the potential holdup problem. However, such contracts are only applicable when the environment is stable enough to encode all future relevant states of the world and each party’s actions into contractual terms at a reasonable cost. Only in such cases would parties be willing to forgo the benefits of ex post adjustments.

Finally, we discuss how current blockchain technologies support the implementation of the theoretical construct of strong smart contracts to varying degrees. We then elaborate on what other technological and societal institutional changes need to take place to increase the scope of the impact of smart contracts on society, which is currently limited due to the lack of fully reliable digital infrastructures for strong smart contracting and high contract specification costs. These costs may decline as such infrastructure is built and society creates increasing amounts of data, allowing for better prediction of future states and cheaper contract enforcement costs.

## Transaction Cost Economics Overview

## Three Key Transaction Governance Modes

TCE theory posits that firms and markets are alternative institutional structures for governing transactions among economic agents (Williamson, 1985). Firms govern transactions by directing and controlling agents’ actions through managerial hierarchies. Markets, on the other hand, govern transactions through contractual agreements enforceable through a public court system. Later writings provided additional nuance to TCE’s initial binary choice between firms and markets by adding “hybrid contracting,”

a mixture of firm and market institutional forms (Williamson, 2002, 2008).

TCE assumes that agents are opportunistic (“self-interest seeking with guile”) (Williamson, 1985, p. 47) and boundedly rational. Bounded rationality is associated with human cognitive limitations (Simon, 1957) and thus impedes comprehensive contracting since it is infeasible to specify how parties will act in all possible contingencies (Williamson, 1985, p. 5). The incompleteness of a contract can give rise to opportunistic behavior ex post when one or both parties need to adapt to new conditions, such as supply and demand fluctuations or technological change (Williamson, 1971, p. 115). This ex post opportunistic behavior is commonly referred to as “Williamson’s holdup problem” (Teece, 2010, p. 168). This gives rise to disputes, which hierarchical governance resolves ex post by managers imposing order from above (Williamson, 1985, p. 165). If ex post disputes arise within markets, they are resolved by public courts interpreting contractual terms within contract law (Williamson, 1985).

All three institutional forms face ex ante and ex post transaction costs, and the comparison among them shapes predominant governance modes in each institutional environment (Williamson, 1985). Ex ante transaction costs inside the firm are assumed to be very high; these bureaucratic overhead costs involve hiring managers and running a system for monitoring, reporting, and dispute resolution, even in the absence of disputes (Williamson, 1985). Ex ante transaction costs in the market include the costs of selecting trustworthy partners and writing, negotiating, and safeguarding well-designed agreements (Dahlman, 1979; Dyer, 1997; North, 1990; Tadelis & Williamson, 2012; Williamson, 1985). Market contracts also face ex post contract enforcement costs, such as the cost of collecting payments and resolving disputes. Even if such disputes are settled out of court, significant costs accompany arbitrage, renegotiating, or simply ignoring the conflict (Galanter, 1981; Williamson, 2002).

Hybrids are a type of market contract and involve a mixture of transaction costs associated with markets and hierarchies (Williamson, 2002). Specifically, hybrids are used when both parties understand that the contract is risky and managerial structures are established to safeguard against risks and resolve potential disputes. Thus, while hybrids help reduce the holdup risk without the full fixed costs of running a bureaucracy, they have higher ex post adjustment costs than either markets or firms (Williamson, 1991). Table 1 summarizes the relative transaction costs of different governance modes.

These three modes of governance are regulated by different legal doctrines (Williamson, 2002). Firm transactions are subject to the “forbearance doctrine” (Williamson, 1991). This means “that parties to an internal exchange can work out their differences themselves or appeal unresolved disputes to the hierarchy for a decision” (Williamson, 1991, p. 276). Contract law (or “classical contract law”) governs “ideal” market transactions, where the identity of contracting parties is irrelevant to dispute resolution and only contractual terms matter for courts (Williamson, 1991, p. 271). Finally, hybrid contracts rely on the “excuse doctrine,” which relieves parties of the strict enforcement of contractual terms by leaving contract adjustments to the bureaucratic structures set up for overseeing the relationship (Williamson, 1991).

TCE postulates that asset specificity, transaction frequency, and uncertainty shape the predominance of different governance modes (Williamson, 1985, pp. 68-84). An asset is specific to a transaction if its value in its next-best use outside a given transaction is lower than within it. Asset specificity becomes problematic when transactions are infrequent and there is high environmental uncertainty, defined as “disturbances for which unprogrammed adaptations are needed” (Tadelis & Williamson, 2012, p. 165). In such cases, the holdup risk rises, the ease of public contract enforcement vanishes (Williamson, 1985, p. 79), and the fixed cost of running a bureaucratic system becomes increasingly justified. This is particularly true when the same parties contract with each other (i.e., “transaction frequency” increases). Hybrid contracts generally fall in the middle of the spectrum for all three parameters (Williamson, 2002, 2008).

Over the last 50-plus years, researchers have been investigating TCE and adding nuance to the original theory. For example, they have further characterized different types of asset specificity, distinguished environmental uncertainty from three types of internal uncertainty, expanded the theory of hybrid contracting, and challenged Williamson’s assumptions of bounded rationality, opportunism, and risk neutrality (see Cuypers et al., 2021). Yet the empirical work testing TCE has produced mixed results (Carter & Hodgson, 2006; Cuypers et al., 2021; David & Han, 2004). It is unclear if these mixed results indicate TCE’s theoretical limitations or are due to researchers incorrectly operationalizing TCE’s key constructs (e.g., Cuypers et al., 2021, p. 117). This problem is exacerbated by the fact that outside the international business literature, relatively little attention has been paid to institutional factors (Cuypers et al., 2021, p. 117). This is especially problematic in light of the major institutional changes associated with new technologies, such as digital platforms and blockchains (Cuypers et al., 2021, p. 137). We propose to address this shortcoming by drawing on Williamson’s concept of shift parameters.

## Shift Parameters

Williamson takes particular care to explain that the choice of governance mode is greatly influenced by the nature of the institutional environment in which economic transactions occur (Henisz & Williamson, 1999; Williamson, 1991). He discusses how changes in the environment can be characterized by shifts in four key parameters: property rights, contract law regime, reputation effects, and uncertainty (Williamson, 1991). Shifts in these institutional environment parameters, also referred to as “shift parameters” (Jain & Thietart, 2014), lead to changes in the comparative costs of governance. Table 2 summarizes the institutional shift parameters identified by Williamson (1991).

Property rights are a shift parameter that refers to the ownership of assets and how they can be used. Williamson (1991) gives an example of a change in the property rights regime experienced during the fall of the Soviet Bloc, where property rights law changed from supporting government control over most property rights to respecting private property rights. With digitization, there was a shift in property rights because information goods are often subject to the “leakage” of property rights (Williamson 1991, p. 289).

Williamson (1991) argues that changes in contract law regimes, including the laws governing markets, hybrids, and firms, constitute another shift parameter. For example, improvements in the enforcement of commercial contracts in the economy would increase reliance on market contracts and hybrids (Henisz & Williamson, 1999; Williamson, 2005, 2008). Similarly, the “mistaken forbearance doctrine,” which refers to the willingness of the courts to litigate business disputes within firms, increases reliance on markets and hybrids (Williamson 1991, p. 290). While these institutional shifts have a unidirectional effect, not all changes in contract law regimes produce such clear results. For example, it is unclear how changes in the excuse doctrine, such as the ease of creating a digital audit trail allowing for the stricter enforcement of contractual terms by courts, would impact hybrid contracts.

The reputation effects shift parameter refers to the speed and accuracy of disseminating information about potential transacting parties (Williamson, 1991). If reputation effects associated with market and hybrid contracts are strong, the incentive for opportunistic behavior decreases, thereby disfavoring firms compared to the market and especially hybrids (Williamson, 1991). While Williamson (1991) also discusses reputation effects within the firm, economics, and strategy scholars generally reserve this term for dealing with market reputations (e.g., Rindova et al., 2010; Wang, 2002).

The uncertainty shift parameter characterizes an environment according to the frequency of disturbances or an increase in the variance of disturbances that require a coordinated response (Williamson, 1991, p. 291). Given that subsequent work on TCE has distinguished among many types of uncertainty, it is important to clarify that the uncertainty shift parameter in Williamson (1991) is best aligned with “external environmental uncertainty” in Carter and Hodgson’s taxonomy (2006, p. 466). We have seen big changes in this parameter, for example, in the high pace of evolution of digital technologies and during the COVID-19 global pandemic. While all three governance modes experience increased governance costs when uncertainty increases, Williamson (1991) argues that hybrid contracting is most vulnerable to this change “because hybrid adaptations cannot be made unilaterally (as with market governance) or by fiat (as with hierarchy) but require mutual consent” (p. 291).

Subsequent work on TCE has discussed other types of shift parameters, the most prominent being the “the level of trust” in society (for reviews, see Cuypers et al., 2021; McMackin et al., 2022). A higher level of trust can shift economic activity away from market contacts toward other governance modes (Cuypers et al., 2021). National culture, which is closely related to the level of trust in society, has also been proposed as a shift parameter (Handley & Angst, 2015). Finally, several attempts have been made to incorporate firm characteristics such as the firm’s capabilities (Jain & Thietart, 2014), the degree of centralization (Weigelt & Miller, 2013), and market power (Shervani et al., 2007) as potential shift parameters. Still, the TCE literature has done little over the last 50 years to further develop the notion of shift parameters, leaving them largely as a “black box” (Cuypers et al. 2021, p. 117). We believe there is an opportunity to draw on this concept to characterize the major environmental changes associated with advancements in digital technologies, including the rise of blockchain systems.

## TCE versus Contract Theory in Economics

TCE shares ideas with contract theory in that both focus on firm boundaries to explain why certain economic activities take place in markets versus firms (Tadelis & Williamson, 2012). Contract theory includes work on incomplete contracts, property rights, and principal-agent incentive alignment (Aghion et al., 2015; Grossman & Hart, 1986; Hart & Moore, 1990). Yet TCE is quite different from this theory when it comes to its assumptions and goals. Contract theory centers around “fully” (rather than boundedly) rational agents making strategic choices. Contract theory aims to provide such agents with a modeling framework to calculate the optimal contractual incentives and property ownership decisions before signing the contract. Because of the assumption of full rationality in all transactions, societal institutions and thus institutional shifts are not the focus of this theory. Instead, market contracts, hybrids, and firms are characterized as fundamentally belonging to the same institutional regime.

In contrast, TCE embraces the Simonian assumption of bounded rationality and explicitly aims to describe alternative institutional governance structures that govern transactions ex post (Gibbons, 2010; Tadelis & Williamson, 2012). Each institution offers boundedly rational agents a set of different “default” templates for dealing with the problem of adaptation to a changing environment (Tadelis & Williamson, 2012). It does not assume that agents are able to calculate all risks involved in setting up the best contractual incentives, given their human inability to fully analyze all possible contingencies.

## Digitization of Transaction Terms as Institutional Change

IS research uses TCE and contract theory as a key theoretical basis for understanding how the digitization of transaction terms reshapes the predominance of transaction governance modes (see Schermann et al., 2016 for review). IS literature has been at the forefront of describing and explaining these changes. We propose to integrate this literature by introducing the concept of the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter. We draw on three seminal IS papers that provided an understanding of how the digitization of transaction terms was likely to shift preferred modes of transaction governance before the impact of these institutional changes was evident (Malone et al., 1987; Gurbaxani and Whang, 1991; Clemons et al., 1993).

## Ease of Contract Specification Due to Digitization of Transaction Terms

Malone et al. (1987) predicted a shift in economic activity from firms to markets. The authors suggested that digitization would reduce product complexity and enable the writing of more precise contracts, thereby reducing ex ante contract specification costs (Table 1). Secondly, they argued that “an overall reduction in the ‘unit costs’ of coordination would reduce the importance of the coordination cost dimension (on which markets are weak) and thus lead to markets becoming more desirable in some situations where hierarchies were previously favored” (Malone et al., 1987, p. 489). They also discussed possible second-order effects, including the impact of digitization of production technologies on reducing asset specificity due to increased standardization. In that sense, the digitization of production technologies makes asset specificity a new type of TCE shift parameter.

The empirical analysis of longitudinal, economy-wide data has largely supported Malone et al.’s (1987) claims, showing that IT investments are associated with a reduction in firm size (Brynjolfsson et al., 1994). Moreover, IT investments may also lead to smaller firm scope (Dewan et al., 1998; Hitt, 1999). Empirical research also provides evidence for the mechanisms proposed by Malone et al. For example, Argyres’s (1999) study illustrates how digital technology implemented within the aerospace industry enabled an increase in external contracting by creating a shared grammar that helped ease contract specifications and standardize communication among transaction partners. The argument that digital standardization of communication interfaces is a key driver of the shift from firms to markets is also supported by studies showing that the degree of subcontracting increases when firms adopt shared technological platforms, such as the internet and EDI (electronic data interchange) (McElheran & Forman, 2019), open standard platforms (Tafti et al., 2013), and enterprise management systems (Bardhan et al., 2007).

In the more than three decades since Malone et al.’s (1987) publication, electronic markets have become a commonplace phenomenon. Their rise is largely attributed to digitized contracts, standardized transaction terms (e.g., APIs), digital payment systems, and automatic execution (Fan et al., 2000; Gawer & Cusumano, 2014; Parker et al., 2016; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005).

## More Efficient Digital Transaction Monitoring and Enforcement

Gurbaxani and Whang (1991) built on Malone et al.’s (1987) work, both extending and challenging prior arguments. Gurbaxani and Whang challenged parts of Malone et al.’s paper, arguing that digitization of transaction terms may not only reduce the costs of market contracting but may also reduce the costs of running hierarchies. Specifically, Gurbaxani and Whang argued that Malone et al. did not pay sufficient attention to the incentive issues inside firms, focusing instead on communication and coordination issues. Focusing directly on these incentive issues (e.g., the principalagent problem inside the firm), Gurbaxani and Whang argued that automatic monitoring systems may benefit firms (via the reduction of ex ante bureaucratic overhead costs) just as much as they benefit markets (via the reduction of the ex post contract enforcement costs).

Thus, based on the theoretical arguments alone, one cannot predict the impact of digitization of transaction terms on firm boundaries. Brynjolfsson et al. (1994) examined historical changes at the industry and economy level, offering empirical support to the argument that digitization favors markets more than firms as the firm size has decreased with digitization. However, they did not examine the underlying mechanisms empirically.

## Ease of Ex Post Adjustment Due to Standard Digital Contracting Infrastructure

The next key idea in understanding the impact of the digitization of transaction terms on the preferred modes of transaction governance was the focus on ex post adjustment costs. Williamson’s (1985) initial writing primarily considered ex post transaction costs to be associated only with the need to enforce contracts in public courts. Yet the discussion of the environmental uncertainty shift parameter in Williamson (1991) pointed out that there are nontrivial ex post adjustment costs associated with environmental adaptation in both hierarchies and hybrids. While Williamson (1991) did not name these costs explicitly, he concluded that hybrids have the highest ex post adjustment costs as they carry the costs of both markets and hierarchies and that they will be phased out when the environmental uncertainty is very high and there is a need for frequent adaptation (Figure 1 in Williamson 1991, p. 292).

It is these ex post adjustment costs that are at the heart of the so-called “move to the middle” hypothesis proposed by Clemons et al. (1993). They argued that standard digital infrastructure (e.g., EDI) for contracting would reduce ex post adjustment costs and thus increase the prevalence of hybrids in transaction governance (Clemons et al., 1993; Clemons & Row, 1992). They also argued that in the pre-digital era, the investments made in specifying transactions in hybrids created a new relationship-specific investment and thus an increased holdup risk. This situation was remedied by standard digital infrastructures where the digital encoding of transaction terms does not need to be relationship-specific and where the ease of switching porters ex post increases (Clemons et al., 1993; Clemons & Row, 1992).

Generally, empirical research on EDI and similar B2B (business-to-business) exchanges shows an increased reliance on hybrids (Aral et al., 2017; Mani et al., 2012; Susarla et al., 2009, 2010). This effect is amplified when firms rely on open (rather than proprietary) communication standards as predicted by theory (Tafti et al., 2013). Yet some research has challenged Clemons et al.’s (1993) arguments. Specifically, despite the presence of a standard digital infrastructure for contracting, at higher levels of uncertainty and with significant bilateral dependencies, “digital hybrids” increase the holdup risk. The additional digital contract specification costs indeed increase asset specificity, leading to digital hierarchies being preferable to digital hybrids (Anderson et al., 2000; Sklaroff, 2017). For example, suppose suppliers incur high relationship-specific contract specification costs. In that case, they may drop out of the market, leaving only a handful of alternative suppliers and increasing the holdup risk (Levi et al., 2003). Moreover, additional contract specification costs may limit the ability to switch partners (Gosain et al., 2004). Thus, the magnitude of the move to the middle may be reduced and may depend on the trade-offs between lower ex post adjustment costs and increased ex ante contract specification costs, especially if the latter creates a new relationship-specific investment.

Along the same lines, a set of prominent qualitative studies on the limitations of hybrid contracting was conducted by Lacity and Willcocks (1998, 2001). They studied high uncertainty and high bilateral dependency relationships such as the outsourcing of large IT operations. Lacity and Willcocks demonstrated that firms that have implemented hybrid contracting in such cases incurred an unmanageable holdup risk. As a result, shorter-term, fee-for-service contracts and hierarchical governance performed better than hybrids (Lacity & Willcocks, 1998, 2001). Quantitative survey-based studies have also corroborated these findings (e.g., Ross & Beath, 2006; Whitten & Leidner, 2006).

Reflecting on the findings of this literature, we see the key importance of a standard digital contracting infrastructure in the “move to the middle” hypothesis. The digitization of transaction terms per se may not increase the predominance of hybrids; rather, it is only when hybrids are aided by a standard (and especially open) digital infrastructure that the shift occurs.

## Defining Digitization of Transaction Terms Shift Parameter

Relating the IS literature to Williamson’s (1991), we can argue that it has described an institutional shift impacting the predominance of transaction governance modes. We define the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter as referring to the level of digitization of transaction terms in a given environment.

The notion of the digital (“electronic”) codifiability of a contract has been discussed in IS literature and defined as “the ability to precisely characterize in electronic format the nature of the product/service contracted for, including delivery requirements and any other contractual/fulfillment requirements that may pertain to a specific transaction, in a manner understandable to relevant parties” (Levi et al. 2003, p. 79). We define digitized transactions as agreements where the expected actions of each party are fully encoded in “computable,” where “the parties have arranged for a computer to make automated, prima-facie assessments about compliance or performance” (Surden, 2012, p. 636). The digitization of transaction terms enables digitized contracts, which are different from semantic contracts— contracts written in a natural language that do not require computability, even if stored digitally (Sklaroff 2017).

Using this terminology, we can say that Malone et al. (1987) described how the digitization of transaction terms parameter moves from no digitization to digitization of transaction terms. Gurbaxani and Whang (1991) added automatic monitoring systems to this societal shift. Finally, Clemons et al. (1993) added standard digital contracting infrastructure. The arguments made in these seminal papers that examine the impact of the digitization of transaction terms on governance modes within the TCE framework are summarized in Table 3.

In addition to the direct impact of the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter on transaction governance, the IS literature has also described indirect effects. For example, Malone et al. (1987) anticipated the rise of digital reputation systems, which would correspond to the change in the reputation effects shift parameter. Consistent with Williamson (1991), Malone et al. (1987) argued that this shift could benefit both “electronic markets” and “electronic hierarchies.”

Malone et al. (1987) also discussed additional possible impacts of digitization on transaction costs beyond the digitization of transaction terms. For example, they argued that digital production technologies can reduce asset specificity as they can enable finding alternative vendors to reduce holdup as such vendors can use flexible manufacturing to meet customerspecific needs. Yet digital technologies can also increase asset specificity. Investing in IT often relies on complementary investment in firm-specific intangible assets (e.g., Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2000). This increase in asset specificity may be particularly noticeable regarding specialized knowledge investments (Autio et al., 2021). Future research could analyze production-related digitization as its own shift parameter. However, such an analysis is beyond the scope of our paper, as we are interested in analyzing how new types of digital infrastructures for contracting (such as platforms for smart contracting) impact governance.

## Smart Contracts Within the TCE Framework

The seminal IS papers reviewed above theorize the anticipated institutional changes in transaction governance as society transitioned from analog to digital technologies. Today, a large part of this transition has already taken place. Currently, blockchains are being widely discussed as a trigger of a new major institutional change (Davidson et al., 2018; Lakhani & Iansiti, 2017; Tapscott & Tapscott, 2017). One of the key applications of blockchain technologies promising to transform transaction governance is smart contracts, which are seen as a new legal option in contracting (Davidson et al., 2018; Howell & Potgieter, 2021; Wright & De Filippi, 2018). In the following, we theorize how smart contracts might reshape transaction governance by using the digitization transaction terms shift parameter proposed above.

## Strong Smart Contracts within Contracting Law

Smart contracts were conceptually introduced by Szabo (1997) and popularized by the Ethereum blockchain system (Alharby & van Moorsel, 2017). To theorize the potential impact of smart contracts on transaction governance, we draw on legal scholarship that discusses smart contracts as a novel form of contracting (Holden & Malani, 2021; Raskin, 2016; Savelyev, 2017; Sklaroff, 2017; Werbach & Cornell, 2017; Wright & De Filippi, 2015, 2018). Legal scholars define the smart contract concept as “agreements whose execution is automated” (Raskin 2016, p. 309), arguing that with smart contracts, “automation ensures performance, for better or worse, by exercising human discretion from contract execution” (Raskin 206, p. 306). Unlike traditional contracts, whose execution can be stopped by court orders, smart contracts must be executed once initiated (Wright & De Filippi, 2018).

Parties to a smart contract must specify their rights and obligations in digital form, which are then expected to be automatically enforced based on digitally encoded triggers. Szabo (1997) used an example of a vending machine to illustrate this concept. A vending machine is a “spot contract” that meets all the smart contract’s defining criteria. A customer gets the product automatically once the button is pressed and the machine receives a “valid” payment. Szabo (1997) then discussed how such logic can be applied to dynamic contracts, i.e., contracts that unfold over time. Szabo (1997) illustrated this with a futuristic example of a car lease contract: if the lessee failed to make payments, the car would automatically lock itself based on a digitally recorded trigger, and the control would revert to the lender. Of course, encoded triggers need to include special provisions for safety and other reasonable contingencies, but with these provisions, smart contracts will be more efficient by bypassing costly court systems (Szabo 1997).

Szabo (1997) envisioned two major advantages of smart contracts over other types of contracts: reducing the holdup risk and ensuring automatic execution. Indeed, the automatic execution of such contracts should reduce contracting costs. The reduction in the holdup risk assumes that renegotiation is impossible (e.g., a lessee cannot keep negotiating a payment extension with the lender while still driving the car). This is not guaranteed, however, since public courts may still be able to modify and even reverse smart contracts.

To differentiate between smart contracts that can be modified ex post and those that cannot, legal scholars have proposed distinguishing between weak and strong smart contracts, where “strong smart contracts have prohibitive costs of revocation and modification, while weak smart contracts do not” (Raskin, 2016, p. 310). In other words, the costs of ex post revocation and modification are so high that it would not make any sense for the contracting parties to incur them (Raskin, 2016). Indeed, blockchain technologies became associated with smart contracts precisely because permissionless blockchain systems, under certain conditions, could make it prohibitively expensive for courts to modify a smart contract ex post—making strong smart contracts practically feasible (Holden & Malani, 2021; Raskin, 2016; Werbach & Cornell, 2017). Thus, strong smart contracts correspond to Szabo’s (1997) promise: a new contracting mode that eliminates the holdup risk by preventing any possibility of ex post modification. If a weak smart contract were used in Szabo’s (1997) car lease scenario, a person who missed a payment could still negotiate new payment terms with the bank after the nonpayment event.

## Smart Contracts as a Subset of Digitized Contracts

Despite their novel aspects, smart contracts are an outgrowth of a decades-long process of the digitization of transaction terms. We can think of weak smart contracts requiring an additional capability, namely automatic execution, from the standard digital contracting infrastructure described by Clemons et al. (1993) and captured in Table 3. Thus, weak smart contracts are digitized adjustable contracts with automatic execution. In contrast, strong smart contracts are digitized contracts that execute automatically and are prohibitively expensive to adjust ex post (“nonadjustable,” for short). ## Strong Smart Contracts as a New Mode of Transaction Governance

We propose that strong smart contracts are qualitatively distinct from all other modes of transaction governance within the TCE framework. TCE’s key concern is an adaptation to an unpredictably changing environment while facing the risk of opportunistic behavior. All three key governance modes allow for the possibility of the ex post adjustment of contractual terms unilaterally, bilaterally, or through the courts. TCE then focuses on how these ex post adjustments enable opportunistic behavior because of relationship-specific investments. Strong smart contracts eliminate the possibility of ex post adjustment. The benefit of eliminating ex post adjustment is that it also removes the potential for opportunistic behavior (or the holdup risk); the downside is that it also removes any opportunity to adjust to a changing environment.

Strong smart contracts require the institutional environment where they are written and executed to have an infrastructure for guaranteeing automatic execution without means of revocation. This infrastructure needs to remove human (potentially partial) discretion from contracting by having a reliable sociotechnical system that guarantees these properties. While, in theory, having a semantic contract with a clause prohibiting renegotiation (Maskin & Moore, 1999) might seem equivalent to a strong smart contract, in practice, such contracts still need to be enforced by courts and thus leave room for haggling among parties (e.g., to avoid costly court fees).

Currently blockchain technology, under certain conditions, can enable the emergence of sociotechnical systems that support strong smart contracts. For example, Lumineau et al. (2021) argued that the blockchain governance mechanism is fundamentally different from contractual and relational governance mechanisms in that it relies on a self-contained and autonomous system of rules that is automatically enforced and encoded in a programming language (p. 506). Traditional governance mechanisms rely either on courts or bureaucratic controls to enforce rules and do not require encoding or automation (Lumineau et al., 2021, p. 506). However, other technologies can also be envisioned for enabling strong smart contracts. For example, if public courts and hierarchies never reversed the execution of orders in vending machines (e.g., didn’t provide a number to request a refund), they could also support strong smart contracts. We propose that the environment where infrastructure supporting strong smart contracts exists corresponds to a new level of the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter (visually equivalent to adding a new row to Table 3)

More generally, our argument can be interpreted as follows: regarding the reduction in the holdup risk, the non-reversibility of strong smart contracts serves as a substitute for public courts in markets, for bureaucratic control in hierarchies, and for both of these mechanisms in hybrids. This observation allows us to consider the prevalence of diverse governance modes in different institutional environments. For example, Williamson (1991) and subsequent research on the role of the law regime shift parameter argued that a reliable third-party player for ex post dispute resolution, such as public courts, would increase the role of markets, whereas corrupt or weak courts would lower it (e.g., Boehm, 2013; Brown et al., 2004). We can extend this logic to strong smart contracts: if the law regime in a given society endorses digital infrastructures (such as permissionless blockchain systems) that support strong smart contracts as legally non-reversible contracts, then the other governance modes will give way to strong smart contracts for some transactions. In some sense, much like the forbearance doctrine for firms and the excuse doctrine for hybrids, a given law regime will need to develop a new doctrine (e.g., a “nonreversibility doctrine”) for strong smart contracts.

For example, if an infrastructure both supporting strong smart contracts and respected by a law regime were available for supply chain exchanges, we would expect some transactions with a high holdup risk to move away from hierarchies and hybrids, opting for strong smart contracts instead. For some transactions (e.g., purchasing goods with certain characteristics), if suppliers and buyers could encode all their expected actions and all potential future states of the world into strong smart contacts without any ambiguity, they would eliminate potential ex post haggling or legal disputes. Barring fully unexpected environmental changes (e.g., a global pandemic limiting shipments), ex ante investments in costly bureaucratic controls would become unnecessary.

Besides eliminating the need for courts and hierarchical governance, it is likely that the strong smart contract governance mode would also reduce the role of reputational incentives. Strong reputational incentives enhance both market (Tadelis, 1999) and hybrid governance (Brown et al., 2004) by helping to reduce the holdup risk. However, since strong smart contracts fully eliminate this risk, the need for reputational incentives diminishes. This is aligned with prior TCE analysis. For example, Lafontaine and Slade (2012) argued: “Explicit incentives become less important when either reputation or repeated interaction, and associated rents, are present and thus can be used to support transactions. Conversely, explicit incentives become especially important in the absence of reputation or expectations of future interactions as long as the legal system is capable of enforcing those explicit provisions” (p. 1001). Because strong smart contracts are all about explicit (fully computable) incentives, we expect strong smart contracts to serve as a substitute for reputational incentives.

While eliminating the holdup problem is the key advantage of strong smart contracts, high specification costs constitute one of their key disadvantages. Digitized contracts may incur significant contract specification costs (Bajari & Tadelis, 2001; Levi et al., 2003; Surden, 2012): To fully specify any transaction digitally, one would need to adequately (1) encode actions that each party is expected to take (e.g., lock the leased car) (2) based on certain inputs from sensors or information inflows (e.g., not receiving a loan payment) and (3) accounting for all relevant future states of the world (e.g., only if it is not endangering lives). However, many known difficulties are associated with adequately encoding human intentions (Mik, 2017; Sillaber et al., 2021). In writing a digitized contract, one may deliberately or inadvertently (1) allow for actions that were unintended, (2) encounter unexpected or missing values from inputs, or (3) fail to consider some relevant potential states of the world. Because strong smart contracts explicitly prohibit ex post adjustments, they are only relevant to highly codifiable transactions and even then incur very high specification costs (Howell & Potgieter, 2021; Mik, 2017).

To conclude, we argue that strong smart contracts represent a new mode of transaction governance, distinct from the three key TCE modes. This mode offers a different set of trade-offs among key economic forces at play within TCE. It is fully different from the market in that it has very low contract enforcement costs and fully eliminates the holdup risk. At the same time, it is fully different from the hierarchy in that it allows for no ex post adjustments. Table 5 summarizes these differences.

## Strong Smart Contracts Displacing Other Transaction Governance Modes

As a new mode of transaction governance, smart contracts, with their benefits and risks, can be expected to reshape the predominance of transaction governance modes. First, we note that since weak smart contracts are a subtype of digitized adjustable contracts, we expect that their impact on transaction governance would not be different from the impact of other digitized adjustable contracts already captured in the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter in Table 3. One may argue that there is an additional impact of weak smart contracts, namely the automatic execution of transactions, which can create further efficiencies within the standard digital infrastructure for contracting, as described by Clemons et al. (1993). These additional efficiencies, however, are applicable to all three traditional modes—markets, hybrids, and hierarchies. For example, an automatic execution of a payment request upon the receipt of a particular digital input (e.g., a ride completed in a ride-sharing app) could still be reversed by a higher-level authority (e.g., a customer service office for a credit card dispute or a court order for large-scale financial fraud). Similarly, an automatic payment request within an organization (e.g., an automatically approved travel reimbursement) could still be reversed by a manager. Thus, we do not see a significant shift in preferred transaction governance modes with the introduction of weak smart contracts.

What is truly distinct for strong smart contracts is the preclusion of ex post adjustments. This distinct feature removes the holdup risk associated with specific investments (Casey & Niblett, 2017; Holden & Malani, 2021) at the expense of practically eliminating ex post adjustment (even when both parties might benefit from it) and requiring very high contract specification costs. This makes strong smart contracts less attractive when the frequency of environmental disturbances is high. Thus, strong smart contracts are particularly attractive for transactions susceptible to the holdup risk—those with a non-negligible level of specific investment—at fairly low frequencies of environmental disturbance. Since transactions susceptible to the holdup risk have previously been governed through hybrids and hierarchies in traditional TCE, strong smart contracts could replace these two modes of governance when environmental uncertainty were low. Moreover, strong smart contracts can also reduce contract enforcement costs by bypassing expensive court systems (Raskin 2016; Savelyev 2017; Sklaroff 2017; Surden 2012; Werbach and Cornell 2017). These costs are only faced by markets and, to a lesser degree, by hybrids. Finally, because strong smart contracts would be less attractive for transactions where the specification of contractual terms is difficult, the digital codifiability of transaction terms would moderate both of the above effects. Figure 1 summarizes three key propositions capturing these arguments.

To sum up, we argue that strong smart contracts will likely lead to institutional change in the predominance of transaction governance modes. They are likely to displace (at different degrees) market, hybrid, and firm governance for transactions with high levels of digital codifiability at low levels of environmental uncertainty and non-negligible levels of asset specificity.

## An Illustrative Example: The Fresh Produce Supply Chain

To illustrate some of our theoretical analysis, we present an illustrative example focusing on supply chain management since this area is often discussed as being ripe for institutional transformation due to the emergence of smart contracts (Schmidt & Wagner, 2019). Food industry giants like Dole often own farms, which allows them to gain a market premium attached to specific aspects of agricultural product quality (e.g., aesthetics and freshness) and/or production processes (e.g., sustainability and fair trade) (Karst, 2019). Dole exercises control over farming processes through managerial hierarchies to ensure that various customer value parameters are met. If farmers were to operate as independent vendors, they may be forced to make a specific investment into some aspects of production for which only a particular buyer (e.g., Dole) is willing to pay a premium. If Dole were to renege on a contract (e.g., by purchasing less than the initially requested quantity), these farmers would lose part of their investment. In other words, independent farmers would face a high holdup risk due to a high degree of asset specificity and some degree of uncertainty. Finding itself unable to solve this problem, Dole opts to own its own farms.

The advent of digitization of transaction terms could help Dole’s hierarchical management reduce costs associated with owning and operating remote farms. To ensure that the required quality and process benchmarks are met, Dole could digitize its farms to control farming practices while reducing managerial oversight. For example, digital sensors could be used to ensure that certain sustainability practices are being followed—e.g., they could be used to assess the chemistry of the fertilizers a farmer is using. If the farm were found to be not compliant, the farm’s employees could be reprimanded or even fired. Such internal transaction automation would reduce the cost of running Dole’s managerial hierarchy. Moreover, if sensors could enable cheaper contract specification and the monitoring and legal costs were not prohibitive, smart contracts could allow Dole to move from ownership to hybrid contracting with independent farmers. Such digitized hybrids could rely on relationship management hierarchies and digitized adjustable contracts to allow Dole to adapt easily to its environment (e.g., respond to new environmental regulations).

Introducing strong smart contracts into this situation could displace the digitized adjustable contracts used to support the hybrid relationships described above. If the digitization of contractual terms were precise enough to represent each party’s requirements and the environmental uncertainty is low, strong smart contracts could replace hybrids by eliminating the bureaucratic overhead costs without increasing the holdup risk. For example, if a buyer like Dole were interested in ensuring sustainability, they could digitally specify what sustainability practices they expect farmers to follow. If a farmer were to follow these guidelines and deliver their products as specified, Dole’s payment would be guaranteed. A transaction governed by a strong smart contract must be fully digitally codifiable. First, each party should be able to precisely encode their expectations into contractual terms (e.g., the exact compounds allowed in fertilizers and the exact payment release schedule). Second, the parties must agree on which kind of digital sensor and information inflows they will rely upon (e.g., certain sensors in the soil, ways of sharing sensor inputs, and a protocol guaranteeing payment release). Third, due to its automatic enforcement, it is crucial for the contract to account for all future states, including rare events (e.g., a sensor malfunction) and highly unlikely events (e.g., a global pandemic). If everything works as intended, strong smart contracts would enable farmers to avoid the holdup problem and sign contracts with Dole at higher levels of asset specificity than before (prior to strong smart contracts).

When information inflows yield incomplete or unreliable inputs, parties involved in setting up a strong smart contract need to specify default actions for automatic enforcement. Such actions, however, can become very costly if a situation that arises ex post was poorly predicted. For example, default terms might state that “if a farmer significantly delays a shipment, their contract will never be renewed again.” Such terms might not benefit Dole or the farmer if a shipment were delayed due to a government-imposed lockdown (e.g., in the case of a pandemic). To avoid such situations, it is critical to invest in carefully specifying actions in all potential future states of the world. Thus, specifying strong smart contracts will be costlier than specifying digitally adjustable market and hybrid contracts or automated transactions within a hierarchy. The other three governance modes do not need to account for all potential future states of the world, as humans can adjust them if needed.

A full specification is much easier when environmental uncertainty is not very high. Dole’s extra costs associated with writing a strong smart contract by fully specifying actions pertaining to all potential future states may be worth Dole’s and its supplier’s investment in a relatively stable environment. Such investment would be recouped through the savings associated with eliminating all bureaucratic oversight as well as through the lower prices that the independent farmers would be willing to offer if the risk of Dole reneging were eliminated. In an unstable environment, however, the costs of specifying actions for all potential future states of the world may become inordinate (e.g., creating sensors for all potential climate conditions, finding authoritative long-term digital sources for all types of geopolitical disasters, etc.) and would likely exceed the benefits. Moreover, in an unstable environment, it may be impossible to specify all contingencies a priori, and the costs of giving up ex post adjustments of any kind may never be recouped.

In summary, this hypothetical example illustrates the potential shift in the preferred mode of transaction governance in an institutional environment where strong smart contracts and complementary investments in digital sensors would be available to support this new transaction governance mode. When the environment is relatively stable, strong smart contracts can replace hierarchical or hybrid governance for some transactions. However, the downsides of removing the possibility of ex post adjustment may become too costly if the environment is very turbulent. Even if one could program the possibility of a global pandemic into a strong smart contract, specifying proper automatic actions for all the different contingencies (e.g., pandemic, earthquake, or a government regime change) would likely be too expensive, and not specifying them properly would likely be too inefficient.

## A Blockchain System for Supporting Smart Contracts

The process of digitizing contractual terms in the last several decades has been supported by the proliferation and institutionalization of shared technical infrastructures for contracting (Constantinides et al., 2018), including shared standards, hardware, and software such as EDI (Damsgaard & Truex, 2000), cloud computing (Makhlouf, 2020), and digital platform markets (Parker et al. 2016). For strong smart contracts, blockchain technologies are seen as enablers of this infrastructure (Raskin 2016; Sklaroff 2017; Holden and Malani 2021).

Indeed since blockchain technologies have entered the mainstream conversation, they have been discussed as key enablers of an institutional shift in firm boundaries that has disrupted existing institutions, such as hierarchies and governments (Ahluwalia et al., 2020; Beck et al., 2018; Lumineau et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2019; Schmidt & Wagner, 2019). However, not all blockchain designs aim to provide the properties required for supporting the strong smart contract concept. For example, permissioned blockchains—blockchains with agents who have special rights to control information written on the ledger—can be used to alter smart contract terms ex post (Halaburda, 2018; Treiblmaier, 2018). On the other hand, permissionless blockchains—where anyone can participate in the system to validate transactions—are designed to guarantee that strong smart contracts are automatically executed and are prohibitively expensive (impractical) to reverse ex post (Holden & Malani, 2021; Mik, 2017; Raskin, 2016; Sklaroff, 2017). Because the protocol in such systems prohibits any modifications after smart contracts have been written into the ledger, no one party, or even a court of law, has permission to overwrite prior records (Holden & Malani,

2021; Mik, 2017; Raskin, 2016; Sklaroff, 2017). Even if contracts executed on permissionless blockchains violate existing laws (e.g., terrorist financing) or fail to represent each party’s intentions (e.g., Ponzi schemes), public courts are rarely in a position to intervene, especially because the agents involved in these contracts are often anonymous (Davilla, 2022; Sklaroff, 2017).

The most popular permissionless blockchain with smart contract capability, Ethereum, promises to “transform law, finance, and civil society” (Epstein, 2015) by providing a Turing-complete programming language for specifying contractual terms and accessible infrastructure for automatically executing a variety of strong smart contracts. Turing-complete programming language means that “any function” can be encoded (Hütten, 2019). While permissionless blockchain systems are designed to support strong smart contracts, they do not fully guarantee them in practice. For example, if smart contracts reside on a permissionless blockchain system that enables the formation of powerful coalitions, such platforms may still result in a de facto reversal of smart contracts ex post. This was the case with the infamous “DAO” event on Ethereum (DuPont, 2017).<sup>2</sup> In this case, a malicious agent took advantage of the coding error in a smart contract and stole about \$50 million worth of tokens (about a third of the value of the DAO). While the smart contract technically permitted the action, it did not represent the intentions of all contractual parties. To counteract this theft, Ethereum’s leaders orchestrated a coordinated collective action (“rearrangement”) that effectively reversed the theft by forking (splitting) the system. Thus, the smart contracts were de facto reversed.

Finally, another issue arises from the fact that smart contracts refer to agreements needing future execution and enforcement. Automatic execution and enforcement promises can only be fulfilled if the hosting infrastructure is expected to operate continuously until the agreement is executed. For example, if a strong smart contract is written and needs to be executed and enforced decades later—will Ethereum still be around? If the long-term existence of the smart contract infrastructure is not ensured or changes frequently (e.g., forking in blockchain projects), the appeal of strong smart contracts may be limited to short-term agreements.

To conclude, while permissionless blockchain systems offer a practical solution for supporting the legal concept of a strong smart contract, they do not guarantee that all the properties of such contracts will be implemented in practice.

Nevertheless, the wider adoption of such systems and their acceptance as part of the legal “contract law regime” will increasingly make them an institutional reality rather than a theoretical concept.

## Complementary Institutional Conditions

For a new technology-enabled practice to be diffused widely in society, expectations about this practice need to become institutionalized (Attewell, 1992; DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). If practices supporting a strong smart contract concept were to become institutionalized, agents who write such contracts would start taking it for granted that such contracts are nonreversible, paving the way for changes in preferred transaction governance modes.

At the time of writing, many attempts to implement strong smart contracts on permissionless blockchain systems have failed to ensure the unique economic characteristics of this concept (Halaburda, 2018; Treiblmaier, 2018). Moreover, courts currently have no legal obligation to treat such contracts as nonreversible (Raskin 2017). These failures to implement the concept thus far have given rise to confusion and impeded institutionalization processes (Murray et al., 2021; Sillaber et al., 2021). Still, we can discuss what kinds of future technological and social conditions may facilitate the institutionalization of strong smart contracts.

First, the widespread adoption of reliable permissionless blockchain systems supporting strong smart contracts could increase their utilization. IS literature shows how key industry players like SAP and Oracle played a critical role in the diffusion and eventual institutionalization of enterprise resource planning (ERP) and supply chain management systems (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). Similarly, the U.S. government and military played a critical role in the diffusion of technical standards (e.g., Argyres, 1999). These investments promoted the diffusion of digitized contracts by overcoming the adoption inertia associated with the network effects and the need for coordination among users (Katz & Shapiro, 1994).

For blockchain systems, Ethereum and new players like Solana (Pierro & Tonelli, 2022) have invested in open digital infrastructures supporting the implementation of a variety of strong smart contracts. Moreover, platforms are also emerging that support special-purpose strong smart contracts. Contemporary examples include platforms for NFT trades (e.g., Flow), for users sharing computer storage (e.g.,

Filecoin), and for paying musicians royalties when their music is played (e.g., Audius) (Benet & Greco, 2018; Rumburg et al., 2019; Zaucha & Agur, 2022). Overall, the developers of blockchain systems could reduce this inertia through topdown coordination, common standards, and subsidies for early adopters (Hsieh & Vergne, 2022).

Once a reliable standard infrastructure for contracting that guarantees automatic execution and non-reversibility is available, we expect to see a shift in preferred governance modes. An early example of this can be seen in finance. Traditionally, big banks have formed hybrid relationships with stock exchanges to provide liquidity. A decentralized finance (DeFi) application called Uniswap (see Table 6) is replacing these hybrid relationships with strong smart contracts that allow small-scale token holders to provide trading liquidity (Aramonte et al., 2021).

Second, another institutional change that needs to take place to facilitate the adoption of strong smart contracts is a further increase in the digital codification of transactions inside and outside the firm. The IS literature has shown how the increased digitization of transactions inside the firm could lead the firm to convert some of its customized transactions to more standard ones (Tafti et al. 2013). This could occur because when local tacit knowledge is codified explicitly, organizational members realize that local demands are not so specific and can be met with standard vendor offerings (e.g., Levina & Ross, 2003; Vaast & Levina, 2006). Accordingly, internal codification of transactions can enable the “move to the middle” (Clemons et al., 1993).

Regarding strong smart contracts, the increased codification of transactions can be aided by digital infrastructures feeding into strong smart contract platforms. For example, AXA Insurance created flight delay insurance enabled by a strong smart contract (see Table 6). The wide availability of data from airline and airport information systems enables “full” digital codification of flight delay events. Moreover, digital consumer banking systems allow for immediate payment if a customer experiences an insurable delay. Further digital codification of other insurable events (e.g., luggage delays) could increase the adoption of such products, replacing market transactions with a high holdup risk (insurance refusing to pay even when justified).

Similarly, the expanded use of special-purpose digital platforms could speed up the standardization of transaction terms. For example, the proliferation of contractual work in online labor markets has facilitated standard template creation (Popiel, 2017). In this regard, the growth of platforms supporting strong smart contracts would ease their adoption by providing standardized strong smart contract templates (Oranburg & Palagashvili, 2018; Sillaber et al., 2021; Tönnissen & Teuteberg, 2018; Werbach & Cornell, 2017). Such templates are already available on platforms facilitating the use of strong smart contracts, such as OpenSea (Halaburda et al., 2022; OpenSea, n.d.). OpenSea connects buyers with creators and resellers of nonfungible tokens (NFTs). NFTs are created as strong smart contracts. While creating one from scratch is relatively difficult and error prone, OpenSea users can create NFTs as strong smart contracts on Ethereum through easy standard interfaces.

Moreover, the integration of digital sensor data with strong smart contracts could further increase the codifiability of terms, as in the example of DDrive—a peer-to-peer smart contract-based car-sharing system that ensures that car lease conditions (e.g., maintenance) are fulfilled during the lease term (see Table 6). The current attempts to implement such systems are based on a centrally owned IoT infrastructure (e.g., a corporate vehicle fleet), leading to limited scalability (Auer et al., 2022). The increased traceability of each party’s action (e.g., lessor’s vehicle accident history and lessee’s maintenance actions) through the widespread adoption of an open sensor infrastructure could aid in the move from firm governance (e.g., corporate ownership of vehicles) to peer-topeer strong smart contract governance (e.g., consumer-toconsumer lending).

Third, improving the prediction of the future state of the world could be an enabling condition for increasing the adoption of strong smart contracts because the decision to use strong smart contracts is highly influenced by environmental uncertainty. As transaction terms become digitized, data analytics and new types of artificial intelligence (e.g., generative AI) could improve predictions of the future, thereby reducing environmental uncertainty. The improved statistical analysis capabilities associated with the growth of computing power and advancements in the science of prediction could enable such an impact. For example, for peer-to-peer car leasing, tracing real-time information about vehicles such as battery levels or mileage could enable machine learning algorithms to identify mileage fraud based on the analysis of prior records (Ma, 2020). Similarly, blockchain-based ad platforms could counteract ad fraud while guaranteeing immediate payment for true impressions (Cui et al., 2021). Such systems can already be programmed to offer different pricing for expected changes in business volume (e.g., a higher number of clicks during a Super Bowl TV ad campaign), but they are not yet able to deal with rare events such as wars and pandemics. As more potential future states of the world become digitally codifiable, machine learning could be used to predict what actions should be encoded in strong smart contracts for each such event.

Lastly, providing strong legal protections for non-reversibility is a critical condition needed to further the adoption of strong smart contracts. If an innovative technology is expected to bring significant benefits to the public, governments could play a role in creating a favorable environment for using such technology. For this, governments could take on a critical role in delineating the legal power of strong smart contracts. To take advantage of their unique characteristics, governments could endow strong smart contracts with legal authority so that their enforcement would not be challenged in courts ex post (Werbach, 2018). Governments could go even further by “outlawing” the reversal of strong smart contracts by either party, even if it is technically feasible. For example, if a permissioned blockchain system claims to support the writing of strong smart contracts, laws could mandate that such contracts be treated as non-reversible by both parties as well as the courts.

This idea of creating legal support for strong smart contracts is similar to the current legal status of what is known as “Ricardian contracts.” Ricardian contracts refer to registering legally valid documents, including electronic signatures, in a machinereadable format (Grigg, 2004). Ricardian contracts state the contracting parties’ intentions and related actions in a humanand machine-readable format but, unlike smart contracts, they do not require automatic execution. Smart contracts are an extension of Ricardian contracts. While the weak and strong smart contract legal framework has yet to be fully developed, current legislative frameworks have endorsed Ricardian contracts as legally binding (Wright & De Filippi, 2018, p. 74).

## Contributions

Our paper follows an IS research tradition that promotes TCE as an insightful tool for theorizing how technology-enabled change reshapes the institutional environment for transaction governance (Malone et al., 1987). Blockchain technology is a key recent example of digital technology reshaping the institutional environment of transaction governance, making TCE a particularly fruitful theory for theorizing its societal impacts.

We contribute to the IS literature by developing the concept of the digitization of transaction terms shift parameter, which integrates three seminal IS papers on institutional changes in transaction governance modes (Clemons & Row, 1992; Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Malone et al., 1987). While these papers have made significant contributions to the IS and organizational scholarship, they tend to analyze factors shaping transaction governance all at the same time, making it hard to distinguish complementary versus conflicting effects. We identified how digitization effects related to transaction terms highlighted in each of the seminal papers—namely, digitization of transaction terms (Malone et al. 1987), automatic transaction monitoring (Gurbaxani & Whang 1991), and standard digital infrastructure for contracting—impacted each economic force within TCE (Table 3). We then used our analysis to disentangle the impact of smart contracts from the impact of other aspects of digitization. Providing this analytical clarity should help researchers understand how future digital innovations can reshape transaction governance. It also contributes to the TCE literature by opening up the black box of the shift parameter concept (Cuypers et al. 2021)

The key contribution of our work lies in our argument that the legal construct of strong smart contracts constitutes a new transaction governance mode distinct from the three traditional TCE modes in that none of these modes eliminates any possibility of ex post adjustments. We argue that the automated execution and non-reversibility of strong smart contracts create a new set of trade-offs between key TCE parameters: while they incur high ex ante contract specification costs and eliminate the possibility of ex post adjustment, even when desirable by both parties, this leads to the elimination of the holdup risk and ex post contract execution costs. Based on these trade-offs, strong smart contracts are likely to displace market, hybrid, and firm governance for transactions with high levels of digital codifiability at low (but non-zero) levels of environmental uncertainty and non-negligible levels of asset specificity.

Our work also contributes to the emergent literature on blockchain technologies (Rossi et al., 2019). Not surprisingly, TCE theory has been evoked in several theoretical publications discussing the impact of blockchains. These papers generally propose that blockchain technologies lower transaction costs in markets, making contracts more attractive than firm governance (e.g., Ahluwalia et al., 2020; Schmidt & Wagner, 2019). Unfortunately, these papers do not consider the prior IS literature (reviewed in Table 3), which documented how the digitization of transaction terms had already enabled much of this shift long before the rise of blockchains. By separating the effects of strong smart contracts from the effect of digitizing transactions (including weak smart contracts), we can moderate “grand” claims (Ahluwalia et al., 2020; Schmidt & Wagner, 2019) and outline specific conditions under which strong smart contracts can replace other transaction governance modes.

Our work also contributes by differentiating the legal concepts of weak versus strong smart contracts from their sociotechnical implementation through blockchain systems. Prior literature (Ahluwalia et al., 2020; Felin & Lakhani, 2018; Murray et al., 2021; Schmidt & Wagner, 2019) has often analyzed the impact of blockchain technologies on transaction governance without separating the impact of (weak or strong) smart contracts from other ways of using blockchain technologies for improving efficiencies. For example, non-reversible distributed ledger technologies may improve electronic markets’ functioning, but this impact would benefit any digitized adjustable contract (Halaburda et al., 2022). By clarifying these differences between theoretical concepts and the technological platforms on which they are currently implemented, in contrast to prior arguments (e.g., Lumineau et al. 2021), we show that blockchain technology by itself may not reshape transaction governance. At the very least, it needs to be aided by law regime changes honoring the non-reversibility of strong smart contracts. Moreover, our theoretical concept allows for multiple sociotechnical implementations. This is because not just technology but also societal institutions shape the process of institutionalization for any new form of transaction governance.

Finally, we analyzed complementary institutional changes that could increase the scope of impact of strong smart contracts in the future. There are many factors that limit the use of strong smart contracts today—especially those related to the limitations of today’s blockchain systems (Du et al., 2019). Our work delves into four specific types of institutional conditions that can influence this situation. We analyzed several current examples of attempts to use smart contract-based solutions to change dominant modes of transaction governance. Based on this analysis, we argue that the impact of this new governance mode can increase with (1) a wider adoption of reliable strong smart contract infrastructures, (2) the increased digital codifiability of transaction terms, (3) the improved predictability of future states of the world, and (4) legal protections for the non-reversibility of strong smart contracts.

## Opportunities for Future Research

Next, we suggest several future research opportunities focused on empirically testing and theoretically extending the ideas we propose.

## Opportunities for Empirical Research

An important aspect of testing TCE-based propositions is that as an institutional theory, it requires institutional-level data. Given that our theory concerns institutional shifts over time, the ideal data for testing our theory is long-term longitudinal data or cross-sectional data collected from diverse contexts, such as different countries, regions, or industries. For example, Malone et al.’s (1987) predictions about institutional shifts in governance modes were tested on longitudinal data covering multiple industries over two decades from 1971 to 1990 (Brynjolfsson et al., 1994). We expect that our predictions about the shifts associated with the institutionalization of strong smart contracts will also take a long time to be reflected in data and will not be uniform across diverse institutional conditions. For example, strong smart contracts may take off faster in cases where supply chain relationships have already been digitized for a long time. Similarly, we would expect a higher proliferation of strong smart contracts in environments with poor reputational effects (e.g., for spot transactions with unknown partners).

We also recognize the role of qualitative research in extending and enhancing our theoretical analysis on an organizational level. For example, many empirical papers supporting the “move to the middle” hypothesis (Clemons et al. 1993) are based on qualitative case studies. This work often focuses on explaining why managers favor hybrids over firms and markets in such diverse contexts as strategic military contracting (e.g., Argyres, 1999), offshore outsourcing (e.g., Aron & Singh, 2004; Levina & Su, 2008), and fresh produce trade (e.g., Dolan & Humphrey, 2004). Qualitative studies have also analyzed when switching from firms to any sort of contracting engenders too much risk (Lacity & Willcocks, 1998). In trying to explain complex organizational phenomena, qualitative research tends to discuss TCE-based factors together with knowledge-based factors such as the advantages of hybrid contracting for growing supplier capabilities (e.g., Aron & Singh, 2004; Levina & Su, 2008), making it hard to draw conclusions for TCE theory alone. Still, qualitative studies of organizations can provide valuable insights into the relevant microlevel processes and may reveal important factors missed by theoretical analysis. Some qualitative research on smart contracts is already underway including qualitative studies of e-government platforms for smart contracts (Benbunan-Fich & Castellanos, 2018) and factors guiding the adoption of blockchain systems by organizational decision makers (Du et al. 2019).

Finally, there is a need for more design science research exploring different design mechanisms for applying a strong smart contract concept to diverse business problems. Indeed, there are already a number of studies focused on designing smart contract-based platforms for trading surplus electricity (Thomas et al., 2019), sharing vehicles (Auer et al. 2022), and purchasing used cars (Preikschat et al., 2021). Our work could enrich design science research by suggesting how various design choices may impact TCE-based predictions. For example, researchers could explore trade-offs associated with using platforms with and without permanent identities. Writing contracts on platforms without permanent identities precludes relying on reputation effects but also reduces the costs of joining such markets for newcomers. Such platforms would be particularly attractive for strong smart contract markets but not for platforms supporting hybrid and market contracts. Because reputation effects are only valuable for safeguarding hybrids and market transactions, agents seeking to improve their reputations should join platforms (including weak smart contract platforms) supporting permanent identities.

## Future Theoretical Extensions

While TCE provides useful insights into the institutional impacts of a new general purpose technology that promises improvements in contract enforcement efficiency, TCE alone does not provide a full answer to this question (Rossi et al., 2019; Treiblmaier, 2018). More generally, TCE theory encountered a great degree of criticism for being entirely focused on transaction cost economizing and ignoring resource-based and other considerations (Carter & Hodgson, 2006; Lacity et al., 2010, 2011). Future theoretical research should consider how the resource-based view (RBV) (Barney, 2001) of the firm may play a role in the institutionalization of strong smart contracts. Similarly, several papers reviewed here invoke information processing theory (Galbraith, 1974) when discussing the impact of digitized contracts on transaction governance (Argyres, 1999; Malone et al., 1987; Murray et al., 2021). Finally, even within IS economics, researchers have pointed out that diverse contract theories produce different predictions in certain circumstances. For example, in the case of software-as-a-service adoption, TCE, incomplete contracts, and property rights theory result in somewhat different predictions (Xin & Levina, 2008). Future studies could compare and even integrate propositions derived on the basis of these diverse theories (e.g., Argyres & Zenger, 2012; Tiwana & Bush, 2007) and test the strength of diverse propositions (e.g., Hart & Saunders, 1997; Rai et al., 2015).

Lastly, an institutional economics theory focused on commonpool resources (Ostrom, 1990) might play a complementary role in explaining the adoption of strong smart contracts. This theory is often invoked in relation to blockchain technologies because it theorizes institutional regimes in which external parties (e.g., courts) respect community self-governance claims (Davidson et al., 2018; Rozas et al., 2021). This theory could be used to extend and challenge our analysis, especially regarding not-for-profit settings.

## Conclusion

Our paper draws on TCE and legal scholarship to analyze the concept of strong smart contracts. We carefully separated the effects of the digitization of transaction terms, in general, as a shift parameter that has characterized our environment over the past 50 years, from the relatively recent effects of strong smart contracts—a concept largely associated with the rise of blockchain systems. We then focused on major economic forces shaping the predominance of transaction governance modes and drew on seminal IS literature to analyze how different levels of digitization of transaction terms have impacted each force. Based on the analysis of economic forces at play, we argued that strong smart contracts constitute a new mode of transaction governance because no other mode fully precludes ex post adjustments. We proposed that strong smart contracts are likely to prevail over other modes when transaction terms are fully codifiable, asset specificity levels are non-negligible, and environmental uncertainty is low. As decision makers navigate this transformation, a careful consideration of the trade-offs between ex ante specification costs and the loss of ex post flexibility will become crucial. Our work shows that a nuanced understanding of institutional conditions can provide valuable insights into the transformations in transactional governance in the digital era.

## Author Biographies

Hanna Halaburda received her Ph.D in economics from Northwestern University and is an associate professor at New York University Stern School of Business. Her main research interest centers on how technology changes economic forces. Currently, her research explores blockchain technologies and digital platforms. She has served in a number of editorial positions at Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Economics and Management Strategy, ACM Transactions on Economics and Computation, and Review of Network Economics.

Natalia Levina received her Ph.D. in information technology from MIT’s Sloan School of Management and is a Paganelli-Bull Professor at the New York University Stern School of Business. She is also a Distinguished Research Environment Professor at the Warwick Business School. Her main research interest centers on how people span organizational, professional, cultural, and other boundaries while developing and using new technologies. Currently, her research explores the adoption of AI in organizations, open innovation practices, platform governance, and blockchain technologies, as well as the epistemic underpinnings of innovation practices. She has served in a number of editorial positions at Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, and Information & Organization. She is a Fellow of the AIS and co-founded and chaired AIS Special Interest Group (SIG) on Grounded Theory Method.

Semi Min received her Ph.D. from the New York University Stern School of Business and is currently an assistant professor at the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. Her main research interests are in understanding the adoption of emerging technologies in society and how discourse plays a role in these processes. Currently, her research explores theories of smart contracts and the adoption of blockchain and contact-tracing technologies using both qualitative and quantitative methodological approaches
