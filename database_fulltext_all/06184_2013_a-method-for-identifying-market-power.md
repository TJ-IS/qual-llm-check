## a b s t r a c t

An ability to exercise market power by suppliers may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in restructured electricity markets. Many studies have been performed to develop an effective tool to identify market power based on indices. Most often it is rather dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use an index-based method for real-time analysis. This paper provides a method to identify market power that can be used in real time. The purpose is to analyze price-dispatch sensitivity to determine whether there is a generator or a subset of generators that have the ability to increase its own price pro<sup>fi</sup>tably. Case studies performed on a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed IEEE 118-bus system are presented.

## 1. Introduction

A key requirement for a market to be competitive is the concept of substitutability for a good or service. That is, if two goods are substitutable the demand for one of them is increased when the price of the other good is increased and vice-versa. Therefore, like goods are differentiated solely on the basis of price. Recently, much of the U.S. electric power system has been deregulated in the hope that the creation of a competitive wholesale market for generation would improve the economic ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the system. In a world of perfect competition the premise is that the electric power generated by any generator or subset of generators can be substituted for that of others. However, because of the existence of transmission systems needed to deliver the electricity, it is possible that a subset of generators located at a particular site can have very low substitutability with generators at other sites. In these cases we say that market power exists; i.e., such generators have an ability to increase the price they charge for their electricity without fear of replacement because they have no substitutes.

In economics, market power exists when one or more of the participants have an ability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence price [2]. In certain electricity power markets, market power is deemed to be present when a generator or subset of generators has the ability to raise the price they are paid above the competitive level with impunity [14]. If generators that have market power choose to exercise it, market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency will decrease under the situation of price-elastic demands or productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency losses. Several methods have been proposed to monitor market power for the purpose of developing procedures to mitigate or eliminate the effects [1,5,8–10,12,13,15]. The most widely used methods are based on indices such as the Hirschman–Her<sup>fi</sup>ndahl Index (HHI) [4] or the Lerner's Index (LI) [7]. These indices are not suitable for real-time market monitoring because they require ex-post data for evaluation [5]. The interconnected transmission network plays an important role in creating market power and that market power comes and goes in real time as operational conditions change. So real-time evaluation is important otherwise procedures will be developed that can penalize generators who have market power rarely and choose not to exercise it.

When price is perturbed, the dispatch and the revenue of the participating generators change accordingly. The perturbations satisfy the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions [6]. The matrix of dispatch-price sensitivities, denoted by M, relates how incremental changes in prices, Δ will result in changes in dispatch Δg, i.e.,

$$
\Delta g = M \Delta \rho\tag{1}
$$

where g is the vector of optimal generator real power injections (MW); ρ is the locational marginal price (LMP) vector; and Δρ and Δg represent the changes in price and generation dispatch respectively. Since generator revenue is equal to the product of price and dispatch, the matrix of revenue-price sensitivities, denoted by N, is simply related to M by

$$
\Delta r = \left[ D _ {\rho} M + D _ {\mathrm{g}} \right] \Delta \rho = N \Delta \rho\tag{2}
$$

where r is the vector of generator revenues, and $D _ { \mathrm { { \rho } } }$ and $D _ { \mathrm { g } }$ represent diagonal matrices of generator nodal prices and generator dispatches respectively.

It is important to be clear about what we mean by dispatch-price sensitivity. In electricity markets, generator offers are typically submitted in discrete blocks, and a uniform clearing price will often differ from the block offer price at the generator location. The generator block offer curve is essentially a discontinuous marginal costs curve that is not amenable to direct sensitivity analysis. To obtain a meaningful incremental model, we substitute the generator nodal clearing price or LMP for the generator offer price. This is sensible in our incremental model because we want to know the effect on dispatch when a generator incrementally increases its offer over the LMP. By construction, an OPF run with these substituted offers will give the same result as the original OPF. By starting with the solution of an optimal power <sup>fl</sup>ow and replacing the generator offers with LMPs we essentially make all the generators marginal units, and then calculate the sensitivity of the dispatch to changes in price.

Under the assumption that generators all offer their prevailing LMP, our goal is to identify the possibility that some generators have an ability to raise price pro<sup>fi</sup>tably. By pro<sup>fi</sup>tability we mean an ability to raise their own price by raising their offer without disturbing the dispatch. Since revenue is dispatch times price, a generator with this attribute can, if it chooses to do so, raise its own revenue without affecting the dispatch of others participating in the market. It is noteworthy that even though only one generator needs to take an action (change its offer), the prices at all buses can change (as a result of the offer change). Consider, for instance, a lossless three-bus system with three lines of in<sup>fi</sup>nite capacities. Suppose that each bus has a single generating <sup>fi</sup>rm, and their offers are 100 MW at \$1/MWh, 100 MW at \$2/MWh, and 100 MW at \$3/MWh; and that the system load is 250MW so that all of the generators need to be dispatched. The LMP at each bus is then \$3/MWh. If the marginal generator raises its offer above \$3/MWh, the LMP will increase at every bus even though the dispatch does not change. This involves the exercise of market power with only a single generator (single pivotal bidder), but the prices at all the buses are changed.

A mathematical derivation of the dispatch sensitivity matrix M and the revenue sensitivity matrix N with respect to ρ is presented in ref. [10]. The revenue sensitivity matrix N plays a critical role in assessing market power [9,10]. However, due to the limited availability of information such as offer data, the approach is rather dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for market participants to apply in a real-time market. However, it is possible to estimate M with high precision using publicly available data [11].

Besides the issue of data availability for the sensitivity-based approach, there exists the issue of computation complexity [9]. A complete search can be performed to assess market power using the N matrix. However, a complete search is not practical for a large system because it is a combinatorial search and its computation cost, measured in terms of computer cycles, increases exponentially with the size of the system. Recently, a heuristic method called a clustering algorithm [9] has been suggested as a screening tool. However, while a useful screening tool, the performance of the heuristic depends on, for example, the choice of threshold and path.

## 2. Market power

## 2.1. Two types of potential market power

The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of market power that we use for an electricity power market is different from that in economics [2]. Market power in electricity markets, according to FERC, is the ability to increase price pro<sup>fi</sup>tably above the competitive level. Market power as generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned by economists is present when a subset of <sup>fi</sup>rms that are participating in a market have the ability to alter either the total quantity or the prevailing price of a good or service [2]. In this study, two different types of potential market power are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows: Market power of Type 1 occurs when a generator or subset of generators have the property that changing the price will not affect the system dispatch; and Type 2 market power occurs when a generator or subset of generators have the ability to alter the dispatch of other generators and can do so in a way that increases their own revenue. Note that in the case of Type 1 market power, since the dispatch does not change, the systems ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (measured in terms of losses) will not change although economic ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (measured in terms of deviation from competitive pricing) will change if this market power is exercised. Price volatility, however, can occur for Type 1 market power because <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives exist to increase pro<sup>fi</sup>ts by raising price. For Type 2 market power the situation becomes more complicated. If a generator with this type of market power increases its revenue by reducing price and increasing its own dispatch, market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency may improve. Note that this analysis focuses on assessing or evaluating market power. Whether or not market participants exercise this market power when they have it is not germane to this discussion. However, electricity market experiments using human subjects have shown that market participants that have market power will eventually exercise it, and once exercised, market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is reduced [10].

In general, because the amount of real power dispatched depends on the offers of all generators in the market, it is not possible to assess market power without considering all generators' prices. However, generator pricing affects dispatch to different degrees. Therefore, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of market power involves <sup>fi</sup>nding groups of generators in terms of their effect on dispatch. In ref. [11], Oh and Thomas provide a derivation of the dispatch to price sensitivity matrix, M. They discuss the impact on dispatch and revenue near the original operation point. Note that the screening tool proposed here might not work well for identifying the type of market power (i.e., Type 1 or Type 2) due to oligopolistic market concentration (e.g., the oligopoly models in refs. [1,4,7]) because the tools discussed here addresses incremental changes in price relative to dispatch.

## 2.2. Characterizing Type 1 market power

In general, the sensitivity of a change in dispatch is given by Eq. (1). In the case of Type 1 market power, the group of Type 1 generator dispatch changes is characterized as follows:

$$
\Delta g = M \Delta \rho = 0\tag{3}
$$

That is, the dispatch, g, will not change no matter the change in price, $\Delta \rho$ for those vectors $\Delta \rho$ satisfying Eq. (3). Mathematically these types of price changes lie in the null space of matrix M. So, to characterize this type of market power we need to compute M and its null space. It turns out that we can <sup>fi</sup>nd the null space of M without computing M directly [11]. To do this let matrix E be a sub matrix of the power transfer distribution factor (PTDF) matrix composed of; 1) the collection of row vectors corresponding to the congested lines in the transmission system; and 2) the collection of the column vectors corresponding to the buses that generators are connected to. The product of the change in dispatch, Δg, and matrix $E ^ { T }$ represents the changes in <sup>fl</sup>ow that will occur over the congested lines. But lines congested mean they are at the limit of their <sup>fl</sup>ow capability. Therefore any change in <sup>fl</sup>ow on a congested line under price perturbation will be zero.<sup>1</sup> Because the changes in <sup>fl</sup>ow are zero over the congested lines for a marginal change in dispatch, matrix $E ^ { T }$ also lies in the null space of M. Let V be the matrix containing $\rho$ and the $E ^ { T }$ matrix; i.e., $\hat { V } = \left[ \rho \quad E ^ { T } \right]$ . Because the number of pivotal generators is $m + 1$ where m is the number of congested lines [11], V fully spans the null space of M. Note that V spans the null space of M but its basis is not orthonormal. An orthonormal basis can be found by performing a QR factorization of V as follows:

$$
V ^ {n \times m + 1} = Q ^ {n \times n} R _ {1} ^ {n \times m + 1} = \left[ Q _ {1} ^ {n \times (m + 1)} \Big | Q _ {2} ^ {n \times (n - m - 1)} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} R ^ {(m + 1) \times (m + 1)} \\ 0 ^ {(n - m - 1) \times (m + 1)} \end{array} \right]\tag{4}
$$

where n is the number of buses; and $Q _ { 1 }$ and $Q _ { 2 }$ partitions contain basis vectors for the range and the null space of $V ,$ respectively. Because V spans the null space of the M matrix, $Q _ { 1 }$ and $Q _ { 2 }$ span the null and the range space of the M matrix, respectively. If a price change is a linear combination of column vectors in the $Q _ { 1 }$ matrix, then the price change will not affect the dispatch (Type 1) because M and $Q _ { 1 }$ are orthogonal; i.e.,

$$
\Delta \rho = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m + 1} c _ {i} q _ {i} = Q _ {1} c\tag{5}
$$

where c is a vector whose elements are scalar coef<sup>fi</sup>cients $c _ { i } ,$ and q is the ith column vector in the $Q _ { 1 }$ matrix. Because an objective of a generator may be to increase its pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, the generator could increase prices if they have the capability to do so without penalty by being dispatched at a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreased quantity. If their price change lies close to the null space of the M matrix then

$$
\Delta g = M \Delta \rho = M (Q _ {1} c + \varepsilon) = M \varepsilon\tag{6}
$$

Note that the product $M Q _ { 1 }$ is zero because the column vectors of $Q _ { 1 }$ are orthogonal.

## 2.3. Origin of Type 1 market power

With a non-congested network, the dimension of the null space of the M matrix is one; i.e., $V = \rho .$ Consequently, the $Q _ { I }$ space has a dimension of one. Therefore, changes in ρ parallel to price do not alter dispatch; i.e., no generator or subset of generators has market power because a change in price requires the participation of all u generators where u is the number of generators. For a noncongested network, the prices of all generators are very close to each other (the only reason they are separated at all is because of the losses of the connecting transmission lines). This is the reason market power in the sense discussed here does not exist if a system is not congested. Another way to think about this is to consider a lossless non-congested transmission system. First, in the lossless case the marginal unit has market power only to the extent it does not exceed the offer of the <sup>fi</sup>rst rejected unit. If there are no rejected units, of course the marginal unit would have market power (as indicated by the dimension of the null space). Second, none of the remaining (u 1) units will have market power since raising their offer will not change their dispatch unless they increase their offer beyond that of the marginal unit. In this case they will run into the <sup>fi</sup>rst rejected unit problem as well. Note that this is consistent with our interpretation as discussed in the three-bus example shown in Introduction, namely, all units are marginal in the lossless case (because all units would have the same nodal price) for the purpose of the sensitivity analysis. Consequently, there is no need for a screening tool in this case.

When the classical Gram–Schmidt method is applied for the QR factorization of $V ,$ the <sup>fi</sup>rst column vector in $Q _ { I }$ is parallel to the price vector that is the <sup>fi</sup>rst column vector in V [3]. The price vector is different from other basis vectors of $Q _ { 1 } ;$ its elements have the same signs since prices are non-negative in general. Furthermore, for a system with m congested lines, there are m+1 marginal units [11]; thus, one can identify $m + 1$ different price groups. Congestion affects the price ρ by the shadow price of the congestion times the sensitivity of the <sup>fl</sup>ow with respect to the bus injections, $E ^ { T } .$ In general, two buses at both sides of the congested line have the largest value for the sensitivities in magnitude with the opposite signs. As a result, the boundaries between groups cut congested lines if groups are formed using Lesieutre et al.'s “clustering” algorithm [9]. Because the price difference within a group is small, there are many non-zero elements in the q vector. Therefore, “load-pocket” [5,8,9] can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed using the clustering algorithm [9] relatively well because the algorithm utilizes the price, $\rho ,$ only. Note that a load-pocket exists only when at least one line is congested. If a subset of generators in a load-pocket possesses market power, an ideal screening tool should detect the group with other vectors in $Q _ { 1 }$ than one parallel to price. Consequently, the non-sparse vector in $Q _ { 1 }$ parallel to $\rho$ is excluded from the examination in this screening.

Assume that a group of generators has market power and belongs to a candidate set from the q vector. If q is almost parallel to a column vector in the $E ^ { T }$ matrix, it is clear that the group has market power due to the congestion of the line corresponding to the column vector. One can quantify how close the vectors $\bar { q }$ and e are “parallel” with each other as follows, i.e., a necessary condition for Type 1 market power:

$$
\theta = \cos^ {- 1} \left(\frac {\| \overline {{q}} \cdot e \|}{\| \overline {{q}} \| \| e \|}\right) = \cos^ {- 1} (\| \overline {{q}} \cdot e \|) \approx 0\tag{12}
$$

where e is the normalized PTDF of the congested line. This implies that the subset of binding constraints is responsible for market power. Once identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, modifying the corresponding line can mitigate market power.

## 3. Simulation results and discussions

To test the method described above as a screening tool, a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed IEEE 118-bus system, shown in Fig. 1, is used. The market is cleared based on the typical uniform price auction [16] using an AC optimal power <sup>fl</sup>ow. The process is to <sup>fi</sup>nd an optimal solution without violating economic, operation, and engineering constraints. After <sup>fi</sup>nding a solution, the true M matrix is calculated by using the method in Ref. [10].

In Area 3 and Area 4, demand is greater than the quantity offered in the area. In most cases, the capacities of the lines are high enough so that no <sup>fl</sup>ow constraints are binding. For some cases, power <sup>fl</sup>ows from Area 1 and Area 2 to the other areas that have multiple congested lines. However, it is not clear who has market power since there are many generators located in the areas.

A complete search can be performed using matrix N for assessing market power. The search needs to check the feasibility of all possible combinations of generators:

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {u} _ {u} C _ {i} = \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {u} _ {u} C _ {i} - 1 = 2 ^ {u} - 1 \Big | _ {u = 5 4} \approx 1. 8 \times 1 0 ^ {1 6}\tag{16}
$$

Note that there are 54 generators in the system. For practical reasons, it is rather dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for many generators to cooperate. As more generators are involved, the number of combinations grows exponentially. In this study, groups with more than three generators are not considered. The feasibility check for a complete search requires:

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {3} u C _ {i} = u + \frac {u (u - 1)}{2 !} + \frac {u (u - 1) (u - 2)}{3 !} = \frac {u ^ {3} + 5 u}{6} = \vartheta \left(u ^ {3}\right)\tag{17}
$$

For u equals 54, the number of combinations is 26,289.

The computational cost increases are on the order of $u ^ { 3 }$ where u is the number of generators. Consequently, a complete search is computationally expensive for a large u. Lesieutre et al.'s clustering algorithm [9] reduces computational expense signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, but its performance depends on the threshold value of the price difference and the path formed through a cluster. The choice of a threshold and path is a design choice and the outcome dependent on them. Small-sized clusters may screen out subsets of generators identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by a complete search, and large clusters may include irrelevant generators. ## 4. Conclusion

This paper shows how congestion plays a critical role in determining which generators, if any, have market power. In this paper we proposed an approach to identifying market power. More importantly, it is possible to identify why a subset of generators may possess market power. By using the algorithm suggested here, one can monitor the type of market power described in real time, and mitigation methods can be designed. The algorithm could also be used as transmission expansion planning tool for expanding transmission to alleviate or eliminate consistent occurrences of market power opportunities. The algorithm developed here requires signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly less computational effort than a complete search without sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing accuracy.
