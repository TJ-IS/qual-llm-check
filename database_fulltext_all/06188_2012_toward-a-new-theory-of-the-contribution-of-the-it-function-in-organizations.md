# TOWARD A NEW THEORY OF THE CONTRIBUTION OF THE IT FUNCTION IN ORGANIZATIONS $^{1}$

Manon G. Guillemette
Faculty of Management, Université de Sherbrooke, 2500 University Boulevard,
Sherbrooke, Quebec CANADA J1K 2R1 {manon.guillemette@usherbrooke.ca}

Guy Paré
HEC Montréal, 3000 Côte-Ste-Catherine Road,
Montréal, Quebec CANADA H3T 2A7 {guy.pare@hec.ca}

Because changes in organizations and information technology environments are enduring, the alignment of the IT function with business objectives must not only be understood, but constantly renewed and adjusted. This is amply reflected in recent surveys of CIOs, which consistently suggest that the notion of alignment is a top challenge and management priority. Many CIOs face a double challenge when addressing the issue of alignment: they must first clarify top management's expectations and assumptions about IT, which may be contradictory, and then understand their implications for how the IT department should be managed (i.e., translate the function's strategic mission into an IT management model that adds value to the organization).

The characterization of the IT function has constituted a central and growing subject of research in the information systems field. Although the extant literature has much to teach us, knowledge in this area is nevertheless fragmented and has not been properly integrated. In response to these limitations, this study proposes and tests a new theory of the contribution of the IT function. Specifically, our objective is to offer an explanation of the contribution of the IT function in organizations with a typology of ideal profiles.

A field study was conducted in 24 large Canadian companies in order to validate a set of research propositions. Our results first suggest that there are five distinct “ideal” IT management profiles in organizations and each of these profiles tends to focus on specific sources of value. Next, we observed that IT functions that are close to the ideal of any given profile seem to be outperforming those with hybrid profiles. Finally, our findings provide a compelling explanation as to how ideal IT management profiles are adopted in organizations. The article concludes with a discussion of the theoretical and practical implications of the proposed theory.

Keywords: IT function, IT management profile, IT contribution, CIO, theory building, typological theory, field study

## Introduction

The information technology function has undergone fundamental changes since it first appeared in organizations about 50 years ago. Technology has evolved, organizations have been transformed, IT uses have changed, and, consequently, the management of IT has been impacted (DeSanctis et al. 2000; Ross 2003). Because changes in organizations and IT environments are enduring, the alignment of the IT function with business objectives must not only be understood, but constantly renewed and adjusted (Sabherwal et al. 2001). This is amply reflected in recent surveys of CIOs, which suggest that the issue of alignment is the number one challenge and management priority (Kontzer 2009; Luftman and Ben-Zvi 2010; McGee 2008).

Many CIOs face a double challenge when addressing the issue of alignment. First, they must develop a good understanding of their organizations' needs and expectations about information technology. Prior research has provided CIOs with a few well-known frameworks for developing a better understanding of these expectations and assumptions and, hence, for addressing the issue of business–IT alignment from a strategic perspective. The strategic grid framework (Applegate et al. 1999; McFarlan and McKenny 1983) is one of the most highly recognized and widely cited conceptual models used to assess top managers' perceptions of the strategic significance of IT in an organization. These authors proposed that the centrality of IT to a firm can be positioned anywhere along continuums ranging from low impact and relevance to high impact and relevance in two dimensions: the strategic impact of the applications development portfolio and the strategic impact of existing systems. The combination of future/current portfolio with high/low impact presents four types of IT environments: strategic, turnaround, factory, and support. For instance, in the strategic environment, top executives perceive IT as having a high level of strategic impact on the firm's performance, and IT planning must be closely aligned with corporate planning. In stark contrast, in support firms, top management considers that IT plays a traditional back-office-support role without any potential for dramatically altering the organization's current or future operations and/or direction. In short, the strategic grid framework proposes that each of the four IT environments represents very different organizational needs for information systems and that understanding top management's position in this grid is critical to developing appropriate IT management models (Raghunathan et al. 1999).

A more recent framework is provided by Kaarst-Brown and Robey (1999) and Kaarst-Brown (2005) who argued that top managers' cultural assumptions about IT influence how IT executives and their function are viewed, and, ultimately, the resulting IT management model. Specifically, these authors observed five categories of assumptions that are important to CIO status and IT management, namely: control assumptions (who should control IT direction?); centrality assumptions (how central is IT to business strategy?); IT skill value assumptions (what is the value of IT skills and knowledge?); justification assumptions (what justifies further IT investment?); and beneficiaries assumptions (who benefits and who loses when IT is used?). They uncovered these assumptions and the five clusters they form, mainly by studying IT-related assumptions in 31 business units of two large insurance companies. For instance, they observed that in the fearful archetype, business managers consider information systems and technologies strictly as a survival measure, while in the controlled archetype, top executives believe that IT should be used to reduce costs and be controlled and championed by corporate management.

While the aforementioned frameworks help CIOs to better understand the different assumption environments they face, it is not clear how they inform them about which specific IT management model might contribute most to their organization. Another stream of research has proposed various taxonomies that characterize IT management models according to one or several key attributes, including the nature of the relationship between the IT function and business units/external partners (e.g., Peppard and Ward 1999; Ross et al. 1996; Venkatraman and Loh 1993), the types of structural arrangements (e.g., Gordon and Gordon 2002; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999; Zmud 1984), forms of governance in IT functions (e.g., Weill and Ross 2005), the wide range of activities performed by IT staff (e.g. Peppard 2003; Rockart et al. 1996; Ross et al. 1996; Ross et al. 2006), and the IT managers and professionals' profiles (e.g., Feeny and Willcocks 1998a; Lee et al. 1995; Leidner and Mackay 2007; Preston et al. 2008). However, as explained in greater detail later, although prior research has much to teach us, knowledge in this area is nevertheless fragmented and has not been properly integrated.

In light of this limitation, the primary intent of this study is to propose and test a new theory of the contribution of the IT function that will help CIOs rise to the challenges of meeting top managers' expectations about IT and adopting the IT management profile that brings the most value to their organizations. To reach our objective, we offer an explanation of the contribution of the IT function in organizations with a typology of ideal profiles. $^{2}$ Ultimately, the theory we propose approaches a Type IV theory (Gregor 2006), which implies understanding underlying causes and prediction, as well as describing theoretical constructs and the relationships among them.

Following a systematic review of the extant literature to establish a base theory, a field study in 24 large Canadian companies was undertaken to confirm and extend the initial set of research propositions. Our results suggest that there exist five distinct ideal profiles of the IT function in organizations, each of which focuses on a specific type of contribution. Second, we found that IT functions that are close to the ideal of any given profile seem to be outperforming those with hybrid profiles. Last, our findings provide a compelling explanation of why a given ideal profile is adopted in an organization.

We continue this paper by discussing the key theoretical foundations for a new typological theory of the contribution of IT function. Next, the methodological approach is detailed, followed by the presentation and discussion of our findings. We conclude with a presentation of the study's limitations and contributions.

## Theoretical Background

As stressed by Grover et al. (2008, p. 45), “innovative theorizing comes in different forms and needs to be formulated and assessed differently in different contexts.” In this study, we found it necessary to use a typological theory (Doty and Glick 1994) to ground our field work. While our conceptualization of the IT function builds on the extant literature, it goes beyond traditional characterizations by proposing a typology that integrates four recurring dimensions, organized in coherent profiles and associated with the IT function’s specific contribution to the organization. Although there is a long history of using typologies in other fields (Greenwood and Hinings 1988), we observe that typological theories have not been extensively developed in the information systems area to study phenomena of interest to our community. Typological theories facilitate the study of complex phenomena that cannot be captured by linear theories (Doty and Glick 1994) and, hence, must help us deepen and strengthen our understanding of the contribution of IT functions in organizations.

## Typology of Ideal Profiles of IT Management Models

The terms classification scheme, taxonomy, and typology have been used interchangeably in much of the relevant literature (Doty and Glick 1994). The first two terms refer to classification systems that categorize phenomena into mutually exclusive and exhaustive sets with a series of discrete decision rules. For example, Barki, Rivard and Talbot's (1988, 1993) IS classification scheme provides nine clear top-level categories, each one decomposed into subcategories that define the IS research domain. In contrast, typology refers to conceptually derived interrelated sets of ideal profiles. Unlike classification systems, typologies do not provide decision rules for classifying organizations (Miller and Friesen 1980; Rich 1992). Instead, typologies identify multiple ideal profiles, each of which represents a unique combination of attributes that are believed to determine the relevant outcomes (Doty and Glick 1994). Some of the best-known typologies are Mintzberg's (1980) typology of organizational structures and Miles and Snow's (1978) typology of organizational strategies.

Typologies contain two distinct constructs. The first construct is the ideal profile. Ideal profiles are used to represent holistic configurations of multiple constructs. They are intended to “provide an abstract model, so that deviation from the extreme or ideal profile can be noted and explained” (Blalock 1969, p. 32). These ideal profiles are theoretical abstractions thought to result in a specified level of an outcome. For example, Miles and Snow described the prospector, the analyzer, and the defender as ideal profiles of organizations that attain maximum effectiveness. Ideal profiles are described in terms of multiple dimensions called first-order constructs (Doty and Glick 1994). Mintzberg described his five ideal profiles using first-order contextual constructs such as age, size, and environmental uncertainty, and first-order structural constructs such as formalization, specialization, and centralization. As a result, each ideal organizational profile represents a unique combination of the values associated with the fundamental dimensions.

According to Doty and Glick (1994), the method for specifying ideal profiles that is most consistent with the logical structure of typologies is theoretical specification. Theoretical specification requires that a group of experts, often the original theorists, develop a set of ideal profiles. Experts base their judgment strictly on their own interpretation of the extant literature. Theoretical specification provides the advantage that the theory development process is not constrained by the sample because the ideal profiles are not specified with organizations in the sample, but rather with theory (Doty and Glick 1994). This allows researchers to specify ideal profiles for organizations even if real organizations that closely resemble the ideal profiles are not included in the sample.

## Development of the Theoretical Profiles of IT Management Models

In order to derive the theoretical configurations that best describe coherent IT management profiles in organizations, we followed Webster and Watson's (2002) literature review guidelines and conducted a comprehensive search of the prior IT function/IT management research. For the purpose of this study, the inclusion criteria consisted of (1) English language publications in peer-reviewed journals and (2) studies that presented one or several IT management configurations or profiles. We searched the ABI/Inform Global database $^{3}$ using the following keywords: IT function, IS function, IT organization, IS organization, IT department, IS department, IT management, and IS management. The original search resulted in 156 peer-reviewed articles through 2010. From these, 102 articles were deemed irrelevant based on title or abstract. The remaining 54 articles were reviewed by the investigators to determine whether they were appropriate for inclusion. We relied on the following exclusion criteria: (1) studies that investigated how IT functions are designed or organized but did not offer any particular configurations or profiles (e.g., Khan 1991; Porra et al. 2005; Tai and Phelps 2000); (2) descriptive studies of specific IT management practices such as strategic IT planning and IT implementation processes (e.g., Lederer and Sethi 1988; Ranganathan and Kannabiran 2004); (3) studies that investigated environmental or organizational factors that affect the performance of the IT function (e.g., Brown and Magill 1994; Feeny et al. 1992); and (4) essays or conceptual papers that provided general advice or prescriptive guidelines about how to effectively manage the IT function (e.g., Gray and Hovav 2007; Rowe 2007). Of the 54 articles, 40 were excluded based on these criteria. As a result, 14 articles were considered for further analysis (see the full list in Appendix A).

We first read carefully through the detailed descriptions provided in the selected articles and identified the core dimensions most often used to characterize the IT function. We identified four recurring dimensions, namely, the range of activities performed by IT professionals and staff; the nature of the relationship between the IT function and business units or external partners; the skills and knowledge of IT professionals; and the forms of IT governance models. As shown in Appendix A, we then grouped the articles according to their degree of similarity in terms of (1) the overall role or mission of the IT function (as specified in the articles) and (2) the values associated with one or several of the fundamental dimensions. For instance, the technologist form of IS partnerships (Brown et al. 1996), the infrastructure planner archetype (Cross et al. 1997), the platform model (Agarwal and Samba-murthy 2002), and the modular architecture stage (Ross 2003) were grouped under the same theoretical profile. Next, we synthesized the narratives provided in the selected articles and composed a generic and coherent description for each of the five theoretical profiles which make up our typology. As a final step, we attributed a name to each profile. As shown in Table 1, we named these profiles partner, systems provider, architecture builder, technological leader, and project coordinator. Each of the five ideal profiles will now be described in turn.

## Ideal IT Management Profiles

Architecture Builder: Under this profile, the IT function seeks to build and manage an IT infrastructure that supports business processes and reduces architectural complexity (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Brown et al. 1996; Ross 2003). In some cases, the IT function will implement data warehouses, or middleware applications, to share access to data, integrate transactions, and enable organizational agility (Ross 2003). Consequently, IT professionals have solid technical abilities related to the integration of technological platforms and very good knowledge of best industry practices (Cross et al. 1997; Ross 2003). Instead of defining the solution and looking for the best technology, the IT function negotiates the best possible solution taken from existing technological platforms (Ross 2003). Under this profile, business managers rarely participate in the development of IT architecture, deferring the responsibility for setting technological policies and standards to the IT function (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Ross 2003). Business managers clarify strategic intent, and the IT executive proactively targets IT architecture accordingly (Cross et al. 1997; Ross 2003). Finally, the IT function is accountable for issues such as IT efficiency, reliability, and integrity (Ross 2003).

Partner: Under this profile, the primary mission of the IT function is to be an active partner in business transformation and organizational innovation (Clark et al. 1997; Earl and Sampler 1998; Venkatraman 1997). IT professionals and line managers, therefore, work together (Brown et al. 1996; Ross 2003) to understand business opportunities, determine needed functionalities, and choose from among available technological options (Clark et al. 1997; Earl and Sampler 1998; Venkatraman 1997). Accordingly, the IT function ensures that line managers throughout the organization understand the potential of IT and how to use this resource more effectively in their business processes (Brown et al. 1996). Vigorous collaboration on a day-to-day basis between IT professionals and organizational members can, therefore, be observed (Brown et al. 1996). To achieve their objectives, IT professionals have an excellent understanding of business processes (Ross 2003), act as catalysts for business change (Venkatraman 1997), work effectively in teams, and keep their technical abilities up to date (Clark et al. 1997). From a governance standpoint, both IT and line managers accept responsibility for IT projects and participate in key IT investment decisions (Earl and Sampler 1998; Ross 2003).

Project Coordinator: Under this profile, the IT function acts as coordinator of IT-based activities. In order to give the organization greater flexibility, the IT function manages relationships between software vendors, systems integrators, and business units (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Feeny and Willcocks 1998a; Rockart et al. 1996). The IT function not only manages and coordinates current IT projects but also plans for the future by developing strategic partnerships with vendors and business units (Feeny and Willcocks 1998a, 1998b; Willcocks and Feeny 2006; Willcocks et al. 2006). The IT function also articulates the needs of business units in requests for proposals and assists business units in evaluating submissions (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002). In order to carry out these activities, IT professionals must possess technical, negotiation, and interpersonal skills (Feeny and Willcocks 1998a; Rockart et al. 1996). It is also common to observe a strong collaborative business–IT relationship (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002). Business units decide how and where to invest in IT, and the IT function is responsible for providing efficient IT and strategic flexibility (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Rockart et al. 1996).

Systems Provider: Under this profile, the primary mission of the IT function is to respond to requests from business units. As a result, the IT function organizes its activities around the development (or acquisition), maintenance, and support of IT applications (Peppard and Ward 1999; Venkatraman 1997). Under this profile, IT professionals rely mostly on their technical skills (Cross et al. 1997; Ross 2003), and they interact with users sporadically and reactively to solicit their information needs (Brown et al. 1996; Vaast and Levina 2006). Systems or business analysts usually serve as the interface between the business units and the IT function (Vaast and Levina 2006). From a governance perspective, business units make proposals after consulting with IT staff, subject to approval from upper management (Ross 2003). IT personnel are responsible for the design and deployment of IT applications, and business unit managers are accountable for the business impacts of IT projects (Cross et al. 1997; Peppard and Ward 1999; Ross 2003).

Technological Leader: Under this last profile, the IT function plays a leadership role in identifying new business opportunities (Reich and Nelson 2003) and focuses on new ideas to create competitive advantage. The IT function helps develop completely new business strategies based on the potential of IT (Venkatraman 1997). Business cases are usually prepared by IT managers and endorsed by top management. In addition to their technical skills, IT professionals possess strong leadership skills and excellent knowledge of the industry in which the firm operates (Reich and Nelson 2003). They are also able to share their knowledge, acquire new knowledge from members of business lines, and negotiate with and influence others (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Reich and Nelson 2003). A distinct characteristic of this profile is the strong CEO–CIO relationship (Reich and Nelson 2003). From a governance standpoint, the IT function is accountable for its contribution to the development of IT-based organizational capabilities (Curley 2006; Reich and Nelson 2003).

As mentioned earlier, our contention is that, for each of these profiles, the properties associated with the core dimensions are compatible, thereby resulting in a set of coherent ideal profiles. For instance, Appendix A and Table 1 show that the values of the four dimensions for the partner profile are clearly compatible, as all of them help the IT function to deliver IT-enabled business capabilities to support current strategies. But if the skills and knowledge needed by the partner were replaced by the skills and capabilities associated with the architecture builder profile, for instance, then the partner profile would become much less coherent. Indeed, IT professionals would lack the business knowledge as well as the team and interpersonal skills needed to successfully embark on business process reengineering ventures, to develop a vigorous and ongoing relationship with business units, and to assume shared responsibility with business units for IT investment decisions.

In sum, we propose that there exist five ideal IT management profiles in organizations. These profiles are called partner, systems provider, architecture builder, technological leader, and project coordinator. Each ideal profile represents a unique and coherent combination of properties associated with four fundamental dimensions, namely, the critical activities carried out by IT specialists, the most important skills and abilities for IT professionals, the nature of the relationship with business units and external partners, and IT governance issues (how decisions about IT investments are made and who is accountable for IT success) (working proposition #1).

## Nature of the Contribution Associated with Each Ideal Profile

Typologies are complex theoretical constructs designed to predict the variance of a dependent variable (Doty and Glick 1994). Several criteria have been proposed for measuring the effectiveness or success of the IT function in organizations (Chang and King 2005; Seddon et al. 2002). Indeed, different performance measures exist for IT functions, including operational efficiency, adequacy of system development practices, user satisfaction, and IT impact on strategic direction, and the relevance of each varies according to a set of contingency factors (Saunders and Jones 1992). For instance, Ranganathan and Kannabiran (2004) observed that the ultimate performance of the IT function depends on five key parameters: top management's role, the nature of the CEO–CIO relationship, the IT department structure, the strategic IT planning process, and the IT implementation process. For their part, Saunders and Jones (1992) argued that as the IT function matures, performance measures are likely to change from a focus on operational efficiency and user satisfaction to a more unstructured concern for IT's impact on strategic orientation.

Because we believe in the existence of a variety of ideal IT management profiles that we consider “equal,” we are not concerned with the specific measures (e.g., costs, cycle times, productivity measures, etc.) that organizations use to benchmark their IT function performance or effectiveness. In our opinion, an IT function needs to fulfill its objectives in order to be effective. Hence, we concur with Ranganathan and Kannabiran that the main objective of an IT function is to improve and enhance the role of IT in improving the overall business performance of a firm. For this reason, we seek to gain an understanding of the nature of the broader contribution or sources of value associated with the IT management profiles in our typology.

In line with Venkatraman (1997), who recommends that every IT function focus on all possible sources of value but stresses that their relative importance is often dependent on the specific business context and objectives, we propose that each of the ideal profiles included in our typology will provide a distinctive source of value to organizations (working proposition #2a). By applying an inductive approach, we will be able to identify the nature of the contribution associated with each ideal profile.

As mentioned earlier, typologies highlight internal consistency among first-order constructs within an ideal profile. This internal consistency is very important for explaining a specific outcome. Typological theories, therefore, state that similarity between an actual organization and an ideal profile is a good predictor of the level of a dependent variable. For example, Miles and Snow (1978) argued that, under the prospector strategy, product innovation will result in greater organizational effectiveness, since innovation is consistent with the strategy and structure of this ideal profile. They also posited that product innovation will be less effective in organizations that pursue a defender strategy, since innovation is not consistent with the strategy and structure of this archetype. Following this logical argument, we posit that IT functions that are perfectly aligned with or close to any given ideal profile will be more effective in delivering value to the organization than IT functions that are not aligned with any of the profiles (working proposition #2b).

## Factors That Influence the Choice of a Particular Ideal Profile

The third and final building block of our theory aims to understand why a particular ideal IT management profile is adopted in an organization. Indeed, as soon as one accepts that there may be several ideal profiles and that each one makes a specific contribution to meeting organizational objectives, it becomes important and relevant to understand how decisions are made.

Using the aforementioned typology of IT management profiles, we investigated the transformation of the IT function over a 10-year period in three large Canadian companies (Guillemette and Paré 2011, 2012). We inductively identified three contingency factors that trigger a radical transformation in the IT function in these firms (i.e., a change from one ideal profile to another). More precisely, our results suggest that IT management profiles are transformed in response to changes in (1) senior management's perception of the centrality of IT to the organization, (2) the CIO's influence or status in the organization, and (3) top executives' knowledge of IT. Extending this line of reasoning, we posit that the CIO's own reading of these contingency factors is likely to influence his or her choice of a particular IT management profile. As explained below, these factors have also been identified in the extant literature as having a major influence on IT management practices in organizations.

We first posit that a CIO's decision to adopt a particular IT management profile is most likely to be influenced by his/her reading of top executives' perception of IT centrality to the organization. Indeed, we believe that CIOs need to develop their own interpretation of how top managers perceive IT and what they expect from IT in order to implement an appropriate IT management model. The underlying notion of centrality of IT was first introduced by McFarlan and McKenny (1983) and has been taken up by other authors (e.g., Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Kaarst-Brown 2005; Rockart et al. 1996; Tukana and Weber 1996; Venkatraman 1997). Simply stated, centrality of IT refers to the idea that IT might or might not be viewed as strategic by top executives—that is, as central and critical to achieving the organization's goals and business strategies (Kaarst-Brown 2005). The strategic significance of IT can be positioned anywhere along a continuum ranging from essential to business success to not at all significant. Furthermore, it must be stressed that the centrality of IT differs not only from one organization to another, but may evolve in a given organization over time. Indeed, the IT function that does not have strategic importance today may be deemed very strategic in the future, and vice-versa (Raghunathan et al. 1999). Prior studies have shown that the centrality of IT influences organizational needs for IT management. For instance, in organizations where IT is viewed by top managers as central to business strategy, IT planning must be closely aligned with corporate planning, and a considerable degree of senior management commitment to IT activities is required (McFarlan and McKenny 1983). Conversely, in companies where IT is not considered vital to achieving the organization's strategic objectives, the IT function is more likely to adopt a traditional back-office-support model (Raghunathan et al. 1999). Extending this stream of research, we argue that the CIO's own interpretation of the importance of IT for senior management will influence his or her choice of a particular IT management profile.

Second, we propose that CIOs need to assess whether their status or strategic influence in the organization may impede their choices. Indeed, to meet organizational expectations about IT, CIOs need to have credibility and the necessary status or strategic influence to make the appropriate choices and decisions for the IT function. Status, or strategic influence, is related to the recognition from the organization of the IT function's capabilities and value (Kaarst-Brown 2005). Strategic influence concerns business decisions that have a significant and lasting impact on organizational performance (Preston et al. 2008). In this sense, it is distinguished from tactical or operational decision-making influence. When a CIO reports directly to the CEO and plays an active role at board meetings (rather than sitting in as an observer), he or she is able to influence the organization's strategic direction (Applegate and Elam 1992) and, as a result, the role that the IT function plays in the organization (Karimi et al. 1996). The informal influence exercised by a CIO on other members of management may also influence the IT function's role in an organization (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Smaltz 2000; Smaltz et al. 2006; Stephens et al. 1992). Finally, when the CIO's strategic influence is low, the IT function plays a support role without any potential for dramatically altering the organization's direction (Smaltz 2000). Based on this research, we posit that the CIO's own reading of his or her strategic influence (both formal and informal) in the organization is likely to influence his or her choice of a particular IT management profile.

Third, until very recently, the responsibility for acquiring, implementing, and maintaining IT investments has belonged to IT specialists. Yet current IT research suggests that the management of IT should be shared between IT professionals and line managers (Ross and Weill 2002). As reported in Bassellier et al. (2001), business managers are now expected to deploy IT effectively and strategically (Silver et al. 1995), to assume ownership of IT projects within their domain of business responsibility (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1994), to develop a partnership with IT professionals (Ross et al. 1996), and to take the lead in IT implementation (Rockart et al. 1996). In fact, when members of senior management have extensive IT knowledge, they are more inclined to form partnerships with the CIO and the IT team and actively participate in the deployment of IT projects and IT decisions (Bassellier et al. 2001; Raghunathan 1992; Smaltz 2000). Extending this research, we suggest that a CIO's interpretation of top managers' knowledge of technology, applications, systems development and implementation methods, and/or management of IT is likely to influence his or her decision to adopt a particular IT management profile.

In sum, we posit that a CIO's own interpretation of senior management's perception of the centrality of IT to the organization, how influential he or she is in the organization, and how IT literate top managers are, is likely to sway his or her decision to adopt a particular IT management profile (working proposition #3).

## Methodology

In order to validate the ideal IT management profiles described above, to discover the particular contribution of each ideal profile to the organization, and to explore the main contingency factors that influence the adoption of ideal profiles in organizations, a field study was conducted in 24 large Canadian organizations (sales of at least CAN \$100 million per year). In each organization, our unit of analysis was the IT function and our key informant, the CIO. Demographic data from our sample are provided in Table 2. Members of the research team had no prior knowledge of these CIOs and their respective IT functions.

## Data Collection

Data were primarily collected through semi-structured interviews. Each interview began with a broad question that asked the respondent to describe the IT function in his or her own terms. Then the interviewer asked more specific questions to ensure that all of the themes or dimensions (i.e., activities, skills and knowledge, relationships with business units, governance) were covered. It must be noted that CIOs in nine organizations provided us with not one but two in-depth descriptions of IT management models, typically by comparing a former situation with their current one. Consequently, our final sample was composed of 33 empirical profiles of the IT function.

In the second part of the interview, the five ideal profiles described in Table 1 were presented blindly to the respondent (i.e., the names of the profiles were not shared, in order to avoid influencing the answers). The interviewee was then asked to identify the profile(s) (identified as Profile A, Profile B, and so on) that best represented their IT function (past and/or current). This allowed us to determine the apparent validity of the typology. In the last part of the interview, we asked specific questions about the particular contribution made by the IT function to the organization and why the IT function had adopted its particular profile.

Each of the interviews was recorded and transcribed, producing a total of 524 pages of verbatim transcript. The interviews lasted 90 minutes, on average, and varied from 60 to 150 minutes. The notes taken by the researcher during the interviews and the various other documents collected (mission statements of the IT function, company annual reports, presentation slides, newspaper articles, etc.) were also analyzed after the interviews.

## Data Analysis

In qualitative research, coding tools are used to support researchers during the early analysis phase. Codes are especially useful for data reduction. In the present study, two approaches were used to create our coding scheme. For one thing, we relied on predefined codes using the descriptions of all five ideal profiles provided in Table 1 (working proposition #1) and the literature associated with the three contingency factors discussed earlier (working proposition #3). For instance, the values or properties associated with the CIOs' interpretation of how central IT is perceived by top management were derived from Kaarst-Brown's (2005)

conceptualization of this construct. Then, we developed the codes for the distinct sources of value associated with the ideal profiles (working proposition #2a) only after some initial exploration of the data had been done, using an immersion style (Crabtree and Miller 1999). Our final coding scheme is presented in Appendix B.

How data are analyzed and interpreted represents another key issue in qualitative research. In the present study, pattern-matching (Yin 2008) was used to compare the 33 empirical profiles observed in the field with the five theoretical profiles presented in Table 1 (working proposition #1). Appendix C provides more detailed information on the coding and data analysis process associated with the ideal profiles. Furthermore, pattern-matching was used to find a clear and convincing answer to working propositions #1 (see Appendix D) and #3 (see Appendix E).

As mentioned earlier, we adopted an inductive analytical approach (Glaser and Strauss 1967) to provide an answer to working proposition #2a. It was the absence of relevant and useful empirical work on this question that led us to adopt this approach. Taking the interviews one by one, we first extracted all excerpts dealing with each IT function's contribution to value creation. We then created a table (not shown here) to identify and summarize the subcategories in order to better qualify the nature of the contributions made by all IT functions and to isolate the sources of value specific to each IT function profile.

Finally, following Miles and Huberman's (1994) recommendations, we built several tables (see the “Results” section) which allowed us to look for within-group similarities (i.e., identical ideal profiles) coupled with intergroup differences (i.e., different ideal profiles) in terms of value sources (working propositions #2a and #2b) and contingency factors (working proposition #3).

## Results

## Ideal Operational Profiles of the IT Function

We first wanted to ensure that the typology presented in Table 1 represented reality, as seen by our group of experts, and did not exclude any major ideal profile. We based our analysis on the second part of the interview, in which we presented our IT executive respondents with the typology of ideal profiles for the IT function. We asked them if they thought that the typology represented all the IT functions of which they were aware. Our findings show that for the 24 CIOs who participated in our study, the five ideal profiles appeared to represent or characterize all of the IT functions found in organizations. We then we asked them to use the typology to characterize their IT function. As illustrated in the following quote, the respondents referred to the typology to describe the past and/or present profile of their IT departments:

I would say that, from 1996 to 2000, we were [a Technological Leader], and since 2001 our profile is more that of [an Architecture Builder]. [CIO #13]

In short, the CIOs who participated in our study considered the typology both comprehensive and exhaustive, and all of them were able to use it to describe their own IT functions.

Our second objective was to ensure the representativeness of the typology. Based on the first part of the interviews conducted with the 24 CIOs, we were able to code 33 empirical profiles. In this situation, the values for activities, relationships, skills and knowledge, and governance (as shown in Table 3) were inferred from the first part of the interview with the CIO. Then, we inferred the profile directly from each transcript (see the data collection section and Appendix C for details) independently of the association made by the CIO in the second part of the interview. As shown in Table 3, all five ideal profiles were observed in our sample, providing empirical support for working proposition #1. Interestingly, 21 empirical profiles were perfectly associated with one or another of the five theoretical profiles. These perfectly associated profiles comprised 2 partners, 12 systems providers, 3 architecture builders, 3 technological leaders, and 1 project coordinator, effectively covering all of the theoretical profiles. In addition, we were able to associate eight empirical profiles with a single theoretical profile for all but one dimension of the profiles. Among these almost perfectly (three out of four dimensions) associated profiles were seven systems providers and one architecture builder. Finally, a total of four hybrid profiles were identified: one systems provider/partner and three architecture builder/partners.

## Contribution of the IT Function to Organizations

Our results indicate that, independent of the ideal profile adopted by the IT function, it is hardly possible for any IT department to make a specific contribution to an organization if its clients are not satisfied, systems are not available, and projects are not delivered on time and within budget. However, when the minimal requirements are met, the IT function is in a good position to add value to the organization. As shown in Table 3, our data suggest that the more closely an IT function conforms to one of the five ideal profiles, the better its chance of achieving the IT contribution associated with its profile.

The IT function that perfectly or closely matches a partner profile contributes greatly to improved organizational productivity by initiating change in the organization and acting as a catalyst for business process reengineering (Venkatraman 1997). Indeed, in this profile, the IT function mainly helps develop IT-based capacities at the organizational level by integrating innovative uses of IT into business processes (Clark et al. 1997; Ross 2003; Venkatraman 1997).

In the systems provider profile, the IT function mainly helps to improve the organization's financial performance. To this end, the IT function strictly controls its own operational costs (Cross et al. 1997; Venkatraman 1997), compares itself with benchmarks, and reduces the organization's overall operating costs. The systems provider will also use strong financial metrics to select IT projects that will lower operating costs (Venkatraman 1997).

We have to make sure that our costs are at their lowest if we want to support organizational competitiveness. [CIO #8]

We used benchmarks to compare IT costs to the industry. We need to demonstrate that we efficiently exploit IT investments. [CIO #7]

In this industry, when IT costs are low our organization wins contracts. Here, IT costs represent 30 percent of the fixed charges. The lower IT costs are, the greater the revenues. Our organization is very satisfied with our performance. [CIO #3]

The architecture builder profile largely adds value at the organizational level by enhancing organizational agility. By planning and maintaining a flexible and uncomplicated architecture, the organization can become agile enough to quickly seize business opportunities as they arise (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002). The architecture builder also helps improve integration and knowledge sharing in the organization by introducing large integrated systems (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002; Ross 2003) based on best-in-industry practices.

The organizational strategy is to be agile. We directly support this strategy. By simplifying the architecture, we can more quickly meet the organization's needs. That's the key. The organization will be able to grow and painlessly respond to environmental pressures. [CIO #23]

The technological leader profile mainly fosters positive change (Curley 2006) and organizational transformation directly at the level of the company's corporate strategy by introducing high-potential technologies that generate long-term competitive advantages (Reich and Nelson 2003).

We were the first company in the world to sell that kind of product on the Web. We were at the leading edge, both with the technology and in our way of doing things. We've been accredited for that, and we've won prizes. [CIO #13]

Finally, the project coordinator profile supports strategic flexibility by establishing a flexible and efficient IT sourcing strategy (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002). Moreover, by improving IT and project management knowledge among organizational members and their ability to assess the potential of new technologies (Peppard and Ward 1999; Rockart et al. 1996), the IT function helps promote informed decision making in the business units and more efficient use of IT resources (Agarwal and Sambamurthy 2002).

Our role, our contribution as a department is to facilitate and to teach managers in business units to make good decisions regarding IT investments. [CIO #14]

To sum up, our results support the notion that there is a minimal contribution that the IT department must make to the organization, independent of the ideal profile adopted by the IT function. In addition, as summarized in Table 4, each of the ideal profiles in our typology has the potential to contribute in a unique way to the organization's success. In light of these findings, we revisited our working propositions 2a and 2b as follows:

Proposition #2a: There is a minimal contribution that each IT function must make to its clients in the business units, independent of its profile. When this minimal contribution is secured (i.e., systems are available, projects are delivered on time and within budget, and clients are satisfied), each ideal profile has the potential to make a unique contribution to the success of the organization.

Proposition #2b: The more closely the IT function conforms to one of the five ideal profiles, the more effective it will be in making the contribution associated with that profile. The unique source of value associated with each ideal profile is as follows: improving organizational productivity (partner); reducing organizational costs (systems provider); improving organizational agility (architecture builder); facilitating strategic organizational transformation with technology (technological leader); and supporting organizational flexibility (project coordinator).

## Contingency Factors Influencing the Adoption of Ideal Profiles

Finally, we aimed to understand what leads an IT function to adopt a particular ideal profile of the typology. We proposed that a CIO's own interpretation of how IT is perceived by top management, how influential he or she is in the organization, and how IT literate top managers are, are likely to influence his or her decision to adopt a particular IT management profile. The quotes below, as well as the data in Table 5, $^{4}$ confirm that all three factors play a significant role in explaining the adoption of particular ideal profiles in organizations.

In our company, IT is a key part of the organizational strategy. Technologically speaking, we're at the cutting edge, and we provide leadership in all business units as well as in the company's management and strategy. [CIO #21]

As long as I don't sit on the management committee, I can't get IT to play a leading role in the company...and my department can't be considered a full partner. $^{5}$ [CIO #14]

It's clear that if the business managers have a good understanding of IT and the role it can play in their respective units, as they do here, then this will have a big influence on our relationship with the business units and how we interact with them....The more members of the management team know about IT and are interested in IT, the more we need to show leadership. [CIO #3]

Our findings first indicate that when IT is essential to corporate success (and implicitly the CIO's strategic influence and top management's knowledge of IT are both high), IT executives are likely to adopt a technological leader profile. In stark contrast, when IT is perceived as insignificant (and implicitly the CIO's strategic influence and top-management knowledge of IT are both low), then a systems provider profile is privileged. As illustrated below, in such an environment, the IT function supports the organization by providing high-quality information systems at the lowest possible cost.

In light of these findings, we revisited our working proposition #3 (now P3a) and added a new research proposition (P3b):

Proposition #3a: The CIO's understanding of senior management's perception of the centrality of IT to the organization, the CIO's own view of his or her strategic influence in the organization and the CIO's reading of top-management knowledge of IT are, in order of importance, the key contingency factors influencing the adoption of particular ideal IT management profiles in organizations.

Proposition #3b: The adoption of each ideal profile is dependent upon the following configurations of contingency factors:

(i) Centrality of IT to operational/tactical goals, high strategic influence of the CIO, and low top-management knowledge of IT (partner).

(ii) Centrality of IT to operational/tactical goals, low to moderate strategic influence of the CIO, and low top-management knowledge of IT (architecture builder).

(iii) Centrality of IT to corporate success, high strategic influence of the CIO, and high top-management knowledge of IT (technological leader).

(iv) Centrality of IT to operational/tactical goals, low strategic influence of the CIO, and moderate to high top-management knowledge of IT (project coordinator).

(v) IT is not significant, low strategic influence of the CIO and low top-management knowledge of IT or centrality of IT to operational/tactical goals, low to moderate strategic influence of the CIO, and moderate to high top-management knowledge of IT (systems provider).

## Discussion

The main intent of this study was to propose and validate a new theory of the contribution of the IT function in organizations. Based on a systematic review of the extant literature supplemented by in-depth interviews with CIOs from 24 large companies, we have generated and tested a set of research propositions, summarized in Table 6.

As a first step, we validated the theoretical profiles in Table 1. By combining what we had gleaned from the literature with the extensive professional experience of our respondents, we confirmed the existence of five ideal IT management profiles that we called the partner, the systems provider, the architecture builder, the technological leader, and the project coordinator. As recommended by Doty and Glick (1994), these ideal profiles were based on the same set of fundamental dimensions, namely, the critical activities carried out by IT specialists, the knowledge and abilities of IT professionals, the nature of the relationship between the IT function and other business units or external partners, and, finally, governance of the IT function. When considered together, the values taken by the four dimensions created five internally coherent profiles. Our results show that all five profiles were represented in our sample. Of utmost importance, in most empirical profiles (29 out of 33), the IT function was either perfectly associated (in all four dimensions) or closely aligned (in three of the four dimensions) with a specific ideal profile, providing additional support for Proposition #1.

Another important finding emerged from the discovery of hybrid IT management profiles in organizations. As shown in Table 3, four empirical profiles provided evidence of hybrids, all of which include a combination of the partner profile with either the architecture builder profile or the systems provider profile. The presence of a hybrid profile in two of the four organizations is explained by the fact that the IT function had tried to transform itself in response to upper management's new expectations. In both cases, the transformation was incomplete when we collected our data, and the two IT functions presented mixed profiles. In connection with this explanation, Greenwood and Hinings (1988) mention that there may be times when organizations fail in their attempts to transform because they are experimenting with new approaches that they have not mastered or because they face considerable resistance, and that such failures often leave organizations in an unstable state between two different archetypes. Our observations lead us to believe that this situation also applies at the level of the IT function. In the other two organizations, further analysis of our data revealed that the presence of hybrid profiles was associated with a lack of alignment between what top management expected from the IT function (as perceived by the CIO) and what the CIO truly wanted to accomplish. This finding also concurs with observations made by previous authors. For instance, Kaarst-Brown and Robey (1999), and Leidner and Kayworth (2006) argued that organizations maintain many conflicting assumptions about IT that influence the IT function and its daily management. Hence, hybrid forms of IT management models might result from conflicting views across business units and/or hierarchical levels that cannot be easily reconciled by IT executives.

Whether this is due to an incomplete or failed transformation or to an alignment problem, we believe that the presence of a hybrid profile in an organization for an extended period of time is not ideal. We posit that being in a hybrid profile makes the management of the IT function more challenging. Indeed, managing an IT function under conflicting assumptions challenges the perceived “performance” of the IT function and consequently affects its credibility in organizations (Hirschheim et al. 2003). This situation might also make it difficult for a CIO to obtain necessary resources, and it challenges the added value that the IT function is delivering to the organization (Bashein and Markus 1997; Rockart et al. 1996).

In line with our second proposition, our study found that each of the ideal profiles has the potential to make a distinctive contribution to organizations. However, we also found that it is impossible for an IT function to make a special contribution to an organization if it cannot provide competent basic services. When this minimum level of contribution is attained, the IT function is then in a good position to add value to the organization (Smith and McKeen 2003). Our results also revealed that the more an IT function conforms to one of the ideal profiles, the better it will be at achieving the specific contribution associated with that very same profile, namely, improving organizational productivity (partner), reducing organizational costs (systems provider), improving organizational agility (architecture builder), facilitating strategic organizational transformation with technology (technological leader), or supporting organizational flexibility (project coordinator).

As shown in Table 3, we observed that nine IT functions were unable to provide more than the minimal level contribution. Three of these were hybrid profiles. As we discussed previously, IT functions that are not close to one ideal profile (the hybrids) have found it difficult to provide added value at the organizational level. Our data shows that the remaining six IT functions were confronted with important technical and/or managerial problems that prevented them from acting according to their profile. It seems that when confronted with out of control projects or technical problems that cause information systems to be unstable, the IT function was unable to produce expected added value and was rather in “survival mode,” trying to fix these problems. This implies that even if ideal profiles are relatively stable characterizations of the IT function, under some circumstances, the IT function is unable to act in accordance with its profile and consequently is unable to make a significant contribution to the organization.

Finally, we have provided a compelling explanation of why one ideal profile is chosen over another in a given organization. We observed that three contingency factors explain the adoption of specific IT management profiles, namely, the CIO's interpretation of how central IT is perceived by top management, how influential he or she is in the organization, and how IT literate top managers are. As stressed by Eisenhardt (1989), in qualitative research it is important to look at the data in different ways to avoid developing premature conclusions as a result of information-processing biases. We wanted to gain even more confidence in our approach. Therefore, we challenged our own findings by examining the influence of many other contingency factors, taken from the organizational context of the IT function, that were identified in the literature as potentially important. We also let the data inform us about other potential forces. For example, we examined the roles of industries, organizational structures, organizational strategies, organizational size, and demographic characteristics of the CIOs such as age, gender, academic background, etc. and tried to relate each of them to the adopted IT management profiles. However, we found no evidence of a steady influence of any of these factors, nor of any combination of these factors, on the IT function's profiles.

## Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

We first acknowledge that the theory proposed herein will require further validation. Although we remain confident that our results are reliable, it may prove worthwhile to test our research propositions against a new sample of organizations.

We also feel it would be relevant to perform this validation by developing a quantitative measure for each ideal profile and its associated contributions before conducting a survey. This would enable the validation of our results in a new manner and with different analytic techniques. Results similar to those presented here would build confidence in the theory and would undoubtedly encourage its use by the scientific and professional community. This approach has been used in other disciplines; the typology proposed by Miles and Snow (1978) was validated this way, making it possible to conduct further research on a set of phenomena associated with business management.

Another limitation of this study is the fact that it only considered the points of view of CIOs. While we do not doubt the wisdom of this choice, we nevertheless believe that future research should cast a wider net, considering the perceptions of members of the senior management team. For instance, it would appear both relevant and important to unify the realms of top managers and CIOs and test the link between the five archetypes derived from the cultural assumptions about IT proposed by Kaarst-Brown (2005; see also Kaarst-Brown and Robey 1999) and the IT management profiles proposed in this study. We feel that researchers and IT executives alike would benefit from capturing top managers' assumptions and expectations about IT. Such a study would definitely contribute to our understanding of how particular IT management profiles are adopted in organizations. It would also be worthwhile investigating how the actual contribution of the IT function in organizations reinforces or alters senior managers' views and assumptions about IT over time.

Finally, while we believe that it is important to validate propositions #3a and #3b (i.e., how ideal IT management profiles are adopted) with other samples of CIOs, it is also important to understand why one profile is more desirable or suitable than another in a given context. Indeed, we need to push our theory one step further in order to advise CIOs on which profile to adopt, taking into account the environmental and/or organizational context in which the IT function operates. In other words, future research should go beyond the issue of profile adoption and investigate the appropriateness of the choices made in organizations. In fact, in the interviews, several CIOs indicated that they would be very interested in understanding which profile best suits their organization.

## Theoretical and Practical Contributions

As shown in Table 6, the present study has several implications for both research and practice. From a research perspective, our study makes a solid theoretical contribution to the information systems field by offering a new theory of the contribution of the IT function in organizations. Although the extant literature has made a substantial contribution to our understanding of the IT function, knowledge in this area remains fragmented. In response to this limitation, we proposed a new theory that not only characterizes common IT management profiles but also provides preliminary, yet compelling, explanations of the link between these profiles and the nature of their respective contribution to organizations and of why one ideal profile is chosen over another in a given company.

We were also particularly interested in eliciting the nature of the IT function's contribution to an organization. Rather than treat this contribution as “positive or appropriate” or “negative or inadequate,” we chose to go deep inside this construct to develop a better understanding of the precise nature of the contribution that an IT function can make in an organization. Moreover, we proposed an explanation of how this contribution is created, or where it comes from. By suggesting that the profile adopted by an IT function is associated with its contribution to the organization, we proposed a mechanism through which IT functions can create added value in organizations. By incorporating empirical knowledge into theoretical knowledge, our theory is one of the first to propose a contingency explanation of the contribution made by IT functions in organizations.

We suggested that by perfectly adopting an ideal profile, the IT function assumes the best possible position for making a specific contribution to the organization. Organizations that are close to the ideal of any profile should perform better than companies that are not (Doty and Glick 1994). This implies that it is very important for CIOs to make careful decisions when managing their IT function. It also suggests that, although in some organizations a multitude of expectations toward the IT function are expressed, it may simply not be possible to be all things to all people (Hirschheim et al. 2003). Finally, our theory proposed a compelling theoretical explanation of why particular ideal profiles are adopted in organizations. Specifically, we found three contingency factors that proved significant in explaining the adoption of specific ideal profiles, namely, the CIO's interpretation of how IT is perceived by top management, how influential he or she is in the organization, and how IT literate top managers are.

As for practice, our theory provides IT executives with a diagnostic tool for identifying the ideal profile that best describes their own IT function. The typology thus becomes a powerful tool that can structure IT executives' thinking about other ideal profiles that their function might adopt in the organization. Our theory allows CIOs to think through the ideal profiles for their own organizations and evaluate the impact of adopting a particular profile on the expected contribution of the IT function. Moreover, the CIO will be able to use the profiles to make internally coherent choices regarding critical IT activities, IT professionals' skills and knowledge, the relationship between the IT function and business units/external partners, and IT governance issues, effectively maximizing the function's potential for adding value to the organization.

Furthermore, our theory provides a tool that can help organizations recognize the real contribution made by their IT functions (i.e., the specific contribution made by a given profile). This might help avoid situations in which the IT function is evaluated with performance measures that do not apply to its particular profile. This research could also serve as a basis for the development of innovative measurement systems for assessing the success of the IT function. We think that this typology could help CIOs secure better recognition of the added value that their functions provide to organizations. Interestingly, one of the study's participants mentioned having used our model in a strategic planning exercise in order to clarify their function's positioning in the organization and its contribution. The exercise made it possible to support and guide upper management through a rethinking of the current and future contribution of their IT function.

## Conclusion

Designing effective IT functions has attracted the attention of several researchers for almost four decades. Our main intention has been to shed new light on this topic by proposing a new theory of the contribution of the IT function that (1) includes a typology of ideal IT management profiles, (2) proposes a contingency explanation of the contribution made by IT functions in organizations, and (3) explores the circumstances under which particular ideal profiles are adopted in organizations. We believe that our rigorous qualitative approach and our findings have met this objective. We sincerely hope that the theory developed herein will be taken up by other researchers and used by many practitioners to continue expanding our understanding of the contribution of the IT function in organizations.

## About the Authors

Manon G. Guillemette is an associate professor of Information Systems at Université de Sherbrooke, Canada. Her research interests include management of the IT function, the contribution made by IT to organizations, and the organizational aspects of business intelligence. She is also interested in the process of change as it relates to IT and transformations of the IT function in organizations. Findings from her work have recently appeared in the Canadian Journal of Administrative Sciences, Systèmes d'information et Management, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, and International Journal of Business Intelligence Research, and in the proceedings of the International Conference on Information Systems, the Americas Conference on Information Systems, the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, and the Administrative Sciences Association of Canada.

Guy Paré is a professor of Information Technologies at HEC Montreal and Canada Research Chair in Information Technology in Health Care. He has recently performed a number of important editorial duties and serves as a referee for many journals in the information systems and medical informatics fields. His current research interests involve the implementation and impacts of IT in health care organizations, the barriers to adoption of e-health in the Canadian healthcare system, and the contribution of the IT function in organizations. His publications have appeared in top-ranked journals including Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association, Journal of Medical Internet Research, Social Science & Medicine, Journal of Information Technology, European Journal of Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly. Professor Paré's expertise has been sought by numerous organizations including the World Health Organization, the Department of Health in France, and Canada Health Infoway.
