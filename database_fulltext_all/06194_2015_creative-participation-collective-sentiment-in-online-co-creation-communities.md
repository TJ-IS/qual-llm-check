# Creative Participation: Collective Sentiment in Online Co-Creation Communities

## Abstract:

Co-creation communities allow companies to utilize consumers’ creative thinking in the innovation process. This paper seeks to understand the role of sentiment in user cocreation. The results suggest that management style can affect the success of co-creation communities. Specific employees’ communication styles, the sentiments embedded in the messages, task-oriented content, and proactiveness all can influence individual user sentiment. The aggregation of these individual user sentiments, resulting in collective sentiments, affects co-creation performance. Increasing negative collective sentiment results in decreased subsequent creativity and increased future participation. Conversely, growing positive collective sentiment leads to a lower level of participation.

Keywords: collective sentiment; affective influence; online co-creation; collective creativity; user participation

## 1. Introduction

In recent years research about online communities has proliferated. Co-creation communities, in particular, have gained significant popularity among scholars and practitioners. Examples of co-creation practices abound across industries. Companies such as BMW [32], Ducati, Samsung [50], IBM [9], LEGO [74], Starbucks [48], Proctor & Gamble internet users. Co-creation seems to be an effective way to enhance customer relations and improve business process efficiency [e.g., 55]. Heineken, for instance, launched its IdeasBrewery website in 2012, where users can submit ideas for a number of challenges focused on reinventing beer-drinking experiences or packaging design. While the economic benefits of this co-creation practice may not be immediately apparent, there is evidence that companies may ultimately increase their profitability. For example, Proctor & Gamble started its co-creation practices with non-employees in 2001. In three years, innovative ideas contributed from outside the company increased from 20% to 35%, which resulted in a 20% reduction in their R&D investment [38].

One of the typical co-creation practices, and the type discussed in this paper, is the establishment of company-hosted consumer communities in which direct benefits to the company (such as actionable ideas on improving current products and services or creating new ones) largely result from the creative participation of a crowd of consumers. Similar to new product development in the organizational context, creativity in such online communities can be broadly defined as the “production of novel and useful ideas” [2, p. 1155]. Most prior literature investigating how to increase a community’s creative output revolves around structural and technical issues, such as the design of virtual interaction tools [e.g., 29]. Despite indications that affective factors contribute to developing creativity [68] and

improving user co-creation experience [73], there has been little attention paid to how an affective environment can help sustain continuous creativity and ongoing user participation in co-creation communities.

This paper proposes that collective sentiment is one of the potentially important affective factors that have been overlooked. “Sentiment” refers to positive and negative affective expressions in communication [35]. In the online environment, sentiment is found to help predict the popularity of products and the virality of information [e.g., 8]. Yet little is known about how collective sentiment affects collective online activities—in particular, cocreation output. It is only recently that collective sentiment, or the total number of affective expressions relayed in a community, has been empirically demonstrated to influence the development of discussions in online forums [11]. As another factor, affect-relevant cues from company communications are found to influence future participation intention in cocreation activities [73]. Building on prior research, this paper aims to understand how sentiment functions in online co-creation communities and the way it can be affected by employees’ behavior.

In this endeavor, it is important to examine both the antecedents and the effects of sentiment on community performance. Prior research of online communities suggests that affective tones of community moderators and users’ perceived affect-relevant cues are crucial in influencing user behaviors [72, 73]. In line with this, we investigate if employees participating in co-creation communities can directly affect user sentiment by varying their affective communication style. Whether employees are reactive or proactive and whether they adopt a task- or social-oriented approach are factors that can potentially affect user sentiment. Subsequently, we examine how the collective sentiment may help sustain cocreation communities by influencing collective behaviors. Drawing on signaling theory [10] and message framing [62], we argue that collective sentiment serves as a contextual cue that influences the activities of individual users, which consequently affect and comprise the collective online community performance. The performance of co-creation communities typically includes user creativity (i.e., contributing creative ideas) and user participation (i.e., commenting on the ideas of others).

This research makes several important contributions to the literature. While most prior literature focuses on cognitive and rational aspects of community management, we highlight the importance of affective management. Just as group leaders are expected to manage the collective affective states of groups in offline contexts, our study sheds light on how companies can influence user sentiment through the participation of employees in the online arena. For example, employees who act as community moderators can have a critical influence on motivating and sustaining member contribution [e.g., 70]. By defining the concept of collective sentiment and introducing it into our analysis, we demonstrate how the affective characteristics of a community can drive its user activities; and we simultaneously enrich understanding of the nature of affective states in online co-creation communities.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the concept of sentiment and collective sentiment, followed by a discussion of how the communication style of employees can influence user sentiment. Next, we discuss the influence of collective sentiment on community output. In Section 3, we briefly describe the methodology used in our study, followed by our findings in Section 4. In Sections 5 and 6, we conclude with comments about managerial implications and future research.

## 2. Collective Sentiment and Co-creation Communities

## 2.1 Collective sentiment

Prior literature in information systems often empirically measures collective sentiment as the aggregated sum of individual sentiment in the community [e.g., 11, 12, 52]. Sentiment, as one type of affect, is a person’s positive and/or negative emotional disposition toward another person or object [28]. These affective expressions serve as informational cues for others to evaluate the social environment, thereby shaping individual behaviors [59]. In general, positive sentiment is believed to result in positive responses, whereas negative sentiment elicits negative reactions. Individual sentiment is influenced by that of others. Sentiment affects individual behaviors and subsequently influences group behaviors. For example, in the organizational literature, it was found that individuals could manipulate others to achieve certain goals by adjusting their own affective expressions, which subsequently influence the group affective states [e.g., 14]. In a group, the collective affective states are critical to maintain the viability of a group in achieving its common goals [33].

In an online community, users may share their sentiment with each other through textual interactions. Distinct from offline contexts where verbal and facial communication is used, affective expressions of oneself in an online environment can be reflected only by the written use of emotional words. Prior research in online communities has found evidence that collective sentiment may determine the individuals’ evaluations of online communities [61], which subsequently influence their behaviors. In the context of internet communication, written texts are especially found to facilitate the creation and moderation of collective sentiment, which is critical to sustain the communities [11, 12]. A person’s perceived intensity of affect-relevant cues embedded in the co-creation community is found to directly influence user intention for future participation [73].

It is important to note that while the use of emotional words is significantly associated with individual affective states, their use does not fully represent the feelings that an

individual experiences [66]. In other words, in an online environment, the term “collective sentiment” does not indicate how users feel collectively or individually at all times; it only represents the affective state of the whole community at a certain point in time. Collective sentiment, being the total number of sentiment expressions that occur in the community, merely represents the composition of various sentiments exhibited, which is not necessarily the sentiment that is shared in an online community. We argue that the affective environment that is created by all community members plays a crucial role in influencing future behaviors of users. To better understand the functions of collective sentiment, it is necessary to first discuss the antecedents of the formation of individual sentiment.

## 2.2 Employee communication and user sentiment

In online communities, one way that companies can facilitate the sentiment of a group is by managing the individual sentiment. In line with prior research [e.g., 72], we argue that individual sentiment may be influenced by having direct or indirect contact with employees who are acting as moderators. In general, sentiments can be affected via two pathways: primitive emotional contagion and entrainment of behaviors [13]. The assumption of emotional contagion is that the affective expression of an individual can elicit similar affective reactions from the receivers. Through the process of emotional contagion a person’s affective displays, which can be triggered by the affective displays of others, could influence affective states at the group level [18]. In recent years, a handful of studies on the effects of emotional contagion in an online context have revealed that affective expressions through written forms trigger emotional contagion and influence the affective responses of readers. For example, reading a message with negative sentiment would result in a higher number of negative words used in the replying messages [34].

##

Individual sentiment is also affected via entrainment of behaviors, which occurs when individuals interact with each other in such a way that their communication styles (such as gestures, mannerisms, and lexical choices) synchronize over time. Prior research in marketing and communication suggests that communication styles of individuals can elicit relational perceptions from others. Holding positive sentiment toward a person, such as increased liking and affiliation, would increase the likelihood of having entrained behaviors. In turn, the perceived synchrony of affects and actions with conversational partners would encourage positive responses toward each other [44]. This phenomenon is observed in online contexts. Several scholars have found that online conversations within a group have a tendency to exhibit matching linguistic style with similarities in choices of affective words [e.g., 47]. For example, the more often particular emotional words are used to express sentiment, the more those same words would occur in subsequent comments [37]. In general, communications that contain affective expressions not only are more likely to receive responses, they are more likely to receive responses that express the same sentiment.

While the two pathways provide distinct mechanisms, the outcome of behavioral entrainment is the same as that of emotional contagion, i.e., individuals express similar sentiments. Prior research in offline contexts has extensively studied how managers and team leaders can regulate their affective displays to influence the affective states of receivers. For example, in the service environment, Du, Fan, and Feng [23] found that positive emotional displays by employees increased the number of positive emotional displays by customers, as well as reduced their negative emotional displays. Employees operating on the internet (whether considered managers or moderators of the community) have direct contact with online users. Thus we argue that employees in co-creation communities may influence individual user sentiment via similar mechanisms.

#

Unlike studies in the service and organization literature where receivers are all assumed to have a form of direct contact with leaders and personnel [e.g., 67], on the internet employees have limited means to participate in the process of users formulating sentiment. In organizations, managers can recognize and influence the group affective states at the collective level through group meetings and face-to-face communications. Conversely, in an comments. While these messages tend to be made publicly online, they are not necessarily seen and read by other users. This is especially challenging in an online community where discussions are organized per thread. Individual users may browse and search only for certain threads that interest them. As a result, individual users may never experience direct emotional interactions with employees. Thus we argue that employees are unlikely to have a direct influence on the collective sentiment of the community. Rather, they can influence those who have direct contact with them, i.e., users who participate in the same discussion threads with employees and/or receive direct responses from employees. In line with prior research [23, 37], we propose that positive user sentiment may be enhanced by positive employee sentiment and reduced by negative employee sentiment. Similarly, positive employee sentiment can decrease negative user sentiments, whereas negative employee sentiment can increase them. Accordingly, we formulate Hypothesis 1 as follows:

H1a. Positive employee sentiment increases positive individual user sentiment.

H1b. Positive employee sentiment decreases negative individual user sentiment.

H1c. Negative employee sentiment decreases positive individual user sentiment.

H1d. Negative employee sentiment increases positive individual user sentiment.

Moderators of company-hosted online communities, commonly being employees of the companies, are often instructed to communicate with users in a certain style. The communication style shapes the quality of employees’ communication and signifies the quality of the relationship between a company and its users [42]. Several prior studies have discussed various communication styles of online community moderators that could influence the desired outcomes [15]. For instance, the focus of the communication has been found to influence communication efficiency. In general, these styles can be separated into taskoriented communication, which is directly related to the assigned tasks, and social-oriented communication, which focuses on building relationships. It is suggested that task-oriented communication indicates a good quality of communication, because it is more relevant and reduces uncertainty during the information exchange [1]. However, prior studies have shown that task-oriented communication leads to a tendency for cognitive-based processing and only indirectly influences the affective-based thinking process [15]. In other words, when a message is framed with a task-oriented focus, users are more likely to concentrate on the tasks rather than having emotional responses. Prior empirical online research supports the theory that there is less emotional communication in a task-oriented environment compared to a social-emotional one [21]. We suggest that when users are encouraged to think critically through employees’ task-oriented communication, they are less likely to express their comments affectively. Therefore, we posit Hypothesis 2 as follows:

H2. Employees’ task-oriented communication decreases positive and negative user sentiment.

#

Furthermore, recent theorizing and research on online communication has established the importance of companies adopting a reactive or proactive approach. A proactive communication style, which welcomes online users and encourages their active engagement in online activities, may lead to more favorable user behaviors [41]. Likewise, proactive communication on the part of companies is considered more effective on firm-initiated platforms than on consumer-generated platforms [69]. This is because the purpose of firminitiated platforms, such as company-hosted co-creation communities, is to allow companies to engage proactively in conversations with online users [40]. Similarly, during service encounters a proactive employee is found to enhance a company’s relationship with customers and encourage customers to return [20]. However, proactive communication does not always result in favorable responses. Organizational studies show that proactive behaviors may be perceived negatively by supervisors due to their suspicion of individual motives [43]. In fact, in an online environment, proactive communication is found to result in less affective reactions in interactive conversations compared to reactive communications [46]. In line with this, we suggest that in co-creation communities, proactive communications from employees may result in a lower level of affective reactions. We hypothesize the following:

H3. Employees’ proactive communication decreases positive and negative user sentiment.

2.2 Collective sentiment and community output

Collective sentiment, i.e., the accumulation of thoughts and feelings expressed by the community, represents the affective state of the group at a given point in time. While the

outcomes of co-creation communities depend on the aggregated contributions of individuals, it is only recently that scholars have tried to understand the role of affect in influencing behaviors related to innovation [e.g., 71]. In this research, we focus on creativity as one of the measures for co-creation community performance. Creativity can be broadly defined as the “production of novel and useful ideas in any domain” [2, p. 1155]. While creativity can be seen as a personal capability for generating meaningful and new ideas, in the context of company-hosted co-creation communities, as in our study, creativity is assessed by the sum of creative ideas that a community collectively supplies.

Drawing on signaling theory [10] and message framing [62], we propose that collective sentiment may have direct influence on collective creativity. Signaling theory posits that people rely on peripheral cues as signals to make decisions about their actions [e.g., 5], while message-framing theory holds that whether a signal is positively or negatively framed would influence the receivers’ actions [e.g., 45]. Affective framing of communication is found to create attention and to trigger cognitive involvement of the receivers [62]. In the context of an online community, messages embedded with sentiment appear to receive more responses than those without sentiment. In particular, positive collective sentiment tends to indicate a welcoming state of the community, which represents a positive environment for individual users to explore their creativity [27]. This in turn leads to a higher total number of creative ideas. On the other hand, an online community with lots of negative sentiments signals that many individuals have expressed their negative affect through words. This negative state may not be welcoming or motivating for people to contribute their ideas, as the negative frame indicates a dangerous and risky environment [30]. Users might worry that their ideas will be criticized, given the proportion of negative sentiments that are already expressed in the community. This results in fewer ideas, and in turn, fewer useful ideas.

#

Moreover, affect is directly linked to individual creativity. While no research exists on the relationship between sentiment and creativity, we can draw on emotion research to understand how affective factors can influence creativity. Extant studies suggest that positive emotions lead to a higher level of creativity. People who experience positive emotions tend to be more flexible and to think creatively [e.g., 64]. They are cognitively wide-ranging and able to engage in tasks such as product design. Participants in co-creation communities also claim that experiencing positive emotions such as excitement, fun, and joy motivates them to contribute more ideas [29]. Conversely, negative emotions can hinder cognitive processing [39], which consequently inhibits creative thinking. For example, negative emotions such as anger, anxiety, and envy have been found to reduce a group’s efficiency, particularly its creativity [56]. For a community in which the environment encourages (discourages) more creative thinking, there is a higher possibility that doing so will lead to more (fewer) creative ideas. We argue that sentiment would result in similar consequences as those created by emotions. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows:

H4a. Positive collective sentiment has a positive impact on collective user creativity. H4b. Negative collective sentiment has a negative impact on collective user creativity.

Users may participate in co-creation communities by making comments on the ideas of others. The level of user participation can be observed by the total number of comments made in the community. Affective communication is believed to result in a higher level of responses [37]. Although positively framed messages typically lead to positive responses and negatively framed messages lead to negative ones, past studies have observed a “negativity

bias.” Not only may negative comments lead to a higher impact on readers’ evaluations, people tend to generate more negative comments than positive ones [e.g., 3]. Accordingly, we suggest that when users receive a positively framed signal in the co-creation community, i.e., a collective positive sentiment, they are less inclined to make comments compared to when a negative sentiment is observed. Moreover, a negative environment, i.e., a community that has collective negative sentiment, has been empirically shown to trigger more thread comments [12]. We thus predict that when negative sentiment is prominent in the community, users are more likely to continue commenting, compared to when positive sentiments are prominent. Consequently, positive collective sentiment would result in a lower number of user comments. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows:

H5a. Positive collective sentiment has a negative impact on collective user participation.

H5b. Negative collective sentiment has a positive impact on collective user participation.

## 3. Methods

3.1 Sample and data collection

#

To test the hypotheses, we analyzed a well-developed company-hosted co-creation community, Dell’s IdeaStorm. Since its official launch in February 2007, this community where users can freely register to contribute ideas and make comments has been complimented as one of the best crowd-sourcing practices [63]. Dell constantly updates the status of each idea. Once the ideas are considered and evaluated internally, they remain on the community site with the status indicating whether they are implemented, partially implemented, or not implemented. Unlike many other co-creation practices where companies minimize their involvement during the crowdsourcing process, Dell encourages employees to actively participate in the community, and some of the initial employee activities have been previously investigated [e.g., 22]. There have been few attempts to addre the creativity of this particular community, with studies focusing instead on individual user characteristics [7]. Our study builds on prior research methods while focusing on determining the influence of collective sentiment on collective community output.

We extracted all discussion threads from the community using a web crawler written with Java, including all the ideas, comments, authors, and their time stamps when available. We gathered data from the first posts that appeared at launch on February 12, 2007, to those posted on April 30, 2011. Community users and employees were identified and categorized based on their profiles. During the four-year period, 14,404 ideas were documented, among which 427 (2.96%) had been implemented or partially implemented by Dell; 84,784 comments were extracted, among which 81,054 (95.60%) were posted by 9,436 unique users, and 3,730 (4.40%) were posted by 91 employees. To examine how collective sentiment influences creativity and participation, the longitudinal observations of all comments were aggregated into daily data for further analysis. To study the impact of employees on user sentiment, the data were aggregated into a longitudinal panel structure across users.

First, to inspect the influence of employee communication style on user sentiment, we coded the employee and user comments separately. To examine the influence of employees on individual users, we first verified the number of users who had direct contact with the employees. It turned out that 91 employees responded to a limited number of discussion threads; we focused on those users who appeared in the same threads with the employees and traced their comments after the encounters. Sentiment in our study was coded by counting the number of emotional words in the texts. They were computer-coded using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) [53], based on the percentage of positive and negative emotional words written. The approach has proven useful in previous studies of online communication [e.g., 8]. To externally validate the dictionary, two independent coders were trained to code comments as including positive and/or negative sentiment. A small subset of the overall sample was randomly selected, with 412 comments coded. The differences between the coders were resolved through discussions. The results were then compared with the categories that were assigned based on LIWC coding by considering the intercoder

reliabilities. The Cohen’s Kappa was 0.84 for positive sentiment and 0.82 for negative sentiment, which are sufficient.

Based on the LIWC results, we calculated the average percentage of positive words among all comments posted by user i on day t to represent the user average positive sentiment $\mathrm { ( P O S I T I V E _ { i t } ) }$ and likewise for average negative sentiment from user i on day t $\mathrm { ( N E G A T I V E _ { i t } ) }$ . The sentiment of employees was computer-coded similarly. We coded the employees’ average percentage of positive words during all encounters with user i between the day the user had previously made a comment and day t, on which a new comment from user i was made, as $\mathrm { e P O S I T I V E _ { i t - 1 } }$ , and likewise for negative sentiment $\mathrm { e N E G A T I V E _ { i t - 1 } }$

The other two employee communication factors, namely task-oriented communication style and proactive approach, were manually coded. Task-oriented communication was coded following the same method as in Adjei et al. [1]. Adapting their study, we defined taskoriented comments $( \mathrm { e T A S K } _ { \mathrm { i t - 1 } } )$ as “employee comments that were related to the initial idea posts.” We calculated the average percentage of comments that were classified as taskoriented during the previous encounter(s) with user i until day t, i.e., (task-oriented comments/total number of comments)\*100. Lastly, based on definitions by Kohler et al. [41] and van Noort and Willemsen [69], the comments initiated by employees without any solicitation on the part of users were coded as proactive $\mathrm { ( e P R O A C T I V E _ { i t - } }$ <sub>1</sub>). We then calculated the percentage of comments that were classified as proactive during the prior encounters with user i until day t, i.e., (proactive comments/total number of comments)\*100.

Two coders independently analyzed each employee comment; they also read the title and content of each idea, and the user comments that were written prior to the employees comments. The coders practiced the coding scheme on $5 0$ comments, and necessary changes were made to ensure that the instructions were clear. The two coders then coded all employee comments separately. The intercoder reliability suggested a sufficient result. The Cohen’s Kappa for task-oriented communication and proactiveness was 0.89 and 0.87, respectively. After the independent coding process, the two coders resolved any differences through discussion.

Then we investigated the influence of collective sentiment on community performance. Following the literature, we investigated two dimensions of community performance, namely creativity and participation. While creativity can be seen as a personal capability to generate meaningful and new ideas, in the context of company-hosted co-creation communities, as in our study, creativity is assessed by the sum of creative ideas that a community collectively puts forth. Although companies often rely on users’ commenting and voting to assess which ideas the users prefer, hosting companies mostly determine the final evaluation of the ideas. It is unclear, however, how novelty and usefulness are assessed (separate or combined). Prior research suggests that quality of the community output depends on the number of ideas that can potentially be implemented by the companies [7]. Depending on the purpose of cocreation communities, the emphasis on novelty or usefulness may vary. Since our study focuses on company-hosted co-creation communities, we measure the level of creativity by counting the number of ideas that are implemented by the company. The implemented ideas are a direct reflection of the hosting company’s view on creativity—whether it is useful, new, or both. Thus, CREATIVITY<sub>t</sub> is defined as the number of ideas published on day t that were eventually implemented or partially implemented by the company during the course of observation; PARTICIPATION is defined as the number of comments posted on day t. The time stamps of these activities were based on the initial date that the posts appeared on the platform.

Collective sentiment was coded the same way as individual sentiment. Following prior research [24], we measured the possible short-term and long-term effects of collective sentiment with two distinct variables: daily collective sentiment and cumulative collective sentiment. The positive collective sentiment on day t of the community (POSITIVE<sub>t</sub>) was Similarly, the negative collective sentiment on day t (NEGATIVE<sub>t</sub>) was calculated as the average percentage of negative words from all comments on that day. Cumulative positive collective sentiment on the community forum from day 1 until day t (CUMPOSITIVE<sub>t</sub>) was calculated as the average percentage of all positive words from day 1 until the observing day. Likewise, cumulative negative collective sentiment (CUMNEGATIVE<sub>t</sub>) was calculated as the average percentage of all negative words on the community forum from day 1 until the day of observation. We chose to measure collective sentiment by calculating the average percentage of all positive and negative words expressed by the whole community, instead of the average percentage of all positive and negative messages. The reasons for this choice are three-fold. First, collective sentiment in our study is defined as the aggregated sum of individual sentiment, so taking the average percentage of affective messages will not fully represent the aggregation of sentiment. Second, we believe and observed that positive and negative sentiment may occur at the same time. Consequently, categorizing comments as positive or negative would not fully reflect the affective contents. Finally, in this particular community, the majority of the comments (71.6%) used affective words, but the percentage of affective words used varied $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 5 . 9 , \mathrm { S . D . } = 9 . 5 )$ . In sum, we argue that the average percentage of affective words better represents collective sentiment than other methods. These measurements include both employees and users because they represent the affective states of the community as a whole.

## 3.3 Control variables

When exploring how employees can influence user sentiment with their communication style, we controlled individual user differences and other elements that have been previously identified to influence employee communication quality. Prior research suggests that some people are, in general, happier than others due to individual differences in cognitive and motivational processes [49]. Therefore, we established the baseline of the users’ affective profile according to their overall average emotionality and positivity. Based on the definitions put forward by Berger and Milkman [8], user $\mathrm { E M O T I O N A L I T Y _ { i t - 1 } }$ was quantified as the cumulative average of the total percentage of words that were classified as containing either positive or negative sentiment of user i until the day before day t. The $\mathrm { P O S I T I V I T Y _ { i t - 1 } }$ <sub>1</sub> was quantified as the average of the difference between the percentage of positive words and negative words in each comment from user i until the day before day t. In addition, as it is known that negative and positive affect can occur simultaneously [25], we controlled for the opposite sentiment when predicting user sentiment.

Other employee communication quality measures including timeliness, frequency, and duration of encounters were coded according to Adjei et al. [1]. Timeliness was operationalized as the response latency between an employee’s comments and the prior user comments. In estimating the influence of collective sentiment on community performance, we took into account the short-term and long-term effects of previous community outputs and made them control variables. According to previous literature, internet users are drawn by popular items [24]. The more popular a discussion thread, the more likely that people will continue adding comments to it. We therefore controlled for the participation and number of creative ideas presented in the community until day t, as past activity levels may serve as informational cues that influence users’ decisions to participate in a community. ## 3.4 Analysis methods

We employed Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) to analyze the impact of employee communications on user sentiment at the individual level. This method was chosen to account for the potential inter-correlation between multiple observations per individual user that were due to unobserved individual characteristics [31] and also to avoid potential heteroskedasticity and to produce robust results. To help evaluate the patterns of variable importance within each scale and across scales, we also ran the same analysis with the standardized variables.

Moreover, in our study, creativity was calculated based on how many creative ideas were posted per day. This approach included the underlying assumption that the idea went through a two-stage process. First, there needed to be at least one idea posted, and only from there could an inflow of creative ideas proceed. In addition, the implementation rate has been low on the platform; merely 3% of the ideas were ultimately implemented. These two factors have resulted in many zeros in our data; out of 1,448 days of valid observations, 1,165 days (80.45%) had zero creative ideas. The distribution of CREATIVITY<sub>t</sub> was positively skewed (6.64) and zero-inflated. To model the influence of collective sentiment on creativity, we thus adopted the Hurdle Negative Binomial Regression model (HNB). Following the arguments of Hinz, Skiera, Barrot, & Becker [36], HNB is used to correct the data structure that is highly skewed, over-dispersed, and contains a large share of zeros. The influence of collective sentiment on participation was estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. We applied the Durbin–Wu–Hausman test to investigate whether an instrumental variable is preferred over OLS. The result suggests no evidence of endogeneity $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 . 4 6 , p = 0 . 2 3 )$ and reveals that there is no need for structural modeling [19]. The result of the C-test suggests the same pattern (0.921, p = 0.34). Moreover, because the cumulative positive collective

sentiment and cumulative negative collective sentiment included in our models are highly correlated, multicollinearity was a potential problem. Similarly, the control variables CREATIVITY<sub>t</sub>, CUMCREATIVITY<sub>t−1</sub>, PARTICIPATION<sub>t−1</sub>, and

## 5. Discussion and Implications

## 5.1 Findings and theoretical implications

The surge of online co-creation communities has increased interest in understanding what contributes to the success of such practices. It is clear that affective factors play a key role in motivating user contributions on these platforms, but less is known about how sentiment can be influenced by employees and how it affects the community collective performance. Although online-community research has examined how a certain management style and platform design can enhance user experiences, little is known about how employee contributions can influence user contributions with sentiment as a key mediator. The curren research examines the influence of employee communications on individual users. Furthermore, this study focuses on collective sentiment, i.e., the aggregated sum of individual sentiment, and the relationships between how people express themselves and collective

outcomes, namely creativity and participation. By understanding how companies can manage collective sentiment in ways that subsequently influence community output, we shed light on what drives successful co-creation practices.

Our findings contribute to the existing literature about online co-creation communities. First, they highlight the importance and challenges of managing user sentiment. The results illustrate that although sentiment may not fully represent the true affect that users are experiencing, sentiments are the observable and manageable activities that companies could influence. While negative user sentiment seems hard to change, positive user sentiment increases when positive employee sentiment increases; and user sentiment decreases when employees express negative sentiment. Furthermore, an employee with a proactive communication style appears to decrease user affective expressions, both positive and negative, whereas a task-oriented communication style generally increases the use of emotional words.

We provide empirical evidence that, to a certain extent, employees can manage the user sentiment through their own affective displays, along with other communication styles. Similar to prior studies, our results suggest that implicit affective influence is not restricted to face-to-face communication and can indeed occur in computer-mediated communication. It is important to note, however, that employees have a limited impact on user collective sentiment. This is because, in an online environment, one cannot guarantee that the interactions will be seen by other members who are not part of the conversations. We demonstrate and argue that employees’ influence on individual user sentiment may be somewhat restricted to those who have direct contact with them

In addition, negative user sentiment seems less likely to be influenced by employees, compared to positive user sentiment. Employees can only increase or reduce positive user

#

sentiment with their own emotional displays. Negative user sentiment is more difficult to suppress or to change [58]. This may be because in an environment in which people are used to negative emotions, people may resist emotional change [16]. The fact that people are more likely to comment when they have negative rather than positive sentiment may contribute to this propensity. Even if users are positively influenced by employee positive sentiment, they may still do nothing to change their own negative displays and hence cannot be observed. This makes it challenging for companies to address negative user sentiment. While most of the prior research on online communication focuses on handling negative comments, this research highlights the importance and effectiveness of managing the positive content.

Along with affective displays, we found that employees’ task-oriented comments and a proactive communication style significantly influence user sentiment. Proactiveness in particular reduces user affective expressions, both positive and negative. This suggests that even in company-hosted co-creation communities where direct feedback is welcomed and needed [22], proactive participation by an employee may not always lead to favorable outcomes. While proactiveness softens negative user sentiment, it also reduces positive user sentiment. This might help explain why proactive participation by an employee is not always appreciated by forum users [26]. Because user participation (commenting behaviors in particular) is driven mainly by negative affect, as confirmed in this study, users may not be motivated to contribute to a less emotionally charged environment.

On the other hand, task-oriented communication of employees was found to elicit both positive and negative sentiment, which is opposite to what was hypothesized. This may be because of the actual content of the employee communication. Drawing on affective events theory, feedback about task performances (failure of a task in particular) is regarded as an affective event in the workplace, which can induce emotional responses [17]. Similarly, in the context of co-creation communities, task-oriented comments by employees may be perceived as a direct feedback to user ideas and preceding discussions, which leads to subsequent user emotional responses. This may be an interesting aspect for future research.

The present research introduces the concept of collective sentiment in studying online co-creation communities. We propose that collective sentiment can be used to predict the cocreation community’s collective creativity and participation. Positive collective sentiment ha no impact on creativity and lowers the level of participation, whereas negative collective sentiment reduces creativity and increases user participation. Previous studies have focused on functional aspects of co-creation platforms and suggestions for modifying the platform design to enhance user experiences [e.g., 41]. However, hedonic motivations such as the pursuit of pleasure and enjoyment, which are often associated with emotions, are important for increasing co-creation participation [4]. The results of the present study support our hypothesized framework that collective sentiment signifies the affective environment of the community, which accounts for subsequent idea submissions and comment making. Our study provides the theoretical accounts for the possible underlying mechanism. Based on signaling theory and framing theory, we suggest that collective sentiment, revealed through texts embedded with either positive or negative words, serves as a cue to assist individuals making decisions on how to contribute in the community.

It is worth noting that collective sentiment has an opposite effect on creativity and user participation. In particular, the opposite effect of negative collective sentiment decreasing creativity and increasing user participation is evident in our study. This highlights the importance of making a distinction between contributing novel ideas (i.e., initiating a discussion) and making comments (i.e., participating in discussions in future co-creation studies). The results also suggest that negative collective sentiment may indeed function as a contextual peripheral signal. Users may interpret it as a cue indicating that the community has a risky and problematic environment [30], which discourages creative thinking but encourages contributions of comments to help solve this problem.

One may argue that the large number of comments triggered by negative collective sentiment may also be more negative, which is generally not preferred by companies when hosting online communities [e.g., 51]. However, just as allowing users to engage in negative dialogues can enhance customer bonding in online brand communities [65], we found that comments with negative sentiment could still be useful in online co-creation communities. For instance, responding to an idea of improving a Dell online shop in Germany, one user commented, “i [I] do totally agree, the marketing in germany is crazy: prices going up and down…prices are obfuscated…very very bad marketing! and: well I´m very upset about dell keeping systems sold in europe up to date.” The comment, while being negative, continued the discussion initiated by the first post and contributed more insights into the potential problems to be improved. This suggests that not only positive comments in co-creation communities may be “good” participation. Thus, the challenge remains that companies need to decide which types of online activities they wish to emphasize. The paradoxical effect highlights the importance of managerial choices to design a highly creative community with less discussions or a less creative one with more discussions.

Previous studies on the influence of affect have focused on personal, short-term, and immediate influence [e.g., 8]. Our results suggest that it is mainly the cumulative collective sentiment that shapes the user activities. It is perhaps less likely that users will judge the affective environment of a community based on what has happened on a particular day. The long-term collective sentiment serves as a better signal to assist user decision-making. In particular, it takes time for a community to establish its collective sentiment with aggregated contributions from all users. Cumulative collective sentiment also indicates that a certain sentiment is recurring over time and accounts for a higher percentage in the total composition of sentiment. While sentiment was found to partially represent the users’ feelings [64], the cumulative collective sentiment may be a better representation of how users actually feel. This is in contrast to short-term collective sentiment of the day, which may not allow users to identify their own sentiment.

## 5.2 Managerial implications

Our study sheds light on how companies can manage co-creation communities by closely monitoring and managing the collective sentiment. If companies decide to have employees participating in co-creation communities, it is more efficient to regulate user sentiment through adapting the employee communication style of being task-oriented or proactive, instead of controlling employee affective displays. This is perhaps less demanding for employees, as overwhelming emotional labor can cause work withdrawal [60]. When employees are experiencing emotional exhaustion in an unwanted emotionally charged interaction, they may end up suppressing affective displays even if they are instructed not to. However, it is worth noting that companies can either evoke overall emotional responses by posting task-related comments or reduce the sentiment by proactively participating in online conversations. This indicates that it may be difficult to control and demand particular user sentiment. It is critical to carefully monitor the development of collective sentiment and to adapt the style in a timely manner. For example, when dealing with unwanted negative sentiment, employees may proactively engage in the conversations but be restricted to administrative content instead of task-oriented comments.

#

Moreover, companies should be aware that employees seem to have an indirect influence on collective sentiment via influencing individual sentiment. Companies tend to assume that as the conversations between employees and consumers take place publicly online, there will be positive spillover effects with their positive responses. In fact, based on our study, the effects may be limited to those people who are actually part of the conversations. This would mean that in online communities, companies need to have employees interacting with as many users as possible in order to generate positive impacts. Conversely, the negative conversations between employees and users may not be as damaging as companies generally anticipate, since most users may not even be aware of them.

Since negative collective sentiment has opposite effects on two community outputs— namely creativity and user participation—companies should choose a managerial approach based on their purpose for establishing a co-creation community. The counter-effects set a paradoxical challenge for companies to determine the goal of a community prior to its full development and to adjust company communication style accordingly during the process. In particular, if the priority is to maximize the creative output of innovation platforms, companies should focus on reducing negative sentiment. On the other hand, when the priority is to encourage comments and conversations, negative sentiment is less of an issue. Yet companies should be aware that a continuing negative sentiment would eventually reduce the willingness of users to submit creative ideas. This approach would be more suitable for companies that allow consumers to give comments on company-initiated ideas rather than encouraging the users to comment on user-developed ones.

## 5.3 Limitations and future research

#

Even though our research offers valuable insights into how sentiments influence community performances, it has some limitations that require further research. Since the research involved only one specific platform, the results need to be treated with circumspection. In particular, combining other functional design factors that are suggested to environment may influence the collective outputs. In addition, we note that employees may have limited influence on user sentiment in our study, which is based on a relatively largescale open community. Employees may have better and more significant influence on user sentiment in a tightly knitted small community. It would be interesting for future research to investigate under what conditions employees may have a direct impact on collective sentiment. Moreover, due to the exploratory nature of investigating how collective sentiment works in the context of online co-creation, specific sentiments have not been identified or specified. Prior research in organization literature suggests that specific emotions such as anger and joy would influence group performance better than others [e.g., 6]. It would be interesting to further identify which specific emotional dispositions can most powerfully influence collective community outputs and how.

In addition, in organization literature, emotions that are shared by a group of individuals are commonly referred to as “collective emotions.” Collective emotions occur when individuals experience the same emotions and convert into an emotionally homogenou group [57]. Individual contributions to collective emotions may vary, because everyone experiences emotions differently. In our study, collective sentiment is theorized and operationalized as the aggregated sum of individual sentiment, where individual sentiment contributes equally to reflect the affective states of the community at a given moment. It would be worthwhile to investigate how individuals interpret the collective sentiment and translate that into shared sentiment. Especially of interest is the use of emotional words,

which are significantly correlated with individual emotional dispositions yet do not fully represent them. While sentiment, as operationalized in our study by counting emotional words, proves useful in helping companies predict and monitor co-creation communities, the relationships between experienced and expressed sentiment are worthy of further investigation to establish the full effects of sentiment on users.

Similarly, regarding the employee communication style, it would also be useful to further clarify what specific sentiments are elicited by employee task-oriented communication. Considering that it generates both positive and negative sentiment, it is critical to understand exactly how users respond to employee task-related comments. Related to this, future research might also examine how sentiment interacts with the cognitive information in a message. The task-oriented communication style is a starting point to better understand how the content of employee communications should be formulated. In addition, prior research on collective emotion in organizational literature demonstrates the importance of the group leader identifying the dynamics of the emotional composition of groups [57]. The success of managing collective sentiment partially depends on how well employees can detect the current aggregate sentiment in the community. On top of investigating the relationships between experienced and expressed collective sentiment, future research might examine how the emotional intelligence of employees influences the development of cocreation communities. This study, though only partially identifying the effect of affective influence of employees on users, does suggest that the detailed study of how sentiment functions in an online community is a promising line of future inquiry.

## 6. Conclusion

#

Our study suggests that user activities in co-creation communities are influenced by sentiment. Employees may manage the community output by effectively influencing individual user sentiment through adapting company communications to the style of users. The results offer an alternative approach for maintaining and designing user co-creation communities. Different from the current focus on identifying user motivations [e.g., 4] and platform design [e.g., 29], our study suggests that community development is an ongoing process. With the change of collective sentiment, communities develop their creativity and user participation. Cumulative negative collective sentiment in particular can increase future user participation but reduce subsequent creativity. Collective sentiment—aggregated sentiment contributed by all participants—is critical in shaping collective community outputs.
