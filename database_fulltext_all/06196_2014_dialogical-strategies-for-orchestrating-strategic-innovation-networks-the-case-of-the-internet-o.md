# Dialogical strategies for orchestrating strategic innovation networks: The case of the Internet of Things

Karl Prince <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Michael Barrett <sup>a</sup>, Eivor Oborn <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Judge Business School, University of Cambridge, Cambridge CB2 1AG, United Kingdom

<sup>b</sup> Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, United Kingdom

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 18 February 2013 Received in revised form 3 May 2014 Accepted 5 May 2014 Available online 22 May 2014

Keywords: Strategic innovation Innovation networks Dialogue Values Interests Legitimacy RFID Internet of Things

## a b s t r a c t

Strategic innovation has been shown to provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value for organisations whilst at the same time challenging traditional ways of thinking and working. There is less known, however, as to how organisations collaborate in innovation networks to achieve strategic innovation. In this paper we explore how innovation networks are orchestrated in developing a strategic innovation initiative around the Internet of Things. We show how a hub actor brings together a diverse group of actors to initially create and subsequently orchestrate the strategic innovation network through the employ of three dialogical strategies, namely persuasive projection, re<sup>fl</sup>ective development, and de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control. Further, we illuminate how different types of legitimacy are established through these various dialogical strategies in orchestrating strategic innovation networks.

## 1. Introduction

“A world, where everything that moves can talk to everyone, everywhere, all the time” [(RFID Conference Presentation)]

This is a vision which started in the early 2000s and has become commonly known as ‘The Internet of Things’. Essentially, it reframed the Internet as not just connecting computers but envisioned a future where everyday objects would become part of computer networks. The RFID-based innovation was perceived to create novel and unprecedented forms of value by making fundamental changes to the way companies and industries operate, along with potentially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on wider society. Such innovations constitute what is often referred to as strategic innovation, which is focused on capturing high growth and generating signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value through the rede<sup>fi</sup>nition of markets, customers, and business and operating models (Govindaraian & Trimble, 2012). However, the strategic innovation literature has traditionally focused solely on the organisational level as to how leaders and managers develop new strategic initiatives to gain new markets and beat competitors (c.f. Teece, 2009; Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997; Schlegelmilch, Diamantopoulos & Kreuz, 2003; Sillince, Jarzabkowski, & Shaw, 2012). This leaves a gap in our understanding of how wider innovation networks might work together to develop novel strategic directions, whilst remaining in formal competition with each other.

In this paper we seek to understand how such strategic innovations are brought about through the collaborative efforts of an innovation network guided by a hub organisation. In developing our understanding of collaborative strategic innovation, we draw on our longitudinal in-depth case study of the PhysNet innovation network to examine the underlying dynamics of a network brought together to create an ‘Internet of Things’. Whilst earlier work has tended to focus on the types and structures of these networks, the factors that affect successful innovation, and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they bring about (e.g. Kastelle & Steen, 2010; Klincewicz, 2009; O'Shea, Allen, Chevalier, & Roche, 2005; Steiner, Gil, Ehret, & Ploder, 2010; Williams, 2005), our particular emphasis on dialogical strategies builds on the less developed research on the processes of innovation within technology innovation networks (Swan & Scarbrough, 2005), particularly around the achievement of strategic innovation (Grant & Marshak, 2011)

Recent work (Heracleous, 2002; Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005) has highlighted the role of dialogue as an enabler of strategic innovation, particularly in the reframing of mental models. Dialogue enables the critical review of existing mental models and an opportunity for new mental models to be formed (Ford & Ford, 1995; Gergen, Gergen, & Barrett, 2004) with potential for fostering the fundamental changes needed for strategic innovation. Furthermore, dialogue, which draws on stakeholders' diverse values and interests, can expose assumptions and (re)shape values and interests, enabling innovation and knowledge creation (Tsoukas, 2009). It can reveal the differences between stakeholders, including variances in values and interests, facilitating mutual challenges to perspectives and consensual legitimation (Calton & Kurland, 1996, p. 170), particularly relevant in strategic innovation networks made up of heterogeneous actors.

Additionally, dialogue can legitimate management practices enabling leaders and managers to reasonably pursue particular valued goals (Green, 2004 cf. Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999). As highlighted by Garud and Rappa (1994), ‘without legitimacy, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to attract others to participate in developing the technology to a more advanced state. Thus, a new technology is in a precarious state during its early stages of conception (p. 358). An important challenge then for innovation networks, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally those leading the innovation process, is to establish a degree of legitimacy, since it may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to attract others to participate in its development (Birkinshaw, Hamel, & Mol, 2008; Schilling, 2005; Stjernberg & Philips, 1993).

Our paper focuses on how leading actors within innovation networks coordinate, direct and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the network members (Nambisan & Sawhney, 2011) through, for example, facilitating knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows and stabilising the network (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006) in establishing legitimacy. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c research question is: How does a hub actor orchestrate a strategic innovation network and what is the role of dialogu in such an orchestration?

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal three dialogical strategies used by the hub actor responsible for assembling and orchestrating the innovation network, as well as for excluding some actors from participating in the development of strategic value. In so doing, we contribute novel insights on the dialogical strategies facilitating and constraining strategic innovation within innovation networks.

## 2. Strategic innovation and innovation networks

Strategic innovation is concerned with fundamental changes to the way organisations operate (Hamel, 1998). Given that maintaining competitive advantage is transitory (Ghemawat, 2002; Markides, 1999), strategic innovation can aid continuous innovation, avoiding the danger of going along with what may appear to be best practices but offer no sustainable uniqueness because all competitors are similarly engaged (Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005). In their review, Schlegelmilch et al. (2003) offer the following de<sup>fi</sup>nition of strategic innovation:

Strategic innovation is the fundamental reconceptualisation of the business model and reshaping of existing markets (by breaking rules and changing the nature of competition) to achieve dramatic value improvements for customers and high growth for companies. (p. 118)

The authors highlight that strategic innovation involves asking fundamental questions about the business and the assumptions that govern the way business is conducted. Questioning the rules enables organisations to break these rules, making competition irrelevant. Strategic innovation is not about making incremental advancements but instead making signi<sup>fi</sup>cant step changes in value.

Strategic innovation can be fostered in a variety of ways. These include adapting culture to create a questioning attitude, changing process to include creative exploration, engaging with the right people to access wider perspectives, and using effective resources enabled by forming strong relationships with networks of partners (Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005; Schlegelmilch et al., 2003). Govindaraian and Trimble (2012) highlight the role of learning through strategic experiments and the use of theory focused planning. Strategic innovation can be achieved by managing paradoxical tensions (Baden-Fuller & Stopford, 1994; Sillince et al., 2012) through pushing conceptual and cognitive limitations and encouraging sensemaking and creative thinking (Tse, 2013).

Whilst the focus of literature on strategic innovation has revolved around organisations and the strategic thinking of the organisation's leaders, the innovation literature has increasingly focused on collaboration between multiple organisations and networks. Interacting through networks offers many potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Hotz-Hart, 2000), including better access to information, knowledge skills and experience, improved linkages and cooperation between network members, improved response capacity, and reduced risk. Studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed various network arrangements such as alliances, consortiums and joint ventures (e.g. Baum, Calabrese, & Silverman, 2000; Gulati, 1998; Inkpen, 2000; Ring, Doz, & Olk, 2005). Within collaborative networks, actors with different worldviews and interpretive schemes are brought together, providing sources of new ideas and ways of innovating.

As McLoughlin, Koch, and Dickson (2001) point out, there is to some degree a paradox in organisations coming together as a network. Whilst there are gains in terms of reducing uncertainties in dynamic and unpredictable market contexts there are also risks in collaborating with, for instance, competitors and working in new organisational forms. There are often occasions of con<sup>fl</sup>ict between actors as they seek to support their own interests versus that of the network, for example, where there is a lack of trust or cooperation (Child & Faulkner, 1998), or where there are attempts to manipulate others (Constantinides & Barrett, 2006). In one sense innovation networks can be seen as political systems where there are both coincidental and competing motivations, and politics, in terms of intentionality, motives, actions and behaviours, is an important part of how innovation is brought about (McLoughlin et al., 2001). These tensions also increase the challenge for both legitimating the technological innovation (Birkinshaw et al., 2008; Garud & Rappa, 1994; Stjernberg & Philips, 1993) as well as network collaboration as a valid entity and organisational form (Human & Provan, 2000) for strategic innovation. Given the current turbulent context organisations face today, collaborating in an innovation network centred on a strategic innovation effort is likely to be questioned as organisations will also seek to support their individual competitive and strategic advantages. With no hierarchical controls, coordinating networks is a challenge and the role of the hub actor in orchestrating innovation processes is a key element of its functioning (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006).

In Dhanaraj and Parkhe's (2006) model of innovation networks, the central role is occupied by a hub <sup>fi</sup>rm with the prominence and power to establish and accomplish leadership by integrating the distributed resources and competences of the innovation network members. Orchestration in this sense is about ensuring that knowledge can be acquired, shared and deployed in the network (knowledge mobility), ensuring that knowledge development activities occur in an agreed framework and ultimately deliver value to the innovation network rather than to competing external entities (innovation appropriability). The hub entity is also critical in establishing network stability needed for positive growth and <sup>fl</sup>uidity of membership (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006). Yet, beyond conceptual understanding of important tasks that hub entities need to accomplish, there remains a gap in our understanding of how these processes can be orchestrated, and particularly how this is accomplished in the absence of a hub entity holding central or positional power in the network. As highlighted by Nambisan and Sawhney (2011), further investigation of innovation networks should be researched, to reveal the different nature of orchestration processes. In this article we examine the orchestration processes of strategic innovation networks and unpack the dialogical strategies used by the central hub entity in building, maintaining and disbanding the collaborative network.

## 3. Facilitating dialogue in strategic innovation networks

Dialogue has been shown to be an important facilitator of strategic innovation by enabling existing mental models to be questioned, and shaping their emergence which can lead to a shift in mental models and a revision of strategic paradigms. This, in turn, may result in the fundamental changes and radical advances in the value of a strategic innovation initiative (Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005). The strategy innovation process is particularly effective when it is supported by dialogue that traverses functional and hierarchical boundaries engaging multiple actors and groups in a meaningful way (Floyd & Wooldridge, 1994; Hamel, 1996, 1998; Hamel & Prahalad, 1989). In addition, dialogues that span organisational and industry boundaries increase the chance of new strategic insights emerging by engaging with the wider perspectives of external actors (Krinsky & Jenkins, 1997; Markides, 1999; Tushman, Anderson, & O'Reilly, 1997).

From the perspective of a hub entity attempting to create and orchestrate an innovation network, dialogue as a form of rhetoric plays an important role in legitimising the practices and processes it undertakes (Green, 2004). Legitimacy is necessary for the hub entity to justify its central position and reinforce its role as orchestrator, setting up its credibility, enhancing the continuity of its efforts and making its message and actions comprehensible to others (Suchman, 1995). Legitimacy here is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a perception ‘that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, beliefs and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions’ (Suchman, 1995, p. 574). It can be categorised as pragmatic (gained by demonstrating the value an entity can provide to others), moral (whether an entity is adjudged to be doing the right things), and cognitive (whether an entity's actions are comprehensible and can be perceived as taken-for-granted to which no alternatives exist) (Suchman, 1995). As highlighted by Birkinshaw et al. (2008) innovation leaders need to seek out stakeholders that will validate and make sense of an emerging innovation in order to build its internal and external legitimacy. This can be accomplished through discursive theorisation and labelling, thereby verifying the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of current industry challenges and the validity of the proposed innovation as a response (Birkinshaw et al., 2008).

In their study of dialogue in strategic innovation within organisations, Jacobs and Heracleous (2005) show how dialogue, as re<sup>fl</sup>ective conversation, has a dual nature. The <sup>fi</sup>rst being a diagnostic moment, where actors critically review their existing mental models, and the second being a generative moment, where new, emergent mental models are shaped. These moments together enable the collective thought process to surpass the individual thought process (Schein, 1999). Within dialogue it is necessary to avoid enforcing a single perspective, to set aside assumptions so that multiple points of view can be heard (Ford & Ford, 1995; Tsoukas, 2009). Given this orientation, disagreements can be regarded as opportunities to explore assumptions as a means to reach consensus (Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005).

Through dialogue, as a form of discourse, actors draw on deep seated ideologies and values to proactively frame others' interpretations (Barrett, Heracleous, & Walsham, 2013) as they seek to support their individual interests. Whilst dialogue may be a route to reaching consensus it is framed by the values and interests that actors bring with them. Values can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the principles around which individuals and groups organise their attitudes and behaviours (Walsh, Hinings, Greenwood, & Ranson, 1981) and refer to commitments to sets of ideas, norms, attitudes and beliefs (Young, 1977). They represent standards of desired ends and underlie speci<sup>fi</sup>c purposes and intentions (Ranson, Hinings, & Greenwood, 1980). A consequence is that ‘values act to legitimate some actions and structures rather than others’ (Walsh et al., 1981, p. 138). Importantly, values as deep structures are highly stable (Barrett et al., 2013; Heracleous & Barrett, 2001).

Values, though, are not isolated or idealistic but are connected to material interests. Whilst values relate to desires and preferences, interests can be related to costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Saunders, 1979). Individuals and groups have stakes in the distribution of resources and it is in their interest to ensure that resources are distributed in their favour (Carlile, 2002). The result is that some interests become dominant whilst others remain repressed or at most attempt to challenge existing dominant interests (Heracleous, 2006). From a collaborative innovation perspective, the concept of interests acknowledges that the various actors involved in strategic innovation networks are, to varying degrees, wanting to serve their own interests whilst paradoxically wanting to serve the collectivity of the network. Ultimately, outcomes are determined by actors according to both their values and interests in opposition to other actors with opposing values and interests (Swan & Scarbrough, 2005; Walsh et al., 1981).

In this paper we uncover dialogical strategies in innovation networks by examining how network entities come together and are orchestrated to innovate strategically around an ‘Internet of Things’. We focus on how dialogue is framed by competing values and interests in challenging the existing industry viewpoint. Through dialogue some values are privileged, and others may be excluded with network stakeholders unable to effectively challenge the prevailing interests and values across the network. Thus an important orchestration role is to define the possible content of the conversations, who can participate in the conversations and how. New meanings and new strategic possibilities are born, in part out of bringing together diverse and competing stakeholder values but also by framing whose and which values and interests are relevant to participate in the network dialogue in the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance.

## 4. Research methodology

The research conducted a two year longitudinal in-depth case study based on an interpretive methodology (Walsham, 1995). A particular aim is to gain an understanding of the innovation process in its natural setting, which “involves getting inside the world of those generating it” (Rosen, 1991:8). As researchers we attempt to construct interpretations that explain the subjective meanings created and sustained as part of these social processes, by exposing how meanings and practices are formed and informed by norms, values, and language.

The data collected for this research was obtained from a number of sources. Firstly, data was collected through participant observation in which the authors worked within the ConnectNet Centre developing case studies for sponsor companies, recording notes and re<sup>fl</sup>ections from these various interactions. This enabled the <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers to develop relationships with these actors which not only enabled insights as to their perspectives but also facilitated access for data collection, including access to a variety of strategic and operational meetings, documentation, meeting minutes and presentations. Secondly, 30 semi-structured interviews were conducted with individuals from the Centre, sponsor companies and privacy advocates. Interview length varied but the typical interview lasted about 1 h. All the interviews were recorded and transcribed. Thirdly, we collected a wide range of secondary data on the development of PhysNet as an instantiation of the Internet of Things. This technology innovation was closely followed in the media, particularly as the Internet of Things became increasingly central in privacy debates. We utilised as much of the relevant news articles as was possible sourcing articles from a range of industry speci<sup>fi</sup>c reporting e.g. RFID Journal, Information Week, to more general news reporting such as the Washington Times, The Guardian, and BBC News.

The initial coding of the data identi<sup>fi</sup>ed broad themes of interest within the innovation process, such as the attempts to manage the development of the innovation, the roles of various actors and the relationships between them, the successes being made and the challenges that were faced. At this stage privacy concerns were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a particular point of interest as interviewees independently raised the issue from various perspectives. This highlighted the difference in perspectives within and beyond the innovation network, drawing the researcher's attention to examine, through additional coding rounds, the approach to the coordination and control of the innovation network as orchestration processes by the hub organisation, as well as the interaction of the innovation network with the external context. Further coding revealed the nature of these interactions and highlighted the central role of dialogue as a mechanism for both facilitating and delimiting the innovation process. See Table 1 for an example of the coding process. Additionally, during and between coding rounds we iterated between the empirical material and theoretical concepts in order to support our selection of codes but also to offer new insights and perspectives on the data we were examining (Klein & Myers, 1999).

As this was a longitudinal study, we were able to use later interviews to check the relevance of our initial coding, re<sup>fi</sup>ne themes, and identify new ones based on the additional data we were collecting. We also corroborated and further developed our themes and interpretations across the various data sources by comparing our own experiences in the Centre, the interview data, documentation we had obtained, and the news articles published.

## 5. Case description

The ConnectNet Centre was founded by a group consisting of a renowned university, two global standards bodies, and two global fast moving consumer goods companies, as a central organisation to coordinate the development of a new innovation, the PhysNet as an innovation enabling the Internet of Things aimed at radically transforming their industries. In the four years of its operation the ConnectNet Centre grew and changed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, expanding from one university-based research centre to six centres — see Table 2. By the end of its operations the Centre had over one hundred companies join its initiative. Member companies were split between end users as those companies who were intending to use the technology, and technology vendors, as those companies who would develop and sell the technologies and systems. This split was deliberate as the Centre aimed to control the behaviour of various companies, e.g. prevent vendors from selling solutions to users and prevent the formation of splinter groups working on individual solutions. Overseeing the Centre's operations were an executive director, research directors each representing one of the university-based research centres and two Boards, one representing end users, and the other technology vendors. Funding was raised through joining fees that companies paid when becoming members of the Centre. In return member companies would be given access to research progress and results as soon as it became available. They would also have the opportunity to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the innovation process through being able to propose technology requirements and contribute to standards setting processes. The Centre was at the outset keen to constrain the time allotted to the innovation process. The leadership wanted to avoid a drawn-out innovation effort, instead focusing on making suf<sup>fi</sup>cient progress for the underlying designs and formative technologies to be developed with the ultimate aim of enabling the different stakeholders to pursue their individual interests independently.

The ConnectNet Centre's focus was the development of the PhysNet, which was envisioned by the Centre as a ubiquitous Internet of Things capable of identifying billions of products uniquely in an interconnected network that included everyday physical objects. The innovation was based on radio frequency identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (RFID) technology. Similar to a barcode, a RFID tag, consisting of an integrated circuit chip and an antenna, is placed on an object in order to uniquely identify it and facilitate tracking of the object. Unlike a barcode, a RFID tag does not need to be scanned in line of sight and in particular orientations but is more robust as regards obstructions between tags and readers, and the orientations of the tags. Furthermore, by creating a network connection the tag could be linked to databases containing product information. In this way, ordinary objects could become part of the broader network that we call the Internet which, rather than only connecting computing-related devices would connect these non-computing objects: hence the term, Internet of Things.

After a few years of initial development the PhysNet innovation had become more widely known. Widespread adoption of PhysNet would likely result in an increase in data collection, an increase in the number of transient events being permanently recorded, and data being gathered almost anywhere at any time. Some of these issues, together with the wider concern posed by RFID, have been noted in the academic literature (Taghaboni-Dutta & Velthouse, 2006). As such, questions were being raised as to the potential privacy violations the technology may hold. Privacy advocates voiced most of these privacy concerns, their arguments centring on consumer privacy. The main privacy issue raised by privacy advocates concerned the fact that since PhysNet would be capable of identifying, tracking and tracing individual products it would be possible to identify, track and trace people associated with those products.

## 6. Orchestrating strategic innovation networks

## 6.1. Creating a strategic innovation network

An early challenge in orchestrating the innovation network was to attract support for the innovation process. Creating a network was key if the innovation was to enable signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change for individual companies as well as the wider industry ecosystem. In order to do so, the Centre focused on creating and communicating the strategy underpinning the innovation which would capture the attention of the intended audience.

Nobody has ever been able to draw such a massive attention to the technology for common supply chain applications. … the [ConnectNet Centre] has been [able] to draw the attention of the industry. That there was a possibility [of] a better way to do things using this RFID technology. [Senior manager of a sponsor company]

The Centre successfully focused its efforts on creating dialogues about the innovation with and between potential collaborators in order to get them to join the network.

What the [ConnectNet Centre] did extremely well was I believe the communication of the potential. … Without the [ConnectNet Centre] drive, RFID would not be a very much spoken about technology, only maybe amongst the geeks. So the ConnectNet Centre really brought it mainstream. [End user RFID team manager]

Whether those dialogues were completely accurate was debatable but the Centre's main interest, particularly at the outset, was to raise awareness and get organisations to join the network, which the Centre was able to accomplish through its discussions.

The [ConnectNet Centre] could tell the end users, ‘we're doing things different so we can make cheaper tags.’ No one could really verify that story and lots of people said, ‘that's not true, we doubt that,’ but [the Centre] … got the end users convinced. [ConnectNet Centre research team member]

The Centre created the dialogues with the industry and business communities in a planned and deliberate manner. It did so by embarking on an extensive marketing and media campaign in an attempt to engage with a wide audience.

The main way of getting messages across [was through] a lot of events … my key job for a year and a half … was to think about communication worldwide and write … about what we had to say to whom and how we were going to get adopted. [ConnectNet Centre manager]

In particular, the role of the Centre leadership through its Executive Director was seen as a key element of success. His ability to communicate was recognised as highly effective. By communicating the Centre's interests in a targeted fashion using a variety of channels to connect with the right people the Centre was able to attract the type of membership it needed to both start and maintain the innovation network. In this early phase, dialogues were characteristic of marketing strategies that sought to draw stakeholders' attention and make them captive to the message and build on the innovation's strategic potential.

[The Executive Director] got the technology in Time Magazine, in USA Today … He got it in places where the executive would read it and understand its implication on their industry. You've got to provide different mediums for different ears and I think that that's what they did. [Technology vendor senior manager]

Thus the early phases entailed gaining interest and participation from a range of stakeholders. They worked to initiate and determine what the strategic conversation would be about and which industry perspectives were valuable. Yet, as a strategic innovation it was not entirely obvious how stakeholders would immediately bene<sup>fi</sup>t from joining the Centre's efforts. There was, however, a recognition that the innovation held the potential to change the landscape for both organisations and industries and that being part of the innovation network was key to gaining access to potential competitive advantages over time.

I sit here as a manufacturer who's going to make no business case out of this for a long period of time, it's gonna cost us a lot of money and what am I telling my business? … get involved and do something about it … we should be there … We don't want to be sitting on the outside banging on the door saying, ‘can we join in.’ [End user manager]

[End users] believe that as they trial this technology they're gonna discover more and more ways to use it and … they're going to be able to apply it more ubiquitously and … costs are going to fall and the ROI is gonna be there. [Chairman, Board for end users]

Stakeholders were persuaded that in order to gain strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the innovation, they would need to participate in shaping the technology and take part in the network discussion on the innovation development. In this way, they aimed to demonstrate that the innovation would yield valuable outputs in return for their efforts. As dialogue around PhysNet developed, a momentum built up around the innovation so that the Centre was able to attract many organisations willing to pay to become part of the innovation network and thereby support the innovation process.

The ConnectNet Centre became a self-perpetuating story because the more the people envisaged where it was going and talked the game up at a senior level, the more the technologists wanted to meet the challenge. … And as a result all the industry players and everybody started coming along with it as well. … You could see it being done. You could see it being manipulated. [Technology vendor RFID team manager]

## 6.2. Orchestrating for collaboration

Having convinced a range of organisations to join the innovation network, the ConnectNet Centre set about creating the space to collaborate and develop new forms of value. It was only through collaboration of the wider group that the novel PhysNet could be developed; the Centre sought to ensure that the innovation network was a place where people could share ideas openly. In this way open and frank discussions became normatively considered as procedurally consistent with network collaboration dynamics. As an established norm, open sharing of ideas became valued as an important means for achieving innovation, enhancing moral legitimacy for collaboration.

I thought the way [the ConnectNet Centre] set about creating an atmosphere to share was very good. Their style of conducting the meetings, the style of language, their intent, was all extremely collaborative. So it almost made you feel like you'd be letting the side down if you didn't at least give an honest opinion … As a result of this sort of continuous and consistent climate … I think people felt very con<sup>fi</sup>dent to share their view. [Technology vendor manager]

Setting up the collaborative environment gave end users an opportunity to work more closely with technology vendors. By enabling dialogues between stakeholder groups from diverse perspectives, the Centre gave the companies the space to share requirements and solutions whilst identifying their interests in how the innovation developed. Open and re<sup>fl</sup>ective discussion about the innovation was as important as a practical development for <sup>fi</sup>nding a path through the unclear innovation landscape that lay ahead.

One of the advantages companies could see in joining the Centre was the ability to discuss the innovation with others. This advantage was two-fold. In the cases where organisations already had a relationship outside of the innovation network (e.g. customer–supplier relationships), being part of the same innovation process could further strengthen these relationships, ensuring the innovation would meet their mutual requirements. But the Centre afforded an opportunity to have discussions with other organisations they were not doing business with, in order to learn from their experiences, and potentially form new relationships.

I guess our third motivation [for joining the Centre] as a service provider was clearly it gave us the chance to interface from an end user point of view with similarly like-minded players who were part of our customer base, although in some instances they're not customers. … it gave us a chance to talk to people that we weren't otherwise talking to in terms of their thoughts and use of the technology. [End user manager]

The diversity of the various actors engaging in dialogue was seen as an important part of generating learning as worldviews and values were challenged.

Once you start to describe things in a fundamental way, people start to understand. And that's what I found with these different groups coming together with different views of the world. Like if you get people from software companies talking to people from telecoms. [End user manager]

How these dialogues unfolded was regarded as important, perhaps even more so than actual <sup>fi</sup>eld trials. Through discussions the diverse actors could learn a great deal from each other, particularly about the different potential aspects of the innovation in a variety of contexts. As the hub of the innovation network, the Centre focused on maintaining an environment to nurture these discussions and encouraging further dialogue in order to ensure that the innovation process unfolded in line with the original goals.

Part of the secret of doing any kind of industry voluntary standards organisation is to share the information. Nothing is secret, every activity is open, everything has to get a hearing. You've got to set up the relationships with the associations to help you with that … making sure that we had a consensus that industry would go in this direction. We had to reveal what we were doing and tell people about it and push it all over the place. [ConnectNet Centre co-founder and standards organisation member]

## 6.3. Orchestrating around diverse interests

The ConnectNet Centre faced a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenge in setting up the innovation network as a collaborative space. It recognised that the industry organisations, particularly technology vendors were motivated by their individual interests. In their attempts to ensure their own long-term survival they would always promote their own technologies and services.

On the vendor side the RFID industry [in general] is littered with self-interest and in<sup>fl</sup>uence and certainly the whole issue of intellectual property is a massively loaded issue.

The Centre leadership therefore deliberately structured the network such that the end users and technology vendors were separated in order to ensure that there was controlled dialogue between the two groups, in particular limiting the ability of technology vendors to attempt to sell their individual solutions to users. Controlling these dialogues was particularly challenging since vendors were driven strongly by their underlying values of survival through competition and exclusion. At the start of the Centre's operations, therefore, the technology vendors found it a particularly challenging environment which required them to operate in a different way.

I'd say in the beginning it was something which technology vendors felt a bit worried about, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally those related to RFID, didn't feel comfortable because they felt they had the great new way of doing it and why should they give it their competitors? It was a very tough challenge, very, very tough. [End user senior manager]

Since technology vendors were usually in competitive relationships with other vendors, their actions were necessarily directed to supporting their own interests. To overcome this challenge the Centre management foregrounded discussion stressing the importance of collaboration within the innovation network and attempting to ensure that this became a taken-for-granted way of working.

The Centre management kept reminding particularly the technology companies that this was a collaboration more than a competition … There are some companies that are still in my opinion trying to manipulate the system to, if you will, game the project towards their own ends. But I think everyone is cognizant of the need to balance companies against one another in the technology <sup>fi</sup>eld. [ConnectNet Centre management team member]

Despite technology vendors acknowledging that collaboration with other vendors was in their interests, the underlying value of ensuring their own futures meant they needed to balance collaboration with competition, to only share where it made sense and in so far as it served their interests to do so. In addition, end users were only prepared to work with technology vendors in the innovation network if it matched their overall internal strategy. They had already developed relationships with their chosen technology providers and whilst the PhysNet innovation offered potential for new strategic gains there was a sense of caution in working with new partners.

If you think about the interests of the end users in this industry and if you talk to end users they'll tell you this explicitly. What they'll say is, ‘look, I am willing to consider using new innovative technology for data collection at the edge of my network. So I have no problem with [Company A and B] … providing me tags and readers … because it looks like they have a real edge …. But in these early days it's not mission critical. When it comes to my core information systems … there's not a chance I'm going to entrust that to anybody but the people who are doing it now.’ [Technology vendor senior manager]

Like technology vendors then, end users were also motivated by their own interests, given that they too were only prepared to invest in the innovation process in so far that they could see their value of self-preservation supported.

If you're going to invest in this [innovation process,] time, money and effort, at some point you turn from being collaborative to being somewhat proprietary. You need to take a dual path. You need to remain collaborative where it makes sense. … So we can talk collaboratively about what kind of [technology works] … issues and things that we've learnt we can share [and] gets everybody moving the right way. [End user member]

The Centre, as a hub actor in the network, provided a way to manage the diverse interests of the heterogeneous network of collaborators, balancing self-interest with the wider goals of the innovation process as actors acquiesced to the need for conforming normatively to a collaborative environment.

One means to maintain the Centre's neutral stance was to make academic research their core value. Academics, without the drive of commercial self-interest, were regarded as a group who would ensure that the innovation developed in a direction that would bene<sup>fi</sup>t wider industry and business communities rather than a few dominant actors. Without a pro<sup>fi</sup>t motive, the academic community was considered to have a more neutral perspective, and they could balance the views of technology vendors who were otherwise likely to focus on their proprietary interests.

They needed the university because if they would have done this themselves the tech vendors it would have always been biased. [ConnectNet Centre researcher]

By positioning academic research as a key way of working alongside industrial actors the ConnectNet Centre found a means of appearing legitimate to a wider range of audiences interested in the innovation. Their status as independent experts, through publications and as conference presentations, helped verify the validity of their evaluation of the technology. Through this orientation, the ConnectNet Centre offered a sense of neutrality to what otherwise could be seen by actors external to the network as a partisan innovation process serving the interests of particular groups. These external actors could recognise that the academically led Centre was conducting the innovation process in an acceptable manner, encouraging wider collaboration.

Maintaining the positive normative standing of the Centre to establish legitimacy and as knowing the ideal way for the innovation network to operate was not without challenge however. There was the perception within the network that the theoretical perspectives of the academic collaborators were far removed from the pragmatic world of industry-based vendors. In terms of actually using the innovation, vendors had a more relevant understanding of what was required to ensure that the innovation was developed appropriately.

Along with distrust on both sides, the university feeling that the [industry] community is only interested in short term results and the [industry] community saying that the academic community doesn't pay enough attention to [the issues], it comes up with solutions before it identi<sup>fi</sup>es the problem. Those are traditional dialogues. [ConnectNet Centre researcher]

The differences between academic and industry contexts were emphasised by the way the two groups operated in the Centre. The different ways of working delivered results that re<sup>fl</sup>ected the different motivations of the academic and industry groups.

Basically we had this academic world with lots of good ideas, we had the technology vendor with all his ideas, both with different interests. [ConnectNet Centre researcher]

Whilst technology vendors came up with ideas that would meet their interests of pro<sup>fi</sup>table solutions, academics' ideas could be potentially different without these constraints. To establish adequate legitimacy, it was necessary, to have both the impact of commercially driven companies and the constraint of a more independently oriented research community.

And without driving companies you can't do anything. It's only dangerous if you don't have kind of a stabilising or neutral … organisation in the background, which has been the [research centres]. The combination of having an international network of research centres and [researchers] who were not dependent on something because they [say], ‘well actually we like what we do’ … in combination with powerful companies, that's good. [ConnectNet Centre research director]

The concern for the Centre management in engaging academic researchers was curbing their tendency to solve interesting problems rather than problems relevant to the innovation. Furthermore, academics were generally not noted for their business acumen. The Centre needed to create the strategic innovation that supported industry and this could only be done by industry and academia working together.

But the [research] Labs … as businessmen they are the world's worst business people. And if they're going to do something that requires structuring, they're idiots. We've got to advise them, we've got to help them, we've got to direct them. … We've got to work with them close enough. [ConnectNet Centre co-founder and standards organisation member]

Thus an ongoing challenge to network orchestration was enabling dialogues in a context where values and interests clashed. Yet importantly for the strategic intent of the innovation to be successful, it was necessary to enable diverse values to challenge current thinking from multiple perspectives. In this regard the Centre's efforts to structure the Centre in a particular way and establish collaborative innovation processes gave it the legitimacy to be seen by network actors as having developed the ‘right way to do things’ which would enable a wider range of interests to be met.

## 6.4. Controlling and shaping dialogues beyond the innovation network

As the innovation process progressed the PhysNet became more widely known. The mainstream media had drawn the attention of wider society, with privacy advocates taking particular interest in the innovation. Privacy advocates became concerned over the potential for PhysNet to violate privacy. Yet much of what they observed in the media had not overtly addressed privacy. Privacy advocates felt it was their role to raise awareness of PhysNet's associated privacy concerns to challenge those within the innovation network, and their vested interests in its implementation. In particular they questioned the assumptions that the Centre and the innovation network made about the positive outlook of PhysNet and the logical need for technological progress. They questioned whether PhysNet was really a necessary and useful advance when taking into account the negative impacts on society.

And every technology that comes in always promises to bring in a better world and always promises to usher in a better society. And history shows us that that's not always the case. … You need someone to say, ‘look this could happen, this is the potential.’ They're not going to come up with it on their own. [Privacy advocate]

To accomplish this they sought to create a dialogue with a wider audience, particularly the consumers who were likely to be impacted by the creation of PhysNet. Engaging with the public on privacy concerns of PhysNet would raise awareness, which privacy advocates regarded as a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in addressing concerns

The fact that consumers were unaware of this and indeed had not been brought into the dialogue was very worrisome to me. So I think my <sup>fi</sup>rst response was to see basically how to get this information out to consumers [Privacy advocate]

I'm trying to get people to talk about it and I'm trying to get people to debate it. And I don't care if people come on and say it's, you know, what are we all doing, are we all mad? Or if people come on and say it's all brilliant and everything. What I'm trying to do is stimulate some sort of debate. [Privacy advocate]

But privacy advocates also wanted to ensure that their concerns were heard and addressed by those directly involved in the innovation process. Ideally, they wanted to communicate directly with the ConnectNet Centre in order to clarify concerns and talk through the issues. Yet in ‘not bothering’ the central actor deemed privacy advocates as not being relevant for framing the purpose and content of the innovation network

They have not brought [privacy] advocates and the consumer issue into their planning in a meaningful way. And the <sup>fi</sup>rst time that they had an opportunity to interact with [privacy] advocates … was the November 15th conference at MIT. And, you know, they were invited to be there and the major players in RFID just plain didn't show up … They had an opportunity to rub elbows with us and they didn't bother. [Privacy advocate]

It was apparent to privacy advocates that the Centre was not going to engage in direct dialogue with them. They acknowledged that the Centre was paying some attention to privacy issues but questioned the commitment and level of understanding in them. In their view, network entities spoke about privacy as an afterthought.

Usually when I go to events … privacy issues are mentioned all the time but they're usually mentioned by people who don't have much of an idea of what they actually are and who also have very little ideas of how the problem could be resolved. They just say, ‘and then we have to take note of the privacy issues also, of course.’ When you ask a bit there's actually not very much of an idea. [Privacy advocate]

Privacy advocates reasoned that the ConnectNet Centre and its sponsors would skew the innovation process and the dialogue around it to focus on their taken-for-granted needs for a pro<sup>fi</sup>table innovation. Privacy concerns were centred on consumers and were therefore not a central consideration for the Centre's efforts.

If you take a big picture look at this, this technology was really developed by industry and for industry … This technology was not developed with an eye to bene<sup>fi</sup>ting consumers, it was developed with an eye to bene<sup>fi</sup>ting very large corporations … I think even in terms of the way in which it was developed, the way in which that dialogue occurred, [it] was really unilateral. [Privacy advocate]

The lack of engaging in a direct dialogue between the Centre's network and privacy advocates meant that any communication with the Centre was indirect, via wider debates on issues of privacy. As in other experiences, these debates were relatively hostile in that there was more advantage for one party than another. From the privacy advocates' perspective, the underlying values and belief system of network organisations, focused on securing their futures and gaining materially, were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from those of ordinary citizens.

This retailer lobbyist I was talking to mentioned the example of a garment that you put into your washing machine and your washing machine will tell you can't wash this garment at this temperature. So this seems to be one of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts retailers think they might use to make the RFID technology more attractive to users … [but] the RFID that this information sits on will be carrying much more information. What you think about this information … depends on your values and background. [Privacy advocate]

From the ConnectNet Centre' perspective, however, the privacy concerns had been recognised relatively early on during the innovation process. As part of the innovation process the Centre acknowledged that privacy concerns could be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenge when PhysNet became more widely known and that they would have to be prepared to talk about these concerns. In particular, they sought to control how the innovation was portrayed so that their view of the innovation retained predominance, preventing others from shifting the dialogue in unproductive ways.

[We commissioned a] case study of technologies that went bad … One of the key things that [it] talks about was controlling the dialogue and controlling it early … What a lot of organizations do fail to do, was that they failed to respond and they failed to respond in the correct manner. So … having a sort of very open, sort of familiar online dialogue as early as possible is, is very important for reassuring people. [ConnectNet Centre manager]

In gaining an understanding of how the dialogue with a wider audience of consumers should be shaped the Centre conducted consumer workshops to gauge how speaking about the innovation in<sup>fl</sup>uenced what was perceived. In these workshops they would discuss the innovation in a neutral way. They discovered that moving from a purely business or engineering focus to a focus on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts was interpreted as attempting to sell the innovation to consumers, and seen in a negative light. What the Centre determined was that the dialogue should instead be framed around choice, on knowing whether a PhysNet tag was being used on an item, that consumers could choose whether to buy the item, or choosing whether to disable the tag or not.

Normally when you have … something you're trying to sell, you sell on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. But in this case if you sold on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts you ended up with all these negatives. So the only thing we could do was to try and do everything we can to take the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts away… They wanted to be able to know when and where it was being used and they wanted to have a choice about whether they bought … or not. And this choice was really, really important. And the ultimate choice is killing the chip. [ConnectNet Centre manager]

As part of the PhysNet innovation therefore, an ability to disable the chip was designed into the technology. This would, the Centre calculated, appease consumers' major privacy concerns. The Centre was therefore attempting to manage how the innovation was perceived by the wider consumer audience. Rather than merely attempt to get positive support for PhysNet because of its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the Centre aimed to position the innovation as legitimate and relatively benign, as a non-disruptive advance to the norm. Ideally, it wanted to position the innovation as an inevitable next step towards a future they depicted in their innovating vision. An issue for the Centre, however, was trying to communicate this approach when dealing with privacy advocates

In addition to altering the innovation, the Centre also sought to create a privacy policy through a committee that would draw input from a wider network of commentators beyond the Centre's own innovation network. In this way it hoped to respond to the criticism that it was not engaging in dialogue beyond the innovation network.

There's a lot of [privacy] advocacy groups around. There's some that are very well respected and there's some that are fringe and they're fanatical and no matter what you say it will make no difference. We've worked with what we believe are the ones with the most respect and the most credibility. [ConnectNet Centre Manager]

The Centre controlled with whom it would dialogue, based on whom it thought would contribute in a constructive way. There was a recognition that some privacy advocates could not be convinced that the innovation had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t for stakeholders beyond industry organisations and engaging in dialogue with them would be ineffective.

The Centre and innovation network actors questioned the ability and legitimacy of privacy advocates to participate in a dialogue on the innovation. In particular they questioned their lack of understanding, their attempts to only focus on delivering a particular perspective of the innovation rather than a holistic view, and the fact that they feared technological progress.

Reading some of what I've read about what's been published by the advocates for getting rid of [PhysNet] … it doesn't seem to be that they really understand totally the limitations of the technology. [Senior manager of a sponsor company]

People don't understand the technology. It's just … the old 1984 feeling … whenever there is something new coming up.

The Centre and network actors had intimate knowledge of the innovation and they regarded their perspective of its potential and impact therefore more accurate and relevant for wider audiences. By implication their own views were not one sided, whereas they framed the privacy advocates as one sided and irrational.

As with all these lobbyists, they are fantastic communicators, they're great at delivering single-sided messages. And that is the point, it is a single-sided message … they can only ever be inhibitors, not promoters of the value. [End user manager]

The alternative views that privacy advocates promoted were simply not relevant or plausible and involving them holistically in discussions would be deemed procedurally inconsistent to seeking new forms of innovation value. To maintain its legitimacy as the custodian of the PhysNet innovation the Centre positioned its plan as inevitable and privacy advocate's views as barriers to this progression.

In developing a strategic innovation where the opportunities were yet to be fully developed and business models yet to be de<sup>fi</sup>ned, the sponsor companies were wary of closing off any potentially rewarding avenues. They were not prepared to reframe the ongoing dialogues in order to address the values and interests of privacy advocates. For network members the focus was in creating ecosystem-wide strategic advantages through the development of the technical innovation. This was evident in the technical orientation of the people that were engaged in the network. The Centre, therefore, in their attempt to reframe existing dialogues and shift perspectives found that they needed to access company representatives beyond the technically-oriented ones who were participating within the Centre.

Because we were [focused on aspects such as ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency innovations], [we had] the guy who's the head of supply chain, he's not a marketing person, he's not a consumer person … he's not part of a legal team, he doesn't understand the public policy issues … You can't get the person who is capable of having this dialogue with you, you get somebody who isn't experienced in making [these] decisions on behalf of their company. [ConnectNet Centre manager]

The combination of companies wanting to keep their options open and not having the correct people to engage with meant that the Centre appeared to be slow in responding to privacy concerns. Once the dialogues beyond the Centre were making headlines in the media though, many of the sponsors saw the need to react.

It became an issue when there was a negative press backlash … And then suddenly everybody said, ‘why don't we have a policy?’ And we said, ‘we've had a policy paper for the last year that we'd been trying to communicate to the guys and trying to advise you about how you need to move forward but you haven't paid attention because you didn't think it was important.’ [ConnectNet Centre manager]

The inability of the Centre to bring together all the sponsors in a dialogue to orientate the diverse values and interests around privacy concerns resulted in aspects of the innovation process being less controlled than was considered ideal. Some sponsor companies chose not to hear what the Centre was saying, which led to real world trials being exposed by privacy advocates as violating consumer privacy.

And when [Company X] did their store tests, they didn't listen in terms of what sort of policy they should have in place and how they should be handling [it]. And because of that there was a lot of negative publicity … around that test, which was a shame, it could have been handled much better. [ConnectNet Centre manager]

There was less opportunity to control the dialogue when the media could interpret discussions to suit their interests which at this stage had moved on from being something positive with the potential to change the world for the better to an innovation with the ability to increase surveillance to a degree that appeared nefarious.

The media's interest lay in, as an interviewee put it, the ‘creep’ factor, something that would make a good story. And whilst it was a particular aim of privacy advocates to highlight what they considered to be the dangers of the innovation, they tried to ensure that they communicated the real concerns rather than the hyped stories that were appearing in the media. Their attempted reframing of the dialogue was skewed by the media.

And it's been one of the things that I spend a lot of my time dispelling among consumers is this notion that somehow these things can track you from a satellite. [Privacy advocate]

One of the consequences of the Centre not orchestrating direct dialogue with privacy advocates was that the Centre and sponsor companies would attempt to speak on behalf of privacy advocates, and vice versa, based on their interpretation of the motivating values and interests they held. With a lack of direct dialogue there was no way to clarify assumptions about each other or to alter existing mental models.

## 6.5. Coordinating and orchestrating network dissolution

A further approach the ConnectNet Centre took in attempting to orchestrate the strategic innovation network was to speak about the end of the innovation process from early on during the network's operation.

[The Executive Director] wanted a de<sup>fi</sup>nite end date and at the beginning he wasn't clear what would've happened by then … but he did know that there had to be an end and that the end had to be in about [Year X]. [ConnectCentre management team member]

As such, there was an attempt to mark a point in time when the innovation network was to have accomplished its goals and when the innovation was ready to move beyond the group of collaborators ‘out of the lab and into the marketplace’. Part of this decision was to avoid network members waiting for the innovation to become even better and cheaper. Whilst improvements could always be made there was value to be gained in using earlier versions of the innovation. The Centre's leadership therefore continued to speak about when the innovation would be ready and therefore, when the strategic network would no longer be needed.

So why buy one now? It will be better to wait. And yet [the Executive Director] was very <sup>fi</sup>rmly of the opinion that it's still better to buy one of those now because it's going to save you time, save you money. … Companies should be deploying this technology now, not putting it off … He didn't want to have the ConnectCentre just rumble on and on for a long time. … So there was a stick put in the ground … saying … that's it. [ConnectCentre management team member]

Communicating this message served to remind network entities that the Centre was focused on particular goals which had to be met within a particular timeframe. The dialogues aimed at getting the diverse groups together were supported by these dialogues reminding everyone of the time constraints since activities that were not aligned with a collaborative approach could be seen as delaying the delivery of the innovation. Whilst companies wanted the innovation to be perfect and as cheap as possible they also wanted to move the innovation from a research orientation to a focus on implementation. They needed to know that they were going to be able to move from a strategic innovation network developing the innovation to a point where they were able to establish competitive advantages for their individual organisations.

## 7. Discussion

Our empirical case study provides insight as to how a hub actor orchestrates a strategic innovation network composed of a diverse group of actors, and in so doing responds to Dhanaraj and Parkhe's (2006) call to examine the individual actions of hub actors in creating, forming and developing effective innovation networks.

Building on Dhanaraj and Parkhe's (2006) research, we found a need for the hub entity to orchestrate network formation and coordination and to integrate knowledge across diverse actors of our heterogeneous network. Thus, a <sup>fi</sup>rst step to orchestrating the strategic innovation network was for the hub entity to create and structure the network by attracting, informing and enrolling organisations to enable innovation and deliver the necessary outputs (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Nambisan & Sawhney, 2011). This was done via a deliberate strategy to engage particular types of organisations that could contribute to the innovation process. Network formation is an important part of the orchestration process, as subsequent effectiveness of the hub actors' role as orchestrator will depend on leveraging the right skills and knowledge from these <sup>fi</sup>rms which in turn will in<sup>fl</sup>uence how actors acknowledge the hub actor's legitimacy to guide the innovation process. Instead of relying on market dominance to legitimate its role (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006), our hub entity needed to articulate a compelling yet adequately ambiguous strategic vision for the innovation (Sillince et al., 2012). The structuring of the network is not only a part of design but is also a continual process of orchestration in order to maintain innovation coherence (Nambisan & Sawhney, 2011). The hub actor has to constantly work at reinforcing the roles of network actors, to remind them of what they ought to be doing and why. This also entailed developing and sustaining network legitimacy (Human & Provan, 2000) in terms of its suitability to foster strategic innovation across a wider ecosystem.

In orchestrating strategic innovation networks we also show how dialogue is a mechanism by which the hub entity engages with diverse groups of actors whilst coordinating and managing the innovation process. Building on a dialogical perspective (Gergen, McNamee, & Barrett, 2001; Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005; Tsoukas, 2009) as a means of building a new reality, our study develops new insight into how dialogue across inter-organisational settings shapes strategic innovation; we highlight the important role of the hub actor in enabling (and constraining) dialogue. Innovating within a strategic innovation network raises signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges for dealing with wider perspectives than may be found in a single organisation. Bringing together diverse actors enables dialogues which can yield new strategic insights beyond incremental business plans for the whole network (Krinsky & Jenkins, 1997; Markides, 1999; Tushman et al., 1997). Yet this bene<sup>fi</sup>t is offset by the need for individual network actors to support their internal values whilst meeting their interests. The orchestration challenge for the hub actor is to identify the particular meanings and perspectives which should be central to the innovation whilst balancing the values and interests motivating network actors' actions. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute three inter-related dialogical strategies that hub actors can employ as part of orchestrating strategic innovation networks, namely: persuasive projection, re<sup>fl</sup>ective development, and de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control.

The dialogical strategy of persuasive projection is important for the formation of the strategic innovation network; it is fundamentally concerned with getting stakeholders to join the conversation thereby establishing connections between different stakeholders. With little power to coerce actors into joining the network, a hub actor has to persuade others that joining the network is advantageous. Intimately connected with a strategy of persuasion is what we term projection or the ability of the hub actor to provide an organising vision for the innovation process (cf. Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, 2004) despite signi<sup>fi</sup>cant uncertainty as to how the innovation process may unfold. This is to be expected given that strategic innovation entails making fundamental changes, rethinking rules and focusing on new possibilities (Schlegelmilch et al., 2003). The hub actor can orchestrate the innovation network processes by projecting a course through the unfamiliar territory (Mische, 2009). Through dialogue with network actors, the hub actor can set signposts in the temporal landscape in order for the innovators to see how the uncertainty can be attained. For example, early on, the hub actor persuasively projected an end point for the strategic collaboration by marking its transition ‘out of the lab and into the marketplace’. This is particularly salient given the multiple stakeholders constituting the network each with divergent temporal rhythms for making future milestones and projections (Venters, Oborn, & Barrett, forthcoming).

As seen in our case, the ConnectNet Centre created a discussion around what the PhysNet innovation would be and what value it could offer. Many of the technological components existed in some form already but no organisation had previously drawn together the disparate ideas to create new possibilities. Crucially the ConnectNet Centre spent a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of effort in dialogue with potential collaborators, telling them about the innovation and how a network approach had strategic potential. These stakeholders had different projections for the future. For example, researchers focused projections on the development of novel ideas whilst end users focused their projections on the usability and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the innovation. Creating the network of researchers, technologists, and users was an important part of demonstrating the hub actor's ability to guide and coordinate the innovation processes that would follow. Thus a vital part of persuasive projection dialogues is to establish the hub actor as having legitimacy to play the orchestrating role by gaining suf<sup>fi</sup>cient interest in starting a network. Through dialogue, the hub actor can demonstrate a bene<sup>fi</sup>cial exchange relationship for others, positioning itself as a legitimate network orchestrator. As Suchman (1995) indicates, an actor acquires pragmatic legitimacy by showing stakeholders how their interests are met by supporting the hub actor's endeavours. By employing a dialogical strategy of persuasion, the hub actor framed its discussions of the present as well as projections into the future in a way that anticipated and fed into the interests of potential collaborators. Getting organisations to invest signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in a strategic innovation process that was purposely ambiguous (Sillince et al., 2012) means that potential collaborators need to be persuaded of the material gains in their cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, despite its uncertainty. One potential advantage of the ConnectNet Centre, which was regarded as a neutral actor and perceived to have limited power, was that it was not seen as a threat to potential network entities, being neither dominant nor a competitor Instead, the ConnectNet Centre openly declared the initiative's dependence on collaboration as a means to persuade others.

Having initiated a conversation to persuade diverse stakeholders of their projections about the strategic innovation, the hub actor employed reflective development dialogue as a strategy to get all the network entities working together towards achieving the innovation goals. As strategic innovation is about making fundamental changes (Hamel, 1998; Schlegelmilch et al., 2003), and requiring actors to recognise and incorporate unfamiliar perspectives into new ways of thinking and working (Gergen et al, 2001: Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005), the hub <sup>fi</sup>rm started to deploy a re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogical strategy to expose, acknowledge and connect different values and interests. In doing so the hub actor supports the <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge within the innovation network as well as facilitating processes of innovation leverage. That is, the hub organisation enabled actors to leverage each others' capabilities and innovation assets and ensured that all network actors were able to extract value from the innovation process (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006).

Importantly, reflective development dialogue is a way to ensure that actors are not alienated by particular approaches but are rather made to understand the potential gains that can result (Tsoukas, 2009). The approach taken by the hub actor is to foreground the values and interests of certain groups at the expense of others in order to ensure that the innovation develops in a particular direction. In our case the ConnectNet Centre foregrounded end users as driving the innovation, particularly from a requirement perspective; through a re<sup>fl</sup>ective development dialogue, technology vendors perceived that gains could be achieved by yielding to other's interests.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also reveal the need for re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogical strategies to move beyond achieving pragmatic (exchange) legitimacy and demonstrate that the hub entity has the moral right to orchestrate the network. The hub actor can acquire moral legitimacy (Suchman. 1995) by demonstrating that it is doing the right and proper activities, and not competing with other network entities. For example, this can be accomplished by demonstrating expertise, publishing papers and a proven track record of credentials and innovative ideas (Birkinshaw et al., 2008) which, through elite academics, the hub actor was able to do in a non-competitive manner. Within the ConnectNet Centre, academic researchers were positioned as experts with a neutral view on what solutions would be best for the innovation to succeed, increasing their legitimacy of being morally <sup>fi</sup>t for the role. Network entities evaluate the hub actor's legitimacy on arbitrating and guiding the values being exposed and promoting the welfare of the innovation network as a whole. To gain moral legitimacy the hub actor created appropriate structures and processes (Suchman, 1995) such as organising the vendors into a single group with representation and attempting to control their interaction with others in the network. It was by being found to be morally legitimate by network entities that the hub actor was able to enact a re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogical strategy in identifying and orchestrating which actions and meanings were appropriate to be pursued.

Definitional control of the dialogues is a third strategy for network orchestration. By employing dialogical strategies of de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control the hub actor de<sup>fi</sup>nes content and basic assumptions of what dialogues are permitted as well as who is invited to participate. As highlighted by Birkinshaw et al. (2008), this can be seen as a legitimacy-seeking strategy to “select among multiple environments in pursuit of an audience that will support current practices” (Suchman, 1995: 587). The hub entity assumed that wider scrutiny and debate of the innovation network and innovation process would not be appropriate for effective development of the strategic innovation. Instead, the hub organisation can frame the dialogue through de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control to include only those stakeholders who perceive the innovation as the logical solution to current industry challenges by positioning the innovation as relatively benign, as a non-disruptive advance to the current norm. In this way they manipulate the environmental structure to create and appeal to appropriate new audiences and legitimating beliefs (Birkinshaw et al., 2008; Suchman, 1995) where they can validate the network's strategic interests. De<sup>fi</sup>nitional control of dialogue thus excludes those who have different worldviews; this risks that the innovation network may miss opportunities to enhance the strategic innovation, given that a key requirement is to view organisations and industries in a new light (Jacobs & Heracleous, 2005). Yet, exclusion of particular conversations or actors by the hub entity does not mean that different worldviews will not be more widely debated by stakeholders external to the network and its conversations, in forums such as the media, thereby challenging the taken-for-grantedness of the need for an Internet of Things. Our study builds on earlier work on orchestration processes (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Nambisan & Sawhney, 2011) which tend to be inwardly focused, to show how hub actors hold an important role in de<sup>fi</sup>ning which stakeholders, including those external to the network, may participate based on rules underpinning the network guiding their dialogical strategies.

The use of language and discourse to include or exclude contributions is one such dialogical strategy of de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control. In our case, the use of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and technological terminology provided a legitimate way to de<sup>fi</sup>ne why certain actors were not capable of participating in the innovation discourse. The views of privacy advocates were thus dismissed as irrelevant because network entities believed they did not understand the language of science and underlying technology, framing them as lacking in credibility regarding the basic assumptions of innovation. Through de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control, the taken-for-granted boundaries of the dialogue in terms of who might participate and what might be discussed were af<sup>fi</sup>rmed. Thus there seemed little point in dialoguing with privacy advocates as they would not be capable of comprehending and participating in the logic of innovation arguments (Birkinshaw et al., 2008). The legitimising justi<sup>fi</sup>cations of hub actors establish appropriateness and rationality (Lamertz & Baum, 1998), whilst legitimating and connecting the emerging new innovation to nature and reason (Green, 2004). Having established a level of cognitive legitimacy for the strategic innovation across the network (Suchman, 1995), the hub actor aims to convince potential audiences to accept the innovation as an inevitable next step in the progression of the world as constructed by the network. To justify the adoption of a new innovation, the hub actor scans the commonly held assumptions ‘taken-for-granted’ by diverse audiences to produce justi<sup>fi</sup>cations that support the actor's claim about the innovation. Whilst acceptance of the innovation network and its activities as inevitable may accord with some external actors, until an innovation has become institutionalised divergent views about the inevitability or necessity of the innovation will persist (Green, 2004).

## 8. Conclusion

This paper brings together the strategic innovation literature and the network innovation literatures to develop new thinking in the area of strategic innovation networks. In particular, we consider the role of the hub entity in orchestrating this network. In so doing we make two contributions to the literature. First we contribute to literature on collaborative innovation networks, through our example of achieving strategic innovation amongst stakeholders in a network having competing values and interests. Building on Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) we show how network orchestration can be carried out by entities that are deemed neutral and with relatively limited power that nonetheless are able to control more powerful network actors by achieving legitimacy. We suggest that these hub entities may be particularly suited in orchestrating strategic goals given the need to balance the competing values and interests whilst unearthing new forms of meaning and possibilities. Second we contribute three dialogical strategies, and show how persuasive projection, re<sup>fl</sup>ective development, and de<sup>fi</sup>nitional control can be used to orchestrate strategic networks. We also show that the orchestration processes extend to those beyond the innovation network by seeking to control what dialogues are permitted and who is allowed to participate.

The dialogical strategies we have proposed are based on a single case. Future studies could explore the use of dialogical strategies where the hub actor is a more central dominant hub <sup>fi</sup>rm with more capability to coerce or exert power relations. Additionally, future research could attempt to measure the effect of particular strategies on the innovation process. For example, could the use of persuasive projection dialogues be detrimental to the innovation process by, for instance, attempting to paint too detailed a picture of the future that may ultimately not be met. Further studies could more closely examine the temporal separation of the dialogical strategies, when certain strategies predominate and to what effect. For example, whilst persuasive projection is typically key in enrolling network actors initially, there may be a need for this dialogical strategy at other points during the innovation process where other perhap more powerful actors need to be enlisted to support changes in direction or provide much needed resources. Finally, a re<sup>fl</sup>ective development strategy may be needed as a support to manage the disruption that may result in developing the existing innovation process.

Our study not only shows how various dialogical strategies can aid hub actors in orchestrating innovation networks more effectively but it also highlights how they are related to cycles of legitimacy. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show how re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogical strategies can help to establish moral legitimacy which in turn enable a re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogical strategy. Future work could usefully further our understanding of this relationship between dialogical strategies and cycles of legitimacy in the development of strategic innovation networks. This could include studying other similar cycles of legitimacy as well as the inter-relationship between types of legitimacy (e.g. pragmatic and cognitive) and dialogical strategies.
