# A time-based assessment of the influences, uses and benefits of intermediary website adoption<sup>§</sup>

Tina Harrison <sup>a,\*</sup>, Kathryn Waite <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, The University of Edinburgh, 50 George Square, Edinburgh EH8 9JY, UK <sup>b</sup> Department of Management, School of Management and Languages, Riccarton Campus, Heriot Watt University, Edinburgh EH14 4AS, UK

Received 15 September 2005; received in revised form 23 March 2006; accepted 28 September 2006 Available online 7 November 2006

## Abstract

An e-enabled distribution chain has many advantages: a key element is the company website which combines sales and marketing functions. Intermediary websites interface between the supplier and the marketplace; consequently, adoption and use of websites by intermediaries can benefit an extended supply chain. We examined intermediary e-commerce development, focusing on the adoption and use of websites, the factors influencing initial adoption, the characteristics of adopters and patterns of website use. Based on interview and survey data from financial services intermediaries, we used Rogers’ model of innovation to identify adopter groups. Statistically significant differences were found between the five adopter categories in terms of key company characteristics, key factors influencing the initial decision to develop a website and its subsequent use. There are implications for providers in widening intermediary participation in website technology. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Internet; Innovation adoption; Intermediaries; Supply chain; Financial services

## 1. Introduction

E-commerce is now viewed as an integral part of marketing distribution systems [32]. Distribution chain constituents can form digital links to share information, to buy, sell and distribute products or services, and to transfer cash flow. An e-enabled distribution channel has many advantages, such as increased business efficiency, enhanced information flows, improved transaction speed, wider geographical spread, increased temporal reach, and competitive differentiation [20,44]. In order to realise the benefits upstream members of a distribution channel work with distributors to overcome barriers. In addition, ‘‘the Internet offers direct links with customers, suppliers and distributors and thereby control access to customers’’ [43]. Hence, e-commerce has the potential to change channel structure and may even pose a threat to certain distribution channel members.

The terms ‘‘disintermediation’’ and ‘‘reintermediation’’ describe the changes resulting from e-commerce. Disintermediation occurs where the ultimate supplier of a good or service sells directly to the consumer [12]. Reintermediation occurs when a new intermediary emerges employing a lower-cost distribution method. Some e-marketing literature has proposed that disintermediation will be widespread and electronic market will automatically reduce the need for brokers [15,8]. Examples of these have occurred in several industries, most notably travel, where consumers have been dealing online directly with the providers or online intermediaries, by-passing established ‘‘bricks and mortar’’ stores [34]. Drivers for disintermediation include the desire to differentiate and compete [11].

However, there are studies that identify the importance and variety of functions provided by intermediaries, including specialised information, professional advice, customisation to consumers’ needs, and reduction of uncertainty [24]. Thus, a company bypassing a distribution network to pursue commerce in cyberspace can be exposed to considerable risk [16]. Zank and Vokurka, in a survey of manufacturing distribution channel constituents, found that most companies perceive that the Internet had positive supplementary impact on distribution channel relationships. Therefore, it is important to determine the forces that are shaping e-commerce within the distribution channel. Co-ordinated and collaborative development amongst channel members may increase effectiveness and strengthen linkages [4], but fragmented adoption might result in inefficiency and weakened links between members. Business-to-business marketing practitioners need to understand e-commerce requirements of their distribution partners in order to maximise benefits and to ensure that e-commerce investment generates a return [41]. Knowledge of the factors driving e-commerce capability amongst channel members help in determining the nature, scope, and need for marketing support to facilitate adoption.

Our descriptive research measured the extent to which specific events are perceived as driving the diffusion of a specific technology (websites) for a specific purpose (pension distribution) within a specific social network (IFAs). The study used an established theory of innovation diffusion [31] in two ways. First, we measured the importance of innovation attributes and events for the adopter groupings within the social network. Second, we specified the diffusion pattern within the social network. We thus viewed the development through the eyes of those adopting the innovation to understand why the innovation was adopted.

Meade and Islam [27], in their review of diffusion research, comment that though the body of diffusion research was impressive ‘‘few research questions have been finally resolved’’. They reasoned that ‘‘the diffusion of innovation is a complex process, involving large numbers of individual decisions’’. Uddin [40] wrote: ‘‘the innovation and its diffusion under a particular situation should be looked into both from producer and user points of view keeping in mind their interdependence’’. Moreover, Greenhalgh et al. [17], in their review of diffusion research in service organisations, concluded that ‘‘empirical studies of implementing and maintaining innovations in service organisations (1) had been undertaken in a pragmatic rather than an academic perspective, (2) were difficult to disentangle from the literature on change management in general, and (3) were impoverished by a lack of process information’’.

## 2. Research focus and context

Our study focused on the development and use of websites, examining the forces shaping website development amongst intermediaries within an extended marketing distribution system. The distribution system chosen was that of the UK pension distribution channel (see Fig. 1).

There are typically two to four constituents in this distribution channel. At its simplest level individuals can purchase directly from pension providers/insurance companies, though this is not common. More often, purchase is made via an intermediary (the Independent Financial Advisor, IFA) and/or the workplace. Hence, the employer can act as an intermediary. According to Sandler [33], over half of life and pensions business is sold via IFAs with only 5% on an execution-only basis (i.e., using a direct route that bypasses the financial advisor through a traditional sales person or via the Internet).

IFA status was created by the Financial Services Act 1986, and shows an intermediary who deals with savings and investment products from a range of financial services providers rather than an agent who deals with only one supplier [19]. The IFA is a critical channel to market for financial services institutions in the UK, as consumers demand impartial advice on savings and investment products whilst product providers have focused on them as a pivotal channel in their overall distribution strategy [9,10].

Vasudavan and Standing identified three key tasks performed by intermediaries: (1) information brokering: passing information between customers and providers, (2) transaction processing: completing forms and forwarding payment to providers, and (3) customer advising. In addition, they note that intermediaries have specialist knowledge and access to specialised sources of information. IFAs obtain their professional status by becoming licensed in the UK and their specialised information is not generally available to the consumer, being accessed through subscription and membership of online portals, reading dedicated publications, and through professional organisations and trading networks. These support the IFAs as experts in integrating the financial needs of consumers and create the links that constitute a social system.

A recent UK government report on the medium and long term savings industry stated that co-ordination and co-operation difficulties had resulted from a lack of customer facing sites, low levels of capital investment, poor integration of legacy systems, and a lack of common standards. Due to successive and proposed legislation to remove entry barriers and cap administrative charges, providers were attempting to reduce their processing costs. Investment in technology in turn increased the necessity for IFAs to have enhanced technological capability. Furthermore, within the banking sector there were indications that consumers valued the relative advantage of online banking [25]. However, the technological capability of IFAs was relatively poor and hence the market presented a significant ‘‘technology catch-up’’ opportunity.

## 3. Conceptual background and hypotheses development

Diffusion research has been conducted by researchers from a variety of different disciplines and its origins can be traced back to Tarde in 1903. However, in recent years, there has been an integration of concepts and generalisations. Although not without criticism of its application to IT [26], Rogers’ model is widely accepted as useful in an examination of critical characteristics of technology adoption [1].

According to Rogers, organisations within an established social environment will not all adopt a specific innovation at the same time. He suggested that it is possible to classify organisations into one of five adopter categories: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards.

Raymond [30] argues for enriching the model in an interorganisational context, particularly when adoption decisions may be linked to those of business partners. Prior research supports the inclusion of four determinants of innovation adoption: the characteristics of the innovation, the characteristics of the organisation, the environmental context, and the characteristics of the individual decision makers [23,28,29,38,39]. Innovation characteristics are compatibility, complexity, relative advantage, trialability, and observability; of these, relative advantage, compatibility, and complexity have been found to significantly influence the adoption of systems technologies.

Organisational characteristics are usually measured by the firm’s size: number of employees or revenues. Blili and Raymond [7] recognised that SMEs encounter unique problems: limited financial resources, low skills, and minimal strategic management. Additional organisational factors identified as influencing IT adoption include: top management support, quality of IS, user involvement, product champion, and resources. External environment characteristics that may influence the adoption of IT include competitive pressure, support from technology vendors, pressure from buyers and suppliers. Competitors, B2B vendors, suppliers and customers are all potential sources of innovation communication and can be viewed as change agents, who influence innovation decisions. Finally the characteristics of the individual decision maker have also been found to influence adoption. It has been shown that the combination and interplay between factors within specific contexts is important. Rogers outlined the need for context specific research into variables related to the factors that explain the rate of adoption and the role of communication channels at different stages.

Consequently, we decided to test the following hypotheses in the context of financial intermediaries development and adoption of websites for e-commerce purposes:

H1. The larger the intermediary in a value chain, the earlier the adoption of a website.

H2. The greater the importance of internal factors on the decision to develop a website, the earlier the adoption of a website.

H3. The greater the importance of external factors on the decision to develop a website, the later the adoption of a website.

H4. Earlier adopters make more extensive and more advanced use of their websites.

H5. Earlier adopters realise greater benefits than later adopters.

## 4. Research method

A two stage research design was considered appropriate. The qualitative phase was used to develop a list of internal and external factors that IFAs identified as being important to their initial decision to develop a company website. The use of qualitative research captured the ‘‘social actors’’ points of view [6] and thus met the objective of exploring the perceptions of the social system.

Data was collected through 20 UK-wide individual interviews conducted from November 2003 to May 2004. Participants volunteered to take part after a request was circulated through a professional network. Interviews were conducted in major UK financial centres such as London, Bristol, and Edinburgh plus other key areas, such as the Home Counties and the North East. Participants were drawn from different sized IFA companies ranging from those with turnovers of more than £5 million to sole traders. They represented IFAs at different stages of website development. The interviews were transcribed and analysed using the critical incidence technique, which employs the content of stories or ‘‘critical incidents’’ as data where critical incidents are ‘‘certain important facts concerning behaviour in defined situations’’ [13]. Events that were described as shaping website development were clustered around themes. Themes that were mentioned most frequently were then used to formulate a set of 12 statements, as shown in Table 3.

Following this phase, a survey instrument was designed and piloted among a sample of IFAs to check its clarity and relevance and then to estimate the anticipated response rate. The questionnaire was mailed to a random sample of 5000 IFAs, stratified by size of firm, purchased from a commercial database company. There are 26,000 active advisors in the UK working in an estimated 5000 firms, of these 37% are operating as sole practitioners. Questionnaires were distributed in June 2004, with a follow-up in July 2004. Six hundred and ninety-two usable questionnaires were returned, thus yielding a 14% response rate.

The questionnaire captured data on the web presence of the IFA, including length of time on the web, how the website is being used, key company characteristics, and the critical factors that had influenced the original development of the website. The frequency of the incidents mentioned in the qualitative phase may not have reflected their importance; respondents may have been reporting memorable events rather than critical incidents [14]. Therefore, to test whether the incidents actually were critical, respondents were asked to rate the importance of key incidents on a five-point Likert scale [2].

## 5. Findings

Since the prime objective of our study is to provide insight into the development and adoption of websites by IFAs, only a sub-set of the sample was relevant. The sub-set consisted of those firms that had a website and had been in business for at least the same time as the company which was found to have had a website for the longest time, thus the analysis focused on all companies that had been in business for at least 10 years. This represented 388 firms, out of which 44% had a website, 12% were in the process of developing one, while 44% did not have one. The final sample, thus, consisted of the 217 companies.

In terms of type of firm, 56% of the final analysis sample were limited companies, 28% were partnerships, and 16% were sole traders. In addition, approximately 6% of the sample belonged to a network (roughly equal numbers of sole traders and limited companies; few partnerships belonged to one). Thirtyseven percent of the sample had 1000 or less clients, 31% had between 1001 and 3000 clients, and 32% had over 3000 clients. The majority of the sample (76%) had a gross income of less than £1 million, 20% between £1 million and £5 million, and only 4% had over £5 million. Almost all sole traders (98%) had income of less than £1 million. Limited companies were more likely to have higher income, as over one quarter (27%) of the sample had income over £1 million, and around 15% of partnerships also had over £1 million in income.

## 5.1. Classification into adopter categories

Firms were classified into five adopter categories based on the number of years the company had had a website. Examination of the responses for this time indicated natural groupings, as indicated in Table 1.

Beatty et al. [5] noted that commercial web use started around 1993, however the earliest date that any company in the sample developed one was in 1994, 1 year later. Due to the differences in time of first adoption, the overall timeframe of our study, and the industrial context of the sample, it was not possible to retain complete parity with Beatty et al.’s work. However, some overlaps existed; we used the same definition for category (innovators, 3 or more years, etc.). The main difference was in terms of the late majority, which was less than 3 years in this study and less than 1 year in that of Beatty et al.

## 5.2. Relationship between firms’ characteristics and adoption

As all the firms in our analysis were intermediaries in the financial services sector, an underlying assumption was that they were all part of the same social system. However, different types of firms existed. The industry sample had sole traders with fewer than 1000 clients and a very localized presence and also large organisations with a number of outlets, over 3000 clients, and national coverage. It was therefore important to assess whether any differences existed in adoption patterns among firm types.

Table 2 shows the percentage distribution of firms in each of the adopter categories according to key company characteristics (type of firm, size of client base, and income).

It is interesting that 80% of the innovators were limited companies, which contrasts with the distributions in the other adopter groups. Variations in distribution were noted between adopter categories and size of client base and the adopter categories and income. Compared with the other adopter categories, innovators had a much higher proportion of larger client bases. This provided support for H1. At the same time, however, it should be noted that innovators seemed to be polarised between high and low client base sizes; this further distinguished them from other adopter categories.

## 5.3. Factors influencing website adoption

Table 3 shows the 12 statements with their mean and modal scores. The statements were measured using a five-point Likert scale from 1 = not at all important to 5 = very important. Overall, the two factors most influencing the original development of the website were the strategic decisions of the company and the influence of a key personnelle, suggesting the dominance of formal strategic planning. This is somewhat contradictory to the responses of some small IFA firms interviewed. Indeed, Stockdale and Standing [36] cite Hall as saying that SME’s participation in e-commerce is not often characterised by formal strategic planning.

Principal Components Analysis was chosen as most appropriate. Prior to performing it, the suitability of the data was assessed. Inspection of the correlation matrix revealed the presence of many coefficients of 0.3 and above. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin of 0.711 exceeds the recommended value of 0.6 [21,22] and the Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity [3] reached statistical significance, supporting the factorability of the correlation matrix. In addition, the ratio of observations to variables far exceeded the acceptable ratio of 4 or 5:1 recommended by Hair et al. [18]. A varimax rotation was used, as this is considered best for computing factor scores to be used in subsequent analysis. PCA revealed the presence of four components with eigenvalues exceeding 1; these, explained 60.6% of the variance (see Table 4).

The subsequent dimensions were then subjected to reliability analysis to test for the internal consistency of the scale constructs. The results indicated that three of the dimensions showed very good internal consistency, while factor 3 showed weaker consistency. However, since the correlation coefficients for the two items on which this factor loaded were high, we decided to retain the factor in subsequent analysis (Table 5).

## 5.3.1. Factor 1—emergent

These items were all external influences and relate predominately to the opportunities created to develop a website or the fortuitous circumstances surrounding the website development. Hence, website development emerged as a result of them; it was not planned or guided but occurred serendipitously. It might be expected that the early majority, and perhaps some early adopters, would be influenced most by such factors. The initial innovators would have already adopted the technology and external influences may then make use of marketing to widen participation in other segments providing opportunities for engagement.

## 5.3.2. Factor 2—deliberate

These all related to a planned effort to develop a website that was managed and handled internally. Hence, it represented a deliberate effort to develop a website. We would expect that the innovators and early adopters would be influenced by this factor.

## 5.3.3. Factor 3—responsive

The two main items on which this factor loads are the expectations of clients (individual and corporate), however the influence of providers also shows a moderate loading in addition to factor 1. It would appear that companies have responded to the expectations of their clients (and perhaps other supply-chain members); hence this reflects a responsive developmental effort. We would expect the early and late majority to be the most influenced by this—as clients began to realise that companies offered web services, they would begin to expect it to be offered as a standard feature.

## 5.4. Influence of website development drivers on the timing of adoption

In order to test our assumptions, a one-way between groups multivariate ANOVA was performed to investigate differences in factors influencing website development among the adopter categories. The four dependent variables were the regression factor scores of the four dimensions influencing website development: emergent, deliberate, responsive and me-too. The independent variable consisted of the adopter categories. Preliminary assumption testing was conducted to check for normality, linearity, univariate and multivariate outliers, homogeneity of variance–covariance matrices, and multicollinearity with no serious problems noted. Box’s M significant value of 0.96 clearly indicated that the data did not violate the assumption of homogeneity of variance–covariance matrices (it was greater than 0.001). Levene’s test of equality of error variances also indicated that the assumption of equality of variance for each of the website development influencing dimensions had not been violated (e.g., significant values of 0.38, 0.95, 0.43, and 0.75, respectively, for each of the four factors—all greater than 0.05).

Statistically significant differences were found between the adopter categories on the combined dependent variables (see Table 6). Wilk’s Lambda value of 0.828 and significance of 0.002 (i.e., less than 0.05) indicated there was a statistically significant difference among the adopter groups in terms of the overall factors influencing website adoption. However, we wanted to know to what extent adopter categories differed on all the factors influencing website development. To answer this, it was necessary to consider the dependent variables separately. In order to reduce the chance of type 1 error (a significant result when there is none), it was suggested that a higher alpha level be set [37]. A common method has been to apply a Bonferroni adjustment, which, in its simplest form, involved dividing the original alpha level (e.g., 0.05) by the number of analyses intended (in this case 4 for the dependent variables), giving a new alpha level of 0.0125. Table 6 reveals that none of the dependent variables show significance values less than this cut-off. However, if the alpha level were to be relaxed to 0.1, two of the dependent variables (deliberate and me-too) showed significance levels and the variable ‘emergent was very close to it. The one variable which clearly showed no significant difference was the responsive dimension, which previously indicated poor internal consistency.

To understand the actual differences, the mean scores for each of the variables needed to be inspected. Table 7 shows the mean scores for emergent, deliberate and metoo, along with the standard deviation and rank. We decided to retain the emergent variable as it was close to the cut-off point and to aid in the explanation of the differences between groups.

This showed that Innovators had the highest mean score on the deliberate dimension, indicating that they were most driven by the strategic direction and people who took the lead for website development. Early adopters had the second highest score, which could also be expected. This supported H2. The early majority had the highest score on the me-too dimension followed by the laggards. This could have been expected of the laggards and is perhaps not surprising for the early majority. The late majority had the lowest scores on deliberate and me-too. Laggards had the highest mean score on the emergent dimension, indicating that they were more influenced by opportunism and fortuitous circumstances surrounding website development and supported H3.

(2) As a means of providing information (i.e., product information was provided). (3) As a sales channel (i.e., clients could request and/or buy a product online). Second, IFAs were asked how much of their total business was conducted online.

## 5.6. Relationship between timing of website adoption and benefits realised

We then considered some of the benefits of ecommerce experienced by intermediaries. The three major benefits related to improved efficiency and effectiveness of the business. Less benefit overall appeared to have been realised in terms of market development growth, such as increasing sales revenues or the number of new clients. Table 9 shows the proportions of intermediaries experiencing each of the benefits.

More intermediaries who had developed a website were experiencing benefits than those who simply had access to the Internet. For example, 41% of all intermediaries without a website but with access to the Internet claimed to have experienced an enhanced company image compared with 63% of all intermediaries that had developed websites. The timing of adoption also seemed to have an impact on the benefit experienced. Overall, greater proportions of intermediaries were experiencing a benefit amongst the earlier adopting groups than the later ones. The innovators stood out from the other groups in terms of the greatest benefit realised. For example, whereas only 21% of all intermediaries with websites claimed to have experienced an increase in sales revenues or new clients, 64% of all innovators claimed to be experiencing these benefits. These findings supported H5.

## 6. Conclusions and implications

Using Rogers’ model of innovation as a theoretical underpinning we examined intermediary e-commerce development, focusing specifically on the adoption and use of websites, the factors influencing initial adoption, the characteristics of adopters, and subsequent patterns of website use.

The results showed that there are clear differences between financial intermediaries in terms of the timing of adoption, the factors driving website development, and the way in which the website was used resulting in implications affecting the whole of the distribution chain. Innovators and early adopters were more likely to have a clearer reason for developing a website which was strategically guided, and were making most advanced use of it, with obvious benefits for suppliers and customers.

Comparisons were drawn by examining the development of pension provider websites [42]. A longitudinal content analysis of pension provider website from 1996 to 2003 revealed an evolutionary path from simple mono-directional communication to mature bidirectional communication and an increase in process and transaction enabling tools. We also found that the impact of external drivers and later website development was primarily due to environmental change. Analysis also indicated that internal drivers for change were present and differences existed between earlier and later web developers in terms of both web features provided and apparent influences on development activity.

Adoption of websites among UK financial intermediaries is relatively limited. Innovators and early adopters have been shown to consist primarily of larger firms with larger income. Due to the size and degree of fragmentation of the financial intermediary market, product providers have tended to develop relationships with preferred intermediaries, developing proprietary technology. This may be a barrier to emarketplace participation. Other barriers included a lack of a common framework, misconceptions that the Internet has to be a substitute for established methods and not a tool to enhance business, that website development can facilitate interaction with larger firms, that the industry environment does not encourage innovation, and that there is little incentive to be a first mover. The lack of a common trading platform across the whole financial intermediary sector and the perceived threat of disintermediation continue to be key factors inhibiting the widespread adoption and development of intermediary websites.

A large proportion of the financial intermediary sector comprises small and medium sized enterprises; their barriers to adoption relate to problems such as connectivity and lack of resources. In contrast, the realisation of the benefits of participation generally rests with their ability to identify opportunities and to plan their online trading effectively within the constraints of their environment.

A significant proportion of the sample appeared to be influenced by the emergent driver—opportunities that arose or emerged to obtain a website from external parties, mainly software developers. IT suppliers could act as a further intermediary in the technology supply chain. This would not have to eliminate individual provider–intermediary relationships but could offer a more inclusive approach and speed up the rate at which new technology is adopted.

## 7. Limitations

Our research had limitations and these must be considered in terms of their impact on our findings. One concerned the way in which data was captured about the length of time the website had been in operation: data was not entirely continuous and, limitations were placed on the type of analysis that could be conducted. Indeed, it would have been unrealistic to expect companies to recall the exact month when a website was developed when that event occurred some time ago.

The use of retrospective data is also a limitation on the respondent’s ability to recall critical incidents; the longer the time between events and data gathering, the greater the danger that participants reply with stereotypical responses (e.g., due to a halo effect). In order to minimise this, the comprehensiveness of incidents was compared against the literature as well as with a sample of intermediaries. Fountain noted that when a priori categories were used in studies where existing theories were clear (as in our research), the validity of categories became less problematic than when categories were built inferentially from the data.

Another limitation concerned the scope of web facilities that could be incorporated in the study. Use of the Internet by financial intermediaries was quite low in the UK, hence the scope of facilities covered by the survey was scaled down to reflect usage patterns.
