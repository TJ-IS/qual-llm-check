# Predicting users' continuance intention in virtual communities: The dual intention-formation processes

Keywords: Virtual communities Continuance intention to consumer Continuance intention to provide Past behavior Perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts

## a b s t r a c t

With the rapid growth of virtual communities (VCs), more and more people access, create, and exchange information online with millions of others worldwide. Given voluntary participation and diversity of user behavior in VCs, this research attempts to explore the determinants of future participation that is critical to the survival of VCs. We investigate two intention-formation processes (non-conscious and conscious) through which previous experiences with a VC guide future behavior (continuance intention). Since those two processes may substitute each other, we also investigate the joint effect between them. To re<sup>fl</sup>ect the special features of VCs, we differentiate between two types of continuance intention – to consume and to provide information. Furthermore, for the non-conscious process, we compare two common measures of previous system usage, tenure and extent of usage. This is one of few IS studies that examines past behavior from multiple perspectives and explores its role in a non-traditional IS context. We also investigate four types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts while examining the conscious decision making process. For the non-conscious process, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that tenure and extent of usage play different roles in motivating users' continuance intentions. Tenure is shown to have a direct effect on continuance intention to consume information. Meanwhile, extent of usage is shown to have a direct effect on continuance intention to provide information. For the conscious decision making process, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that perceived information and altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts encourage both continued consumption and provision behaviors. However, perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts do not play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the continuance intention to provide and perceived visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts have no impact on continuance intention to consume. Based on the results, it is very important to differentiate consumption intention from provision intention, since they are affected by previous usage and perceived bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts differently. Theoretical contributions and managerial implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Web 2.0 technologies have enabled the creation of virtual communities (VCs), where millions of empowered Internet users worldwide congregate, interact, create, and exchange information. For example, Tripadvisor, the largest travel community in the world, has attracted more than 60 million unique monthly visitors, and over 75 million reviews and opinions.<sup>2</sup>As an increasingly popular social phenomenon, VC development depends largely on users' voluntary participation, since users create the majority of the contents and initiate social interaction in the communities. Each user is empowered to determine his or her own participation in a VC and, therefore, some users are more active and spend more time in the VC than others.

Given how critical it is for the survival of a VC to attract users and encourage their continuous participation, it is important to understand what drives user behavior.

Prior studies suggest that past behavior may predict future behavior [6,32]. Past behavior has been extensively studied in social psychology (e.g., Theory of Planned Behavior) and has been shown to affect future behavioral intention [3]. IS literature has also revealed that past behavior affects future IS use [6]. However, given the special features of VCs, such as user-generated contents and voluntary participation, results in VCs might not be consistent with the existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the traditional IS context. One noticeable feature is that VC users are not only information consumers but also providers; therefore we differentiate two types of intentions: continuance intention to consume and continuance intention to provide. Both types of participation behaviors are socially desirable and essential to the development of a VC [36].

To fully understand the role of past behavior in the VC context, we simultaneously examine two intention formation processes, non-conscious and conscious [32]. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst process, VC users' prior usage may directly predict their continuance intention due to automatic repetition. In the second process, users may deliberately evaluate various bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceived from the VCs to decide whether they intend to continue their participation. Those two processes co-exist and may substitute each other. So we also study how prior act predicts continuance intention by interacting with the conscious process.

Literature suggests that past behavior, or users' previous system usage, can be quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed by different measures [38]. It is then worthwhile to explore whether different measurements of previous system usage share the same predictive power. This study considers two common ways to assess prior usage [38]. One is tenure (membership since joining a VC) that differentiates novice users from experienced users [6]. The other is extent of usage (time spent in a VC per week) that differentiates light users from heavy users [37]. We also differentiate four types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts suggested by VC literature [10], information, altruistic, social, and visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, while studying the conscious decision making process.

Overall, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm prior research by showing that both non-conscious processing and conscious deliberation co-exist in deciding users' continuous intention in VCs, and they attenuate each other's effects. However, by differentiating two types of continuous intentions, two ways to quantify previous usage, and four kinds of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from VC participation, our model provides more nuanced understanding of the VC user decision making process. One key <sup>fi</sup>nding is that separating consumption intention and provision intention is critical, since they are affected by previous usage and perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts differently. For instance, Tenure is shown to only have a direct effect on continuance intention to consume information. Meanwhile, extent of usage is shown to only have a direct effect on continuance intention to provide information. Based on these results, we offer both theoretical and managerial implications to VC researchers and practitioners.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section we present the theoretical background, develop hypotheses, and present our research model. This is followed by a discussion of methodology, data collection, and data analysis. Research <sup>fi</sup>ndings, theoretical and managerial implications, limitations and future research are presented in the last section.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

The focus of this study is on users' continuance intention in VCs. Intentions “represent a person's immediate behavioral orientation towards engaging in a given target behavior” [17]. Intentions are a strong predictor of future behavior and have been used as a dependent variable by many prior IS post-adoption research [7,21]. Based on features enabled by Web 2.0 technologies, users are not only information consumers but also content generators. For instance, Ahuja and Galvin [1] found that information seeking and providing are the two effective ways employees use to socialize with others in a virtual group (e.g., Email). Therefore, we differentiate two types of continuance intentions, continuance intention to consume and to provide, and examine whether they are motivated by the same antecedents. Continuance intention to consume represents the extent to which a user intends to continue browsing or seeking information in a VC. Continuance intention to provide refers to the extent to which a user intends to continue contributing his knowledge to a VC.

Ouellette and Wood [32] argued that there are two processes through which past behavior guides future behavior – a conscious process and a non-conscious process. Consequently, we investigate these two processes leading to the formation of continuance intention and how they interact with each other. The conceptual framework is summarized in Fig. 1.

## 2.1. Non-conscious processing

During the non-conscious processing, people may have the tendency to repeat their past actions automatically without involving careful reasoning [32]. In this process, well-practiced behaviors will be repeated in a stable or constant context, as long as all internal or external factors remain unchanged [5]. Ajzen [2] also suggested that past behavior has an impact on future behavior independent of the effects of beliefs, attitudes, and subjective norms. Recently, a number of IS researchers have focused on continuing IT use without conscious intention. Limayem et al. [28] indicated that future behavior tends to be performed with almost no cognitive effort, as the individual gains adequate practice in the past and his familiarity with the behavior increases. Kim et al. [26] also concluded that with repeated IS use in the past, the overall role of rational evaluation will diminish in importance as a determinant of behavioral intention.

Previous usage is likely to be a contributing factor in forming continuance intention. In other words, people may have favorable intentions about things they have done before due to cognitive consistency. They may base intentions on easily determined cues such as their prior usage. For instance, if a user has been using a VC before, he becomes familiar with the system features and knows where to <sup>fi</sup>nd the information he needs. As a result, he might plan to consume information from the same site again in the future. From using the VC in the past, a user has chances to get to know other users via interactive capabilities enabled by the site. He might have the tendency to contribute information back to the community that he has engaged in before [8]. Such an automatic mechanism suggests that people might want to continue an act simply because they have practiced it before. Therefore, we hypothesize the direct effect of previous usage as follows:

H1a. Previous usage will be positively associated with a user's continuance intention to consume in a VC.

H1b. Previous usage will be positively associated with a user's continuance intention to provide in a VC.

## 2.2. Conscious deliberation

In the second process, conscious decision making is likely to initiate the behavior when it is not well learned or is performed in an unstable context. From a social psychological perspective, when people are consciously deliberating about acts, they tend to evaluate the outcomes of the behavior (e.g., attitudes) in a careful and thoughtful manner before they form intentions [32]. In the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, it is generally agreed that continuing IS use is driven by a conscious or rational decision making process (e.g., beliefs, expectations and re<sup>fl</sup>ections on past experience) [17,25,31]. The main focus of IS post-adoption research is cognition-oriented behavioral models, including TAM and the IS Continuance Model [19,26]. Based on the Theory of Reasoned Action [15], Karahanna et al. [24] found that behavioral beliefs about using an IS, or perceived usefulness, determine continuance intention. Consistently, Bhattacherjee [7] developed an IS continuance model that theorizes evaluations of system usage based on past experiences as a primary factor in driving continuance intention. Therefore, via the conscious deliberation process, users' continuous intention is driven by their perceptions about system effectiveness in facilitating achievement of various individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when participating in a VC.

As a VC provides a social platform for people to exchange information, Social Exchange Theory (SET) [9] provides a theoretical basis for explaining user participation intention from a sociological perspective. Generally speaking, SET posits that social exchange will be limited to actions that are contingent on rewarding reactions from others. In social exchanges, people do others a favor with a general expectation of future rewards [23]. Social exchange in OCs is a generalized exchange, in that the exchange is not necessarily with the same member a user helped, but with any community members [36]. In addition, social exchange is expected to be reciprocal in the sense that users are not only information consumers but also providers. Xia et al. [43] found that users' provision behavior depends on how much they are getting from the community. In other words, users' conscious evaluation of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they receive from a VC will impact their behavior.

Perceived usefulness refers to a user's ex-post expectations about system effectiveness and the net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of system use based on past experiences [7]. In our research, perceived usefulness means user perceptions about system effectiveness in facilitating achievement of various individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when participating in a VC.

Based on prior VC research [8,11,18,20,21,33,36,39,40,43], we extend the generic perceived usefulness construct into four types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts VC users may obtain via community activities: information benefits, social benefits, visibility benefits, and altruistic benefits.

Information benefits are achieved when users can access expertise and obtain useful information [14,39]. Social benefits are emotional and camaraderie support from other VC users achieved through information exchange and social interaction [10,27,39]. Visibility benefits are related to a user's personal image and reputation based on his involvement in a VC [40]. Altruistic benefits are an individual's psychological happiness when helping others [14].

Users can access superior resources and get advice from others by using a VC. If a user values information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts obtained from a VC, he is more likely to continue consuming information in order to keep getting such bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Given the intense competition among VCs, if a user does not like the information he gets from a VC, he may simply switch to another VC that can satisfy his information needs [22]. On the other hand, given that social exchange in VCs is generalized and reciprocal, valuable information may also encourage users to contribute their own knowledge to others in return. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2a. Information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to consume.

H2b. Information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to provide.

In VCs, geographically-dispersed friends or strangers develop virtual social relationships through user interaction [10]. For information consumers, social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are achieved when others are willing to share their opinions, experiences, or stories with them. Users who receive emotional and spiritual support in this way may feel that there is a group of people who care about them. For information providers, social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are achieved when others actively respond to them by supporting or triggering more discussions. Users who receive social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through this way may feel that there is a group of people who are willing to respond to and interact with them. Regardless of how social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are obtained, when users believe that they will get others' emotional support and response, they are more likely to stay in the same VC and keep in touch with those users. In these regards, we hypothesize:

H3a. Social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to consume.

H3b. Social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to provide.

Visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts occur when users establish reputation by providing information and resources [10]. According to social exchange theory, in order to provide information, users must think that their contribution will be worthwhile and valuable to others but also to themselves [40]. By providing information, social rewards such as reputation and respect are expected, which motivate users to actively participate in content generation. Therefore, many VCs implement effective IT artifacts to acknowledge those who contribute and make them visible to others [29]. Doing so will increase personal image and reputation, and thus, encourage users' continuous engagement. To maintain their reputation, users may also need to consumer information in order to learn from others' opinions. Doing so will help them provide more valuable and relevant information to others. Hence, we hypothesize:

H4a. Visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to consume.

H4b. Visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to provide.

Altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are intrinsic enjoyment from helping others [23]. In contrast to information, social, and visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that are external rewards from participating in VCs, altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are internal rewards for the sake of the activity itself [40]. Prior research has indicated that altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts motivate users to provide information because they value the opportunity to bene<sup>fi</sup>t others [10,39]. Since social exchange is reciprocal, users who enjoy helping others may also expect to consume information from the VC at the same time. They will be discouraged if they cannot get anything back from others. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5a. Altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to consume.

H5b. Altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positively related to users' continuance intention to provide.

## 2.3. Joint effect between the two processes

Both non-conscious processing and conscious deliberation can co-exist in forming continuance intention. Furthermore, those two processes may substitute each other in their impact on continuance intention. Thus, our model also captures their joint effect by using a negative interaction term.

In a meta-analytic review of the Theories of Reasoned Action and Planned Behavior, Hagger et al. [17] found that the inclusion of past behavior tends to attenuate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of attitudes on intention. With increasing past behavioral engagement, “the performance of the behavior becomes less one of a rational evaluation of the consequences of the act and more one of a learned response” [3]. Jasperson et al. [19] also suggested that “as individuals gain experience with what was initially a novel behavior, they tend to engage less frequently in re<sup>fl</sup>ective consideration of this behavior and rely instead on previous patterns of behavior to direct future behaviors”. Therefore, the role of conscious deliberation may diminish when users are becoming more and more experienced with the VC usage [31]. By using a VC in the past, a user may become familiar with the site and tends to engage less in re<sup>fl</sup>ective consideration of his participation decision.

A user's previous usage pattern may also be overridden based on careful information processing and analysis, although such suppression of established behavior is not easy [41]. In the VC context, there are many alternative competing sites available and the switching cost from one site to another is low. If a user perceives that a site cannot provide him enough bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, he can decide to discontinue or reduce his usage fairly easily. Thus, rational decision making based on bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceptions can destroy temporal stability between past and future acts and interfere with automatic repetition of past behavior. Furthermore, the appearance and contents of a VC can change frequently, and users may rely more on conscious deliberation than non-conscious processing in such an unstable context. In this regard, we hypothesize:

H6a. The interaction effect of previous usage and the four types of individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is negative on continuance intention to consume.

H6b. The interaction effect of previous usage and the four types of individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is negative on continuance intention to provide.

## 2.4. Explore two different ways to quantify previous usage

In the prior literature, past behavior has been studied from multiple perspectives, such as how long a behavior has been performed so far [6,35], as well as the frequency of/how often a past behavior is performed [3,5,32,37]. The former is called tenure, measured by how long a user has been a member of a VC. It differentiates novice users from experienced users and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the longevity of previous usage. The latter is extent of usage, measured by time spent in a VC per week, and differentiates light users from heavy users. Those two variables quantify previous VC usage from different perspectives, and we are interested in knowing whether they have the same predictive power on users' continuance intention in VCs. Since guidance from literature to compare different ways to assess previous usage is limited, our investigation is exploratory in nature. We run the conceptual model separately with each measure as a proxy of previous usage, in order to explore whether continuance intention predication depends on how previous usage is measured.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Data collection

In order to differentiate two intention-formation processes, we need to capture various bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceived by existing VC users. A surveybased methodology is appropriate to assess users' bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perception and is commonly used by many prior VC post-adoption studies [11,20,21]. Two VC managers and several researchers reviewed the survey prior to data collection. Pilot studies were then conducted in a sports VC and a graduate-student VC. Based on 54 responses, we revised the survey to clarify ambiguous instructions and terms. After <sup>fi</sup>nalizing the survey, we collected data in a large travel VC, which is one of the largest VCs for tourists to share worldwide travel-related information (e.g., attractions, and places to stay and eat). The VC organizes forums based on destination, such as Africa forums and Europe forums. Users are free to participate in any topic discussion in any forum with no <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives from the VC managers. The VC manager posted the survey as a sticky link at the top of each discussion forum for two days. 284 users participated in the survey. The total usable sample size was 281. To test non-response bias, we followed Ma and Agarwal [29] to compare the means of all variables between respondents on Day 1 and Day 2, and found no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference. Table 1 presents the demographic information.

## 3.2. Instrument development

Whenever possible, measurement items were adopted from prior research and measured using a 5-point Likert scale (1-strongly disagree, 5-strongly agree). The items for four types of perceived individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were newly developed based on prior research on user motivation for participating in VCs [10,40], and were further validated by senior VC researchers and community managers. The items for continuance intention to consume and continuance intention to provide were developed based on IS post-adoption research [7]. All of the multi-item constructs are re<sup>fl</sup>ective. We used single items to measure tenure and extent of usage. Gender, age and education were included as control variables. The complete survey is provided in Appendix A.

## 3.3. The measurement model

We <sup>fi</sup>rst checked the measurement model based on a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis in SmartPLS 2.0. We assessed construct reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity. Reliability is evaluated by computing AVE (Average Variance Extracted), CR (Composite Reliability) and Cronbach's alpha [4]. The general acceptable cut-off values are 0.50 for AVE, and 0.70 for both CR and Cronbach's alpha [4]. Table 2 indicates that all the constructs are reliable. Although perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and perceived visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts have Cronbach's alphas lower than 0.7, their AVEs and CRs are well above the cut-off values. Thus, they could be considered as reliable. Convergent validity is established when the factor loading of an item on its designated construct is 0.60 or more [13]. Table 2 also shows that all the items meet this requirement. Discriminant validity is assessed by examining if the correlation between a pair of constructs is less than the square root of AVE of each construct [12]. Table 3 indicates that all the constructs are distinct.

## 3.4. The structural model

We used SmartPLS 2.0 to test the structural model and hypotheses. A bootstrapping procedure with 500 iterations was performed to examine the statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and R<sup>2</sup> of the overall model [12]. The results of the structural model are shown in Table 4.

As noted before, we used two different ways to measure previous usage and ran the model separately with each measure in order to explore their differences. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst model where past usage is measured as tenure, our model explains 61.2% of the variance in continuance intention to consume and 48.3% of the variance in continuance intention to provide. In the second model where past behavior is re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the extent of usage, the model explains 60.1% of the variance in continuance intention to consume and 50.5% of the variance in continuance intention to provide.

To test the existence of non-conscious processing, we examined the direct impacts of previous usage. We found that tenure was only positively related to continuance intention to consume, while the extent of usage was only positively related to continuance intention to provide. As a result, H1a and H1b are partially supported.

To test the effect of conscious deliberation, we examined the direct impacts of four types of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Table 4 indicates that perceived information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (H2a), perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (H3a) and perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (H5a) positively impact continuance intention to consume. Perceived information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (H2b), perceived visibility bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts (H4b) and perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (H5b) positively impact continuance intention to provide.

The interaction term between mean-centered perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and previous usage demonstrated the joint effect between two intention formation processes. The results show that tenure plays a moderating role in continuance intention to consume, since it weakens the relationship between perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and continuance intention to consume. The extent of usage plays a moderating role only in continuance intention to provide, as it weakens the relationship between perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and continuance intention to provide.

For control variables, education is the only one that is found to be negatively related to continuance intention to provide. Age and gender do not have impacts on either intention.

Since the data was collected via a single survey method, we followed Podsakoff et al. [34] to use multiple techniques to address the common method variance (CMV) concern. All survey items were presented in a random order, so that items making up a construct were not placed next to each other. This may reduce the method effects by item context. We also applied three statistical techniques to test CMV. First, we ran the Harman's one-factor test and the results indicated that there were more than one factor that accounted for the majority of covariance. Second, we adopted partial correlation procedures [34]. After adding the general factor into two models, the increased level of R<sup>2</sup> (0.2% the highest) is much lower than the level suggested by Williams et al. [42]. Last, we used the marker-variable technique [30]. All 31 prior signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlations retained their statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance after the smallest correlation was used as the marker variable to calculate CMV-adjusted correlations. Those analyses suggested that CMV was not a big concern here.

## 4. Discussion

Overall, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm prior research by showing that both non-conscious processing and conscious deliberation co-exist in deciding users' continuous intention in VCs, and they attenuate each other's effects. However, by differentiating two types of continuous intentions, two ways to quantify previous usage, and four kinds of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from VC participation, our model provides more nuanced understanding of VC users' decision making process.

Findings from this research suggest that intention to continue consuming information should be treated separately from intention to continue providing information. The provision behavior requires relatively more efforts from users, since they need to organize, write, and submit their thoughts and ideas in order to become information contributors. Therefore, it can be better predicted by extent of usage, the measurement of previous usage that better re<sup>fl</sup>ects efforts spent by VC users. By spending more time in a VC, a heavy user, compared to a light user, is more likely to involve himself in various activities, such as participating in a topic discussion and writing follow-up posts, other than just seeking information. Such a trend is likely to be carried on in the future. By contrast, using tenure to assess previous usage only captures the length of membership, and long term af<sup>fi</sup>liation does not create obligation to contribute [29,35]. Nevertheless, tenure motivates users' future consumption intention. While new users need to <sup>fi</sup>gure out which VC to join based on a rational decision making process, experienced users may automatically go back to the site they have already been familiar with to look for information.

Furthermore, conscious deliberation behind two types of continuance intention is also different. We incorporate four types of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to examine conscious deliberation. Independent of how previous usage is measured, the same similarities and differences exist between continuance intention to consume and continuance intention to provide. On the one hand, perceived information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts encourage both consumption and provision intention. While perceived information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts capture value of “getting from”, perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts re<sup>fl</sup>ect enjoyment related to “giving to”. Our empirical results con<sup>fi</sup>rm Xia et al. [43] that “getting from” is a stronger force than “giving to” for the continuance decision, since path coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients of perceived information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are larger than those of perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Thus superior information could be used as a strategy to lock in existing users [16]. On the other hand, the roles of perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and perceived visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are different for the two types of intention. Perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts do not play a significant role in the continuance intention to provide. The travel VC studied here is an information-exchange VC, where personal social interactions are primarily limited to public online discussion facilitated by the site. It is possible that users who receive information are more likely to obtain social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than users who provide information. Perceived visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts have no impacts on continuance intention to consume, possibly due to the fact that visibility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts typically occur to information provision behavior, not consumption behavior.

Overall, the majority of VC contents are user-generated and user participation is mostly voluntary, and we need to recognize that users may behave differently when consuming vs. providing information.

The substitution effect between non-conscious processing and conscious deliberation exists but is not very strong. Independent of how previous usage is measured, only one out of eight interaction terms between perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and previous usage is negative and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in each model. It indicates that both processes are important in predicting VC users' continuance intention, and they only cannibalize each other to a very limited extent. Given the intense competition among a large number of VCs and low switching costs, it is fairly easy for users to join a new VC or terminate participation in an existing VC. While VC users still tend to repeat their past actions, they also constantly assess bene<sup>fi</sup>ts received from a VC to reason whether they want to continue their participation.

Among the four types of individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, tenure is shown to only negatively moderate the relationship between perceived social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and continuance intention to consume. In other words, the longer a user has been in a VC, the less the impact of social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on consuming information in the future. If a user has been with a VC for a long time, he might know exactly who the other users are and what kind of social atmosphere he is expecting. As a result, experienced users' participation is more likely to be self-determined with less in<sup>fl</sup>uence from other users. In contrast, extent of usage only negatively moderates the relationship between perceived altruistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and continuance intention to provide. As users spend more time on VC activities, they are more involved in the community. Heavy users' helping behavior could become spontaneous without thinking too much about how they enjoy the process of helping others.

## 4.1. Managerial implications

In order to explore the role of previous usage, we provide two different ways to categorize existing VC users: experienced vs. novice users in terms of tenure, and heavy vs. light users in terms of extent of usage. As shown in the analyses, users behave differently based on their tenure and extent of usage. Understanding this will provide insights on effective mechanisms for market segmentation in VCs. VC managers need to think about how to retain different kinds of users and motivate their continued participation. For example, heavy users tend to keep providing information in the future, and the VC needs to think about how to effectively motivate them to continue staying in the community. One possibility is that VC managers could improve system design to make information provision easy. VCs can also include time spent in the community as a factor in computing users' contribution score to increase their reputation. By doing so, heavy users may feel that time they spend on the VC is rewarded proportionally, their sense of belonging may increase, and they may be willing to continue their prior usage pattern and continue to share information in the community.

Perceived information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are the most important motivator behind continuous intention for all types of users. Therefore, community managers need to be aware that provision of information bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is critical to lock-in users and strengthen their loyalty to the community. They can try to deliver customized information content based on users' prior browsing records. Effective quality control methods can be used to enhance the value of user-generated content; for instance managers can include discussion moderators, expert opinions, and reporting functions to <sup>fi</sup>lter useless messages.

## 4.2. Limitations and future research

Our research has several limitations that call for future research. First, other than tenure and extent of usage, there might be other ways to quantify previous usage. We call for more research to explore other dimensions of previous usage and possibly generate additional insights. Second, the relative importance of previous usage may depend on the research context (e.g., types of VC, and topics being discussed). In our context, the VC is open to the public and is used primarily for information exchange of non-work-related topics. It is expected that the results may vary in VCs for other purposes (e.g., social networking sites, and professional VCs). Concurring with Faraj and Johnson (2011), we call for future research to further explore and conduct a comparative analysis in different types of VCs. Third, this research relied on the survey method in order to measure various bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceived by VC users in the conscious deliberation process. Since the survey was anonymous, we could not track users' real future participation behavior and cross-validate their self-reported measures for tenure and extent of usage. Future research could complement survey with secondary data to more accurately track users' previous usage and future participation behavior. Fourth, as we developed and validated new measurement items for individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, we call for more efforts to re<sup>fi</sup>ne and improve the bene<sup>fi</sup>t constructs theoretically and methodologically.

## 5. Conclusion

To understand the role of previous usage in forming continuance intention in VCs, this research empirically examines two processes of intention generation and their joint effect. As a user stays longer in a VC, he is more likely to continue using the VC for information consumption. Also, a user who spends more time in a VC is more likely to continue participating in the VC for information provision. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perception also plays an important role in motivating users' continuance intention, and the joint effect between previous usage and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts assessment is not as strong as expected. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that VC users are rational decision-makers who carefully evaluate their individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on their own before future participation.
