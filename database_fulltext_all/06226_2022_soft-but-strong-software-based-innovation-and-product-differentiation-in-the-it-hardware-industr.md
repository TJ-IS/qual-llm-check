# SOFT BUT STRONG: SOFTWARE-BASED INNOVATION AND PRODUCT DIFFERENTIATION IN THE IT HARDWARE INDUSTRY<sup>1</sup>

Keongtae Kim CUHK Business School, Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, NT, HONG KONG {keongkim@cuhk.edu.hk}

Jeongsik “Jay” Lee LeBow College of Business, Drexel University Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {jeongsik.lee@drexel.edu}

Anandasivam Gopal Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University SINGAPORE {anandasivam.gopal@ntu.edu.sg}

It has been argued in recent years that software is a significant value-creating factor within the manufacturing sector, leading to an increased interest in understanding the mechanisms by which firms may benefit from software-based innovation. Empirical work in evaluating these mechanisms, however, remains underdeveloped. In this paper, we examine one such process through which software-based innovation may provide value for information technology hardware firms in the U.S. Specifically, we examine the influence of software patents on the extent to which firms are able to benefit from product differentiation in their product markets. Using data on over 380,000 patent grants for innovations filed by 730 public IT hardware firms over the time period 1996-2015, we find that greater levels of softwarebased innovation within the firm are associated with higher levels of product differentiation in product markets: increases in the intensity of software patents within the firm’s patent stock, which we refer to as software intensity, are associated with lower total similarity of product offerings, relative to those offered by rivals, as well as fewer effective competitors in the market. Furthermore, using a different dataset consisting of 23,000 new IT hardware product announcements, we show that firms with greater software intensity subsequently launch a higher number of new products. Moreover, these firms are more likely to launch products that are not only differentiated from those of rivals but also distinct from their own product lines. Our research contributes to the emerging literature on software-based innovation by substantiating an important mechanism through which software helps transform industry sectors, specifically through product differentiation.

Keywords: Software, patents, innovation, information technology, hardware, product differentiation

## Introduction

In many industries that are traditionally manufacturing intensive, such as the IT hardware industry, it has been convincingly argued that software now drives much innovation and revenue (Nambisan et al., 2017). This helps explain the rising share in spending on software development as a proportion of total investment by technology firms in recent years (Allan et al., 2002; Sarrazin & Sikes, 2013). Incorporating software-based functionality and features helps improve the quality of technology products (Svahn et al., 2017), while on the supply side, software-related innovative activities are less costly to develop (Gilliland et al., 2014; Quinn et al., 1996). Thus, the important role of software within existing product lines is helping to reshape market structure and creating new competitive dynamics in the manufacturing sector (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014; Svahn et al., 2017). Indeed, softwarebased innovation is even able to increasingly explain differentials in productivity and output quality across firms and technologies (Arora et al., 2013; Branstetter et al., 2018).

In documenting the role of software-based innovation, previous research has mostly focused on the economic consequences of software-based R&D, for instance in the form of Tobin’s Q (Branstetter et al., 2018; Chung et al., 2019; Hall & MacGarvie, 2010), but remains largely silent on the exact mechanisms by which such consequences manifest for these firms (Graham & Vishnubhakat, 2013). Thus, despite our broad understanding of the role of software-based innovation in shaping firm performance, we know relatively little about how, exactly, software-based innovation influences a firm’s competitive position in the marketplace. Our study fills this important gap, with a focus on the U.S. IT hardware industry, arguably the most proximal yet distinguishable relative of the software industry itself. While an increasing prevalence of software within hardware products, and therefore within hardware firms, can influence firm value in several ways, we singularly focus on establishing the link between software-based innovation and product differentiation. We do so by asking the following broad research question: Does software-based innovation help firms improve their competitive positions in the product market and if so, how?

Indeed, recent developments highlighting the increasingly central role of software in potentially shifting the nature of competition within the IT hardware industry (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014, 2015) suggest that firms integrating greater software-based functionality into their products can better differentiate them from those of rivals. These investments in software capabilities help mitigate competitive pressures faced in the product market, while also increasing barriers to imitative entry (Porter &

Heppelmann, 2014). The benefits of this software-based differentiation advantage include better financial returns and higher market values. Thus, software-enabled product differentiation is likely to be a crucial mechanism underlying the positive association between software-based innovation and firm value found in prior studies (Chung et al., 2019; Hall & MacGarvie, 2010). As the relationship between software-based innovations and firm value has already been documented in the literature (Chung et al., 2019), our goal here is to empirically validate a potential association that underpins this relationship—the effect of software-based innovation on product differentiation.

We examine this empirical question using data on patenting and new product releases in the U.S. IT hardware industry between 1996-2015. We first use patent data to estimate the relationship between an incumbent’s software intensity in its patent portfolio, i.e., the proportion of patent stock that is related to software, and its competitive position in the product market. To measure product market competition, we use the text-based network industry classification (TNIC) scheme, which has been shown to be superior to standard industry classification schemes such as SIC and NAICS in that it more closely captures the effective market boundaries and competitive dynamics between firms within those boundaries (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010, 2016; Kim et al., 2016). The TNIC demarcates and annually updates the effective industry boundaries based on the vocabularies appearing in 10-K business descriptions. As a baseline, we show that software intensity is systematically related to the software content of a firm’s 10-K business profile, validating our use of business descriptions as a correlate of software intensity. More importantly, we find a strong relationship between software-based innovation, measured as software intensity and reductions in the incumbent firm’s product market competition, measured by the number of immediate TNIC-based competitors as well as the similarity across texts describing rival products and services. The results appear robust to potential endogeneity in the degree of software intensity.

To further establish the link, we compile a new dataset using the RavenPack News Analytics (Akbas et al., 2017; Connelly et al., 2019) and construct several measures that quantify the degree of new product development and product differentiation. By regressing these newly operationalized measures on software intensity, we find that firms that invest more in software-based innovation not only develop more new products but are also more likely to launch new products that are distinct from those of rivals as well as their own, and can thereby better differentiate their products. Further, a difference-in-differences analysis around first-time software patenting generates results that are consistent with the differentiation effects of software intensity found on TNICbased product markets and RavenPack new product launches, adding robustness. When viewed together, these results provide strong empirical evidence for the postulated positive link between software-based innovation and product differentiation in the U.S. IT hardware industry.

Our work contributes to the growing literature within the technology management and information systems (IS) areas that documents the enabling role of software-based innovation for firms, industries, and economies as a source of competitive advantage (Branstetter et al., 2018; Nambisan et al., 2017; Svahn et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010). Using the U.S. IT hardware industry as the research setting, we offer early evidence for the increasingly important role played by software-based innovation, even in settings not typically associated with large investments in software, in allowing firms to reconfigure their product portfolios and thereby mitigate competitive pressures in their product market. We complement existing work that documents the value-creating role of software innovation (Chung et al., 2019; Hall & MacGarvie, 2010) by substantiating an important link through which software innovation may explain variations in firm value. Our study also extends the line of work that examines the competitive implications of patents (Cockburn and MacGarvie 2011) and R&D (Hoberg and Phillips 2016) by shedding light on the productdifferentiation effect of software patenting that is above and beyond the more general utility provided by patents and R&D investments. Overall, this study contributes to a better understanding of Marc Andreessen’s (2011) famous aphorism that “software is eating the world,” which appears well-supported in our analysis.

## Software-Based Innovation and Product Differentiation

Software has long transformed industries in which products and services are information intensive, such as books and music (Shapiro and Varian 1998), by significantly changing the way they are developed and distributed as digital goods and services. More recently, software has started to play an increasingly crucial role in industries that are viewed as mainly existing in the physical world (Branstetter et al., 2018; Porter & Heppelmann, 2014; Svahn et al., 2017). Such examples abound, even just within the IT hardware industry, starting with the “slide to unlock” software functionality first featured in first-generation iPhone models and subsequently adopted in other smartphones, triggering a series of decadelong infringement lawsuits involving multiple companies. Many personal computers now come preloaded with software that improves user interface. Hard disks are typically paired with software that facilitates backup management and storage optimization. Versatile software turns printers into scanners. Most digital music devices not only play music but have software functionalities that allow users to find, download, and organize music files on these devices. Wireless earphones and earbuds are enhanced by built-in noise-canceling software or controlled by smartphone apps. The increasing reliance on software-based innovation is further amplified by the rapid development of the Internet of Things, which enables ubiquitous digital connectivity (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2014; Yoo et al., 2010). For instance, routers powered by software-based connectivity solutions boast improved speed, Wi-Fi connections, and communications between these connected devices, thus enhancing their value.

Resource allocations in terms of product development across firms reflect this increasing focus on software. Software accounts for an ever-larger share of product design costs—up to 80% of system design costs (Allan et al., 2002). By 2011, software explained 60% of all corporate IT spending, up from 32% in the 1990s (Sarrazin & Sikes, 2013). Firms in traditionally non-software industries have also started investing considerably in software-based research and development (R&D) (Arora et al., 2013). Between 1990 and 2012, business investments in software increased at more than twice the rate of all fixed business investments—in 2010-2012, software accounted for 12.2% of all fixed investments, compared to the equivalent investment in computers and peripherals of roughly 3.5% (Shapiro, 2014).

It is then unsurprising that this systematic shift in the nature of innovation toward software has created significant performance differentials between countries, firms, and technologies. At the country level, the performance advantage of U.S. firms over Japanese firms has been attributed to the U.S.’s overall advantage in terms of software and software-related research (Arora et al., 2013). Indeed, Japanese firms failed to recognize and adapt to the software-based shift in technological opportunities, thereby falling behind their U.S. counterparts (Cole & Fushimi, 2010). Intercountry differentials in the availability of software engineers may thus be one explanation for the observed differences in innovation productivity across countries (Arora et al., 2013; Branstetter et al., 2018). At the firm level, research shows that manufacturing firms with higher software intensity are more productive and garner greater private returns from their R&D, compared to firms with lower software intensity (Branstetter et al., 2018). Furthermore, prior work shows that patents disproportionately citing prior software patents receive more citations, on average, from subsequent patents (Arora et al., 2013).

Given these observations, it is worth considering how software, and innovative activities focused on software, affects competitive dynamics at multiple levels. The dominance of software in many critical aspects of firm operations across industries has the capacity to fundamentally change the way in which firms design products and services, thereby changing the basis for competition in product markets (Breznitz et al., 2018; Yoo et al., 2010). Broadly speaking, software-based innovation offers benefits for firms on both the demand side and the supply side. On the demand side, software enhances product design and improves speed and agility, thereby helping firms craft unique customer experiences even with traditional manufactured products (Huang et al., 2017; Svahn et al., 2017). Adding software-based functionality allows for the development of new business models even within existing industry segments (Sarrazin & Sikes, 2013). Thus, adding software functionality improves user appeal for products and makes them more easily stand out from other products with less software functionality. The nature of software development also provides supply-side benefits—relative to product development costs in other industry contexts, software development entails significantly lower development costs and time (Gilliland et al., 2014). Software also provides easy portability to alternative platforms, while the intangible nature of software allows procurement and sourcing to be conducted without the traditional constraints of location and time (Herbsleb & Moitra, 2001). Moreover, investments in software R&D likely have cumulative effects on firm-level capability building, which helps defray the cost of the focal investment over a wider spectrum of products over a longer period while potentially lowering marginal cost as firms accumulate experience over time. Hence, on the supply side, adding software innovations costs less.

These software-driven benefits of product differentiation and cost advantage imply a greater competitive advantage for firms that more actively engage in software-based innovation. Consistent with this notion, software development capabilities are considered to be a key differentiator for competition (Gilliland et al., 2014), with a majority of CEOs identifying software development as key to achieving competitive advantage (Ransbotham et al., 2017). As such, software-driven products are likely to shift rivalry by enhancing differentiation within product markets, especially in manufacturing-intensive sectors like hardware, where software development has not typically been a capability (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014). These discussions collectively suggest a positive association between software-based innovation and product differentiation in the market for IT hardware firms. That link is also likely to be one of the main mechanisms for the positive association between software patents and Tobin’s Q that has been observed in the literature (Branstetter et al., 2018; Chung et al., 2019; Hall & MacGarvie 2010).

Thus, our objective here is to empirically establish the relationship between software-based innovation and a clear indication of demand-side benefits—enhanced product differentiation. Empirically demonstrating this relationship will clarify, for managers and researchers, one distinct mechanism through which the observed performance benefits from software have been shown in the literature (Branstetter et al., 2018; Chung et al., 2019). Of course, other mechanisms may also exist, but our main focus here is that the capability to generate a clear set of differentiated products by incorporating software-based functionalities within hardware products should be empirically verifiable. Our literature review suggests that while the broad benefits of software patenting have been demonstrated, the specific mechanism through which they may occur, particularly that involving product differentiation, remains empirically untested. In the next section, we outline the dataset and the empirical tests we conduct to show evidence for the relationship between software intensity and product differentiation.

## Data and Sample

To substantiate the link between software-based innovation and product differentiation, we compiled a comprehensive dataset from multiple data sources, starting with the IT hardware industry. The hardware industry allows us to study how the increasing extent of software-enabled innovation affects product differentiation, where the final product is not software per se, rather than the software industry itself, where software would be assumed to have primacy. The hardware industry, by virtue of its proximity to the software industry, now exhibits the highest software intensity in the broader technology sector (Arora et al., 2013), making it a suitable context to study our research question. Importantly, if existing stocks of software development capability are a crucial factor in achieving a competitive edge, the hardware industry exhibits enough variation on this dimension to allow for testing our relationship of interest.

Following prior work, we start by defining the IT hardware industry as composed of the following 3-digit SIC codes: 357 (computer hardware), 366 (communication equipment), 367 (semiconductor), 381/382 (electrical instruments), and 481/484 (telecommunication) (Benson & Ziedonis, 2009). As a first step, we capture the innovative activities within this industry in the form of patents filed with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) by hardware firms. We obtained front-page information on these patents from the DISCERN (Duke Innovation & SCientific Enterprises Research Network) database, which covers U.S. patents granted between 1980 and 2015 (Arora et al., 2021). This database has two major advantages over other widely used patent databases, such as NBER or the Harvard Dataverse datasets.

First, it covers more recent patents by extending the NBER patent database to 2015. The commonly used NBER patent data does not provide adequate coverage for patents issued by the USPTO beyond 2005. Second, the dataset adjusts more accurately for changes in corporate names and ownership. Any firm-level analysis requires the appropriate and accurate attribution of patent data to the right entity; the DISCERN dataset is more comprehensive in this respect. We refer the interested reader to Arora et al. (2021) for more details on this dataset. While this data provides a full measure of the firms’ patent stock, identifying software patents requires additional steps, since no official classification exists to isolate these specific patents. We therefore used the following approach: we first screened patents using the 11 International Patent Classification (IPC) class groups proposed by Graham and Mowery (2003).<sup>2</sup> We also used a set of software keywords (“software” and “computer program”) to select patents with these keywords in the title or abstract (Bessen & Hunt, 2007). We then took the intersection of the patent class-based and the keywordbased sets to create the list of software patents (Arora et al., 2013). All other patents are thus considered non-software patents for the purposes of our analysis. This process provides a conservative definition of software patents within the firm’s patent stock. Appendix D provides a few representative examples of software and non-software patents according to our classification.

We augment the patent data with firm characteristics data on publicly listed firms obtained from COMPUSTAT to construct several control variables. To match the patent data to the COMPUSTAT data, we used the concordance provided by the DISCERN database. We then used firm GVKEY in the dataset to identify the assignee firm of each patent in our sample and retrieved the firm’s accounting information. Following prior work (Arora et al., 2021), we used the grant year for the time stamp of a patent.

Measuring product differentiation for a firm in its product market remains a challenge in the literature. We therefore adopt two separate ways of measuring a firm’s product differentiation and product development activities in order to establish the robustness of the analysis. First, we utilized a dataset based on a novel way of measuring product market competition using methods in natural language processing, using the firm 10-K reports from the SEC’s EDGAR website as source material (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010). Based on the descriptions in these annual reports, it is possible to determine the firms’ business contents and the relative position of its products and services vis-à-vis other firms in the marketplace. This strategy has been used to construct the

Text-based Network Industry Classification (TNIC) dataset by Hoberg and Phillips (2010). The TNIC is constructed from the textual analysis of business descriptions in 10-K reports for U.S. public firms and captures the degree to which the text in these reports by the focal firm is similar to the text in reports by all other firms (full details are available in Hoberg & Phillips, (2010). We therefore measure product differentiation, i.e., the extent to which the firm’s products or services are distinct from the rivals in its market, using market “similarity,” as defined on the TNIC dataset (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010, 2016). We used the TNIC data from 1996 to 2015, with 1996 being the first year of TNIC data availability. The year 1996 is also considered a watershed that witnessed a substantial expansion of software patentability because of favorable shifts in legal environments (Hall & MacGarvie, 2010).

The second data source we used to measure differentiation is the RavenPack News Analytics database (hereafter, RavenPack), which aggregates important corporate announcements and press releases from all major real-time news outlets. This unique database has been used in finance and accounting research (Akbas et al., 2017; Kelley & Tetlock, 2017; Shroff et al., 2014; Warren & Sorescu, 2017), but remains underutilized in IS research. RavenPack groups corporate news into content categories, such as acquisitions, bankruptcy, and product releases. We thus extracted from RavenPack all news articles (titles) within the category of “product release” involving U.S. public IT hardware firms between January 2000 (the first year of availability) and December 2015. We then downloaded the full texts of all these articles from the corresponding websites. After applying several filters to clean the sample following RavenPack’s guidelines (details provided in Appendix A), the final dataset consisted of 23,184 news articles (titles and full texts) on the hardware firms.

After merging the two datasets using GVKEY, we removed firms with no R&D expense reported or with no patent granted during the sample period to focus on firms that are active in R&D and innovation. The final sample contained 730 public non-software IT firms with 380,645 patents, of which 64,116 (16.8%) are on software. Table 1 provides the descriptive statistics of the variables used in our analysis, which is conducted at the firm-year level. We performed our analysis in two stages—first, we considered the effects of software-based innovation intensity on product differentiation from the TNIC dataset; subsequently, we considered new product launches from RavenPack. ## Analysis and Results

## Software-Based Innovation and Product Differentiation

We first tested the relationship between software-based innovation and product differentiation using the TNIC metrics. We measured software intensity, our proxy for software-based innovation, as the ratio of software patent stock to total patent stock—each depreciated at a 15% rate, consistent with prior work (Kim et al., 2016). As a robustness check, we also used the ratio of software patent stock to total assets and found fully consistent results.

As a first step, it is important to ensure that our measure of software intensity is strongly related to the software content of business descriptions in the 10-K reports, which we used to characterize firms’ positions in product markets in subsequent analyses. This is to show that the presence of software patents attributed to the firm is intentional, rather than a spurious correlation. As shown in Table 2, in which we regressed the (log) counts of the term “software” in 10-K business descriptions on software intensity, firms with higher software intensity used the term “software” more frequently in their business descriptions in the subsequent year. This supports our working assumption that hardware firms with higher software intensity indeed have more software-related activities, particularly products and services, in their business descriptions.<sup>3</sup>

Measuring product differentiation, as captured by the firm’s competitive positions in product markets, is nontrivial. Standard measures used in the literature, such as the Herfindahl-Hirschman index based on SIC codes, are inherently limited, as they rely on industry classifications that are static and rigid (Tang 2006). The static nature of these measures is particularly limiting since market boundaries are increasingly blurred because of technological convergence and the fast-moving nature of competition within the IT industry (Lee et al., 2010; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2008). More critical for our purpose, industry-level measures of competition cannot capture intra-industry changes in firm-specific competitive positions vis-à-vis their rivals. To meaningfully relate a specific firm’s software intensity to its product position, we need measures that capture the focal firm’s competitive positions against its rivals. The TNIC measurement scheme pioneered by Hoberg and Phillips (2010; 2016) allows just that.

Specifically, the TNIC scheme is constructed from the textual analysis of the vocabularies in 10-K business descriptions. Using these vocabularies, it computes the pairwise cosine similarity score between any two vectors of vocabularies, with each vector representing a firm’s product portfolio. Under this scheme, firms using substantially similar sets of vocabularies to describe their products are treated as belonging to effectively the same industry. This allows for a more accurate measure of competition that reflects the dynamic nature of the IT industry.

Moreover, unlike traditional industry classification systems (e.g., SIC, NAICS), each firm has its own TNIC industry with a different set of firms within that industry because, by construction, each firm faces a unique set of vocabulary vectors (i.e., firms) whose pairwise similarities with the focal firm fall above a prespecified threshold. The TNIC scheme and the similarity scores are updated every year as firms report new 10-Ks. This update of the entire TNIC dataset ensures that recent product innovations are reflected in the most recent TNIC and similarity scores.

Furthermore, the similarity of firms within a TNIC is based not only on the focal firm but also on choices made by other firms in the industry subclass, which more accurately reflects competitive dynamics in highly fluid industries (Hoberg et al., 2014). Thus, even if a firm remains “stationary,” it is possible for its product market to become more competitive if other firms choose to move closer in terms of product market space. Thus, in a sense, this measure further reduces the extent to which the focal firm “controls” the outcomes of their strategic decisions since competition here is a composite measure by design. From an endogeneity perspective, this allows us to partially dissociate the focal firm’s actions from the eventual product market competition it faces since this is also driven by other firm actions. These benefits of the structure of the TNIC make it particularly well-suited for studying fast-moving and competitive industries (Kim et al., 2016). Not surprisingly, research shows that the TNIC-based industry definition significantly improves the static industry classification schemes in accounting for many firm-level decisions (Hoberg & Phillips, 2010, 2016). Since its introduction, the TNIC has been extensively tested for validity and generalizability (Frésard et al., 2020; Hoberg & Phillips, 2016) and has been used in IS research as a measure of product market competition (Kim et al., 2016; Pan et al., 2019).

We adopt two measures of the firm’s competitive position within the TNIC scheme. The first is the total similarity score, which is the sum of a firm’s pairwise similarity scores with all other firms within the focal firm’s TNIC industry. A higher value on this measure implies that the focal firm’s product offerings are less differentiated from or more substitutable with those of firms within the same TNIC. Our second measure is the total number of firms within the focal firm’s TNIC in a given year. All things being equal, the more firms in a firm’s TNIC, the less distinct is the firm’s product portfolio. Following Hoberg and Phillips (2016), we relate the year-over-year change in these measures to the level of software intensity using the following baseline model:

$$
\Delta \mathrm{Y} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \mathrm{Y} _ {\mathrm{it+1}} - \mathrm{Y} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} * \mathrm{SI} _ {\mathrm{it}} + \beta_ {2} * \mathrm{X} _ {\mathrm{it}} + \gamma_ {s (i)} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}},\tag{1}
$$

where the subscripts represent firm i and year t, Y the level of product differentiation, SI the software intensity, X vectors of control variables capturing firm-specific characteristics, and $\gamma _ { s ( i ) }$ and $\delta _ { t }$ denoting SIC3- and yearfixed effects, respectively. Note that we use industry- (not firm-) fixed effects, to remain consistent with the regression specifications used by Hoberg and Phillips (2016). Because our dependent variable is the difference between year t+1 and year t, the regression amounts to correlating the level of software intensity with the change in product differentiation in the next year. As controls, we included R&D intensities (measured by R&D stock, constructed analogously to patent stock, divided by assets) and sales (as a proxy for firm size), all of which have been shown to influence product market competition (Hoberg & Phillips, 2016) and new product launches (Katila & Ahuja, 2002; Smith et al., 2005), which we examine in the next section. We also controlled for the overall patent intensity, which may affect new product launches and resulting product differentiation (Argente et al., 2020). Finally, we included industry R&D intensity because the focal firm’s degree of product differentiation should be also affected by the R&D investments of other firms in the TNIC industry (Hoberg & Phillips, 2016). We kept our set of control variables parsimonious so that similar controls could be employed in both analyses of product market competition and new product launches (in the next section). Nonetheless, our results are robust to the addition of more control variables including industry advertising ratio, industry stock return, and industry book-to-market ratio (results available upon request from the authors). We report robust standard errors clustered by firm.

Table 3 reports the regression results. Model 1 shows OLS estimates of the change in total similarity score on software intensity and controls. Here, the coefficient on software intensity is significantly negative, indicating that the greater a firm’s reliance on software patents, the more dissimilar the firm’s products become in its own TNIC. We find a similar pattern when we replace similarity with the number of firms in the TNIC (Model 2). Together, these results suggest that greater rates of software patenting help IT hardware firms increasingly differentiate themselves from rivals. This supports our expectation about the differentiation effect of software-based innovation.<sup>4</sup> Note that the results are obtained after controlling for several firm-level characteristics that closely vary with firm size. Thus, the negative coefficient of software intensity does not simply capture size effects. Intriguingly, the coefficient on overall patenting intensity indicates a strong increase in product similarity. In other words, as firms invest in new technologies, most of which are in hardware because of the nature of their business, they face greater competition from direct rivals who make investments in similar hardware technologies. By contrast, investments in software innovation help firms stand out from the crowd. We consider new product launches from RavenPack next.

## Software-Based Innovation and New Product Launches

In this section, we use new product launch data from RavenPack to provide more direct, product-based evidence of the implications of software-based innovation in the hardware industry. To identify differentiated products, we apply LDA (latent Dirichlet allocation)-based topic modeling technique to the corpus of titles and contents of all new product announcements by our sample firms. The objective here is to be able to classify the various new product launches into groups so that we can understand how firms may choose between these groups in terms of their product launches. This strategy allows us to create a measure of how novel or differentiated the new products introduced by a firm are over time. While the identification of topic-based groups is exploratory at this point, given the relatively low volume of prior IS work using RavenPack, we believe this represents a first attempt at using this dataset to create measures of product differentiation, with more future research needed to fully explore this dataset.

That said, in the current context, we identify eight topic groups from the RavenPack data, with each topic group representing a distinct product group in the IT hardware industry, based on topic coherence scores (Newman et al., 2010). From these topic groups, we create two indicators of product differentiation: differentiated product launch and novel product launch. For the first measure, we first computed the average cosine similarity score between a focal new product and each of all other products launched in the same topic group in the prior three years, with a product represented by a vector of words contained in the announcement.<sup>5</sup> We considered a product as “differentiated” when the product’s average cosine similarity was in the bottom 10% of all new products launched in the same topic group in the same year. Hence, differentiated product launch, a firm-level dummy variable, captures the firm’s record of launching at least one new product that is distinctly different from other products launched in the same year.

## Robustness checks

The level of software intensity can be a strategic choice of firms, suggesting potential bias from the endogeneity in the variable. Although our use of industry-level controls (Table 3) and firm-fixed effects (Table 4) should largely absorb influences from industry-level variations and firm-level factors, it is hard to remove all sources of bias from potential endogeneity. However, to the extent that further testing can help reduce the threats of bias and enhance confidence in our results, we conducted supplementary analyses using a difference-in-differences (DiD) design and an instrumental variable (IV) method. We report on these tests below.

First, the pool of software workers can serve as an exogenous shock to software patenting, assuming simply that a higher supply of software engineers or professionals is likely to lead to greater software patenting in regions, relative to other regions where there are fewer software engineers available. Second, responses to the supply shock should vary depending on the geographical distribution of firms’ software R&D activities, such that the effect of any change in software workers in a state should be proportionately greater for firms with a higher share of software R&D activities in that state, proxied by the state’s share in prior software patents.<sup>8</sup>

While it is conceivable, indeed likely, that firms would locate their development centers in specific regions strategically for the purposes of availing of local talent, we argue that these development centers, once established, tend to remain for many years and are not moved or relocated over short time-periods. Thus, once established, they are prone to local supply shocks of software talent that may emerge from other sources, such as universities and local regulations (Alcacer & Chung, 2007; Tambe & Hitt, 2014). Furthermore, the strategic locations of these development centers in specific regions are not likely to affect the product differentiation that the firm enjoys globally in its product markets—most hardware firms operate in markets that extend beyond the immediate region. Therefore, while the supply of software talent in a region is important, we submit that it can affect product differentiation for the firm only through the pathway of enabling internal software-based innovation, which is what we intend to capture through our analysis.<sup>9</sup>

To construct the instrument, we first computed the state-year number of workers in software-related occupations, NSW, using data from the Occupational Employment Statistics (OES) program at the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The OES occupational codes identify software-related occupations such as computer programmers and systems analysts.<sup>10</sup> For each firm, we also computed the state’s share of software patents among the total patents, w\_SI, with the state based on the first inventor’s address in the firm’s patent (Bloom et al., 2013). Finally, we constructed a firm-year level composite measure of software human capital, $F S W _ { i t } ,$ as the cross-state sum of the software-related worker pool (NSW), weighted by the statespecific firm demand in software technology (w\_SI):

$$
F S W _ {i t} = \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {5 0} w _ {-} S I _ {i s t} * N S W _ {i s t}\tag{2}
$$

As explained above, we contend that the supply shock of available software human capital should affect the software intensity of firms but not product differentiation, except through software intensity, thereby satisfying the requirements of an IV.

The results from this IV analysis (the results are available upon request) confirm our main finding based on TNIC measures: software intensity is negatively related to both the change in the total similarity score and the number of TNIC firms. Thus, the IV analysis renders additional support for the robustness of our main findings. While fully addressing the issue of endogeneity is difficult in secondary data contexts, we believe that these additional tests, albeit imperfect, help attenuate the concerns to an extent.

## Other Robustness Checks

We also performed a battery of additional robustness checks to ensure the validity of our results. These additional tests include varying the measure of software intensity, dropping firms in the telecommunications sector, and limiting the sample to single-business firms. Our main results were robust to these tests, providing empirical robustness to our baseline results reported here.

## Discussion and Conclusion

It is clear that software-based innovation has become increasingly critical to business value, even in non-software industries. Although recent work has provided empirical evidence for these effects (Branstetter et al., 2018; Chung et al., 2019), there remain gaps in our understanding of the specific mechanisms by which software-based innovation creates value. We address this important gap by studying the IT hardware industry and proposing that one such mechanism involves increased product differentiation. Software-enabled innovation allows hardware firms to exploit these innovations to enhance their existing product offerings or launch more distinct products, thereby better differentiating themselves from their rivals in product markets. These effects, in aggregate, are likely to provide such hardware firms with increased market power and performance, as documented in some prior studies (Chung et al., 2019; Hall & MacGarvie, 2010). However, going beyond this broad result, we provide empirical evidence for the relevance of product differentiation as a critical outcome that results from investments in software-oriented technologies for hardware firms.

Our study is also closely linked to the broader literature on digital innovation postulating that embedding software capabilities into traditionally non-software products and services strongly impacts innovation outcomes and processes (Yoo et al., 2012). Software, and more broadly digital technology, has unique characteristics such as reprogrammability that allow for the multifunctionality of digital devices and the homogenization of data that enables decoupling between content and medium (Yoo et al., 2010). Because of these unique characteristics, software-based digital innovation is very different from traditional innovations and more frequently involves disruptive changes in firm strategies and market structures (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014, 2015; Svahn et al., 2017). While this literature sheds light on the fundamental properties of digital technologies and their effects on innovation processes and outcomes, most current work tends to be conceptual and descriptive, with a few notable exceptions (Arora et al., 2013; Chung et al., 2019; Lee & Berente, 2012). Moreover, this line of research offers little insight into how investments in digital innovation within the firm translate to organization-level performance metrics. By focusing on this gap, our work complements the literature on digital innovation and its critical consequences.

Our study examines one specific industry with roots in both manufacturing and digital technologies—IT hardware. Given the near-ubiquity of software-based innovation in today’s businesses, we believe that our findings can be generalized to other industries. However, the extent to which these relationships show up across industries may still vary. Our work here points the way for more future research studying possible interindustry variations on how softwarebased innovation may enhance or hinder efforts at product differentiation. In particular, considering differences in modes of competition and value appropriation across industries will add context and nuance to the results that we uncover within the hardware industry, while adding to the literature on software-based innovation in general.

Our findings suggest that managers at hardware firms should not underplay the importance of developing software capabilities within their product development teams, since software functionality allows them to enhance differentiation in their markets. Given the cumulative nature of returns to investments in capability building (Ethiraj et al., 2005), developing software capabilities likely offers longlasting supply-side benefits by allowing the lower-cost provision of hardware products over time in addition to the differentiation-based demand-side benefits. Of course, managers should not stretch this evidence to the limit, such as solely focusing on software innovation, as it would entirely change the nature of the firm as well as its competitive pressures, which clearly falls outside the scope of our analysis.

Our work has some limitations that need to be addressed. First, we identify software patents using heuristics consistent with prior research; however, the general notion that software cannot be patented remains a limitation in this context. Moreover, not all patents directly contribute to product development and may instead be used for other strategic purposes such as entry deterrence. Most likely, these limitations render our measure of software intensity an underestimate of the extent to which firms are truly investing in software innovation. But in the absence of clearer measures of software-based innovation, we use the best possible alternatives. Second, we develop models using DiD and instrumental variable regressions to address the endogeneity in software-based innovation and obtain consistent results. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that it is impossible to entirely rule out potential endogeneity bias. Third, we include a relatively new dataset for the IS context (RavenPack) and provide exploratory topic modeling results on this dataset. A more rigorous treatment of this dataset from a data-science perspective using advancements in natural language processing is beyond the scope of this paper but remains an interesting avenue for future research. In our specific context, we acknowledge that our analysis of this dataset is not exhaustive and is not meant to be such. Finally, we examine one possible mechanism—product differentiation—that may underlie the observed association between software-based innovation and firm value. This of course does not rule out other possible mechanisms. We believe more work is needed to test them as well.

In conclusion, we demonstrate the empirical reality behind the aphorism that “software is eating the world.” Indeed, the success of the current platform giants (Facebook, Amazon, Netflix, Google), also referred to as FANG, speak to the power of software-enabled innovation in upending the existing market structure. In 2017 alone, these four firms together added more than \$600 billion to the U.S. market cap, representing 13.8% of the increase in total market cap. Given the trends associated with the dominance of software, it is imperative for firms and managers to figure out ways to use software-based innovation in their favor. This is also an important question for IS scholars since software development and software-related capabilities remain a core research area within the IS research tradition (Aggarwal et al., 2015; Boh et al., 2007). By empirically examining how software-based innovation is being played out in the IT hardware industry, our work here adds to the small literature studying these important questions; we hope that more research will explore these questions in greater depth in the years to come.

## Sample Selection from the RavenPack Dataset

We began by downloading from the Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS), which provides a large data table that describes all news articles on RavenPack News Analytics.<sup>11</sup> This data table includes 46 variables that characterize each news article, with each observation in this table representing a news article. We then took the following steps to select the final sample of articles—all news articles about new product announcements made by U.S. public IT hardware firms between January 2000 and December 2015.

1. Keep only the observations where the variables “TYPE” and “CATEGORY” are “product-release.”

2. Keep only the observations where the variable “NEWS\_TYPE” is “FULL-ARTICLE” or “PRESS-RELEASE.” We only retain these the two types of news articles because these news articles contain both headline and full text, while news articles of the other types either only have headlines or are composed of tables.

3. Keep only observations where the variable “PRODUCT\_KEY” is “DJ-EQ,” which indicates news articles that are in the “Equities Edition” and “Dow Jones (DJ) Edition.” Because “all Equities editions contain only entities that are classified as companies,” we use “Equities Edition” to ensure that our news articles can be matched to our patent data at the firm level. RavenPack News Analytics comprises of 4 editions (also called packages): DJ Edition, PR Edition, Web Edition, and Full Edition (an edition combining the other three editions). The first three packages collect news article from different sources. To avoid overlap in news articles across different packages (i.e. news articles from different packages capture the same event), we are advised to use only one of the first three packages. We use the DJ Edition, which has data from the earliest available year (2000).

4. Keep only the observations where the variable “RELEVANCE” equals 100. This condition allows us to select only important firms in news articles.

5. Keep only the observations where the variable “ENS” equals 100. A news article with “ENS” of 100 means that this news article is the newest within a 24-hour time in a particular package. By keeping only the observations with “ENS” of 100, we eliminate duplicate news articles that describe the same event but are reported later in time.

6. Keep only the observations where the variable “ISIN” starting with “US.” This allows us to select news articles where the entities are in the U.S.

7. Construct a new variable “GVKEY” by converting the ISIN to GVKEY and then drop the observations that do not have a GVKEY.

8. Find a SIC code for each firm and select only news articles from IT hardware firms according to the SIC codes identified beforehand.

After applying these steps, we were left with 23,184 news articles about new product announcements by U.S. public IT hardware firm between 2000 and 2015. These articles were then used in subsequent analysis.

For the textual analysis, we needed the actual content of these articles. We thus collected the titles and full texts of articles from RavenPack by searching, using their unique IDs (RP\_STORY\_IDs) on the RavenPack website<sup>12</sup> and then scraping the news articles from the corresponding webpages, which include both titles and full texts for each RP\_STORY\_ID using Python.

## Appendix B

## Topic Modeling Analysis on RavenPack Data

## Text Preprocessing

We collected the titles and contents of news articles about new product announcements from RavenPack using a web scrawler. We then completed a series of preprocessing steps to the texts. First, we removed paragraphs on general company descriptions and customary statements, which contain no information about new products. We also removed stop words, punctuation, URLs, human names, numbers, dates, geographic words, and company brands and names. We lemmatized words, dropping the words with three or fewer letters as they are typically product serial numbers. To account for sentence structure and syntax, we included both unigrams and bigrams. Finally, we dropped infrequent terms (in ≤ 0.5% of documents).

## Topic Extraction

We used the preprocessed texts to generate a document-word matrix of term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF). TF-IDF is a numerical weighting statistic that measures the importance of a token to a document in a corpus. It thus addresses the problem that the most common words among documents tend to be less important. For topic modeling, we applied the LDA model to the TF-IDF matrix using “LdaModel” from the package “genism” in Python.<sup>13</sup>

We ran multiple LDA models that generated different numbers of topic groups and chose the best LDA model according to topic coherence. Based on the idea that a good LDA model should be close to human judgment, topic coherence rates topic models according to their understandability to humans and is widely used to evaluate LDA topic models in research (Mimno et al., 2011; Newman et al., 2010). Specifically, we compared the coherence score, which indicates how often the words appear together within a topic, across different LDA models and chose the LDA model with the highest coherence score. The higher the coherence score of a topic model, the better the topic model. Several measures are available for calculating coherence scores, such as the UCI measure (Newman et al., 2010), the UMass measure (Mimno et al., 2011), and the CV measure, which is a composite of multiple known measures (Röder et al., 2015). We adopted the CV measure, which is shown to outperform other measures (Röder et al., 2015).

In implementing the “LdaModel” in Python, which requires the setting of a series of hyperparameters, we set the parameter “iterations” to 5000 and “random\_state” to 350. We also set “coherence” to “c\_v”. For other hyperparameters, we retained the default values. We tried running the LDA models with the same parameter setting mentioned above and different “num\_topic.” The parameter “num\_topic” means the number of topic groups that a LDA model generates. We varied “num\_topic,” ranging from 10 to 100 with a step of 10 (i.e., 10, 20, 30, … 100). In this range, the LDA model of 10 topic groups exhibited the highest coherence score. We then tried a more fine-grained variation of “num\_topic” that ranged from 2 to 10 with a step of 1. On this variation, the coherence score peaked at 0.575 when the number of topics was 8. Thus, we determined the optimal number of topics in RavenPack new product announcement data to be 8. ## Procedures to Create Differentiated Product Launch

Differentiated product launch is a dummy variable that equals 1 if the average cosine similarity between a focal new product and all other products in the same topic group in the prior three years is in the bottom 10% of all new products launched in the same topic group in the same year. The numerical example below explains the procedures to create this variable.

Suppose that Topic 0 includes 5, 10, 15, and 20 new products from 2000 to 2003, respectively. To identify the differentiated products launched in Topic 0 in 2003, we take the following steps. First, for each of the 20 products in Topic 0 in 2003, we calculate the cosine similarity between the vector of the product and the vector of each product in the same topic group in the past three years (i.e., 2000, 2001, and 2002). This generates 600 product pairs (i.e., 20 \* (5+10+15) = 600 pairs), each of which has a cosine similarity value. Second, we calculate the average cosine similarity for each of these 20 products and then sort the 20 similarity values in a descending order. Third, we create the bottom 10% threshold of the lowest cosine similarity values. If a product has a cosine similarity below the threshold, we assign the value of 1 for this product to the dummy variable differentiated product launch and 0 otherwise. Tables C1, C2, and C3 show the procedures used to create this variable using this numerical example with additional hypothetical data. The same procedures were repeated to generate the differentiated product launch at the firm-year level for each of the eight topic groups and for each year from 2003 to 2015.

## Procedures to Create Novel Product Launch

Novel product launch is a dummy variable that equals 1 if a firm launched at least one new product in a topic group in which the firm had not launched any new product in the past five years. The numerical example below explains the procedures to create this variable. Suppose that a firm had at least one new product in the following topic groups and years respectively: Topics 1 and 2 in year 2000; Topic 1, 2, and 3 in year 2001; Topic 4 in year 2002; Topics 1 and 4 in year 2003; Topics 1 and 3 in year 2004; Topics 1 and 7 in year 2005; and Topics 1 and 2 in year 2006. To generate novel product launch for this firm in the focal year 2005, we would check whether this firm launched at least one new product in either Topic 1 or 7. This includes checking whether at least one new product was launched by the firm in any of these topics in the previous five years. In other words, we would check whether Topic 1 or 7 was observed during the period from 2000 to 2004 as well. For Topic 1, since the firm had already launched new products in topic 1 in 2000, 2001, 2003, and 2004, Topic 1 is not a novel topic for this firm as of 2005. For Topic 7, because this firm did not launch any new product in Topic 7 from 2000 to 2004 but launched at least one new product in Topic 7 in 2005, Topic 7 is a novel topic for this firm as of 2005. Because Topic 7 is a novel topic (although Topic 1 is not a nove topic), the dummy variable novel product launch for this firm in the year 2005 is assigned the value of 1. Similarly, to generate the dummy variable novel product launch for this firm in the year 2006, we would check whether Topic 1 or 2, which were observed in 2006, were also observed from 2001 to 2005. Because Topic 1 was observed in 2001, 2003, 2004, and 2005 and Topic 2 was observed in 2001, neither topic 1 nor topic 2 is a novel topic. Therefore, the dummy variable novel product launch for this firm in the year 2006 is assigned the value of 0. ## Appendix D

## Representative Examples of Software and Non-Software Patents

## Software Patents

## 1. Visual presentation of multiple internet pages (Patent No. 8,347,230)

Abstract: A novel method for graphically organizing and displaying multiple pages of internet content based on how the page was accessed. Pages are determined to be indirectly accessed if they were accessed via a hypertext link or a trail of hypertext links. Pages are determined to be directly accessed if accessed via other means, such as a bookmark or via Uniform Resource Locator (URL). Page groupings are created for each directly accessed page, and each indirectly accessed page is categorized into a grouping associated with a directly accessed page containing a link through which the indirectly accessed page was accessed. The web pages are presented to the user in an interface that is a graphical representation of the page groupings, wherein a user selects a page to view by first selecting the page's page grouping and then selecting the page.

## 2. Password protection for removable hard drive (Patent No. 6,012,146)

Abstract: A device and method for preventing access to data on a hard drive in which a first password is stored on the hard drive so that it is not accessible from a system in which the hard drive is installed, and in which a second password is provided from the system. A comparison of the two passwords is made in a processor in the hard drive to determine whether the two passwords are the same. If the result of the comparison of the first and second passwords is that the two passwords are not the same, access to the hard drive is denied.

## 3. Unlocking a device by performing gestures on an unlock image (Patent No. 8,046,721)

Abstract: A device with a touch-sensitive display may be unlocked via gestures performed on the touch-sensitive display. The device is unlocked if contact with the display corresponds to a predefined gesture for unlocking the device. The device displays one or more unlock images with respect to which the predefined gesture is to be performed in order to unlock the device. The performance of the predefined gesture with respect to the unlock image may include moving the unlock image to a predefined location and/or moving the unlock image along a predefined path. The device may also display visual cues of the predefined gesture on the touch screen to remind a user of the gesture.

## Non-Software Patents

## 1. Device and method for providing a true semiconductor die to external fiber optic cable connection (Patent No. 6,364,542)

Abstract: A low cost device that provides a true die to external fiber optic connection. The device includes a semiconductor die having a first surface, an integrate circuit fabricated on the first surface of the semiconductor die and a package encapsulating the semiconductor die. The device also includes a module mounted onto the package and configured to receive an external fiber optic cable. An opto-electric device, housed in the module is optically coupled to the fiber optic cable when the cable is inserted into the module. To provide the direct die to external fiber optic connection, at least one electrical conductor is provided between the integrated circuit fabricated the semiconductor die, through the package and module, and directly to the opto-electrical device.

## 2. Packaged microchip with isolator having selected modulus of elasticity (Patent No. 6,946,742)

Abstract: A packaged microchip has a stress sensitive microchip, a package having a package modulus of elasticity, and an isolator between the microchip and the package. The isolator has an isolator modulus of elasticity that has a relationship with the package modulus of elasticity. This relationship causes no more than a negligible thermal stress to be transmitted to the microchip.

## 3. Piezoelectric chromic impact sensor (Patent No. 8,544,337)

Abstract: An impact sensor includes a piezoelectric transducer operatively connected to a chromic device. The chromic device includes a chromic material that changes from a first color state to a second color state in response to electric power generated by the piezoelectric transducer when exposed to a given level of impact force. The chromic material is bistable so that the chromic material remains in the second color state for a significant amount of time. An impact force to which the sensor has been subjected may be quantified by observing the chromic device. In one embodiment, the chromic material is an electrochromic material, such as a viologen, that changes through a color gradient of light transmission states from the first color state to the second color state. A printed color gradient may be used to aid in quantifying the impact force. In another embodiment, the chromic device includes a thermochromic material.
