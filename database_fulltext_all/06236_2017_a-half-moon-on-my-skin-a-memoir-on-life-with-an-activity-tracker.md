ETHNOGRAPHY/NARRATIVE

# A half-moon on my skin: a memoir on life with an activity tracker

Elpida Prasopoulou

Centre for Business in Society, Coventry University, Priory Street, Coventry CV1 5FB, UK

Correspondence: Elpida Prasopoulou, Centre for Business in Society, Coventry University, Priory Street, Coventry CV1 5FB, UK E-mail: elpida.prasopoulou@coventry.ac.uk

## Abstract

Experiential computing shifts the focus of IS research on how humans encounter information technologies in everyday life. For this, it invites explorations on the role of the body and its affective propulsions as the locus of human experience. I argue, in this article, that this embodied perspective, studied through the lens of enchanted materialism (Bennett in The enchantment of modern life: attachments, crossings, and ethics. Princeton University Press, Princeton, 2001), needs to be combined with an alternative writing genre to fruitfully explore life in experiential computing. It is for this reason that I present my research as a memoir on the use of wearables – an instantiation of the Internet of Things – in everyday life. The aim is to show that, when studying emerging technologies, an open-ended non-fictional genre like the memoir can produce astute and imaginative insights that may be as informative as traditional academic writing. The memoir produces a thick description of the way people interact with digital devices in experiential computing; their expectations and the subsequent effort to entangle the data in their daily lives. This way, it problematises current organising visions of a data-driven life. I conclude the article with reflections on the contribution that enchanted materialism makes to information systems, as well as the memoir’s potential for academic research. Furonean Journal of Information, Systems (201.7)

Keywords: memoir; human body; experiential computing; enchanted materialism; wearables; Internet of Things

## Introduction

The Internet of Things is reaching a tipping point as a growing number of devices become connected to each other (Raconteur, 2016). Motivating the development of this interconnected cyber-physical infrastructure are visions of ‘sentient’ environments (Tironi & Sanchez-Criado, 2015) that will record data on humans and non-humans alike to offer an augmented living experience (So¨derstro¨m et al, 2014). So far, research is focusing on the technical elements (i.e. connectivity, standards) of this emerging environment (e.g. Atzori et al, 2010). The way humans qua users will live in the Internet of Things receives attention mainly in terms of the services that could be offered by connected devices (Wilson et al, 2015). Yet, the incorporation of sensors into everyday physical objects is not just the technological upgrade of things previously seen as low-tech or even mundane (Andrejevic & Burdon, 2015). The Internet of Things weaves together humans, things, and data in ways envisaged to overcome our current understanding of what constitutes a living space (Rose, 2014). This prompts us to think anew how ‘humans come into being with [a] world’ (Braun & Whatmore, 2010: xviii) where technologies increasingly blur the distinction between physical and digital spaces.

In this article, I focus on the lived experience in the Internet of Things in order to capture how humans, digital devices, and data become entangled through daily use. My objective is to explore how digitally mediated experiences transform familiar, everyday activities. This question has been raised by Yoo (2010) when discussing humans qua users in experiential computing. As he explains, this new form of computing requires the ‘physical, direct, and existential participation’ of human users (Yoo, 2010: 218). Hence, the need to explore how everyday practices are reshaped as humans encounter the Internet of Things. To this end, I show how to trace this process by adopting an embodied point of view, which can capture the ‘concrete, lived human life’ (Kozel, 2007: 5) as it happens. This approach maintains the materialist underpinnings of recent information Systems (IS) research on socio-technical phenomena (Prasopoulou, 2013). It also invites Information Systems research to focus on the body (flesh, feelings, and thoughts) for sourcing knowledge on human–non-human encounters in emerging cyber-physical spaces like the Internet of Things.

Materialist accounts have shown that the capacities of the human body emerge in relation to ‘the technicity that precedes and exceeds it’ (Braun & Whatmore, 2010: xix). This suggests a constitutive entanglement of human bodies and technologies where the latter are not simply the product of human activity but originary of what it means to be human (Zylinska, 2014). Yet, IS research has paid scant attention to how humans, through their bodies, actively compose the world they inhabit. Instead, most research accounts explore materiality in the form of algorithms (Orlikowski and Scott, 2014), robots (Beane & Orlikowski, 2016), and computer grids (Venters et al, 2014). Experiential computing (Yoo, 2010), a relatively new but growing strand of research in Information Systems, shifts attention back to humans. Yoo’s intention is to study how the sensorisation of everyday things and the accompanying data infrastructure alters our everyday lives. In doing so, he introduces an expansive view of users which includes ordinary individuals in their daily lives. As living spaces change, Yoo (2010) continues, the constitution of everyday practices needs to be studied anew focusing on the experience of living in a space which fuses physical things with digital processes.

I argue that part of this endeavour rests on the construction of narrative devices, or alternative genres (Rowe, 2012), that celebrate the mundane as well as the extraordinary (Kozel, 2007) while focusing on both the lived experience and the organising vision that provides the frameworks to interpret them (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). The memoir, as a non-fictional genre, supports the reflective exploration of life. It can infuse the academic text with the ‘sensations, inner voices, and insights that arrive obliquely, unbidden, in the midst of life’ (Kozel, 2007: xvi) as we study how wearables become both intimate companions and recording devices of everyday life. It, thus, provides the requisite narrative device, that is both fluid and rigorous, to bring to the fore the reflective process people use to make sense of the world though their bodies. This way, the memoir paves the way for a critical exploration of the contours of organising visions for the Internet of Things that translate the body into data to be constantly captured, transmitted, and analysed (Viseu, 2013). It permits the writing of an alter-tale to current narratives of how humans will live inside cyber-physical systems. Finally, it offers IS the opportunity to embrace critical aspects of materialist research while incorporating, in its research agenda, the study of humans qua users in the Internet of Things.

The article, therefore, responds to recent calls, in the field of Information Systems, to move beyond organisational contexts and study information technologies in everyday settings (Yoo, 2010), albeit from a poststructuralist philosophical stance (see Howarth, 2013). My aim is to examine how humans encounter new digital devices, like wearables, in the context of their everyday activities. The motive is to provide an empirically driven user perspective of experiential computing while critically reflecting on organising visions of computing in everyday life (Dourish, 2001). Theoretically, I build on Jane Bennett’s (2001) enchanted materialism to explore the lived experience in an emerging world where ‘we become closer to our computers [as] they become extensions of our way of thinking, moving, and touching (Kozel, 2007: xiv). The visceral dimension in Bennett’s (2001, 2010) work allows IS research to introduce affect in explanations of how people encounter new forms of computing. Empirically, I have minutely monitored my everyday experiences of using wearables, an activity tracker in the case of this research. I recorded both the mundane, repetitive actions and the extraordinary moments of life with wearables. In doing so, I followed Bennett’s (2001) advice to observe human–non-human encounters in the midst of life (Merleau-Ponty, 1945); that is as they occur. This allowed me to capture the raw experience of humans in a systematic effort to analyse their encounters with digital devices and data in the Internet of Things.

The article is organised as follows: the next section discusses the memoir as my choice of alternative genre for writing research. Then in the memoir, I explore in depth my encounters with an activity tracker – my first impressions and the process of entangling the device in my everyday activities. In the last section, I delve further into the issues I present in the memoir and link them to current themes in IS research. The article concludes with a reflection on the challenges and opportunities that arise when writing memoirs, and having to follow the conventions of the genre, while still reporting research results.

## Narrating the body in experiential computing

Studying the encounters of humans with digital technologies in experiential computing requires tackling two interrelated issues: (1) how to study the lived experience with technologies designed to become assimilated to the human body and seamlessly capture bodily data; and, (2) how to convey these encounters, at once intimate and impersonal, in a way that will highlight the ‘body’s perpetual becoming’ (Seigworth & Gregg, 2010: 3) with this new (digital) materiality. Part of the answer lies in the choice of writing genre. For narrating these new encounters, one has to capture ‘the sensory, affective, poetic, and corporeal qualities of the lived experience’ (Hansen and Kozel, 2007: 208) while exploring how this experience is altered by digital technologies. The text, therefore, must serve as a laboratory (Latour, 2004) that enables experimentation in tandem with critical reflection on the way the human body senses the world in the Internet of Things.

It is for these reasons that I have opted to write research as memoir. The memoir is a non-fictional genre, which depicts the lives of real people (Couser, 2012). As a narrative device, it can convey a breadth of experiences since it has been founded on an originary human activity, namely the narration of our personal experiences in our own voice. It can also write the human body, increasingly seen in recent research (e.g. Stuart and Todd, 2009), as ‘a kind of ongoing textual narrative of the course of our lives’ (Dow Adams, 2009: x). The memoir invites the exploration of ‘deep human needs, desires, and habitual practices’ (Couser, 2012: 9). This way, it enables humans to speak from their own ‘subjectivity, experiences, and practical (bodily) knowledge’ (Dow Adams, 2009: ix). In the context of such a reflective way of writing the human experience, the memoir also empowers people to critically reflect on their encounters with non-human bodies – in this case wearables – offering an entry point into life in the Internet of Things.

The challenge with technologies that are enthusiastically anticipated while remaining in a state of emergence (Thrift, 2014) is to go beyond organising visions already conditioning our perception of them (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). The Internet of Things constitutes one such technology that has been significantly popularised as a vision through industry white papers (e.g. Accenture Strategy, 2015) and government reports (e.g. GOfS, 2014). These texts are frequently overly enthusiastic of the potentialities lying ahead (So¨derstro¨m et al, 2014; Tironi and Sanchez-Criado, 2015). As a result, life in the Internet of Things is mainly articulated in terms of business opportunities and anticipated economic value (So¨derstro¨m et al, 2014). Human experience is reduced to computational responsiveness (Gabrys, 2014), despite the promise of enrichment (Raconteur. 2015) or augmentation (Viseu, 2013). There remains, therefore, a need to explore how people experience living in data-intensive environments and how such experiences affect their everyday practices or lead to the constitution of new ones. In the case of wearables, the need to tap into the lived experience is more pronounced because of the way they are tethered to the body (Gilmore, 2015) and their potential impact on how people perceive themselves.

In recent years, there were attempts to address this issue with alternative writing genres like science fiction (e.g. Various, 2014). This leads to dystopian images of widespread surveillance and loss of autonomy (e.g. Institute for the Future, 2013) which fail to explain the growing number of people engaging with Internet of Things technologies, especially wearables. Similarly, efforts to explore the lived experience through digital media art (e.g. Kozel, 2007) involve experimental designs of wearables at the fringes of organising visions for the Internet of Things. Thus, they provide little explanation on the motivation to engage in data practices in the context of everyday life. It is only recently that research shifted to the everyday use of wearables (e.g. Sharon & Zandbergen, 2016; Gilmore, 2015; Schu¨ll, 2016) in an effort to understand the constitution of new data-driven practices and their affective qualities for people. Still, the majority of this work consists of conceptual explorations with little empirical grounding for their observations on life in cyber-physical spaces.

In this article, I contribute to this stream of research with a memoir on living with an activity tracker. My motivation is to show that studying experiential computing requires not only new conceptual tools and research designs but also different narrative devices prompting us to reflect upon our own experiences of living in newly emerging spaces. As Kozel aptly explains: ‘writing from the new is writing from the void, and it amounts to a writing from our own bodies, from the moment to moment of our own existence’ (2007: 8). In this context, the memoir is well suited to record human encounters with new technologies. Its primary objective is to enable the author to reflect on life experiences and construct a sense of self through the developing narrative (Couser, 2012).

The analytic reflexivity (Denzin, 2006) characterising memoir, as a genre, can help trace and write the ‘force of encounters’ (Seigworth & Gregg, 2010: 2) that underpins the affective constitution of the human body and its world. In experiential computing, as the author writes her body, the transformation of the human experience, and subsequently the meaning of everyday activities, is revealed. Hence the memoir serves a critical function by providing the medium to transform the visceral/affective aspects of human–non-human encounters into a coherent narrative. In terms of form, a memoir may not seem different from a novel. The narrative is more similar to that of the literature than other non-fictional genres, especially academic writing. This mixing of narration and reflection is the main advantage of writing memoirs. ‘Memoir is a peculiarly open form, inviting broken and incomplete images, half-recollected fragments, all the mass (and mess) of detail,’ Hampl (1999: 33) reminds us. A memoir ‘can present its story and consider the meaning of the story’ (Hampl, 1999: 33). As such, it brings affect to the fore while, concurrently, exploring its meaning for everyday practices. This way, it pierces through the organising visions for the Internet of Things capturing the individual, everyday experience with technologies.

It should be noted that it is not the first time memoirs are introduced in academic research. Turkle (2007) also uses memoir in order to highlight elements of research usually silenced in academic writing. Her book Evocative Objects (Turkle, 2007) is a collection of brief memoirs where people reflect on the significance of specific objects in their lives. Turkle (2007) pairs these personal stories with academic texts, allowing for what Richardson (1997: 2) calls the integration of ‘academic interests, social concerns, [and] emotional needs’. This mixing together of personal experiences with academic concerns is quite important when exploring, as in this article, how people make sense of the world with and through their bodies.

In this attempt to write research in an alternative genre, I follow Solnit’s (2000, 2005) example of short memoirs piecing together autobiographical stories with more theoretical reflections on her subject matter. There is a tendency to think of memoirs as long life stories narrating events in chronological order. Solnit reinvents the genre while concocting a narrative device (Czarniawska, 1998) that allows her stories to be told in ways that evoke emotions but also leave space for theoretical reflection. This way, her texts ‘invert binary categories such as emotional/rational, personal/theoretical, social/individual, and […] collapse these categories into one another’ (Gannon, 2006: 477). Following a similar structure, the memoir I present here narrates how the activity tracker became entangled into my everyday life. It is both an empirically driven account and a critical reflection on the promises of experiential computing for a different, almost somatic encounter between people and technology. To describe such somatic effects, I use the story of a personal accident that altered, for a while, the surface of my skin. It showcases Bennett’s (2001) enchantment; and, explains my quest for an intimate, immediate encounter with wearables.

The finished text conveys the ‘evidence of experience (Gannon, 2006: 476) that grants the researcher the authority to speak, without losing sight of the constructed nature of experience. My wager is that writing research as memoir will problematise taken-for-granted notions of what constitutes valid research in Information Systems. The aim is to open up the field to new theoretical perspectives and methodological approaches in order to bring in new and intriguing IT artefacts that remain outside the purview of existing research.

## A half-moon on my skin: a memoir

I’m on a narrow path in my uncle’s garden. People are walking up and down in a hurry. It’s quite busy. My uncle is getting married and family and friends have gathered for the celebrations. I’m quite young, just five years old. On occasions like this, I would squeeze through the adults making my way to the garden. But this time I cannot. I have a big blister on my left arm. At the hairdresser’s, earlier in the day, I passed by the base of the curling iron burning my arm. So now, I must be really careful as everything can potentially pierce the blister. And my mother made it very clear that this would not be a good thing. So here I am, walking down this path. It is quite a feat! Adults rushing from the house with bottles of wine or plates of food; children following them, yearning for their attention. And, in the midst of it all, me, trying to reach my parents’ table in the garden.

It was a brief journey. It couldn’t have lasted more than a few minutes. But I can still recollect this overwhelming feeling of ‘being in the moment’; the alertness but also the sense of total awareness of my bodily movements the very moment they occurred. I had to renegotiate space, while I was walking. And this was a mental activity equally intense as the physical effort to avoid all possible obstacles. Now, I had to be much more aware of my own body – how it moved in crowded places, and the bodies of others, both people and objects. I was so focused that everything, the chattering of the adults, the screams of the children, and the music that had faded in the background. It was a moment of enchantment. I could feel my powers of concentration ‘turned up and recharged’ the same way Bennett describes surprising encounters, with humans and non-humans alike, that trigger strong somatic effects.

That day, that very moment, I was actively engaging with other bodies, human and non-human, because of the burn on my arm. In my efforts to protect myself, I was meeting the world in ways I did not expect. There was novelty in these encounters; perhaps a bit of awe as well. Things looked the same, yet they felt different. And, I had to figure out new ways to negotiate my encounters with them. There was also a little uneasiness. My existing routines did not serve me anymore. I had to process all this new information and come up with new ways to do simple things like walk down a narrow crowded path. However, this was not an unpleasant experience. On the contrary, the whole wedding felt like an adventure full of challenges and little feats. For its most part, it felt like a game; me against everything but also in tune with my body.

## \*\*\*

Walking in nature, even in a garden somewhere in the Greek countryside, always leaves space for enchantment; encounters we have not foreseen taking us by surprise. This is not always the case with our everyday walking. By that, I refer to the purposeful action of walking to achieve a goal: catch the tube, go to work, shop. It is a repetitive, almost mechanistic activity that does not require any reflection. It is only a means to an end; something that we probably do on a daily basis without truly noticing. Yet, for three years now, this very activity has provided the setting for a new, almost symbiotic relationship with a small digital device. For, I’m counting all my steps: picking up my coffee is 1000 steps, a trip to the supermarket is 500 steps. If I decide to make a longer walk on my way to the grocery shop to clear my head before dinner, I’ll record 2000 steps. Every move I make produces a number of steps. In reality, it’s not really me counting the steps but an activity tracker. Looking like a slim bracelet fastened on my arm, it captures everything I do: walking, sitting, even sleeping. The tracker is always there, tethered to my body, recording my activity. It also monitors my moments of idleness creating a very accurate account of how my body functions.

I first learned about activity trackers at work. I was in London’s Tech City at the time and I couldn’t fail to notice that everyone had a slim bracelet on their arms. I asked what it was and I was handed a colleague’s spare tracker to try out for a while. My first encounter with the device was unremarkable; I could even call it disenchanted. Monitoring my daily step count had its moments of excitement; mainly when I was reaching my goals. But, for the most part, it was a utilitarian activity, a digital record of my steps. So, when I returned the tracker back to my colleague, I did not buy one of my own. Yet, the idea of tracking bodily activity stayed with me. I started thinking that a more substantial dataset was required to get any meaningful insights on my body. So a few months later, as I started training again, I bought an activity tracker to capture a more accurate view of my daily regime. Once there was a target in sight, recording my movement made more sense. I could keep track of my training and monitor my progress. I was eager to generate data in the hope of locating patterns in my exercise. The tracker carried this great promise of unlocking information on one’s own body. It would be a lie to say that I was not influenced by it. So much so, that I systematically followed the suggested use: wearing it all the time, and monitoring my steps. I really wanted to know more about my body, and through it myself. Recording everything was an essential part of this process of discovery. And this is what I did.

## \*\*\*

The idea of a more intimate relationship with our own bodies underpins the development of wearables. As devices, they are tethered to the human body amplifying its capacities or following its rhythms. An activity tracker, a widely commercialised version of wearables, does not simply count steps according to its manufacturer. It constantly records data about what the body does on a daily basis. The aim is to know more about our own bodies. The data offer insights that, purportedly, cannot be captured without the aid of this small digital device. So, the narrative continues, the activity tracker has the potential to change our daily lives. Data can help us modify our everyday routines and reach new levels of activity. But, the question remains: What exactly one ‘sees as a user in the data? The answer to this is not obvious. The app contains several graphs and visualisation that provide a great level of detail. Yet, the aim is clearly accuracy; and through that, accountability. Movement is quantified and continuously recorded in an app. As a user, you suddenly have your own metrics; an objective, accurate account of your activities. And, as a result, you are in a position to assess your own performance on a daily basis.

In my case, the period of recording frenzy lasted for a couple of months. My activity tracker was an early model that required my input on the activities I had performed, specifically their type and duration. I had to be consistent in using it and diligent in recording my activities. This active involvement in the monitoring process kept me engaged with the device. The constant interaction became a daily routine that made me conscious of my activities. It also cultivated a symbiotic relationship with the device itself. The process of inputting data and checking the app transformed the tracker into a meaningful, almost evocative, object in my daily life. Yet, this was a consequence of a set of repetitive actions triggered by the tracker. The metrics themselves – steps, hours of sleep – provided only rudimentary insights to my body inner workings; despite the eye-catching data visualisations of the app.

As a result, I was unable to connect with data from my own body. Once recorded, it became disembodied; removed from the circumstances that produced it; unable to tell a story about what my body was doing during the day. Alas, monitoring the app, as I was chasing the daily target of 10000 steps, did not contribute to a different understanding of my activities, nor offered any insights into my body. And as the excitement of novelty receded, there were no other somatic effects to sustain the active use of the device.

## \*\*\*

The childhood burn became a little scar, the shape of a half-moon, on my skin. Everybody has scars like these scattered on their bodies; little ornaments of childhood adventures that punctuate our lives with moments that we can come back to and explore. What is unique about them is their materiality. Not just the way they transform our skin but their ability to weave together images, sounds, and even smells. I can still remember, during the first moments after the burn, my mother telling me, in her effort to console me, not to worry because I would get a little half-moon on my arm once the burn healed. I believe that we tend to remember these little mishaps of childhood because they were occasions to be more reflexive of our bodies and the world they inhabit. It is the kind of reflection that might not happen when we are children. It probably only occurs when we are adults trying to decipher our bodies: how changes, even little ones on its surface make us renegotiate, sometimes unwittingly, our relationship with the world and the bodies, human and not, that surround us.

This sense of being ‘shaken by the extraordinary that lives in the ordinary’, as Bennett further describes enchantment, is something that I also expected from the activity tracker. Obviously, I was not anticipating a user experience of equal intensity as when I burned my arm. I did hope, however, the data would augment my perception of my body. I wanted my metrics to trigger a reflexive examination of my activities allowing my body to develop a different, more conscious way to experience my everyday life. This was a promise accompanying the device; its core marketing message. Yet, the act of monitoring regimented the body instead of triggering its affective propulsions. Furthermore, the data failed to tell any interesting stories or identify significant events.

I considered to stop wearing the activity tracker but I decided against it. The act of inputting data had made the device part of my routines. So it stayed on my arm. I kept feeding the tracker with data but without the diligence of the early days. During the first months of use, I had calibrated the tracker. It was quite accurate in recognising my activities. So I didn’t really need to monitor it closely to make sure it captured everything. I checked the app only occasionally during the week. It was more of a habitual activity than an active engagement with the data. The tracker had become an element of my daily attire, recording data – that nobody used – in the background. The plateaus and spikes in the visualisations offered by the app did not affect how I was experiencing my everyday activities as a wearables user.

## \*\*\*

I have found myself in a paradoxical situation where the ‘crossing’ between my body and the tracker constituted an active encounter but lacked any affective intensity that would allow me to imbricate the data into my daily life. That, however, changed when I moved to a new job that required a lot of walking across a university campus. Almost every day, I was walking more. The metrics started looking good. And, I found myself checking the app several times during the day. My new daily routines made quantification more meaningful. I now had a daily regime that made sense to monitor through the activity tracker. It was, however, something different than counting the total amount of steps I was taking every day. The tracker allowed me to understand how I navigated the campus. I now used it primarily to quantify activities that would take place regardless of the device.

The metrics became truly my own. They did not simply capture my bodily activities; they also meant something that made sense only in the context of my life. As a result, nowadays, after almost three years of use, I have a rather accurate sense of how much I need to walk to achieve my daily step target. I could even say that for routine itineraries, I have internalised the step count. Depending on my plans for the day, I can take different routes. I know roughly the step count for both the shortcuts and the longer routes. I can therefore navigate the university campus in a way that can fit my targets for the day. I do not check the app on a daily basis. I don’t have to as the tracker – a new model – now notifies me when I’ve reached my target. But if I walk beyond it, I will definitively check since I know my metrics are going to look good.

It is a makeshift use of the tracker; my personal take on its functions. I pursue my plans for the day and use the app as a record of them. Such use of the device is not about establishing trends or discovering patterns. It is more my way of making sure my day-to-day walking enables me to maintain a fairly good level of activity during the week. It is also my way of augmenting activities, like walking on campus, that are otherwise mundane. I have embodied the step count for each routine itinerary rather than using the tracker to wander in the city just to meet the step target. Still, the device is indispensable as it provides a numerical account of my day. This time, the metrics make more sense and, on occasions, contribute to a sense of achievement at the end of really active days.

## \*\*\*

The activity tracker is now something more than a device on my arm. It is, to a certain extent, a companion in my everyday life. If I get out of the house without wearing the tracker, I have a strong sense of uneasiness, perhaps even anxiety, because I am not recording my steps. It feels that my whole effort that day is wasted since there is no tracker to capture it. Such level of entanglement has not been a straightforward process. On the contrary, it required significant effort from me, as a user, to connect with the data and meaningfully incorporate it in my daily activities. The tracker is still relentless in recording my activities; as it was originally designed to do. Yet, my use of the device endows my everyday routines with a sense of stability; and quite often achievement. So, when my latest tracker started failing – not connecting to my smartphone or its buttons getting stuck – I felt an important disruption in my daily routines; perhaps even a little more than that. I was really saddened that the tracker was about to break down. Replacing it felt almost like a betrayal – a surprising feeling for a simple device conveying rather basic information on daily activities.

This was, once more, enchantment; although not experienced in the same way, or with the same intensity as in my childhood burn. It was enchantment developed through repetition and persistence, especially when my initial interest dwindled. For, it was constant use that entangled the tracker into my everyday practices. It was also repetition that infused meaning in the direct, physical relationship with the tracker and, in turn, gave birth to enjoyment in using the device which is why I feel attached to it now. It is finally experimentation with physical spaces that allowed me to augment the way I encounter the world and, at the same time, embody the tracker data. It is a virtuous cycle that has kept me using the activity tracker for three years now. It is also something more: the possibility of exploring new, alternative uses; the playfulness of the process and eventually its assistance in monitoring my body on my own terms.

## Studying computing in everyday life: enchanted materialism and alternative writing genres

Experiential computing is premised on the idea that the sensorisation of everyday objects leads humans to directly encounter digital technologies in (almost) all their daily activities (Yoo, 2010). To this end, studying ‘computing in everyday life’, as Yoo (2010: 217) frames this emerging phenomenon, channels research to the lived experience; the way people access the world in ‘sentient’ environments. As I argued throughout this article, this research endeavour demands a theoretical perspective accommodating the materialist underpinnings of experiential computing as well as a writing genre that can convey the direct experience of living in cyber-physical spaces. These issues are addressed in detail in the following sections where I discuss: (1) the contribution of enchanted materialism to the study of the human body in experiential computing; and, (2) the use of memoir as a writing device that cultivates the required sensibility for IS research to capture the affective propulsions of the human body.

## Enchantment as a lens for studying the human body with wearables

In this article, I argue that our new, intimate relationship with digital technologies shifts scholarly attention to the human body – its movements in space, its gestures and other affective propulsions – as the locus of new functional and aesthetic meanings (Dexter et al, 2011), in experiential computing. The proposed approach is in line with the existential participation of humans in emerging cyber-physical spaces, suggested by Yoo (2010). It also progresses an often neglected aspect of life in the Internet of Things: the visceral elements of our encounters, as humans, with digital technologies. To capture these moments when the human body reveals its capacities in conjunction with new technologies, there is need for a materialism that accounts for affect; the force of these encounters (Seigworth & Gregg, 2010).

For this reason, I turn to Bennett’s (2001) enchanted materialism in order to capture ‘the desire for mobility, for the space to become otherwise, to exercise (our) faculties, play around […] look forward to something (Bennett, 2001: 28) that underpins the lived experience. As a theoretical lens, it allows research to draw attention to the affective elements of experiential computing in order to explain how humans interact with wearables as things in everyday life. It also allows a critical exploration, both as users and as researchers, of the organising visions accompanying such devices. Once we focus on these crossings, as Bennett (2001) calls human–nonhuman encounters, we can start tracing how life in the Internet of Things has the potential to reconfigure everyday practices in surprising and productive ways. We can also see how humans actively engage with new technologies, even ubiquitous ones, to accommodate them in their lives. This suggests a plethora of processes and capacities, emerging contingently once human and nonhuman bodies come into contact (Coole, 2007). To account for these processes, Bennett’s materialism views the human body as an active agent that experiences itself (Coole, 2007) in everyday practices. This allows research to view humans in direct involvement with the world they inhabit through their bodies. It is, however, enchantment, that makes Bennett’s work (2001, 2010)

fitting for the study of experiential computing, as it draws attention to the uncanny, unprocessed moments of human encounters with non-humans. It is such moments that, once captured and analysed, reveal the human body’s affectual doings and the constitution of new capacities with experiential computing.

In the article, I trace these moments in stories that I pick up from the everyday. The memoir narrates my use of an activity tracker; the initial sense of banality in monitoring my body on a daily basis and the subsequent enjoyment of exploring new spaces with the device. In writing my body as flesh and digital technology, I can reflect on the constitution of its capacities. As I showed in the memoir, wearables introduce new metrics (i.e. steps, hours of sleep) for interpreting our everyday lives. Anchoring the story on my body allowed me to move beyond data-driven accuracy, as the main organising vision for wearables, and critically reflect on my own experience as a user encountering the world with a device tethered to my body. The childhood burn, the story I begin the memoir with, helped me in this respect as it vividly conveyed how we experience the world through our bodies. The incident allowed me to develop a narrative for my body as it accessed different spaces with a burn on its surface, an addition to its usual condition. The story showcased the intensity of the experience; the affective aspects of how humans encounter other bodies. It also offered, by drawing parallels with wearables, an empirical angle on how to access the experience of using an activity tracker, which also represented a new addition to my body.

In the memoir, using wearables – once seen through the lens of enchantment – is revealed as an occasion to encounter the world in ways enriched by the tracker’s data. Despite growing fears (e.g. Institute for the Future, 2013), recording bodily movements does not necessarily regiment the human body. In contrast, as I showed in the memoir, the tracker enabled me to tinker with my walking routines, adding a new layer of experience in my everyday life. This shows that life with an activity tracker is not reduced to a daily chase of an arbitrary number of steps. It can become a playful way of being that allows users to navigate routine itineraries in different ways. The memoir traces this gradual entanglement of the activity tracker into my everyday practices starting from the initial feelings of resistance to the daily improvisation (a form of enchantment itself) after three years of use. At a more generic level, the memoir shows the articulatory processes through which humans qua users develop their own meanings for the data captured by the device. Existing research attributes such idiosyncratic use of wearables to the absence of socially meaningful metrics (Nafus, 2014). Yet, wearables are a global phenomenon offering an individual experience. There cannot be a generic, fit-for-all use despite following the same instructions. The memoir, through the lens of enchanted materialism, reveals this intimate nature of wearables as digital devices. It presents in detail how tethering a device to the human body unlocks affective propulsions that can transform a mass-produced tracker into an occasion to renegotiate, as humans, our relationship with our bodies and the space we inhabit (Certeau, 1984).

This take on experiential computing furthers Yoo’s call to study how ‘the contour and possibilities of our everyday experience [in digitally mediated environments] is constantly shaped and reshaped by sociomateriality’ (Yoo, 2010: 222). The article adopts a materialist lens while introducing a perspective so far neglected in IS research; the possibility of entering into productive encounters with digital technologies. Thus far, materialist accounts have primarily showed the importance of matter in social practices (e.g. Scott & Orlikowski, 2012, 2014). Yet, the organisational focus of these research projects does not allow for an in-depth examination of how users, as individuals, negotiate the entanglement of digital technologies with their everyday routines. As a result, existing research paints a rather pessimistic image of human–non-human encounters where people are subsumed to algorithms and other digital artefacts (e.g. Orlikowski & Scott, 2014, 2015). Bennett’s enchanted materialism, as a theoretical lens, introduces to IS the possibility for humans to encounter digital technologies in ways that augment the lived experience while constituting ‘tentative explorations of existing regimes of subjectivity’ (Bennett, 2001: 146). It further reveals the interplay between objective designs and subjective uses of wearables (and the active effort from users this entails) paving the way for a more nuanced description of life with experiential computing.

This approach further problematises conceptualisations of the digital as something immaterial. As shown in the memoir, wearables are constitutive of the human body in all but immaterial ways. Scott and Orlikowsk (2014) have already brought to the fore this assumption that underlies IS research on digital phenomena. In experiential computing, the need to move beyond the ‘immateriality trope’ (Blanchette, 2011) is even more pertinent as failure to do so might close down critical explorations on the encounters between humans and digital technologies. The memoir, as writing genre, is key to incorporating the human body in the study of experiential computing. It is for this reason that I discuss, in the following section, how memoirs allow researchers to compose a cogent academic argument that links the personal with the social (Anderson, 2006).

## Writing research as memoir

Memoirs, and other first-person accounts (e.g. Schultze, 2000), are seldom used in Information Systems. This should be attributed, to a great extent, to the focus of IS research on organisational settings. Yet, in the context of experiential computing, where users are defined, as ordinary individuals performing a variety of everyday tasks (Yoo, 2010), autobiographical accounts, like memoirs, can serve as both a writing device and a research method for reflecting on the digitally mediated experiences of everyday life. This is because memoirs allow us to convey the ‘reflection from the midst’ (Merleau-Ponty, 1945) that constitutes the basis of a materialist reading of human– non-human encounters. At the core of such reading is a critical reflection of ‘our belongingness with the world’ (Kozel, 2007: 17). The aim is to cultivate ‘tactics of the self (Bennett, 2001: 155) and be more responsive to other bodies, human and non-human.

This approach is in line with Yoo’s (2010) description of experiential computing as an assemblage of people and artefacts, both digital and non-digital. It also follows Yoo’s (2010) suggestions to explore how digital and physical materialities interact in the context of experiential computing. It goes, however, beyond current research on the topic as it uses a personal account to direct materialist readings of IS phenomena to the human body and its interactions with digital technologies. The memoir, therefore, serves a dual purpose in this article: (1) it develops further the individual dimension of experiential computing by showing the importance of studying the human body and the way it weaves new meanings in its interactions with digital technologies; and (2) it shows how to convey the lived experience in a way that highlights the human body’s simultaneous passivity, which allows its monitoring; and, agentic sentience (Coole, 2007) which enables it to engage with devices tethered to it. This article thus introduces to IS a writing genre that can capture the dual nature of the human body. This is particularly salient for research on novel technological systems since it needs to capture both the intellectual labour and affective attachments required for a meaningful use.

It should be stated, however, that memoirs approach the world in a different way than academic research. The aim of description is not to ‘show’ but rather to offer the author ‘a place to be in relation with the reality of the world’ (Hampl, 2007: 80). Memoirs offer primarily the possibility for reflecting on past experiences, tracing old senses and interpreting them. In this respect, the author is able to simultaneously show and tell (Hampl, 2007). The aim is to ‘write a story and write an essay – all in the same tale, braided and twined together’ (Hampl, 2007: 77). This is achieved through attention to detail, to fragments that serve as triggers for reflection in order to make sense of the world. The aim, therefore, is not to construct a factual account of specific events in the past but to reflect on fragments of memory so that they can reveal their meaning (Hampl, 1999).

The memoir also shows how the personal is gradually linked to broader societal themes (Gannon, 2006). As this article shows, starting from intimate personal stories one can critically approach emerging technological systems by piercing through the organising visions that construct them. The outcome can be more surprising and informative than traditional research accounts. This is evident in the memoir presented in this article. A minor childhood injury triggers the exploration of how humans experience the spaces they inhabit through their bodies. Memoir, as a writing genre, facilitates access to what Kozel (2007: 19)

describes as the loosening of ‘rationalist structures of meaning […] to permit qualities that are associated with the pre-rational, such as ambiguity of meaning, fluidity of existential and conceptual structures, scope for entirely new thought’. It is through this process of delving deep into their everyday experiences in cyber-physical environments that humans qua users can reflect on their lives.

As such, memoirs do not just serve as an alternative genre for writing research. They are also a form of inquiry themselves in that they offer a way of articulating the lived experience. This is crucial in the study of experiential computing where technology is integrated into everyday things in imperceptible ways. Writing a memoir, in this context, allows humans to reflect on their role as users; realise the effort it takes to integrate technology in their daily lives. It further enables people to find the appropriate way to encounter devices like wearables that are tethered to their bodies. This way, the themes emerging from the memoir go beyond utopian enthusiasm (e.g. Rose, 2014) or dystopian rejection (e.g. Institute for the Future, 2013) of experiential computing by showing alternative ways of being in data-driven environments.

Equally important to such a reflexive mode of research is the transparency of memoirs as a writing genre. In memoirs, the choices made, the alternative paths explored, but not chosen as well as the way new understandings enrich older beliefs that then lead to productive encounters with new technologies, are not silenced as in more formal academic accounts. Memoirs also make explicit the role of audiences. In this case, however, audiences, especially non-experts ones, are not passive recipients of objective truths (Gannon, 2006). They are active members of worlds with shared cultural, aesthetic, and ethical foundations that allow them to be critical of the narrative offered to them. As Castoriadis (1987: 1) eloquently states in the preface of the Imaginary Institution of Society, ‘what is important is the work of reflection and it is perhaps mostly this that an author can make us see. […] Thinking is not building cathedrals or composing symphonies. If the symphony exists, it is the reader who must create it in his own ears’. Elpida Prasopoulou is Research Development Executive for the Centre for Business in Society (CBiS), Coventry University (UK). Her research examines digital innovation focusing on three interrelated aspects of the phenomenon: user experiences in cyber-physical spaces; inclusive innovation with the engagement of users qua emotional level and form community (Richardson, 1997). This way, memoirs both convey research in a systematic way while reaching out to audiences that do not usually engage with academic work.

A final, yet very important, issue in the use of memoirs for academic research concerns the nature of the truth claims made by more personal research accounts. In the case of memoirs, what is important, according to Hampl (1999), is not the factual reconstruction of the memory but the ability to transform experience into meaning. As we explore our past, we stop at images of value, stored in our memory and ponder on their meaning. This does not mean that what is written is not true. It is an occasion for further reflection but also for retracing the feelings accompanying the memory. This is quite important in the way I use memoirs in this article, namely as devices for promoting an embodied understanding of living in the world. It should be noted, however, that autobiographical research accounts are successful as long as they manage to address broader theoretical issues (Anderson, 2006). It is for this reason that my memoir includes also theoretical reflections, which set it apart from more radical views where abstraction is resisted (e.g. Ellis & Bochner, 2000).

There are, therefore, significant trade-offs to be considered when embarking on the writing of research in an alternative genre. The challenge with such writing genres is, as Lincoln (1997: 42) observes, ‘to find ourselves and our voices, since breaking out of our scholarly ‘‘native languages’’ […] will not be easy’. Despite the challenges, however, the memoir can make a significant contribution to Information Systems by channelling research to the study of everyday encounters with digital technologies while allowing research to explore how life is transformed with experiential computing.
