# Everywhere and nowhere: nearshore software development in the context of globalisation<sub>w</sub>

Pamela Y. Abbott<sup>1</sup> and Matthew R. Jones<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Offshore software development has been identified as one of the most striking manifestations of contemporary globalisation and as evidence of placelessness, the idea that information and communication technologies have rendered location irrelevant. Research in the International Business and Information Systems fields, in contrast, has suggested that all locations are not equal and has identified a number of characteristics that may influence the attractiveness of a location for multinational investment and offshoring, respectively. These literatures, however, focus almost exclusively on quantitative, economic characteristics that are seen as fixed and applying uniformly throughout a whole country. They therefore offer little guidance on the suitability of particular locations as offshoring destinations, especially in countries without a track record in offshore software development. Drawing on two cases of nearshore software development centres set up by offshore service providers in the Caribbean, this paper illustrates that, while the initial decision to establish the ventures reflected a logic of placelessness, characteristics of these particular locations affected their subsequent success. Through the findings, we therefore develop a typology of espoused, unanticipated and remediable locational characteristics, which illustrates that locational attractiveness may vary significantly within countries and that offshore service providers and government agencies can modify locational characteristics to their advantage. ## Introduction

Offshore outsourcing of information services is a relatively recent business phenomenon (Willcocks & Lacity, 2006), whereby IT services production shifts to lower-wage, remote locations to supply markets in the industrialised economies. This is an industry that has grown significantly over the last 20 years with a global market size estimated at U.S.\$89–93 billion in 2008 (NASSCOM, 2009). The decision to choose an offshore services provider can be a complex and error-prone exercise (Khan et al, 2011) and, although not the only consideration in this decision, the location of the offshore service provider can prove critical to the sustainability of an outsourcing arrangement.

The selection of suitable locations for offshore production of information services has been of relevance to both academic (Oshri et al, 2009) and practitioner communities (Peterson et al, 2011) alike. With more than 120 countries offering offshored services (Oshri, 2011), some of which have no history of experience in that area (coupled with the effects of the global economic crisis, and increasing costs in traditional offshore locations), there is a need for clarity on which locations offer suitable conditions for providing IT services. Country attractiveness indices and reports (e.g. Carmel, 2003; Heeks & Nicholson, 2004; Minevich & Richter, 2005; Peterson et al, 2011) offer broad categorisations of location suitability, but classify them at a high level of granularity, often ignoring regional differentiation. Beyond the traditional and well-researched outsourcing destinations like India and China, little is known about the relative benefits/ drawbacks of specific regional hubs within outsourcing countries or how to evaluate the combination of synergistic criteria that may prove to be specifically advantageous in newly emerging outsourcing destinations. In addition, service providers use location as a cost differentiator by providing services from a mix of different destinations, such as Infosys’ ‘Global Delivery Model’, or Cap Gemini’s ‘RightShore’ model, but the strategies used by these providers in selecting their offshore sites have been under-researched in the literature.

In this paper we draw on case studies of two nearshore software outsourcing ventures to explore the significance of location from the service provider’s perspective. With ‘nearshore’ or ‘proximity’ software development, the software development facility is located geographically or temporally closer to client sites than traditional offshore arrangements (Abbott & Jones, 2002; Carmel & Abbott, 2007). Nearshoring is seen as one of a number of alternatives to established offshore outsourcing approaches (Outsourcing Institute, 2004) that is growing in importance (Oshri et al, 2009). Specifically, we look at why these ventures were established in the particular locations that they were and how their subsequent development was influenced by characteristics of these locations. The findings show that location choice reflected both a discourse of globalised placelessness and a ‘space-based logic of locational differentiation. A number of characteristics not discussed in prior location literatures were also identified. Furthermore, the findings suggested that, rather than simply treating locations as having fixed characteristics, the service providers establishing nearshore software outsourcing sites, sometimes in association with local government agencies, actively sought to alter location characteristics to improve the viability and sustainability of their particular venture.

This paper therefore makes six types of contribution to the literature: first, it adds to the limited literature on outsourcing from the service provider’s perspective; second, it provides empirical evidence of the continuing significance of location in offshore software development; third, it explores the interplay of logics of place and space in such location decisions; fourth, it identifies aspects of location characteristics of nearshore settings that are not generally considered in the literature; fifth, it illustrates that locational characteristics, that are often treated as static and immutable for a given country, may vary within a country and be amenable to change; and sixth it suggests that the attractiveness of a location may reflect not just the characteristics initially espoused by a company establishing an offshore venture, but may also include characteristics that were unanticipated at the time of the original decision or that the company has actively worked to modify (remediable characteristics) in their favour.

The structure of the paper is therefore as follows. The following section sets the context for the research, introducing the arguments upon which the work is based. Next the literatures on both the placelessness of global software production and offshore location decisions from International Business (IB) and information system (IS) perspectives, which constitute the theoretical foundations of the paper, are presented. Then, following an account of the research methodology employed, the two case studies are presented and discussed in terms of the evidence they offer, for theory and practice, on the strategies employed to select and utilise the chosen locations for software development.

## Background and motivation

Offshore software development has been identified as one of the most striking manifestations of contemporary globalisation processes that are seen as levelling local economic, cultural and political differences to create a ‘flat’ world (Friedman, 2005). An underlying assumption in both the offshoring and globally distributed work literature is that information and communication technologies (ICTs) play an important role in rendering location irrelevant. Heeks (1999 p. 15), for example, speaks of Computer Science graduates in developing countries becoming ‘global info-preneurs’, able to connect themselves seamlessly to the global information economy with minimal investment in ICTs. O’Hara-Devereaux & Johansen (1994, pp. 386–387) also allude to the ‘perception’-shifting experience of ‘cyberspace’ creating an ‘anytime, anyplace global workspace’. These views may be seen to resonate with popular discourses on globalisation emphasising the ‘death of distance’ (Cairncross, 1997), and the increasingly ‘borderless’ (Ohmae, 1990) or ‘weightless’ (Quah, 1998) character of the world economy as it becomes more knowledge-based (Drucker, 2003).

These ideas are reflected in the growing body of IS literature concerned with the management of globally distributed software development teams (Carmel & Agarwal, 2001; King & Frost, 2002; Sosa et al, 2002; Carmel, 2006), which has tended to focus on organisational and technological interventions that can help to overcome the friction of distance and has largely ignored the significance of the geographical location of offshore partners. With a few exceptions (Levina & Ross, 2003; Mao et al, 2008; Palvia et al, 2010; Oshri, 2011), this literature has also predominantly analysed offshoring from the client perspective and has paid less attention to issues facing offshore software service providers, whether independent third parties or ‘captive’ centres owned by an outsourcing company.

Notwithstanding this pervasive globalisation discourse, there is still a significant body of research that emphasises the importance of location when setting up an offshore venture. The IB literature, in particular, identifies a range of characteristics that need to be considered by multinational enterprises (MNEs) when locating facilities in another country (Davidson, 1980; Dunning, 1980, 1988, 1998). Some of the literature in offshoring and globally distributed work also supports this view: for example, in identifying specific locational characteristics that may account for the success of countries, such as India, in the sector (Krishna et al, 2000; Zatolyuk & Allgood, 2004); classifying locations in terms of their suitability for offshore software production (Carmel, 2003; Heeks & Nicholson, 2004; Rao, 2004); or emphasising the importance of proximity to successful coordination and communication in global software teams (Kiesler & Cummings, 2002; Barkhi et al, 2006).

The coexistence of these two contrasting perspectives on the significance of the location of offshore work may be seen to reflect long-standing debates on the tensions between universal, abstract undifferentiated ‘space’ and local, personal, heterogeneous ‘place’ (Tuan, 1977). Following Sahay et al (2003), however, these may be viewed, not as mutually exclusive perspectives, but as dialectically related, where the one requires the other. Barkhi et al’s (2006) assertions about technologically mediated communication in global software development, for example, indicate that place-like substitutes such as shared online workspaces and real-time chat facilities are needed to ensure success of the more dislocated forms of communication common in virtual teams. Thus, rather than seeking to understand offshoring as a manifestation of forces operating in a single direction, either eliminating or strengthening the importance of location, it may be more helpful to see it as subject to forces in both directions.

The tensions inherent in service-provider location decisions may be seen to reflect the interplay of logics of space and place (Sahay et al, 2003). Placelessness provides a rationale for offshoring, but the concept of nearshoring suggests that there may be advantages from being located geographically close to clients. The following sections therefore introduce theoretical perspectives on place and placelessness from a global software production perspective.

## The ‘placelessness’ of global software work

The view that modern information technologies have enabled the ‘disembedding’ (Giddens, 1990) of social (or work) practices from particular geographical locations is not restricted to the offshoring literature, but has been widely discussed in geography, sociology and management. Compared to traditional societies in which work took place in a specific, socially meaningful geographical locale (Giddens, 1990) such as a factory or office, modern communication technologies allow the two to become increasingly separated, as meaningful interactions occur with others outside the immediate locale. At the same time, meaningful aspects of a location may be reproduced in remote physical settings, such as the familiar milieu of the international hotel anywhere in the world. Either way, physical space becomes emptied of its significance, giving rise to the ‘placeless logic’ driving global software work (Sahay et al, 2003, p. 35) and to deterritorialisation, or location substitutability, making local context irrelevant in the choice of work sites (Cox, 1997; Storper, 1997).

A related theme emerges from the work of Castells (1996) who emphasises that ICT-supported placeless connections (the ‘space of flows’), for example between financial centres, increasingly dominate the physical ‘space of places’. Thus, power and wealth come not from location, but through connection to information networks. This may be significant in terms of the relationship between offshoring and economic development, since offshore technology parks or ‘export enclaves’ as Heeks (1999) calls them, where many offshore development centres are located, may operate relatively independently of their physical location.

Such views are contested by other authors, however, such as Goddard & Richardson (1996) who argue that locations continue to be differentiated in terms of their resources. Others argue that the more ICTs break down the physical barriers of space, the more particularities of ‘place’ become important (Cox, 1997). This paper seeks to use these debates related to space- and place-based logic as a lens from which to view location-based decisions in nearshore outsourcing.

## Does location matter?

In contrast to literature announcing the irrelevance of location in the contemporary global economy, other sources suggest that location still matters. This has been a consistent theme in the IB literature, where the debate has centred on the influences on MNEs’ Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) (McCann & Mudambi, 2004), including acquisitions of offshore software development centres (Graf & Mudambi, 2005).

The work of Dunning (1977, 1980, 1988, 1993, 1995, 1998) has been influential in this IB location literature. His Ownership-Location-Internalisation (OLI) model treats location as part of a triad of factors influencing FDI decisions (the others being firm (O)wnership and (I)nternalisation of transaction costs). Dunning’s specifically locational characteristics include ‘not only tangible assets such as natural resources, manpower and capital, but intangible assets such as knowledge, organisational and entrepreneurial skills and access to y markets y and the social, legal and commercial environment in which [resource] endowments are used, and government legislation and policies’ (Dunning, 1993, p. 183). These are later usefully categorised by Mudambi (1995) as infrastructural variables, location-specific risk factors and government policy variables. Subsequent IB authors writing about the FDI location decision, some of which explicitly address offshoring either to third-party vendors or within the firm hierarchy, have augmented these locational characteristics to include: culture (Davidson, 1980; McCann & Mudambi, 2004; Ha¨to¨nen 2009); human capital factors specifically thought to be relevant to services sourcing (Graf & Mudambi, 2005; Kedia & Mukherjee, 2009); and education and other institutional factors (Bunyaratavej et al, 2007; Kshetri, 2007).

The IB literature focusing on the offshoring location decision tends to critique the OLI framework in terms of the O and I aspects of the triad, but augments the L aspect rather than refuting it. For example, Kedia & Mukherjee (2009) have recently challenged the OLI framework in order to reorient the FDI argument towards the reasons why companies make decisions to locate offshore work. In so doing, they have developed a similar triad of influences emphasising (D)isintegration, instead of (O)wnership, (E)xternalisation instead of (I)nternalisation, and have sought to extend the L(ocation) aspect through introducing human capital issues. The IB literature, moreover, focuses on client choices in offshoring to third-party vendors and on MNEs creating captive centres in foreign locations, rather than on service providers entering into offshoring arrangements in foreign locations. In fact, Graf & Mudambi (2005) argue that this literature effectively neglects the vendor perspective.

Notwithstanding this strong tradition of the established IB location literature, it has been criticised for its treatment of countries as homogenous, neglecting regional variations (McCann & Mudambi, 2004; Nair & Prasad, 2004) and as having fixed characteristics that neither change over time, nor are capable of being influenced by local actors (Richardson & Marshall, 1999; Niederman, 2005). More generally, their approach has been seen as focusing exclusively on cost-based analyses to the neglect of less tangible and qualitative features of a location (Schmenner, 1979).

Although some IB studies explore location in relation to information services (Graf & Mudambi, 2005; Ha¨to¨nen, 2009; Kedia & Mukherjee, 2009), the same topic has been addressed independently in the IS literature, with minimal co-citation between the two fields. Most of these IS studies focus on established offshoring centres such as India (Balasubramanyam & Balasubramanyam, 1997; Joshi & Mudigonda, 2008) and derive their location characteristics from empirical work (e.g. Nair & Prasad, 2004; Zatolyuk & Allgood, 2004) or literature reviews (e.g. Joshi & Mudigonda, 2008; Khan et al, 2011) rather than theoretical perspectives. Where theory is used it tends to converge around Porter’s (1990) notions of comparative advantage (e.g. Carmel, 2003; Heeks & Nicholson, 2004) and other strategic management considerations (e.g. Nair & Prasad, 2004).

As with the IB literature, however, the predominant focus in IS studies is on client choices of offshoring location, rather than the service provider’s choice of suitable development sites, and on national level characteristics, that is, locational characteristics that apply to a country as a whole (Zatolyuk & Allgood, 2004; Minevich & Richter, 2005). Some studies attempt to recognise regional variation in these characteristics but do so at a high level of analysis such as, for example, between continents (Khan et al, 2011) rather than between different areas within a country. Thus, IS studies are arguably open to the same criticisms, of focusing on fixed, national-level, economic characteristics, as their IB counterparts. These alleged deficiencies may be particularly significant with respect to nearshore software development, where service-provider sites tend to be located in regions not previously known for expertise in information services outsourcing and that would fail to register as potential sites in conventional analyses. Examining nearshore location decisions may therefore offer insights on whether the characteristics identified in the IB and IS literatures apply in these contexts and whether the IS location literature is in need of similar extension, to address issues such as sub-national variation, variability of characteristics over time and variability resulting from interventions by local actors and qualitative influences, as argued by critics of the IB literature (Richardson & Marshall, 1999; McCann & Mudambi, 2004; Nair & Prasad, 2004; Niederman, 2005).

As Table 1 shows, therefore, a wide range of characteristics are seen as influencing the location decision across the two literatures, with much agreement between them. In both literatures, however, the characteristics are considered to apply to a whole country, and as being static and immutable. Given that they also largely adopt the perspective of MNEs or outsourcing clients and, in the IS context, are based predominantly on established offshore locations, their relevance to vendor companies seeking to establish a nearshore operation would seem open to question. The context of nearshore software development is particularly relevant since it represents a pertinent example of the tensions of place, placelessness and the relevance of location. Geographically or temporally closer development sites occur, for example, from the Caribbean and Central America to U.S. clients, while Central and Eastern Europe are considered nearshore for Western European countries (Carmel & Abbott, 2007). In both cases, these locations are relatively nearer, physically and in terms of time zones, to their clients than, say, India or China. Nearshoring is particularly significant in debates about the importance of location in software production, since, by definition, it implies that where software production takes place does matter else there would be no need to differentiate nearshoring from offshoring in general.

Given the complexities of this context, our research sought to address two interrelated questions. (1) How do nearshore service providers select locations for software development given their particular reliance on proximity as a strategy for delivery? (2) How important are these characteristics for the sustainability of their ventures? In this paper, therefore, we present case studies of two nearshore software development ventures in the Caribbean that may be seen as illustrating different approaches to the task of vendors locating software development centres in new offshoring sites. Both faced challenges in terms of the ‘given’ characteristics of the locations and both attempted, in different ways, to circumvent these challenges with varying success. The nature and consequences of their attempted strategies will be explored. While the two cases cannot provide definitive insight on offshoring location decisions in general, they significantly add to the sparse literature on nearshore development and service-provider perspectives by providing indepth evidence on specific ventures and by connecting this with wider debates on the spatial organisation of information services work.

## Methodology

At the time of this study (early 2000s), the incidence of nearshore outsourcing was very limited, with only a few locations having any extended experience of such work (Hopkins, 1999; King, 1999; Abbott & Jones, 2002). Since then, despite growing popular interest in nearshoring (Economist, 2005; In Tech, 2007), the topic has received little attention in the academic literature (Carmel & Abbott, 2007; Javalgi et al, 2009; Von Stetten et al, 2010). An exploratory approach, employing broadly interpretive methods, was therefore considered appropriate to gain insight into this emerging phenomenon (Walsham, 1995).

## Research context

The Caribbean islands are one of the main nearshore sites for U.S. companies as they enjoy similar time zones to the U.S. East Coast throughout the year and are accessible through short-haul flights from the U.S. South and East coasts. The main islands associated with nearshoring are Jamaica, Barbados, the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico (Bibby, 1996; Zagada, 2008).

The outsourcing of back-end tasks such as data entry to the Caribbean area dates back to the 1970s. American Airlines was one of the first major corporations to pursue this approach, setting up Caribbean Data Services in Barbados in 1983 (Bibby, 1996; Freeman, 2000; Metters & Verma, 2008). The range of offshore work subsequently diversified. primarily into call and contact centres. but also some software development (Bishop, 2002; Zagada, 2005, 2008).

At the time of the study, there were about five companies offering offshore software development services in the Caribbean. Two of these ventures were selected for the study due to the similarities of their offshoring philosophy: both were at the forefront of the development of the concept of nearshore software development and among the first to explore rationales for choosing a location for nearshore development. The first, identified as ZCO in this study, was established in Barbados in 1994 and was hailed by the popular press as a pioneer of the nearshoring model (Hopkins, 1998, 1999; King 1999), while the second (NCO in this paper) was set up a year later in Jamaica, and sought actively to learn from the ZCO experience. The cases were therefore both illustrative (Thomas, 2011) of the phenomenon of nearshoring at its inception, but although similar in their conceptualisation of ‘nearshoring’, their actual strategies in establishing a nearshore presence were somewhat different as were their experiences with the model.

## Research methods

A combination of methods was used to collect primary data for the case studies. These included observations from visits to both sites, reading and coding of published materials and semi-structured interviews with employees. In addition, one of the authors was a former employee of one of these firms that facilitated access and meant that they had detailed knowledge of the company and its operations. Interviews were conducted face-to-face, where possible, or by telephone when interviewees were no longer working at the research site. The interviews typically lasted 45 min to an hour. In four instances where direct or telephone contact with remote participants was not possible, further data were gathered through semi-structured email interviews. In such cases, the questions were emailed to the participants, who emailed their responses back, which were further clarified by more email communication. Our focus in this study was on investigating the rationale for the nearshoring decision, that is, why was this considered a viable alternative to offshoring and what was involved in making and realising the location decision? Thus, the interview protocol (attached in the Appendix) focused on questions concerning; (1) the history and background of the enterprise and its founders; (2) the motivation for nearshoring; (3) the development, and sustaining features, of the nearshore enterprise; (4) how the interviewees interpreted and practiced the nearshore concept and; (5) the progress of the nearshore model over time. Table 2 outlines the case study data sources used.

## Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed and analysed using NUD\*ISTs to extract relevant themes. Given the exploratory nature of the study in a domain with little prior research, an ethnographically informed inductive approach to data analysis, as is widely used in interpretive information systems research (Orlikowski, 2002; Levina & Ross, 2003; Doolin, 2004; Levina & Vaast, 2006; Trauth et al, 2009), was adopted. By deriving characteristics from the data in this way, rather than seeking to confirm the influence of characteristics identified in the IB and IS literatures, the analysis sought to allow the research to remain open to the possibility that nearshore software development might be subject to different locational influences than those found in the IB and IS literatures.

Following Orlikowski (2002) the transcripts were iteratively read and re-read to identify categories from the data. The locational characteristics identified in this way were then organised into recurring themes that appeared to provide stable and common categories (axial codes) linking together several associated characteristics. The guiding orientation for the analysis was the exploration of reasons, expectations and experiences associated with the choice of the location and the linkages to the outcome of the implementation of the characteristic. The collection and analysis of data in this way also helped to build a narrative of events occurring in the process of establishing the nearshore presence. As the approach is mainly data-driven, the following sections rely heavily on detailed exposition of the cases, their analysis and the consideration of the implications of the findings for existing literature on location choice.

## Study limitations

In case 1, access to the research site and participants was limited due to the fact that the company was undergoing major restructuring and legal action at the time of the study. The description below was therefore based on publicly available reports, company documentation, press reports, interviews with those participants who were former employees and thus not subject to gagging orders and the experience of one of the authors while working at ZCO. In case 2, there was unrestricted access to all levels of the company’s organisational structure. The findings related to the two cases cannot be generalised to all instances of location selection based on a nearshoring strategy, but can give insight into specific aspects of a location that may be influential in situations where location characteristics may vary significantly within a country, especially in regions that lack a well established tradition of software development. It should also be noted that the findings are necessarily restricted to two early-mover cases and it is therefore unclear whether location may be expected to have the same influence on later entrants in an established nearshore setting.

## Case descriptions

## Nearshore outsourcing in Barbados

ZCO (Barbados) Ltd. (ZCOB), a software development centre, was a subsidiary of ZCO Group Inc., a U.S.-based information technology solutions integrator supplying IT services to U.S. clients mainly from offshore resources in India. The unwillingness of ZCO’s clients to travel to India (clients are claimed to have cited ‘cows in the hotel lobby’ and ‘vaccination shots’ as deterrents), and restrictions on the movement of labour to the U.S.A. (through visa quotas, etc.) encouraged the company to investigate bringing the outsourcing operation closer to the clients. The idea was to try to provide a place that had software talent like India, but that was not in India and which would be convenient for customers to travel to and to do business. In 1994 the CEO of ZCO, identified Barbados as a potential site for such an outsourcing venture. Contemporary reports described the ZCOB concept:

[ZCO’s CEO] needed brainpower like India’s, but he needed it to be in a better place y since the country he needed didn’t exist, what he would do is invent it. He would invent a place programmers would want to come to, a place they’d want to stay. Build it, he thought, and the links would be forged. (Hopkins, 1998)

Among the factors influencing the decision to locate in Barbados were a liberal government policy on work permits, a favourable wage structure, a low tax rate, a modern telecommunications infrastructure, an Englishspeaking population and convenient access to the U.S.A. ZCOB’s incorporation as an International Business Company (IBC) also made it eligible for certain government incentives such as exemption from import duties and repatriation of revenues earned. ZCOB was expected to provide the impetus needed to jumpstart the Barbadian software industry by helping to provide new infrastructure, jobs for ancillary services and encouragement for other IT investors (Hopkins, 1998; King, 1999).

The attractiveness of Barbados as a tourist destination also played a role in creating a ‘programmer’s paradise’ as highlighted in press reports:

The tennis part, of course, is abetted by the paradise part – an aspect of ZCO’s invented location that attracts employees, customers, and investors alike. It is y a tropical island. When you’re not working, you can play tennis all winter if you want. (Hopkins, 1998)

ZCO’s targeted customers were Fortune 1000 companies in the financial services, banking and insurance sectors, a number of whom supported the project with contributions, such as advance payment for future work, donated design help and construction management expertise in line with their particular requirements, to the value of \$12 million (Hopkins, 1998). ZCO’s relationship with Barbados, therefore, was seen to be a self-sustaining, reciprocally convenient and mutually beneficial exchange of a cost-effective, logistically convenient site for software production on the one hand, for economic stimulation and social benefits on the other (although it might be argued that, in practice, the equation was significantly weighted in ZCO’s favour).

Due to a shortage of sufficiently trained software engineers in Barbados, ZCOB recruited expatriates. ZCOB’s then President explained their hiring strategy as ‘import[ing] brain power from other parts of the world to help Barbados develop that talent internally’. By 1999, approximately two-thirds of the staff were expatriates, most notably from India (King, 1999). One advantage for ZCO in relocating these employees was the easier access to U.S. work permits from Barbados, at a time when quotas from India were being capped (ZCOB Management Report, 1999). ZCOB also sought to create a ‘worryfree, ready-built, turnkey life’ (Hopkins, 1998) for the expatriates by handling all their accommodation, transport and financial arrangements.

ZCOB at first proved to be very successful, growing rapidly to 350 staff, by 1998 (at this point approximately the same amount were stationed in the U.S.A. either in the ZCO headquarters or at client sites). The company promoted its ‘Computer Software Engineering Centres’ and onshore/offshore/nearshore approach, as a significant contributor to its success. Nearshore practitioners and customers familiar with the ZCO case attributed its allure to the proximity, similar time zones and other intangible aspects such as perceived cultural similarity that it shared with the US A :

It’s easier to visit either way – same day is good, just possible to do Barbados in 1 day. It makes a big difference in the psychological effect. London-Madras is a 12-h flight; there is a time-zone shift and culture [change]. Barbados is like the 51st state. (Nearshore Customer)

Clientele is still not too happy coming to India as they would to Barbados, still think India is a country of snake-charmers – Better to come to Barbados. (Former Employee 1)

During 1998 and 1999, however, ZCOB’s profits fell sharply and staff attrition became a problem, with numbers falling to 80 members by June 1999. ZCOB’s problems were attributed to excessively quick growth and, notably, the difficulty in selling Barbados as a destination for software development. One of the primary criticisms levelled at the company was its apparent failure to sell the nearshore concept, the very idea upon which it was predicated. The creation of a place like India seemed not to be convincing enough for it to be regarded in the same light as India:

It appeared that the challenge of selling the ‘near-shore’ programming alternative – the very thing [the CEO] had focused his energy on creating – was greater than anyone had expected. y It was hard enough getting companies to send their programming work offshore, to places like India, but that alternative was at least well understood. (Hopkins, 1999)

Interviews with former ZCO employees confirmed the press reports. For example, it was felt that the cost of the operation was not necessarily cheaper than offshore solutions and therefore other angles should have been considered in order to make the choice of Barbados look more attractive than that of India. Physical proximity, alone, was apparently not enough. It was suggested, for example, that the type of work undertaken by the organisation had not been tailored so as to take advantage of the nearshore locations.

Production support is do-able due to the time zone advantage as opposed to India’s time zone. Connectivity problems exist in India. Better to do this type of work in Barbados y . For application areas in which the client is on unfamiliar grounds e.g. e-commerce, the near-shore model seems to be a better fit. Other areas include – re-engineering, procedural things. These are the types of carrots that can be offered instead of trying to plug the cost advantage [of nearshore]. (Former Employee 1)

Develop any niche areas of expertise (nearshore environment especially good for this) creativity, high-paying business. (Former Employee 2)

In addition, it was felt that the cost of the overseas recruitment (and retention) necessary to create this ‘programmers’ paradise’, should have been considered in the business model.

[It is] difficult to retain Indian staff since most [of them] are intent on running away to the U.S.A. There are not half of these problems with the Caribbean staff; recruitment would be easier. The skill set can be brought up to speed if it is not yet in place. (Former Employee 2)

There were also apparent problems with the choice of location. Although the island had all the necessary criteria to make it a viable offshore alternative, the idea of building something ‘like India’ could not come to fruition, because of factors inherent to that site:

Problems with setting up in Barbados – Too high cost; Small economy (tourism/sugar) – responsible for driving up costs; Property prices affected etc.

y we should have chosen Trinidad/Jamaica; there would have been more business, more capital, more human resources and an Indian population [native to the island]. (Former Employee 3)

The expected bonus to the local economy from ZCOB’s success also seemed to be impaired by characteristics inherent to the community:

y if the host country does not have trained IT resources available, generation of new employment is not going to be too much. Materialisation of the advantages depends on the host country as well as the financial status and growth of the near shore development company. (Former Employee 4)

As an IBC, ZCOB was prevented from competing in the local market to protect local enterprises. As a result, they were unable to take advantage of local work, which led to underutilisation of staff when offshore contracts were in short supply. This also meant that, in practice, opportunities to transfer skills locally were very limited.

An incorrect government policy can limit advantages – in such a business you are getting higher technology experience for clients in more advanced countries – however, y government policy is that this expertise is not allowed to be used to service local clients y . In case of Barbados, for most of the near-shore development there is a total ban on local business. A more prudent country policy would be to allow at least say 25% of the revenue to be sourced from local business. (Former Employee 2)

As an epilogue to this story, in the middle of 1999, ZCOB changed its name and management and continued as a small (circa 80-person, with just over 200 in the ZCO units in the U.S.A.) software outsourcing company until its eventual demise in 2002.

## NCO, a Jamaican nearshore enterprise

NCO was established in 1995 by an Indian expatriate living in the U.S.A., in collaboration with a U.S. entrepreneur with the aim of establishing a ‘near shore alternative to offshore programming’ in the Caribbean to exploit its proximity to the U.S.A. Officials from a Jamaican government agency, the Montego Bay Free Zone, aware of NCO’s interest, approached the entrepreneurs with the possibility of making that investment in Jamaica. The island was eventually chosen because in addition to its proximity, it possessed a cheap, educated workforce of native English speakers and a functional telecommunications infrastructure. Having researched the problems encountered by ZCO’s Barbados nearshore operation, the founders decided on a different strategy for NCO:

y we felt that the way to build [the company] would be to establish a training program, develop our own workforce and then use them y we thought the government would become a bigger partner if they saw us training their peopl and keeping them on for the long haul. (CEO, NCO)

A training school, the Caribbean Software Institute (CSI), was therefore established in collaboration with the government of Jamaica (in the form of the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Communications and the Ministry of Education, through the national training agency, HEART) and Jamaican, U.S. and U.K. academic institutions (University of the West Indies, Jamaica, University of Technology, Jamaica, Furman University, South Carolina and University of Hertfordshire, U.K.). The curriculum was developed by the academic partners and was closely tied to NCO’s needs, focusing on the latest technological areas, such as web and Java programming. Applicants to the program were to possess at least a high-school education, but beyond that no computer literacy was mandated. As a result persons from all occupations and backgrounds were accepted into the program. The 10-month course was expected to bring the complete novice up to the standard of a computer programmer. An ‘incubation’ process, where the graduate would be given on-the-job training at a software development company, would then be initiated. NCO agreed to limit foreign recruitment to what was deemed necessary to staff the ‘incubator’. The depressed economy of Jamaica, that severely limited the opportunities available for educated Jamaicans in their chosen fields, enhanced the attractiveness of this approach:

We had the same, similar cost in Jamaica as India with a lot of unemployment and we thought that we could skim the top of the crop in Jamaica because we were going to offer money that was higher than they could make and in many cases 3 to 3.5 times higher than they could make in any other kind of technology job down there such as call centres. (Chairman, NCO)

NCO hired all the graduates of the first year and a significant percentage of the second group.

NCO provided the start-up money for the Institute and, with Furman University, continues to hold the controlling interest. The Jamaican governmental bodies provided support through infrastructure, operating costs and financing for the trainees, while the academic partners provided ongoing guidance on curriculum design and quality assurance, but the Jamaican government was seen as the ‘driving force’, mainly through the Minister of Trade, Industry and Communications, who gave it his personal endorsement and attention.

The collaboration has endured despite the complexity of the relationships among the partners. Interviewees attributed this to their level of commitment, both the government’s involvement in sponsoring and funding the project, and NCO’s follow-through in hiring the trained programmers even if ‘their utilization factor for external revenue was 50% or below’. Other contributing factors were the presence of senior representatives of the partnership on the managing board of the CSI and the chairmanship of that board by the government training agency HEART. One aspect that seemed to help was a common vision shared by the public and private partners that this project gave ‘the opportunity for an industry to develop to help [the Jamaican] economy’, a view embraced by a government pursuing the path towards diversification of the economy by ‘get[ting] our brighter Jamaicans to upscale into programming’. The need to develop the skills base of local Jamaicans through involvement in local projects was also seen to be important:

And it’s imperative that [the Jamaican] government bring in incubator programmes where the Jamaicans and companies like NCO are afforded some set-aside work, making sure that the industry can build a reputation and a core competence over time. (Chairman, NCO)

NCO aimed to have a diverse spread of clients to help build their capability and thus increase confidence in the nearshore idea and they initiated ‘partnering’ schemes with at least two of their customers, who pledged to help sell the ‘nearshore’ option. The company also had contracts and partnering arrangements with Caribbean and Jamaican ventures, including Jamaican government agencies, demonstrating the level of trust that had been achieved in the collaboration. Another strategy employed by the company was to get a foothold in the local market to use as a springboard for exporting services, taking advantage, for example, of e-government work sponsored by international aid agencies to grow their local talent.

NCO’s Jamaican location appeared to provide some of the expected advantages. For example, proximity was seen as improving communication, collaboration and control and the company executives were able to manage the venture from their base in the U.S.A., needing only a few visits per year to the island. In addition, aspects of the Jamaican culture, such as English being the main language were felt to be helpful, while it was considered that potential customers identified favourably with the familiar Jamaican environment:

You can leave Atlanta, Georgia, Charlotte, North Carolina and be [in Jamaica] in 2.5 h and you’re on the same time zone and you can stay in a Holiday Inn, you can stay in a Ritz Carlton, you can stay in named hotels with good venues while you’re doing your business in Jamaica. (Chairman, NCO)

Notwithstanding the island’s inherent locational advantages, the nearshore entrepreneurs did experience issues in convincing customers to send software work there:

y and one of NCO’s difficulties in attracting clients and business, has been that the image of the Caribbean certainly does not suggest high tech solutions to your business problems. Folks think of the Caribbean as a place to go and relax on a nice vacation, lay out on the beach, and Jamaica has a very laid-back and ‘no problem, Mon’ image in the U.S.A. (Member of Board, Furman University)

The company employed several strategies to overcome this problem. Customers were invited to come to the island to witness the facilities first hand and interact with the staff, something that was facilitated by the island’s proximity and congeniality. NCO sourced its outwardfacing staff from the U.S.A., people with experience in well-known U.S. corporations, but still linked somehow to the local context. For example, NCO’s most senior resident staff member was an expatriate Indian with experience in a U.S.-based firm and previous knowledge of living and working in Jamaica. The CSI also employed the strategy of hiring foreign expertise in the first instance to be replaced eventually by Jamaican staff. It was felt that a certain standard should be kept that would be internationally recognised. The local faculty, when interviewed, felt that they were contributing to the community through bringing in expertise gained from abroad.

At times, NCO’s policies towards attaining ‘international’ recognition seemed to contradict the stated intention of assimilating into the local environment. For example, they adopted North American business practices such as non-surveillance of staff, overtime work shared by both managers and staff to meet deadlines, arrangements for staff to stay overnight on-site and flexible working hours. In the words of one senior manager:

I think another really, really important point is the fact that we run this very much like a U.S. company in Jamaica as opposed to really a Jamaican company. (COO, NCO)

At times, also, for the local organisations interacting with NCO, their image was a distinctly ‘foreign’ one:

There is nothing negative about NCO being a foreign-based company, hiring local and being called a foreign company, that’s what we are encouraging. That’s what we are abroad. (NCO Local Customer)

At the time of the study, numbers of staff had risen from an initial 15 to close to 100, 83 of whom were CSI graduates. NCO’s presence in the U.S.A. amounted only to the executive team of three at the time of the study. The majority of the staff were Jamaican, in technical as well as administrative positions, with expatriate Indians making up only about 10% of the total workforce, although most of these were in senior positions. The venture continues to thrive and has achieved SEI CMM Level 4 competence in software engineering (Zagada, 2005) and has partnerships with leading U.S. and Caribbean companies, specialising in products related to tourism and the hospitality industry.

## Case analyses

Two separate, but related, themes emerged from the analysis of the case studies. One theme focused on the characteristics seen as affecting the location decision. Most of the characteristics found in the data are similar to those identified in the IB/IS literatures, although our analysis provides a more contextual understanding of these characteristics in the nearshore setting than has been conventionally defined in the literature.

The second theme that emerged from the data was more related to how these characteristics informed the location decision. Some characteristics, for example, were evaluated by the companies as explicit criteria in their location decision, while others were only discovered, during the process of developing the sites, to be desirable or useful. Other characteristics were generally negatively viewed with regard to the location decision and the companies actively sought to change these in order to enhance the viability of software production in this location. Such a distinction between characteristics, in terms of the way in which they informed the location decision, does not appear to have been made previously in the IB or IS location literatures. We term these new types of characteristic espoused, unanticipated and remediable as shown in Table 3. While these categories were not mutually exclusive (an unanticipated characteristic could be remediable, for example), they most aptly represented the predominant type of influence of different characteristics. Below, we discuss the cross-case analyses in terms of how characteristics were seen to have influenced the nearshore location decision (using the terminology from Table 3). ## Espoused characteristics

A primary, espoused motivation for both firms in choosing Caribbean locations was to take advantage of the convenience of travel and similarity in time zones that the proximity of these islands afforded. Both firms appeared to benefit from this closeness through quicker, timelier access to and from the U.S.A. and better remote management of communication, coordination and control issues.

Economics is a key driver of offshoring in general, and cost was, not surprisingly, reported as a critical factor in both firms’ choice of location. It was assumed that by setting up development centres in countries with per capita incomes considerably lower than those in the U.S.A., they could attract well-qualified employees. In addition, the wage rates (higher than those in the limited alternative occupations on the islands) would enable the firms to be highly competitive with onshore production and comparable to offshore production, but without the latter’s perceived disadvantages.

The importance of a well-educated local workforce was also recognised, especially by NCO. The existence of wellestablished secondary and tertiary education systems on the islands, based, due to their colonial history, on a wellrecognised British model, was therefore seen as desirable, even if not, in ZCO’s case, essential. That the state of the local economy, particularly in Jamaica, meant that there was substantial under-utilisation of this educated workforce, moreover, further enhanced the competitiveness of the firms’ wage costs and their ability to select the best employees. The quality of the infrastructure in terms of the telecommunications system and the easily accessible international airports in Barbados and Montego Bay also figured significantly in the decision to choose these locations.

The government played a pivotal role in providing a facilitating political environment. Thus Barbados’ reputation for political stability made it particularly attractive for ZCO. Also supportive were the governments’ very favourable investment terms of the IBC incentives in Barbados and the tax free allowances and investment park privileges of the Montego Bay Free Zone. Concessions such as these were helpful in enabling the firms initial start-up. In addition, favourable government policies, for example, on work permits for ZCOB or providing government-sponsored higher education training for NCO, were useful in assisting these enterprises with their specific models of nearshore implementation.

## Unanticipated characteristics

It was found that many locational features that proved to be significant in the development of these ventures were initially unanticipated by the nearshore vendors. For instance, the attraction of Barbados and Montego Bay, as major tourist destinations, which did not significantly influence the initial decisions to set up there, emerged as a source of unexpected benefit in practice. In the case of ZCO, it helped to make the location attractive to programmers and business people alike. The programmers were attracted by the opportunity to work alongside colleagues on software projects for U.S. customers while staying in idyllic surroundings, while the clients were attracted by the pleasantness of an environment that had a ‘51st state’ congeniality. NCO’s executives reported similar success in attracting clients to visit Montego Bay because of its familiar setting, for example with internationally recognised hotels.

In addition, the islands’ economic weakness and dependence on financial inputs from more developed countries, that may be argued to be a legacy of colonialism, gave an advantage to international investors such as the nearshore entrepreneurs. In the ZCOB case, this enabled them to establish a ‘company town’ with few linkages to the local community, while NCO adopted American management styles and externally oriented training objectives at CSI.

The existence of government policies that were complementary to the nearshore vendors, such as alignment with the island governments’ objectives towards expanding the information technology sector, worked to the two firms’ benefit. This was most noticeable in the NCO case, with the proactive role of the Montego Bay Free Zone authority in approaching the entrepreneurs, the commitment of the Ministry of Communication, the government-led bodies’ support for CSI and the government’s sustained resource and financial commitments. NCO also benefited from local software projects funded by international development aid in its initiatives to ‘incubate’ local programmers.

The contribution of the islands’ status as former British colonies to the educational standard of the workforce has already been identified, but this heritage also influenced perceptions of the islands’ political and economic stability and the quality of the telecommunications infrastructure, which had been installed by a British telecommunications company that had been operating in the region for over 100 years. The existence of these linkages created some degree of legitimacy and reassurance for the entrepreneurs and provided a basis on which to build client relationships (Table 5).

## Remediable characteristics

Notwithstanding the advantageous, if unanticipated, characteristics discussed above, some issues still needed to be resolved. The touristic allure of the islands, for example, which meant that they were more associated with relaxing on the beach than with high-tech work, reduced their credibility as sites for software development. ZCOB sought to overcome this perception by creating its ‘programmer’s paradise’ with imported Indian staff and thereby gaining reputation by association with the well-recognised Indian offshore industry. The weakness of this approach was revealed, however, by the expatriates’ use of ZCOB as a stepping stone to the U.S.A., rather than feeling any ties to their new location. These constructed cultural associations similarly failed to hold for ZCOB’s clients whose loyalty to the concept failed to match that for the ‘well understood’ Indian model.

NCO, on the other hand, tackled the image issue by cultivating a U.S.-based outward-facing interface with clients, training local staff in U.S. business etiquette, and promoting U.S.-inspired management techniques, which differentiated them from other ‘Jamaican-run’ companies (as could be easily demonstrated to clients, thanks to the accessible location). At the same time, they also attempted to develop local expertise on local projects so as to build confidence in the Jamaicans’ abilities.

The lack of suitably trained human resources and a significant local software market was similarly addressed in different ways by the two companies. ZCOB developed an export-oriented and externally dependent approach even allowing client investors to influence the design and infrastructure of the buildings so as to align them more with their standards. This made them vulnerable, however, to changes in external market demand as they lacked a local client base (further exacerbating the problem of staff retention).

NCO’s approach to this issue was to develop local expertise and create linkages to the local market, for example through the initial ‘incubation’ of local employees by highly skilled, imported technical staff. In addition, NCO’s ‘partnering’ schemes with government and local agencies helped ensure political commitment to the project and ongoing local work relevant to, and embedded in, the Jamaican context. ## Discussion

The discussion examines the implications of the case analyses for three interrelated issues which relate back to the theory presented earlier in this paper: (1) the interplay between place- and space-based logics in the nearshore location decision; (2) the applicability of IB/IS location characteristics in nearshore location decisions; and (3) the contextual variability of location characteristics.

## (1) Place- and space-based logics in nearshore location decisions

The case analysis suggests that nearshore location decisions are significantly influenced by a ‘placeless logic’ (Sahay et al, 2003). Thus, both companies reported that their choice of location was primarily focused on locationally substitutable generic characteristics (Cox, 1997), similar to those identified in the IB and IS literatures as necessary and sufficient conditions for a viable offshore venture. From this perspective any location possessing these generic, noncontextual features would be as good as any other for the establishment of the nearshoring strategy. This was particularly evident in the case of ZCOB which paid little attention to the particular character of Barbados as a location.

Similarly, while NCO paid greater attention to place, especially with respect to the development of local skills and markets, their strategy was the product of two entrepreneurs seeking a cheap and convenient location for their venture, with no particular allegiance to Jamaica over any other. That the initiative of the Montego Bay Free Zone agency officials alerted them to its suitability and that NCO subsequently sought to develop close links with the locale, came after the fact of the entrepreneurs’ adoption of a ‘placeless logic’ that rendered the idea of nearshore development conceivable in the first place. Had the founders of NCO not subscribed, at least to some extent, to the idea that the world has become ‘flat’, as Friedman (2005) puts it, and that a software development centre can operate as effectively in the Caribbean, a region with little trackrecord in the field, as it could in more established offshore destinations, then they would not have considered a site there in the first place. Without their international connections and experience it might also have been considerably harder for them to set up the initiative. While, over time, NCO’s strategy proved to be much more sensitive to location than ZCO’s, therefore, the logic of placelessness was nevertheless influential on the decision to set up in a location not previously recognised as a venue for software development.

As the cases also illustrated, however, the pursuit of placelessness does not mean that ventures could ignore the influence of location. ZCOB’s ‘enclave’ approach (Heeks, 1999) still had social, economic and political effects on Barbados that had repercussions for the company, such as increased local costs with negligible effect on local employment. The lack of engagement with the local community and economy were also seen to have contributed to ZCOB’s later difficulties. The unintended consequences of the firm’s effect on the generic location characteristics contributed to its failure, a situation that further highlights the contradiction between the placeless logic and the variability in practice of those very locational characteristics on which such choices had been made.

It was not just the espoused characteristics of a location that affected its suitability, but also what we have labelled the unanticipated ones. Thus nearshore entrepreneurs setting up their software development centres in the Caribbean may not have considered these characteristics when choosing where to base their operations, but, once established, they became aware of their influence. Indeed pursuing a ‘placeless logic’ that ignores the particularity of a location may mean that these unanticipated characteristics become an obstacle to the success of a venture. Thus ZCOB’s attempt to use Barbados’ touristic allure to create a ‘programmer’s paradise’ resulted in employees being disembedded from the local context and unable to identify with it. Similarly, they failed to recognise the potential advantage of the indigenous Indian population in other Caribbean islands, such as Trinidad, in providing a culturally compatible environment for their expatriate staff.

The lack of attention to specific local characteristics also meant that ZCOB failed to exploit endemic features of its chosen location. Thus practitioners suggested that ZCOB might have done better to have focused on certain types of software proiects that might be better suited to features associated with the Caribbean locale, such as the ability to visit frequently from the U.S., the existing technical capabilities of the available workforce or the opportunity of specialising in some niche area related to the Caribbean experience, such as the tourism and the hospitality industry work with which NCO was successful.

This predominantly placeless approach also meant that the Barbados government did not play an active role in the ZCO venture, but rather offered exemptions from local taxes and regulations that served to further disconnect ZCO from the local economy. Indeed, their generous tax breaks reduced the economic benefits that the country might have gained from attracting nearshore investment, illustrating the relationship between placelessness and powerlessness (Castells, 1996).

NCO’s strategy, in contrast, was generally more differentiated and integrated with local conditions, as the strategies of ‘incubation’ and ‘partnering’ attest. The positive results of this were demonstrated in many ways, including: active government support and commitment (financially and politically); demonstrable ‘upskilling’ of economically disadvantaged workers; sustainable impact on the economy through encouraging new investment and providing employment; and contribution to economic development of the country through involvement in international development projects.

The case analysis may therefore be seen as illustrating that the establishment and development of these two nearshore ventures was influenced by both a logic of placelessness and by specific characteristics of the locations in which they were set up. While contradictions between space and place-based logics may have been a source of some difficulties for the ventures, the findings suggest that the two are not mutually exclusive, rather there is a complex interplay between them as Sahay et al (2003) argue, which would seem particularly evident in the nearshore context. Thus, while the establishment of the nearshore ventures may have been inspired by a logic of placelessness, which suggests that software development can be located anywhere with an Internet connection (Heeks, 1999), the pursuit of this approach meant that ZCO had little place-like significance, as the expatriate recruits illustrated by their lack of allegiance to Barbados, while NCO’s pursuit of a place-based approach enhanced the placelessness of the venture, creating a skilled local labour force attuned to North American work practices.

Despite facing what might have appeared to be the same environmental location factors, the two companies, responded to and managed these location factors in very different ways, with different outcomes. The reasons why they perceived the same factors differently, why they adopted different responses and the effect of those responses were highly related to their chosen nearshore strategies. Thus, ZCO chose a predominantly placeless strategy motivated by the need to grow and establish an effective workforce quickly in a location closer than India. ZCO’s ‘programmers’ paradise’ that just happened to be located in Barbados meant that they gained little benefit from the particular characteristics of their location beyond those endowed by its physical location, such as climate, touristic allure and proximity to the U.S.A. ZCO was therefore weakly rooted in its location and ran into difficulties when the IB environment with which it had sought to connect itself became less favourable.

NCO, in contrast, while subscribing to a logic of placelessness to some degree in deciding to establish itself in a non-traditional location, actively sought to build links with its locality and to work with the Jamaican government to enhance its position. This more place-based strategy reflected, to some extent, the NCO entrepreneurs’ observations of the problems experienced by ZCO, but also a greater recognition of the significance of location for the venture’s success and their consequent attempts to remedy perceived deficiencies of the location. Contrary to the claims of some proponents of placelessness, location, it seems, still does matter. In effect, both companies exhibited an interplay of place and placeless logics in their decision-making, but the more place-based strategies proved, in the end, to be more sustainable for these ventures.

## (2) The applicability of IB/IS location characteristics in nearshore location decisions

As Table 7 shows, many of the locational characteristics identified in the nearshore cases could be associated with comparable IB/IS characteristics. In general, however, the former were less tangible and more qualitative than presented in the IB/IS literature, as Schmenner (1979) argued. For example, in the unanticipated category economic weakness, although associated with the IB/IS characteristics ‘Economic system’ and ‘Linkages’ were not evaluated so much on a quantitative measure of the size of the island economy or its GDP, but rather on a qualitative assessment of the global economic positioning of an island whose historical colonial legacy placed it in a relatively exploitable position with regard to foreign influences. The case findings also identify new variants of established IB/IS characteristics, such as ‘attractive environment’ and ‘cultural similarity’. Thus attractiveness in the context of the nearshore software ventures was concerned more with the aesthetics of the physical environment, rather than of the business environment, while cultural similarity was not just about national or business culture, but had a more historical dimension relating to educational and social traditions such as the legacy of British standards in shaping the institutional environment, for example in education and politics. Such subtleties were not necessarily recognised in the initial location decision, but emerged as significant over time as experience of working in the location grew. The findings thus supplement and modify the range of location characteristics discussed in the literature, suggesting that a richer conception of some, if not all, of these characteristics is needed if we are to understand their influence in practice. The findings illustrate that the practical influence of these characteristics is rarely reducible to static, quantitative measures.

(3) The contextual variability of location characteristics The cases also illustrated that characteristics of location may be highly specific, suggesting that assessments of opportunities at a regional, or even national, level may be potentially misleading. Thus the islands in the Caribbean, despite some commonalities, each offer distinctive characteristics, such as Trinidad’s significant Indian population, and even within Jamaica, the characteristics of the Montego Bay Free Zone differ from the rest of the island. Consideration of location characteristics therefore needs to be made at a higher level of detail than the IB/IS literature has generally adopted.

The cases also demonstrate that some characteristics of a location are open to change (remediable). In one sense this is what the placeless logic suggests – with enough money any locational disadvantage can be overcome. What this does not perhaps account for is that, in a risky and cost-driven industry, such investments lower competitiveness and reduce the chances that investments will be repaid. Firms may also have limited abilities to achieve such changes on their own and may need to work with local partners. Having few attachments to the locality, as ZCO did, may make this difficult and NCO’s more cautious and collaborative strategy seemed to be more effective.

## Limitations

While the two case studies provide some insight on the, as yet, under-researched field of nearshore software outsourcing, they cannot be claimed to be necessarily representative of practices in this area, and the findings must therefore be considered provisional. In addition, these are two case studies of a very specific phenomenon under very specific circumstances. Thus, the findings cannot be said to be generalisable to a larger population of similar cases. The study also concentrates on the location decision of software providers when selecting sites for offshore development; however, this is but one consideration among many when instituting offshore software development arrangements. Despite these shortcomings, the findings indicate that there may be significant opportunities for further studies to explore the complex interplay of ideas of placelessness and the specifics of location involved in nearshore, and perhaps also offshore, outsourcing.

## Conclusions

This paper raised two questions as motivating the study: how do nearshore service providers select locations for software development given their particular reliance on proximity as a strategy for delivery, and how important are these characteristics for the sustainability of their ventures. Our findings from the two Caribbean cases suggest that location selection by nearshore software vendors is based on both place and space-based logics. Placelessness can be seen as a necessary prerequisite for nearshore software development as it creates the possibility of considering the establishment of a nearshore venture in a location without a recognised software industry, indeed in any location that can provide suitable connectivity to international telecommunications and transport networks. Yet nearshore software development is also based on the assumption that location does matter and that there can be advantages in being closer, geographically and temporally, to clients. Looked at in more detail, the findings also show that the nearshore entrepreneurs considered a range of location characteristics in their investment decisions most of which were similar to those in the IB/IS literatures. When these characteristics are compared, however, to the static, immutable, quantitative conceptualisations in the IB/IS literatures, it emerges that this paper’s typology of espoused, unanticipated and remediable derived from the nearshore findings provides a more contextual and qualitative understanding of the location decision. Our typology suggests that location characteristics may be: (1) dynamic, being initially unanticipated and discovered during the setting up of the venture; (2) mutable, varying over time and responsive to remediation efforts by the nearshore venture and its partners; and (3) qualitative, not reducible to a single number, but based on a broad assessment of a range of attributes of a location. Furthermore, the findings demonstrate that the interplay between the qualitative features of these characteristics and the nearshore strategy chosen affected the eventual outcome of the enterprise, thus suggesting an as yet unexplored relationship between the two concepts.

The rich case evidence on the site selection strategies of two pioneering nearshore software development ventures in the Caribbean, presented in this paper therefore contributes to the literature on offshore software development in several ways. First, it adds to the sparse literature about offshoring from the service providers’ (as opposed to the clients’) perspective, providing insight into the strategies adopted in implementing their service offerings and positioning themselves for particular markets. Second, it has uncovered the characteristics that shaped the decision to locate a development centre in a nearshore (and non-traditional) site, highlighting the significance of the chosen location to the feasibility of the ventures. Third, the cases presented in this paper have illustrated both the power and the limitations of prominent globalisation discourses and IB/IS location literatures in accounting for the strategies of the two nearshore software development vendors, and has provided evidence that both space- and place-based logics are influential in nearshore location decisions. Fourth, the analysis has offered new perspectives on the list of characteristics influencing location decisions offered by the IB/IS literature. seeking to offer a richer and more qualitative account of their operation in practice. Fifth, the findings highlight the variation in these characteristics at a much greater level of detail than the literature has generally acknowledged and has shown that they are not static, time-bound, immutable characteristics, but may be actively managed by actors involved in offshore ventures. Finally, our typology of characteristics as espoused, unanticipated and remediable, extends the understanding of locational decisions in the IB and IS literatures by suggesting that the reasons initially cited for choosing a particular location (espoused characteristics) may provide only a partial account of the influence of location on the viability of a particular venture.

With regard to practical implications of the study, the cases illustrate important considerations in the selection of new locations for offshore software development, especially in contexts where there is little prior involvement in such work (as is generally the case for nearshore sites). With many companies becoming concerned at the declining advantage of established offshore locations and many new locations seeking to establish offshore service offerings, these insights are both timely and significant. Thus, the evidence presented in this paper suggests that vendors considering establishing nearshore, and maybe also offshore, development centres and clients engaging with such vendors, need to treat notions of placelessness with caution. While they are an essential component of the case for offshoring, this does not make location irrelevant. Rather it is in the interplay of placelessness with the specificity of the particular that strategies for nearshoring are implemented.

For example, sites are likely to need not only the easilyidentified and often quantifiable basic infrastructure and physical characteristics that proponents of placelessness suggest are all that is needed for software development, but also less-tangible and qualitative conditions, such as supportive cultural and socio-political environments, if they are to succeed. Some of these characteristics, moreover, may be highly localised, suggesting that opportunities may be quite place-specific. General country-level location characteristics, therefore, may be useful for a preliminary appraisal of a country’s suitability but more contextual knowledge may be necessary to establish the viability of a particular sourcing model succeeding in the chosen environment, especially if that environment is not traditionally associated with offshore software development work. This would seem likely to require either personal familiarity with specific locations through visits (which would also serve to illustrate the convenience, or otherwise, of the site) or, at least, interaction with those with relevant experience.

## Appendix

The interview protocol consists of a set of semi-structured questions that were considered relevant to pose to the participants of the study in relation to the research questions being investigated. The questions were asked in a conversational manner, and there were deviations from the protocol depending on the answers given by the respondent. These deviations occurred, for example, when more information was required about a specific topic, thus there was a need for more in-depth discussion on the matter and further un-scripted questions would be asked. These additional questions, while contained in transcripts, are not contained in the interview protocol.

Section 1: (History and background of the enterprise and its founders)

Representative questions:

1. Describe the organisational structure, local linkages, history of the founding of the company, executive board and other background information about the company.

2. Describe the alliances formed within othis country4 and the relevance to the foundation of the company.

3. Describe the roles played by local government in the foundation of the company.

## Section 2: (Motivation for nearshoring) Representative questions:

1. Explain the main motivations for picking ocountry4 as a site for nearshoring.

2. Describe in your own words what you perceive the near-shore software development model to be.

3. Explain your company’s general philosophy in developing this model.

Section 3: (The development, and sustaining features, of the nearshore enterprise) Representative questions:

1. Describe the perceived advantages of this model for clients.

2. Describe the perceived advantages to the offshore development effort.

3. Describe the perceived advantages to the development of the country hosting the near-shore facility.

4. Are these advantages realised in practice?

5. Describe some of the drawbacks to this model.

Section 4: (How the interviewees interpreted and practiced the nearshore concept)

## Representative questions:

1. What factors should be considered when implementing a near-shore development model?

2. What should be the main areas of focus for such an organisation?

3. What type of work is best suited for this model?

4. What type of clientele works best with this model? Describe customer base and nature of any alliances formed with customers.

5. How did customers perceive the nearshoring model, what worked and didn’t work with customers.

6. What types of people are best suited to work within these environments? Describe recruitment and retention strategies, team organisation, mixture of local to foreign staffing levels and reasons for this.

7. Describe local and foreign markets and types of projects developed.

8. How did the local governmental agencies respond to the venture?

9. Is the company considered a local or a foreign entity and why?

10. Describe the organisational culture, contrast this with a typical ocountry4 company.

11. How did cultural similarity/difference play a role in the relationship with the clients/foreign and domestic staff?

Section 5: (The progress of the nearshore model over time) Representative questions:

1. How did the nearshoring model change over time?

2. How sustainable was the concept and why?

3. What are the lessons learnt from the outcome of the nearshoring enterprise?
